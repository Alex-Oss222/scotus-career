## Decisions and Dispositions

### Consolidated Rail Corp. v. Gottshall, No. 92-1956, including Carlisle under Rule 12.2

Merits decision, June 24, 1994

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued February 28, 1994; decided June 24, 1994. On writ of certiorari to the Third Circuit, reviewing Gottshall, 988 F.2d 355, and Carlisle, 990 F.2d 90. The question is whether and within what boundary FELA permits recovery for negligently inflicted emotional injury, with separate application to Gottshall's summary-judgment record and Carlisle's verdict.

The Third Circuit reversed summary judgment against Gottshall and remanded for trial; it sustained Carlisle's $386,500 verdict. Conrail seeks reversal of both. Gottshall's evidence concerns severe heat, work pressure, a communication failure, unsuccessful efforts to revive his longtime coworker and ensuing psychiatric illness. The summary-judgment record does not establish contested circumstances as facts; water was available, and the extent of breaks and shade remains disputed. Carlisle presented prolonged shifts, staffing and equipment problems, repeated warnings and medical evidence. The jury received ordinary negligence instructions. Testimony concerning coworkers' health was admitted for notice, not substantive proof that Conrail caused their illnesses.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg participated at argument and decision. No case-specific nonparticipation is established.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses both Third Circuit judgments, 5–4, and remands with separate directions: first-instance application of the zone-of-danger rule in Gottshall and entry of judgment for Conrail in Carlisle.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gottshall: judgment reversing summary judgment and allowing trial | Reversed and remanded, 5–4 | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, Ginsburg would affirm | Third Circuit must apply the zone-of-danger rule in the first instance; no exposure, causation or ultimate liability finding is made here. |
| Carlisle: judgment sustaining the $386,500 verdict | Reversed and remanded, 5–4 | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, Ginsburg would affirm | Enter judgment for Conrail because this work-stress claim is outside the rule; no new trial on the same theory. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Negligent emotional injury is actionable within the employee's zone of danger

**Controlling proposition:** FELA permits recovery for negligently inflicted emotional injury when the employee sustains physical impact from the employer's negligence or is placed in immediate risk of physical harm by that negligence; on the threatened-impact branch, the emotional injury must result from fear of physical harm to the employee himself. The employee must still establish negligence, legally cognizable injury and FELA causation; seriousness, genuineness and foreseeability alone do not substitute for the physical-danger boundary.

**Authority:** Justice Thomas's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter. Five of nine participating Justices directly adopt the same operative rule; no combination of separate rationales supplies its force.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that FELA includes qualifying emotional injury without making railroads insurers against every psychological consequence of employment. Buell left this boundary open while preserving otherwise actionable FELA claims against displacement by labor arbitration. Urie establishes that statutory injury extends beyond sudden accidents to occupational disease; it does not eliminate limits on negligent emotional injury. Monessen gives substantial weight to the common-law setting of the statute without freezing every rule at enactment. The zone-of-danger rule accommodates injury without actual impact while tying recovery to the employee's own physical safety. Ellis retains the requirement of negligence, and Rogers's causal-contribution rule does not replace the distinct boundary of responsibility. The contrary approach correctly insists on genuine injury and fault, but those requirements alone do not sufficiently delimit liability for cumulative workplace stress or distress at another's peril. This rule resolves an open question through federal negligence law. The Court does not decide intentional infliction, every fear-of-future-disease claim, or whether physical manifestations must accompany every otherwise qualifying emotional injury.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Atchison, Topeka & Santa Fe Railway Co. v. Buell, 480 U.S. 557 (1987): answers its reserved negligent-emotional-injury question; its protection of independently cognizable FELA claims from RLA displacement remains intact.
- Urie v. Thompson, 337 U.S. 163 (1949): preserves occupational-disease coverage; distinguishes that coverage from the boundary for negligent emotional harm.
- Monessen Southwestern Railway Co. v. Morgan, 486 U.S. 330 (1988): applies the importance of common-law background without making every tort rule immutable.
- Ellis v. Union Pacific Railroad Co., 329 U.S. 649 (1947): applies the requirement of negligence and rejection of insurer liability.
- Rogers v. Missouri Pacific Railroad Co., 352 U.S. 500 (1957): preserves FELA's causal-contribution rule without extending it to eliminate duty, breach or the compensable-injury boundary.

##### Gottshall's exposure and causal application remain for the court below

**Controlling proposition:** The Third Circuit's Gottshall judgment is reversed because it did not apply the zone-of-danger rule, and the case is remanded for that court to apply the rule in the first instance. The Court determines neither whether Gottshall was placed in immediate risk of physical harm nor whether his emotional injury resulted from fear of harm to himself; witnessing and attempting to prevent his coworker's death alone do not establish those predicates.

**Authority:** Justice Thomas's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter. The same five Justices adopt this application and remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a new legal boundary must be applied to the actual summary-judgment record before liability can be resolved. Buell's reservation of the emotional-injury question did not establish that any genuine psychiatric injury warranted trial under ordinary negligence alone. Gottshall offered evidence concerning severe heat, the crew's work demands, failed radio communication, his rescue efforts and subsequent illness. The significance of the working conditions and his own exposure remains unresolved; those matters cannot be replaced by the fact that another worker died. Nor does proof of a serious illness establish that the illness arose from fear for Gottshall's own safety. The zone-of-danger application was not adequately briefed and argued here, so the Third Circuit must perform that task first. Urie's recognition of occupational injury does not justify deciding disputed exposure in this Court. Reversal therefore orders further application proceedings, neither reinstatement of Conrail's summary judgment nor an award of damages or a guaranteed trial.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Buell, 480 U.S. 557: its reservation requires resolution and application of the governing injury boundary, rather than treating emotional injury as already fully defined.
- Urie, 337 U.S. 163: occupational-injury coverage remains effective but supplies no finding about Gottshall's immediate danger or the cause of his distress.

##### Carlisle's work-stress claim is excluded and requires judgment for Conrail

**Controlling proposition:** A FELA negligent-emotional-injury claim arising from the prolonged work stress presented in Carlisle, without physical impact or immediate risk of physical harm within the zone-of-danger rule, is not compensable. The Third Circuit's judgment sustaining the $386,500 verdict is reversed, and the case is remanded with instructions to enter judgment for Conrail, not to retry the same excluded claim.

**Authority:** Justice Thomas's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter. Five Justices adopt both the legal exclusion and mandatory defendant-judgment remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Carlisle's claim fails the legal boundary for this category of injury even accepting the evidence of serious illness and ordinary negligence. Ellis distinguishes a negligence statute from insurance, and Monessen requires attention to the common-law limits informing FELA liability. The trial court charged duty, breach, foreseeability and causation; the Court does not reverse by imagining that those instructions or supporting evidence were absent. Carlisle presented prolonged hours, staffing and equipment difficulties, repeated warnings and medical evidence linking his breakdown to work. Those circumstances do not put this work-stress claim within the physical-impact or immediate-danger category adopted today. Rogers's causal-contribution rule cannot supply that missing predicate merely because work contributed to the illness. The competing view would preserve the verdict through ordinary reasonable-care limits, but the Court rejects that liability boundary. The resulting error is categorical, so another trial of the same theory would not cure it. Judgment must be entered for Conrail; the Court neither assesses a different damages amount nor resolves an unpresented injury category.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Ellis, 329 U.S. 649: applies the distinction between fault-based liability and workplace insurance without discounting the evidence actually offered.
- Monessen, 486 U.S. 330: applies common-law guidance to the compensable-injury boundary, not to reweigh a jury's supported factual findings.
- Rogers, 352 U.S. 500: preserves relaxed causal contribution but does not let causation evidence replace the required injury category.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Buell's reserved question is resolved within its existing protection of independent FELA rights. Urie's occupational-disease rule, Ellis's negligence requirement and Rogers's causation standard remain effective. Monessen supplies common-law guidance without making every tort rule immutable. No earlier holding is overruled. Hawaiian Airlines, June 20, 1994, concerns independence from RLA displacement and supplies no different emotional-injury boundary.

Effective June 24, 1994, the zone-of-danger rule governs FELA negligent-emotional-injury claims. A threatened impact must concern immediate danger to the employee himself, and the emotional injury on that branch must arise from fear of harm to himself. An impact-only bar and freestanding bystander recovery outside that zone are rejected. Ordinary negligence and FELA causation remain necessary. The Court reserves intentional infliction, unpresented future-disease categories and whether physical manifestations are indispensable in every qualifying case. It does not decide Gottshall's actual exposure or ultimate entitlement. The dissents' broader reasonable-care standards and Souter's further methodological explanation do not add separate liability rules.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Souter concurs and joins the Court's entire opinion. Federal courts must develop FELA negligence law with attention to the evolution of common-law principles; choosing the zone-of-danger rule is an exercise of that responsibility, not a declaration that all tort law was frozen in 1908. Buell's reserved question and the developmental approach recognized in Kernan v. American Dredging Co., 355 U.S. 426 (1958), support that account. The concurrence adopts no liability beyond the Court's boundary.

Justice Ginsburg dissents, joined by Justices Blackmun and Stevens. They would affirm both judgments because FELA's injury language and Urie's occupational-disease principle encompass serious, genuine psychiatric harm directly sustained from negligently unsafe railway employment without immediate physical peril. Buell leaves the issue open for a fact-sensitive federal negligence rule. They require unreasonable conditions, reasonable foreseeability, genuine serious injury and FELA causation; an employment relationship, a diagnosis or distress alone does not establish liability. The medical and physical evidence supports genuineness here, but they leave claims without physical manifestations undecided. Carlisle's actual negligence instructions and evidence answer the suggestion that his verdict rested only on severity of illness. Coworker testimony establishes notice, not that Conrail caused those coworkers' illnesses. Their proposed affirmances preserve Gottshall's opportunity for trial and Carlisle's existing verdict, without determining Gottshall's liability or adopting a separate general bystander rule.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela dissents separately and would affirm both judgments. His proposed rule requires serious injury attributable to directly experienced conditions the employer unreasonably failed to address despite an objectively foreseeable risk of serious harm. Ordinary demands, disappointments and personnel disputes do not become negligent because an unusually vulnerable employee suffers; knowledge of particular vulnerability can affect reasonable precautions without making the employer an insurer. Clinical evidence, contemporaneous complaints, duration, similar incidents and available precautions are relevant evidence, not mandatory elements. Section 51's injury language, its altered common-law defenses, Urie and Buell permit this development, although it expands common-law restrictions and sacrifices some predictability. Rogers changes causation, not duty or breach. Gottshall's emergency involvement and alleged conditions warrant trial, not a liability finding. Carlisle's exceptional sustained conditions, warnings, medical causation evidence and actual negligence charge support retaining the verdict. The ordinary negligence inquiry and meaningful legal-sufficiency review should replace a free-floating authenticity screen. The Chief Justice issues no workplace decree and does not reopen unrelated matters in Carlisle.

### Shannon v. United States, No. 92-8346

Merits decision, June 24, 1994

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 22, 1994; decided June 24, 1994. On writ of certiorari to the Fifth Circuit, 981 F.2d 759, which affirmed Shannon's conviction under 18 U.S.C. §922(g)(1). The question is whether a federal defendant who properly raises insanity and requests clarification is entitled to an accurate explanation of the special insanity verdict's immediate commitment consequence.

After an August 25, 1990 firearm incident in which he shot himself, Shannon survived and was prosecuted for firearm possession as a felon. He presented the statutory insanity defense and requested a short commitment instruction or, alternatively, a longer hospitalization instruction. The trial court instead directed the jury not to consider punishment or consequences. No prosecutor or witness is reported to have represented that an insanity acquittee would immediately go free. The jury had no sentencing role, and its actual reasoning is not established. The instruction requests were preserved. The Fifth Circuit denied both entitlement and trial-court discretion; review of the judgment does not require adopting that categorical rule.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg participated at argument and decision. No case-specific nonparticipation is established.

#### Judgment

The Court affirms the Fifth Circuit's judgment, 6–3. Refusal of the requested commitment-consequences instruction was not error on this record.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fifth Circuit judgment affirming Shannon's conviction | Affirmed, 6–3 | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens would vacate and remand for Rule 52(a) review under their proposed instruction rule | Conviction remains in place; no Supreme Court-directed new trial, insanity verdict, commitment or release. |

Affirmance rests on absence of instructional error. The Court neither applies harmless-error review nor adopts the lower court's categorical denial of corrective authority.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Souter | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg | Affirmance on no-error grounds | Entire opinion: no statutory command, no general supervisory requirement, accurate corrective reservation and its application. |
| Dissent | Stone-Zsela | Blackmun, Stevens | Would vacate and remand | Entire proposed limited requested-explanation rule and preserved nonconstitutional harmless-error remedy, including all accuracy and scope qualifications. |

Each Court holding has six direct joins. The dissent supplies no controlling doctrine, and no Marks inquiry is required.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The Act does not require a commitment-consequences instruction

**Controlling proposition:** When insanity is properly raised in a federal criminal trial, 18 U.S.C. §4242(b) requires the prescribed verdict choices, but §§4242–4243 do not require an instruction explaining the commitment and release consequences of a verdict of not guilty only by reason of insanity. The mandatory initial commitment and subsequent judicial procedures do not guarantee any period of hospitalization and do not transfer disposition decisions to the guilt jury.

**Authority:** Justice Souter's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Ginsburg. Six of nine participating Justices directly adopt this statutory construction.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Act's directions about verdicts and custody do not enact a consequences-instruction requirement. Section 4242(b) directs the use of the special verdict when insanity is properly raised. Section 4243(a) requires initial commitment, followed by examination and a judicial hearing within forty days under separate release standards. Those provisions assign tasks to different decisionmakers; their existence does not require explaining every later task to the guilt jury. The contrary argument treats the special verdict's practical importance as if Congress had directed an explanatory charge. It did not. The committee report's endorsement of a practice in which an instruction may be given is not an enacted command, and its treatment of requested omission as discretionary cannot be changed into mandatory omission. Rogers preserves the ordinary division between guilt determination and disposition; Lyles supplies persuasive experience under a different commitment scheme, not a nationwide instruction rule. This construction neither forbids correction of an actual misleading trial event nor adjudicates the constitutionality of the commitment provisions or their burdens.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Rogers v. United States, 422 U.S. 35 (1975): preserves the ordinary separation of guilt determination from consequences; it does not impose a statutory insanity-consequences charge.
- Lyles v. United States, 254 F.2d 725 (D.C. Cir. 1957): considered as persuasive circuit practice, not treated as an existing nationwide Supreme Court command.

##### Federal trial practice does not require the requested explanation here

**Controlling proposition:** A nonsentencing federal jury ordinarily must decide guilt and the insanity defense without regard to verdict consequences; proper submission of insanity and a defendant's request do not alone require a commitment explanation as federal supervisory practice. An appropriate accurate corrective instruction may be required to remedy a particular trial misstatement or error, such as a prosecutor or witness telling the jury that an insanity acquittee will go free, but no such event is reported here, so refusal of Shannon's requests was not error.

**Authority:** Justice Souter's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Ginsburg. Six Justices adopt the ordinary rule, corrective qualification and no-error application; none relies on an alternative harmless-error finding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a jury's task determines the information it ordinarily needs. Rogers requires a guilt decision without regard to sentence, while preserving the need to address misleading jury communications. Olano applies the ordinary assumption that jurors follow a proper limitation, without making it irrebuttable. Shannon's jury was told to decide the offense and defense without considering punishment or consequences. General concern that jurors may equate acquittal with freedom does not establish inability to follow that charge. An explanation emphasizing possible early release could itself encourage a verdict chosen for its disposition. Simmons involved a capital jury choosing punishment and a State-raised future-danger argument; its responsive-information holding does not govern this different task. The Court nevertheless rejects an absolute prohibition on correction. A particular misstatement may require a tailored, accurate response, not a full lecture on commitment law in every insanity trial. The reported record supplies no such misstatement here. Because the refusal was not error, the Court does not decide harmlessness or infer what these jurors believed.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Rogers, 422 U.S. 35: applies the guilt/disposition separation while preserving correction of a communication capable of distorting the jury's proper task.
- United States v. Olano, April 26, 1993: applies the ordinary instruction-following premise at its qualified scope; its forfeited-error rule does not decide a preserved-error remedy here.
- Simmons v. South Carolina, June 17, 1994: distinguishes the due-process right to answer a State-raised future-danger argument at capital sentencing; that holding and its separate grounds remain unchanged.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Rogers's ordinary guilt/disposition distinction applies with authority to correct a particular misleading trial event. Olano supplies the qualified instruction-following premise without deciding harmlessness. Simmons's actual capital future-danger rule is distinguished; Foucha's limits on continued psychiatric custody remain unchanged. Lyles is persuasive circuit experience and is not adopted as a nationwide required-instruction rule. No earlier Supreme Court holding is overruled.

Effective June 24, 1994, the Act supplies no consequences-instruction mandate, and federal supervisory practice does not require the requested explanation simply because insanity is properly raised. The rule preserves an appropriate accurate corrective instruction when a particular trial misstatement or error requires it; the Fifth Circuit's categorical denial of that authority is not adopted. There is no constitutional holding on the Act, no general constitutional instruction ruling, no statutory-borrowing alternative holding and no harmless-error decision. The dissent's proposed requested-explanation requirement does not govern.

The statutory procedures remain distinct from the jury-instruction rule. Under §17, the defendant must establish by clear and convincing evidence that severe mental disease or defect prevented appreciation of the nature and quality or wrongfulness of his acts; mental disease or defect does not otherwise constitute a defense. Under §4242(a), the prescribed insanity notice and a Government motion require an examination and report. Section 4242(b), upon the prescribed notice and motion of the defendant or Government or the court's own motion, directs the verdict choices guilty, not guilty, and not guilty only by reason of insanity.

Section 4243(a) requires immediate commitment to a suitable facility until eligibility for release under subsection (e). A court-ordered examination and report precede the §4247(d) hearing, which must occur no later than forty days after the verdict. At that hearing §4243(d) places clear-and-convincing proof on an acquittee whose offense involves bodily injury to another, serious damage to another's property, or a substantial risk of either; other offenses carry a preponderance burden. The issue is whether release would create a substantial risk of bodily injury to another or serious damage to another's property due to present mental disease or defect. The Court does not classify Shannon's §922(g) charge within either category.

If the court fails to find safe release under the applicable burden, subsection (e) requires Attorney General custody. The Attorney General must make all reasonable efforts to secure acceptance of custody, care and treatment by the State of domicile or trial and must transfer the person when such a State accepts. If neither State accepts despite those efforts, hospitalization continues until state acceptance or a mental condition permitting safe release or conditional release under a prescribed regimen of medical, psychiatric or psychological care or treatment, whichever comes first. Reasonable state-transfer efforts must continue periodically.

Under subsection (f), the facility director's determination of recovery sufficient for safe unconditional or conditional release requires a prompt certificate to the committing court, with copies to counsel and the Government. The court orders discharge or, on the Government's motion or its own, conducts a §4247(d) hearing. If the applicable subsection (d) burden is met, the court orders immediate discharge or conditional discharge under a regimen prepared for the person, certified as appropriate by the director and approved by the court, with compliance an explicit release condition. After a hearing using the same criteria, the court may modify or eliminate the regimen. Under subsection (g), the responsible facility director must report noncompliance; on that notice or other probable cause of noncompliance the person may be arrested and must be brought before the court without unnecessary delay. Recommitment requires a hearing and a determination that noncompliance makes continued release pose the stated substantial risk to another's person or property.

Subsection (h)'s temporary-departure restriction applies to persons hospitalized under subsection (e) in the clear-and-convincing offense category. It permits departure with committing-court approval after notice to the Government and the individual and opportunity for a hearing, in an emergency, or when accompanied by a federal law enforcement officer. These distinct provisions do not authorize a promise of permanent confinement, complete recovery as the only release route or a fixed hospitalization period.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela dissents, joined by Justices Blackmun and Stevens. They would adopt a narrow federal supervisory rule requiring, on proper request when insanity is submitted, a brief accurate explanation that the special verdict produces immediate custody pending a separate judicial process, together with a firm direction to decide guilt and insanity without regard to disposition. The statutory verdict structure and the experience represented by Lyles inform that proposed practice; neither §§4242–4243 nor due process already commands it. The committee report's permissive endorsement remains permissive, and the proposal does not require instruction on every sentence or collateral consequence.

The dissent recognizes that this jury had no sentencing role and that no reported misstatement or jury question established an individualized rebuttal need. Its rule is preventive: it would explain an unusual verdict without finding that these jurors actually misunderstood. The competing danger is a verdict chosen because jurors prefer hospitalization; the dissent would address it through an accurate, limited explanation paired with the guilt-only direction. No instruction could promise indefinite hospitalization, release only upon complete recovery, or any outcome or duration under §4243. Section 17's clear-and-convincing insanity burden would remain intact. Shannon's shorter request raised the issue, so the longer request's overstatement would not justify refusing every accurate explanation. Foucha concerns lawful continued confinement, not this jury's instructions, and underscores the need to avoid a false promise of permanent custody. Simmons's capital-sentencing rule is not extended to this trial.

The dissent would vacate the Fifth Circuit's judgment and remand under Rule 52(a) and Kotteakos, with the Government bearing the burden of showing harmlessness of the preserved nonconstitutional error. Hasting and Bank of Nova Scotia prevent use of supervisory power to dispense with that inquiry. The record does not establish the effect of withholding the proposed instruction, and the dissent does not infer the jury's reasoning from the experts' testimony. If the Government establishes harmlessness, the conviction may remain; otherwise a new trial follows. Neither an insanity acquittal, commitment nor direct release would be ordered.

### Board of Education of Kiryas Joel Village School District v. Grumet, Nos. 93-517, 93-527 and 93-539

Merits decision, June 27, 1994

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 30, 1994; decided June 27, 1994. On writs of certiorari to the New York Court of Appeals, 81 N.Y.2d 518, 618 N.E.2d 94 (1993). The consolidated cases concern whether Chapter 748's creation of a school district coterminous with Kiryas Joel violates the Establishment Clause despite its secular special-education purpose. The petition in No. 93-517 also requests replacement of Lemon and its primary-effect approach; the Court resolves the district's validity on sufficient narrower grounds. 

The trial court granted summary judgment under federal and state constitutional grounds; the Appellate Division affirmed on primary effect. The Court of Appeals affirmed under the federal Establishment Clause and expressly reserved state constitutional law. Citizen-taxpayer respondents proceeded under New York State Finance Law §123 after the association and officers were denied standing in those capacities. The adversely affected districts seek review of the concrete judgment; no new federal taxpayer-standing exception is announced.

The village incorporated in 1977 under general law. Its residents shared the Satmar faith, but its incorporation and their voluntary association are not the challenged acts. Public special services began at a religious-school annex in 1984 and ended after Aguilar and Ball in 1985. Parents reported panic, fear and trauma at outside placements and withdrew most children; that account is not a finding that interaction violated religious doctrine. Wieder in 1988 recognized authority to arrange a local public program, without requiring it. Chapter 748 followed in 1989 and grants all union-free-district powers. The present public program provides secular special education, employs outside professionals, uses coeducational classrooms without religious symbols and serves pupils from neighboring districts. These circumstances do not reduce the board's general power over schools, teachers, textbooks, discipline and property taxation. The act supplies no religious voter or office qualification, rabbinical appointment, special-education-only limitation or sunset.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The State may not select a denomination as the recipient of general governmental authority

**Controlling proposition:** The Establishment Clause forbids a State deliberately to allocate general civil authority to a community selected as a religious community, even when the immediate recipients are elected public lay officials rather than clergy. Chapter 748 violates that rule because its design and context use denominational membership as the organizing basis of the special school district; religious homogeneity or coincidence with an existing village line alone would not establish the violation.

**Authority:** Souter's opinion, Part II introduction and II-A, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens and Ginsburg: five Justices. This is an independently sufficient ground. O'Connor does not join that portion, and Kennedy's separate judgment rationale is not counted as a join.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the constitutional defect lies in the State's allocation of authority, not in a finding that the public school teaches religion. Larkin prohibits a transfer of discretionary governmental power to religious institutions. Chapter 748 does not reproduce its church veto: lay voters elect a public board, and ordinary public-school law applies. The structural concern nevertheless reaches a deliberate legislative choice of a denomination as the organizing recipient of general school-government power. Larson supplies the requirement of denominational neutrality, while McDaniel protects religious citizens from political disqualification. Those principles coexist. The village's lawful incorporation, secular instruction, outside professional staff and service to children from neighboring districts are substantial considerations, but do not cure this particular allocation. Nor do the children's educational needs require a permanent grant of general district powers on that basis. The Court leaves ordinary geographic government and neutral local services available. It invalidates neither voluntary religious association nor a district merely because its residents share a faith.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Larkin v. Grendel's Den, Inc., 459 U.S. 116: applies its civil-authority boundary by structural analogy to deliberate denominational allocation through a lay public board; it does not treat this board as a church or clergy veto.
- Larson v. Valente, 456 U.S. 228: applies denominational neutrality to the State's choice of recipients of general public authority; no general disability on religious communities follows.
- McDaniel v. Paty, 435 U.S. 618: preserves equal political citizenship and distinguishes religious officeholders from governmental allocation by denomination.

##### The particular denominational grant lacks a neutral basis for equal treatment

**Controlling proposition:** Chapter 748 independently violates religious neutrality by conferring an anomalously particular benefit of general school-government power on this selected denominational community without neutral terms or dependable assurance of equal treatment for comparable communities. The defect follows from this enactment's design and context, not a rule that every special act is invalid, every general statute is neutral, or an unproved finding that another religious community has already been refused.

**Authority:** Souter's opinion, Part II-B, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Ginsburg: six Justices. This is an independently sufficient alternative ground; Kennedy expressly declines it.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this exceptional allocation supplies no adequate neutral account of which similarly situated communities may receive the same governmental benefit. Larson requires denominational neutrality, and Amos distinguishes a generally available religious accommodation from preference for a particular denomination. The problem is not simply that the Legislature addressed one difficulty first. Its chosen structure grants broad continuing authority to the identified religious community without a neutral assurance governing comparable claims. A later legislature need not consider or decide another request, and legislative inaction may leave no merits decision whose reasons can be reviewed. That structural concern does not require inventing a prior refusal to another faith. The lawful general incorporation of the village illustrates a different basis for civic organization. Secular instruction and genuine need remain relevant, but cannot alone explain the denominational selection of governmental authority. Neutral criteria or otherwise equal arrangements may permit accommodation; the Court commands no particular statute and does not assume that a formally general law could never conceal the same preference.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Larson v. Valente: applies the prohibition on denominational preference to the particular governmental benefit and its allocation.
- Corporation of Presiding Bishop v. Amos, 483 U.S. 327: distinguishes its general nonprofit religious-employer accommodation from a special denominational franchise; its permission to accommodate remains effective.

##### Accommodation preserves educational access but does not cure this allocation

**Controlling proposition:** A State may accommodate religiously grounded needs beyond what free exercise independently requires, but accommodation does not authorize this denominational allocation of general governmental power. Affirmance leaves the children's statutory special-education rights and lawful neutral local public services intact, without commanding a particular placement, validating the adequacy of earlier services or prescribing a replacement district.

**Authority:** Souter's opinion, Part II-C and the implementing limits in Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Ginsburg: six Justices. This holding rejects the accommodation defense and defines the remedy's limits; Kennedy reaches the judgment on his separate ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that protecting these children's access to secular education is a legitimate objective. Amos permits accommodation beyond constitutionally compelled exemptions, and Zorach distinguishes permissible attention to religious needs from governmental sponsorship. But a legitimate service objective does not cure an impermissible allocation of civil power. The current secular curriculum, professional staffing and participation of children from other districts do not narrow the statute's grant of all union-free-district powers. The State remains able to provide appropriate services through neutral arrangements. Wieder recognized discretion to arrange a local public program without creating this district; it did not require such a program or establish that outside attendance violated religious doctrine. Wolman identifies the possibility of properly administered services at neutral sites, not the adequacy of any particular proposed placement here. The Court does not decide whether earlier services met each child's needs, close private religious schools, invalidate incorporation or extinguish IDEA eligibility. Aguilar, Ball and Lemon need not be revised to decide this special act.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Corporation of Presiding Bishop v. Amos: preserves permissible accommodation beyond free-exercise compulsion, with its denominational-neutrality and application limits.
- Zorach v. Clauson, 343 U.S. 306: preserves lawful accommodation of private religious needs without authorizing this governmental franchise.
- Wolman v. Walter, 433 U.S. 229: preserves the possibility of properly administered neutral-site services; no particular replacement is approved.
- Board of Education v. Wieder, 72 N.Y.2d 174, 527 N.E.2d 767: respects the state court's recognition of local educational discretion without turning permission into a compulsory separate school.
- Aguilar v. Felton, 473 U.S. 402, and School District of Grand Rapids v. Ball, 473 U.S. 373: neither is overruled, modified or used to order restoration of the terminated annex program.
- Lemon v. Kurtzman, 403 U.S. 602: remains governing law; this bounded neutrality decision neither replaces its framework nor imposes a new universal analytical sequence.

##### Authority, limits and questions not reached

The two independently sufficient invalidation grounds have direct majorities. Kennedy's religious-boundary rationale and the principal opinion's equal-treatment rationale are not combined under Marks: Kennedy would reject government religious gerrymanders even if all faiths received equivalent ones, while the principal opinion identifies a defect in this particular unequal allocation. Agreement in judgment does not establish a logical subset or a complete opinion join. Stevens's additional state-supported religious-separation rationale has only Stevens, Blackmun and Ginsburg.

The Court does not overrule Lemon, Aguilar or Ball; adopt a comprehensive replacement establishment test; create an aid-merits holding in Zobrest; revise Shaw's racial equal-protection holding; find a religious qualification for voters or officers; or adjudicate the reserved state constitutional question. A petition requested replacement of Lemon, but the present decision rests on the sufficient narrower grounds. No finding of religious instruction, mandatory assimilation, denial to another faith, permanent religious demographics or free-exercise entitlement to a separate district is made. The judgment does not prescribe an individual service placement or require elimination of culturally responsive secular instruction.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

The holding-specific treatments govern. Larkin's structural boundary applies to the actual denominational allocation while its church-veto facts remain distinct. Larson's neutrality rule and McDaniel's equal political citizenship are preserved together. Amos, Zorach and Wolman leave lawful accommodation available. Lemon, Aguilar and Ball are not overruled. Zobrest decided no Establishment Clause merits, and Carter's statutory reimbursement rule supplies no exception to denominational neutrality. Shaw's rejection of the particular design-only racial territorial claim remains intact; this decision rests on an independently identified establishment injury. Lee's school-worship and pressure holdings, Lamb's Chapel's private-access holding and Lukumi's actual neutrality rules retain their separate scopes. No comprehensive new establishment test is adopted.

Effective June 27, 1994, Chapter 748's deliberate denominational allocation of general civil authority is invalid under a five-Justice holding. Its particular unequal governmental benefit independently fails under a six-Justice holding. Legitimate accommodation does not cure those defects, and secular special-education rights remain. Religious homogeneity alone is no constitutional disqualification, and neither the form of special legislation nor a formally general law conclusively decides neutrality. Kennedy's independent boundary theory and Stevens's additional separation theory remain separate positions. The Court changes no general incorporation rule, racial districting standard, statutory education entitlement or school-aid precedent.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Blackmun concurs, joined by no other Justice. He regards Lemon's effect and entanglement principles as generally sound, and sees the Court's analysis as consistent with them. His position is affirmative adherence, not merely a conclusion that reconsideration is unnecessary. The decision's particular neutrality and allocation rules do not establish his concurrence as an additional opinion of the Court. He also joins Stevens's separate writing.

Stevens concurs, joined by Blackmun and Ginsburg. In his view, the State's affirmative organization of this religious separation supports attachment to the group's faith, beyond merely distributing a secular educational service on equal terms. He distinguishes private religious education, voluntary off-site release time and exemption from a general governmental burden. Current secular teaching, outside staff and lay public governance are accepted facts; his objection does not depend on relabeling the curriculum religious. The proposed concern for tolerance is a constitutional assessment of the State's chosen arrangement, not an empirical finding that a particular child retained a faith because of the district and not an order for compulsory assimilation or a particular integrated placement. The writing supplies a three-Justice additional rationale, not another holding.

O'Connor concurs in part and in the judgment, joined by no other Justice. She joins the particular-benefit, equal-treatment and accommodation analysis, but not the complete Larkin delegation analogy. Ordinarily rights and benefits should not depend on a person's faith, while a genuine religious burden may be accommodated without selecting a favored sect. She recognizes that this community may presently have a distinctive need and that special legislation is not inherently unlawful. The structural difficulty is the lack of dependable neutral terms for comparable groups: a future legislature may simply decline to address their requests, without a decision explaining why. A generally available district arrangement or neutral eligibility administered through reviewable decisions could present a different case; she commands neither. Religious residents remain entitled to political participation and public services. She favors more precise, context-sensitive establishment rules over forcing every category through one rigid Lemon formula, without rejecting every valid insight of decisions applying it. She would reconsider Aguilar in a proper case so that neutral special education might be provided at sectarian premises on equal terms. That prospective view changes neither Aguilar nor Ball here and draws no aid-merits support from Zobrest, which resolved antecedent questions.

Kennedy concurs in the judgment, joined by no other Justice. The decisive objection is the State's own deliberate use of religion to draw the boundaries of a political unit. A geographic description does not cure purposeful religious line drawing; offering equivalent religious gerrymanders to every faith would not cure it either. Private association, neutral incorporation and lines derived from geography or history remain different, and residents do not lose self-government merely because they share a faith. He acknowledges the secular school and preexisting lawful village but regards the Legislature's purposeful adoption of the religious community's line for this new unit as the State's own forbidden criterion. He expressly rejects the principal opinion's lack-of-assured-future-equal-treatment ground. A State may respond to a unique burden without arranging every future accommodation in advance, and a later unequal treatment claim can be considered when it arises, although extension or invalidation may pose remedial choices. Religious practical distress need not violate a specific tenet to support permissible accommodation; it creates no entitlement to this district. Larson and Larkin's religious-allocation boundary and Lukumi's objective neutrality principles support his separate ground. Shaw supplies no racial-design holding to import. He declines unnecessary Lemon reliance and regards Aguilar and Ball as possible subjects for later reconsideration; neither changes now.

Stone-Zsela concurs, joined by no other Justice. The statute creates an elected public board, not a rabbinical veto, so Larkin supplies a structural analogy rather than an identical factual rule. Secular instruction and real educational need count, but the decisive inquiry is whether religious membership actually organized this special allocation of general public authority. The act's design and context establish that defect here; demographic homogeneity alone would not. One-off legislation can be lawful, and a formally general statute can still be engineered for a denomination. Actual eligibility, governmental reasons, powers conveyed and access for comparable communities reveal which is occurring. Shaw rejected racial territorial design alone as the particular equal-protection deprivation asserted within a common electorate. This case rests on a distinct Establishment Clause injury: the State's use of denomination to organize governmental authority. It neither imports the rejected racial-design theory nor requires excluded voters to prove an election loss. Religious residents retain equal civic standing, and children retain equal access to lawful secular disability services. A neutral local program may meet those needs without this allocation. Wieder permits such an arrangement without commanding a placement, while Carter creates no exception to denominational neutrality. He joins affirmance with implementation left to the responsible state authorities and courts, and sees no need to revise Lemon, Aguilar or Ball.

Scalia dissents, joined by Thomas throughout. Chapter 748 creates an ordinary public institution with elected lay officials, secular instruction and general public duties, not a religious institution's veto. Larkin therefore does not establish that the State delegated to a church, and McDaniel protects religious citizens from civic disability. The lawful village line and distinctive educational and cultural problem explain the district without an established religious office qualification or devotional program. Even when accommodation is religiously motivated, Amos, Walz and Zorach allow responses beyond what free exercise commands. He accepts the prohibition on denominational favoritism but rejects the inference that known homogeneity and special legislation establish it here. Absence of guaranteed future accommodations is not present discrimination; courts can address a supported later unequal-treatment claim, including the existing benefit if necessary. He also rejects Kennedy's application because the Legislature used the existing lawful geographic community to provide secular education. The dissent favors abandoning Lemon's unitary method for fidelity to longstanding accommodation traditions and would overrule Aguilar and Ball at an appropriate opportunity. These are noncontrolling positions, not an agreed comprehensive replacement test or a present overruling. Zobrest supplies no aid-merits precedent, and surviving accommodation authorities carry the limited public-school argument. Reversal would remove the federal ground while leaving the reserved state constitutional claim for the state courts. The dissent approves no clergy veto, religious voting qualification, governmental worship or unlimited exemption.

### Turner Broadcasting System, Inc. v. FCC, No. 93-44

Merits decision, June 27, 1994

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 12, 1994; decided June 27, 1994. On direct appeal under 28 U.S.C. §1253 from the merits denial of injunctive relief by the three-judge District Court for the District of Columbia, 819 F. Supp. 32 (1993), required by §23 of the Cable Television Consumer Protection and Competition Act of 1992, 106 Stat. 1500. The Act requires the three-judge court; its separate special appeal clause for orders holding the carriage provisions unconstitutional is not the basis for this appeal. Five consolidated suits challenge §§4 and 5. The questions concern the First Amendment classification of compulsory carriage and the sufficiency of the record supporting summary judgment. Plaintiffs and intervening defenders sought summary judgment; federal defendants sought dismissal. On the developed record, the District Court denied plaintiffs’ summary-judgment motions, granted the federal defendants’ motion to dismiss, and dismissed the must-carry constitutional claims with prejudice. 

Congress found substantial cable penetration, local market power, incentives to disadvantage broadcast competitors, and reliance by subscribers on cable reception. It identified preservation of free television and diverse sources as objectives and also discussed local, informational and educational programming. These are legislative findings, not findings that every station is endangered. The record includes disputes over a 1988 survey reporting that about twenty percent of cable systems had dropped or refused a local signal and about twenty-three percent had repositioned one. Duration, financial impact, displaced cable programming, usable and future capacity, and alternatives remain contested. The lower majority sustained the scheme as neutral market regulation; the dissent accepted a bottleneck problem but found content preference and inadequate proof of danger.

The commercial and noncommercial provisions retain distinct qualifications. Section 614(b)(1)(A) requires at least three local commercial stations on a system with twelve or fewer usable activated channels. A system in that category with 300 or fewer subscribers is exempt from that section only so long as it deletes no broadcast signal; this is neither a freestanding subscriber exemption nor an exemption from §615. Systems above twelve channels devote up to one-third of aggregate usable activated channels to local commercial stations. If eligible stations exceed the required capacity, the operator chooses, but cannot prefer a qualified low-power station over a local full-power commercial station. If it chooses a network affiliate, that affiliate's city-of-license reference point must be closest to the principal headend. Substantially duplicative local commercial signals and more than one local affiliate of a network may be omitted; omission is permitted, not required, and voluntary duplicates count.

Commercial carriage includes the primary video, accompanying audio, line-21 captions, and technically feasible program-related vertical-blanking or subcarrier material; other material is discretionary. The entire schedule travels unless FCC rules prohibit particular programming and authorize substitution. Carriage must avoid material degradation and use technically feasible nondiscriminatory processing. Appropriate technically feasible removal of signal enhancement, with use at the headend, is permitted. At the station's election, channel position is its broadcast channel, its July 19, 1985 carriage channel, or its January 1, 1992 carriage channel; mutual agreement supplies another option. The FCC resolves disputes. Required carriage or positioning ordinarily cannot demand payment or other consideration. Exceptions cover good-quality or baseband delivery costs, increased distant-signal copyright liability and agreements entered before June 26, 1990, which may continue only until expiration. A distant signal is ineligible if the station refuses the increased-copyright indemnity; inadequate headend signals are ineligible if the station refuses responsibility for good-quality or baseband delivery.

Required commercial signals reach each subscriber and connected receiver. Self-installed additional-receiver arrangements have notification and converter-box offer duties. Written deletion or reposition notice is at least thirty days, and no deletion or repositioning occurs during major audience-measurement periods. Notice does not defeat a carriage right. A station first notifies the operator, which has thirty days to carry or give reasons; after a complaint the operator may respond, and the FCC has 120 days to determine the matter. A carriage order lasts at least twelve months; compliance requires dismissal. Section 614(e) abolishes duties to provide or publicize selector switches, without foreclosing argument that a switch could be an alternative.

Section 614(h)(1)(C) permits market adjustments on written request, with particular attention to historical carriage, local service or coverage, other eligible stations' local news or sports and events, and cable and noncable viewing patterns; the signal cannot be deleted during that proceeding. Section 614(c) requires one qualified low-power station on a system of no more than thirty-five channels, or two on a larger system, only when full-power stations do not fill the commercial allotment. Unused public, educational or governmental access channels may carry additional low-power signals with franchising approval. Low-power qualification requires minimum broadcast hours; compliance with nonentertainment, political and candidate, controversy, editorial, personal-attack, children's-programming and employment duties; an FCC finding of unmet local news and information needs because full-power stations are geographically distant; interference compliance; a good signal and location within thirty-five miles of the headend; both relevant areas outside the 160 largest metropolitan statistical areas as of June 30, 1990; a community of no more than 35,000 at that time; and no full-power station licensed in the cable-served county or political subdivision. Secondary status remains. These additional criteria receive separate consideration on remand.

Section 615 generally calls for carriage of every requesting qualified local noncommercial station, subject to its own exceptions. Systems of twelve or fewer activated channels carry one and may carry more; systems of thirteen to thirty-six carry at least one but need not carry more than three and may carry more. Larger systems follow the general duty and duplication exception, including systems expanding above thirty-six channels after March 29, 1990. A system without a qualifying local station imports one of its choice. A system of twelve or fewer channels need not remove programming actually provided on March 29, 1990 for that importation, but must use the first available channel. All systems continue local qualifying noncommercial signals carried on that date unless the operator and station waive continued carriage by mutual written consent. Additional signals may use unused public, educational or governmental access capacity with franchising approval.

On a thirteen-to-thirty-six-channel system already carrying a state public-television network affiliate, substantially duplicative programming permits omission of another affiliate of that network. Above thirty-six channels, a system required to carry three qualifying stations need not carry additional substantially duplicative stations; the FCC defines duplication to promote distinctive services. A carried noncommercial station may not use network-nonduplication rights to delete another carried qualifying station's programming. Primary video, audio, captions and technically feasible related accessibility, educational and language material travel; other material is discretionary. Equal technical capacity and no material degradation are required, subject to receipt of a good-quality or baseband headend signal. Noncommercial channel options are the broadcast channel, the July 19, 1985 channel or mutual agreement; January 1, 1992 is not a separate option. Repositioning or deletion requires thirty days' notice to station and subscribers. Signals enter the lowest-priced tier containing local commercial broadcasts. Payment is barred except delivery costs; a newly added distant copyright signal need not be carried without indemnification, while continued carriage under §615(c) remains distinct. FCC complaints allow response and require a determination within 120 days, with remedial order or dismissal as appropriate.

Noncommercial eligibility covers nonprofit or public-agency stations licensed under the applicable FCC rules whose licensees qualify for Corporation for Public Broadcasting community-service grants, and municipal stations predominantly transmitting noncommercial educational programming; qualifying translators and reserved-channel stations are included. Local status depends on a principal-community reference point within fifty miles of the headend or a Grade B contour encompassing it, unlike commercial market boundaries. Section 614(g)'s original home-shopping transition required an FCC public-interest proceeding considering viewing, competing spectrum demands and competition with nonbroadcast services, followed by qualification or a reasonable programming-transition period. Its initial discretionary-carriage rule alone does not settle June 1994 administration. Section 6 separately provides an election between must-carry and retransmission consent, not simultaneous entitlement to both.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Cable editorial expression receives ordinary First Amendment protection

**Controlling proposition:** Cable operators' editorial selection and cable programmers' expression are protected speech, and compulsory carriage burdens those interests. Cable's physical transmission capacity and market power do not justify importing broadcasting's spectrum-scarcity standard or reducing review of this expressive burden to rational-basis review.

**Authority:** Kennedy's opinion, Parts II-A and II-B as relevant to this proposition, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg: eight Justices. Stevens recognizes protected cable expression but does not join these complete portions and is not counted for their formulation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that operating a cable network includes protected decisions about what expression to present. Leathers recognizes cable's speech and press interests even though some generally applicable economic regulation remains permissible. Preferred Communications likewise treats selection of programming as expression and requires attention to the medium's actual characteristics. Finite cable capacity is not the scarcity of usable broadcast frequencies that supported the distinct treatment in Red Lion. Nor does evidence of a local distribution bottleneck remove the First Amendment from the case. Economic circumstances can explain a regulation's justification and shape the appropriate analysis, but cannot themselves make an expressive burden an ordinary commercial classification. The Government's market-failure account therefore does not authorize a rational-basis shortcut. The Court does not grant cable operators immunity from structural regulation, decide that carriage necessarily conveys endorsement, or settle the constitutionality of every rule governing this medium. It requires the constitutional inquiry applicable to the particular burden imposed.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Leathers v. Medlock, 499 U.S. 439: applies its recognition of cable speech and press; its approval of a broad content-neutral tax does not resolve compulsory carriage.
- City of Los Angeles v. Preferred Communications, Inc., 476 U.S. 488: applies its protection of editorial selection and attention to cable's characteristics; it had left the precise standard open.
- Red Lion Broadcasting Co. v. FCC, 395 U.S. 367: confines its scarcity rationale to the broadcasting setting; it supplies no diminished cable standard here.

##### Content classification depends on what the law selects and why

**Controlling proposition:** A law burdening protected expression because of its content ordinarily must be necessary and narrowly drawn to serve a compelling interest; a burden justified without reference to content is subject to the appropriate intermediate review. A benign purpose or absence of hostility does not neutralize a content-based operation, while a distinction among speakers or media does not by itself establish content discrimination.

**Authority:** Kennedy's opinion, Part II-B, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg: eight Justices. Stevens does not join that portion; his agreement with the case-specific classification below is distinct.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that classification requires examination of the law's operation and justification. Simon & Schuster applies demanding review to a financial burden selected by the subject of protected expression, without requiring proof of hostility to the speaker. Arkansas Writers' Project similarly identifies the danger in taxation that turns on what a publication says. Ward supplies the complementary inquiry for regulation justified without reference to content. Discovery Network confirms that a benign objective cannot save a selective restriction when the selected category has no adequate connection to the asserted harm. These principles permit attention to purpose without making a benevolent explanation conclusive. They also require more than noticing that legislation treats one medium or class of speakers differently. Leathers permits distinctions whose justification does not select ideas. The Justices joining this general formulation disagree about its application to must-carry. The Court does not resolve the separate question of content selection within genuinely unprotected categories.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Simon & Schuster, Inc. v. Members of the New York State Crime Victims Board, December 10, 1991: applies its actual content-based escrow holding; neutral collection remedies remain outside that holding.
- Arkansas Writers' Project, Inc. v. Ragland, 481 U.S. 221: applies its scrutiny of differential burdens dependent on a publication's content.
- Ward v. Rock Against Racism, 491 U.S. 781: applies justification without reference to content as the classification inquiry.
- Cincinnati v. Discovery Network, March 24, 1993: applies its rejection of benign-purpose neutrality for an unexplained content distinction; its commercial-speech fit holding remains bounded.
- Leathers v. Medlock: applies its distinction between permissible neutral differential treatment and censorial or content-based burdens.

##### The general full-power carriage requirements are content neutral

**Controlling proposition:** The general commercial and noncommercial full-power must-carry obligations regulate cable's distribution bottleneck and preservation of independently available broadcast service without selecting the stations' individual messages; intermediate scrutiny therefore governs those obligations. This classification does not decide the distinct low-power local-programming criteria, the market-adjustment provision, or a materially different content-based administration, and creates no general right to compel private editorial publication.

**Authority:** Kennedy's opinion, Parts II-C and II-D, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens and Souter: five Justices. O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas and Ginsburg reject this application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the general full-power obligations address transmission and access rather than the selection of favored ideas. They require carriage of station schedules under capacity, eligibility and duplication rules, without making each program's message the trigger. Congress's references to valued local and educational programming deserve consideration, as does the distinction between commercial and noncommercial stations, but the operative core preserves the availability of independent broadcast outlets. Source neutrality is not automatically content neutrality. Here the cable bottleneck supplies a physical distribution justification beyond a preference for what broadcasters say. Tornillo protects a newspaper's editorial judgment against a compelled reply tied to its own speech; this law does not impose a reply or penalize a cable operator for expressing a view. The relevant reasoning in the fractured Pacific Gas & Electric decision likewise does not erase that distinction. Separately identified broadcast signals need not be attributed to the operator. These limits distinguish the carriage rule without transferring Red Lion to cable or authorizing editorial-balance mandates for other media.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Miami Herald Publishing Co. v. Tornillo, 418 U.S. 241: distinguishes compelled reply and interference with newspaper editorial judgment; its protection is not displaced.
- Pacific Gas & Electric Co. v. Public Utilities Commission, 475 U.S. 1: distinguishes its fractured compelled-access reasoning concerning speech-responsive access and attribution; no unanimous categorical rule is attributed to it.
- Leathers v. Medlock: applies its allowance for justified neutral distinctions between media; the cable-specific burden still requires constitutional scrutiny.
- Red Lion Broadcasting Co. v. FCC: supplies no basis for the classification; broadcast scarcity is not extended.

##### Intermediate scrutiny requires advancement and a proportionate speech burden

**Controlling proposition:** A qualifying content-neutral carriage requirement must further an important or substantial governmental interest unrelated to suppressing expression and must not burden substantially more speech than necessary to further that interest. The Government need not establish that the law is the least restrictive conceivable alternative, but the actual expressive burden and relevant alternatives bear on whether its fit is sufficient.

**Authority:** Kennedy's opinion, Part III-A, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens and Souter: five Justices. This common standard does not establish common agreement about whether the existing record satisfies it.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a lawful structural purpose does not finish the First Amendment inquiry. O'Brien requires a substantial interest unrelated to suppression and a sufficiently limited incidental burden on expression. Ward explains that the Government need not choose the least restrictive or least intrusive means, but may not burden substantially more speech than the interest requires. Those requirements apply to the carriage of full-power broadcast signals even though the law does not select individual messages. The analysis must account for the operators' editorial interests and the programmers whose access may be displaced. A conclusion that Congress may address a distribution problem is therefore different from a conclusion that every resulting reservation of capacity is justified. Conversely, the existence of a conceivable alternative does not by itself invalidate the law. The Court adopts the governing standard while leaving final validity for further proceedings. It does not prescribe a new channel quota, require certainty about every future effect, or authorize an injunction against unrelated portions of the Act.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. O'Brien, 391 U.S. 367: applies the important-interest, nonsuppression and incidental-burden requirements to this content-neutral carriage burden.
- Ward v. Rock Against Racism: applies its requirement against substantially excessive burdens while preserving its rejection of least-restrictive-means review.

##### Preserving broadcast availability, diverse sources and fair competition are substantial interests

**Controlling proposition:** Preserving free local broadcast service, maintaining diverse sources of information and promoting fair competition in television distribution are important content-neutral interests supporting consideration of the general full-power carriage scheme under intermediate scrutiny. Their importance does not itself prove threatened harm, advancement, adequate fit or the scheme's final constitutionality.

**Authority:** Kennedy's opinion, Part III-A, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens and Souter: five Justices. The separate opinions disputing the neutrality of the Act's localism and programming preferences do not join this proposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress may take account of the continued availability of broadcast service to people who do not subscribe to cable. The interest is the survival of a communications source available independently of the cable operator, rather than official selection of the views that viewers should hear. Promoting diverse sources of information likewise concerns the availability of outlets, and fair competition addresses the gatekeeper's ability and incentives to disadvantage another distributor. Leathers permits attention to the distinct economic characteristics of a medium without licensing censorship. O'Brien and Ward require the further inquiry whether the particular burden advances those substantial objectives with an adequate fit. Congress need not wait for completed market damage before addressing a supported prospective danger. But the importance of these objectives does not establish that every broadcaster faces such a danger or that every displaced cable program can readily reach the same audience elsewhere. The Court leaves those evidentiary and application disputes distinct from the character of the interests themselves.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Leathers v. Medlock: applies the distinction between a medium's economic characteristics and selection of protected ideas.
- United States v. O'Brien and Ward v. Rock Against Racism: apply the requirement of a substantial nonsuppressive interest without treating that element as proof of advancement or tailoring.

##### Predictive judgments receive substantial deference and remain subject to review

**Controlling proposition:** In reviewing Congress's predictive judgments relevant to the carriage scheme, a court must give substantial deference while independently assuring that Congress drew reasonable inferences from substantial evidence. Congress need not compile the formal evidentiary record required of a court or agency; this general principle neither decides whether the evidence here suffices nor joins the four-Justice application requiring additional proof.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Part III-B, supported by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun and Souter, together with Stevens's express agreement in his concurrence at this limited level of generality: five Justices. Stevens does not join Part III-B, its real-harm formulation as a complete passage, or its record-insufficiency application. This is direct agreement on the stated proposition across writings, not a Marks inference or a fifth join in the plurality portion.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court's common rule respects both legislative responsibility for economic predictions and judicial responsibility for the First Amendment. Landmark Communications preserves constitutional review despite legislative judgment, and Sable Communications prevents findings from becoming an automatic answer to an expressive claim. Those cases do not demand that Congress conduct a trial before legislating. Congress may assess incentives, market structure and prospective danger through reasonable inferences supported by substantial evidence. A reviewing court gives that predictive judgment substantial deference while examining its constitutional sufficiency; it does not simply substitute its preferred forecast. Stevens expressly accepts this inquiry with the qualification that no formal judicial or agency-style legislative record is required. The four Justices supporting additional factual development and Stevens disagree about application, not this limited reviewing principle. The Court therefore does not make absence of past bankruptcies dispositive, require scientific certainty, or announce an industry-wide or station-by-station failure prerequisite. The disputed sufficiency of the present record remains separate.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Landmark Communications, Inc. v. Virginia, 435 U.S. 829: applies the judiciary's continuing responsibility to review an expressive burden despite legislative findings.
- Sable Communications of California, Inc. v. FCC, 492 U.S. 115: applies the refusal to make congressional findings conclusive; its strict-scrutiny setting does not impose that standard here.

##### Judgment and unresolved application

**Judgment without controlling rationale on evidentiary insufficiency:** The summary judgment is vacated and the case remanded, 5–4, by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy and Souter. Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Kennedy and Souter conclude in Part III-B that the present record leaves material disputes concerning the threatened broadcast harm, carriage effects, displaced programming, capacity and fit; Stevens believes the record sufficient and concurs only in the remand judgment on that question. O'Connor, Scalia and Ginsburg's alternative intermediate-scrutiny conclusion is a final breadth objection, not agreement with further factfinding; Thomas does not join that alternative. No combination of those grounds supplies a fifth vote for the plurality's application, and no logical-subset rationale is established under Marks.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Final constitutionality of the general scheme remains open. The district court must consider the separate low-power criteria in the first instance and may address the market-adjustment provision; neither is independently upheld or judicially severed. The initial home-shopping transition clause does not establish current FCC treatment. The remand orders no replacement quota, damages, blanket injunction against the Act or particular interim relief. Any interim relief requires its own properly presented legal basis. The legal standards and the judgment govern; the four-Justice explanation of why further proof is needed remains identified as such.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

The precise treatments under each holding govern. Tornillo's protection of newspaper editorial choice and the relevant fractured PG&E reasoning remain intact; the transmission distinction creates no general editorial-access mandate. Red Lion is not extended to cable. O'Brien and Ward govern the neutral core with their existing limits. Discovery Network retains its actual content-and-fit holdings, and R.A.V.'s overbreadth holding is neither replaced nor expanded into a rule about content selection within unprotected categories. Ladue's residential-channel holding and MCI's statutory delegation holding are not altered. No precedent is overruled. Marks does not manufacture a common evidentiary application from incompatible grounds.

Effective June 27, 1994, the general full-power carriage obligations are classified as content neutral and assessed under intermediate scrutiny; cable expression remains fully within the First Amendment. The important structural interests must be connected to the burden under the governing standard. Predictive review gives substantial deference to reasonable legislative inferences supported by substantial evidence without requiring a formal legislative record. The party-binding remand leaves final validity unresolved. The four-Justice conclusion that this record requires further proof remains a plurality application, not a five-Justice rule. The separate low-power criteria and market adjustments retain their distinct treatment; the decision establishes no current home-shopping administrative finding or severed replacement statute.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Blackmun concurs, joined by no other Justice, emphasizing the substantial deference due Congress's predictive judgments. Only portions of the legislative record were submitted to the District Court. That material may suffice to defeat the challengers' summary-judgment request while leaving unresolved material issues that prevent summary judgment for the defenders. He neither treats legislative findings as conclusive nor requires each broadcaster to establish impending bankruptcy. His separate emphasis accompanies his complete join in Kennedy's analysis.

Stevens concurs in part and in the judgment, joined by no other Justice. He agrees that the general scheme is content neutral and subject to intermediate review, but finds the existing record sufficient to reject the facial challenge. Congress may act before broadcasters collapse, given cable's audience, distribution control and incentives. Serious injury from exclusion need not await further demonstration in the form the plurality seeks. Congress need not establish that every broadcaster is vulnerable; the separate retransmission-consent option may leave compulsory carriage especially useful to vulnerable stations, a prediction rather than a finding about actual elections. Forecasts of displaced cable programming may themselves be speculative and affected by voluntary conduct. He expressly accepts review for reasonable inferences from substantial evidence, provided Congress need not make a formal judicial or agency-style record. Although he would affirm, he concurs in vacatur and remand so that a disposition commands a majority, following the disposition-accommodation approach discussed in Justice Rutledge's concurrence in the result in Screws v. United States, 325 U.S. 91, 134. His agreement with the judgment does not adopt the plurality's demand for further proof.

Stone-Zsela concurs, joined by no other Justice. Corporate cable operators exercise a genuine editorial function and retain its full protection; control of an important private communications route does not itself constitute state action. Tornillo and the relevant Pacific Gas & Electric reasoning present a serious compelled-publication concern. The permitted distinction is bounded: Congress addresses a physical distribution bottleneck and preservation of independently available broadcast service, and separately identified signals need not be attributed to the operator. Commercial and noncommercial categories, localism statements and actual administration still require attention for message preference. Evidence that some stations were dropped does not alone establish systemic jeopardy, while spare capacity does not eliminate the burden on each affected operator. Supported forecasts can justify prevention without scientific certainty or completed failures. Ladue's practical attention to usable expression supports examining displaced programming and realistic alternatives; MCI's different delegation question supplies little guidance on Congress's enacted choice. He joins remand without final validation, a judicially substituted quota or automatic Act-wide injunction. This concurrence adds no separate Court holding and no general power to compel editorial balance in newspapers or other media.

O'Connor concurs in part and dissents in part, joined by Scalia and Ginsburg throughout and by Thomas only in Parts I and III. In Parts I and III, all four regard the compulsory preference as content based: Congress assigns finite editorial space to favored broadcast expression because of local, informational and educational programming. The absence of viewpoint hostility does not eliminate that selection. They regard localism and viewpoint diversity as insufficiently compelling to justify it; even assuming educational and public-affairs objectives compelling, the breadth fails strict tailoring. That assumption is not a holding that those objectives can never be compelling. The physical bottleneck is real, but does not authorize Congress to select favored speech on private editorial capacity. Neutral competition measures, capacity-access arrangements or common-carriage structures may present different constitutional questions; their mention approves no particular law or taking. Relief would address the challenged carriage regime, not the whole Act.

O'Connor's Part II supplies an independently sufficient alternative, joined only by Scalia and Ginsburg. Even assuming content neutrality, the requirements reach broadcasters and situations in which the asserted threat or anticompetitive danger does not justify the burden. More evidence about how often such dangers occur would not cure that breadth. Congress could direct protection to threatened broadcast service or anticompetitive conduct, but the present extensive preference goes beyond those needs. These three would invalidate under intermediate scrutiny as well as strict scrutiny; they do not join a remand to discover evidence that could cure the scheme. Thomas's nonjoin preserves the distinction between his strict ground and this alternative.

Ginsburg also dissents separately, joined by no other Justice. She emphasizes the lower dissent's account of content preference and the failure to substantiate danger to free television. She does not find viewpoint discrimination, and does not turn the absence of adequate proof into a proved fact that no station could ever suffer. Content-neutral and content-related purposes can be interwoven; the former do not erase the latter. If further proceedings occur and danger remains unsupported, she would require judgment for the appellants. That conditional observation does not constitute agreement with the present remand or its intermediate classification.

### Williamson v. United States, No. 93-5256

Merits decision, June 27, 1994

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 25, 1994; decided June 27, 1994. Reported at 512 U.S. 594 (1994). On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit, whose judgment at 981 F.2d 1262 (1992) affirmed without opinion.

The question is whether Rule 804(b)(3) permits admission of an accomplice's generally self-inculpatory confession, including accusations not themselves against that declarant's penal interest. Williamson also preserved a separate confrontation objection. He seeks relief from his drug-related convictions; the United States seeks to sustain them under the exception and any properly available harmless-error ground.

A consent search of Harris's rental car revealed approximately nineteen kilograms of cocaine. He first described an unnamed Cuban supplier and a dumpster delivery, then recanted that account and said he was transporting drugs for Williamson, who drove ahead and saw the stop. Walton promised to report cooperation but denied promising a reward. Harris expressed fear and declined to sign or record his statements. He refused to testify despite use immunity, an order and contempt; Walton then recounted the custodial statements. The district court found unavailability, penal adversity and corroborating circumstances under Harrell. Those reported findings do not establish a separate inquiry into each material assertion; the silent affirmance supplies no appellate rationale.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg participated at argument and decision. No recusal or nonparticipation is established.

#### Judgment

The Court vacates the Eleventh Circuit's judgment and remands, 9–0. Seven Justices adopt an assertion-specific, contextual construction of Rule 804(b)(3); no common application finding covers every Harris statement.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Eleventh Circuit judgment affirming the convictions | Vacated and remanded, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | No opposing Justice | Further proceedings under the Court's Rule construction and properly available error consequences; no acquittal, automatic retrial or present harmlessness finding. |

The unanimous judgment does not settle application on a single rationale. Blackmun, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg would exclude all the recounted Harris account under this exception and remand for harmlessness and remaining consequences. Stone-Zsela, O'Connor and Scalia would have particular assertions assessed under the narrow rule; O'Connor and Scalia additionally find some portions qualifying, a finding Stone-Zsela does not join. Kennedy and Thomas would remand under their broader rule. The three paths are 4 / 3 / 2, not a binary tie or a seven-Justice application holding. The Government may make any properly available harmlessness argument; its conditional trial statement is no blanket surrender of that opportunity.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Each material assertion must be against the declarant's interest in context

**Controlling proposition:** When an unavailable declarant's statement is offered under Rule 804(b)(3) as a statement against penal interest, each offered material assertion must, in the circumstances when made, so tend to expose that declarant to criminal liability that a reasonable person in that position would not have made it unless believing it true; a generally self-inculpatory account does not carry an independently neutral, noninculpatory or self-serving assertion into the exception. This is contextual assessment, not grammatical atomization: an apparently neutral detail or identification of another participant can qualify if its actual effect sufficiently incriminates the declarant, while neither joint inculpation nor custody alone establishes admissibility or exclusion.

**Authority:** Part II of Justice O'Connor's opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Souter and Ginsburg. These seven participating Justices adopt the same rule, contextual qualification and explanation. Justices Kennedy and Thomas favor a different construction; their judgment votes add no support to this rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the exception follows the assertion's actual adverse effect, not the label attached to a larger confession. The reasonable-person condition supplies the reliability premise: genuine exposure to liability may make a statement credible, but a speaker can admit one fact and falsely assign greater responsibility to someone else. Salerno requires satisfaction of the selected exception's actual conditions; it does not decide the disputed unit here. Lee warns that accomplice accusations can be selectively unreliable, without establishing a categorical ban on every such statement. The broader reading has support in ordinary narrative usage and the Advisory Committee's discussion of related statements. But the note also asks whether the statement is actually against interest and warns about custodial efforts to obtain favor. Considering context answers the objection that separation destroys meaning: the surrounding account may explain an assertion's incriminating force without becoming admissible in its entirety. Harris's changing accounts, admitted transportation, cooperation setting and allocation of roles require that distinction. The Court decides the governing rule, not a common classification of every Harris assertion, and leaves the separate constitutional claim unresolved.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Salerno, decided June 19, 1992: applies its requirement that the selected hearsay exception's actual conditions be satisfied; its former-testimony and similar-motive holdings remain unchanged and do not themselves resolve Rule 804(b)(3)'s unit.
- Lee v. Illinois, 476 U.S. 530 (1986): draws on its warning about selective unreliability in an accomplice's accusation; does not turn its confrontation analysis into automatic evidentiary exclusion or decide the constitutional claim here.

##### Vacatur preserves the distinction between admissibility error and relief

**Controlling proposition:** The Eleventh Circuit's judgment is vacated and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with the assertion-specific Rule construction, without a controlling determination that all of Harris's account qualifies or all of it fails; any properly available harmless-error argument must be considered under the standard applicable to the error actually found. For preserved nonconstitutional evidentiary error, Rule 52(a) and Kotteakos place the applicable harmlessness burden on the Government and require the reviewing court, considering the whole record, to have fair assurance that the error did not substantially sway the judgment, with grave doubt preventing a harmlessness finding; Chapman instead requires harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt only if constitutional trial error is actually reached and found, a new trial follows if error requiring relief is established, and neither acquittal nor automatic retrial follows from rejecting whole-account admission.

**Authority:** Part III of Justice O'Connor's opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Souter and Ginsburg. Seven Justices adopt these shared judgment-consequence limits while retaining the application disagreements described below. All nine Justices vote for vacatur and remand, but Kennedy and Thomas concur in that judgment under their different Rule construction.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the proper evidentiary inquiry and the effect of an error are separate questions. The reported district ruling and unexplained appellate affirmance do not establish the required assertion-specific assessment. Kotteakos supplies the inquiry for preserved nonconstitutional error: the reviewing court must examine the whole record and the error's effect on the verdict, rather than ask only whether sufficient other evidence supports guilt. Rule 52(a) preserves that inquiry. Olano's ordinary defendant burden concerns forfeited error and does not shift the burden for this preserved objection. The prosecutor's conditional willingness to join a mistrial if the judge found improper admission does not establish surrender of every appellate harmlessness argument. Independently linking evidence may matter to harmlessness without making an accusation itself against interest. Chapman is reserved for an actually established constitutional trial error; no such violation is decided today. The Court orders further proceedings, not a present harmlessness finding. Whether a particular Government argument remains properly available depends on the proceedings, and the decision recognizes no unreported forfeiture or new admissibility ground.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750 (1946): applies its whole-record, substantial-influence inquiry to any established preserved nonconstitutional evidentiary error; no present harmlessness result is reached.
- United States v. Olano, decided April 26, 1993: distinguishes its ordinary defendant burden for forfeited error; neither forfeiture nor intentional waiver is inferred here.
- Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18 (1967): preserves the Government's beyond-a-reasonable-doubt harmlessness burden for a constitutional trial error if one is reached and found; does not apply that standard merely because the Rule was violated.

##### Application disagreement and reserved questions

There is no controlling classification of all Harris statements. Blackmun, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg conclude that the recounted custodial account fails Rule 804(b)(3) throughout and would remand for error consequences. Stone-Zsela, O'Connor and Scalia favor particular-assertion assessment under the Court's narrow rule; O'Connor and Scalia additionally regard some portions as qualifying, while Stone-Zsela joins no present selection of qualifying assertions. Kennedy and Thomas favor application on remand under their broader related-statement rule. These are three application paths, with a further scope distinction within the second, not an equal division and not seven votes for one application conclusion. The direct seven-Justice general rule requires no Marks synthesis. The different case applications and different Rule constructions cannot be combined into a controlling application rationale under Marks v. United States, 430 U.S. 188 (1977): the four-Justice exclusion conclusion and the competing remand approaches do not establish one shared rule yielding the same result for these statements.

The Court does not decide whether Rule 804(b)(3) is firmly rooted for confrontation purposes, whether these statements satisfy the Confrontation Clause, or whether the Rule requires prosecution-side corroboration nationwide. The Rule's express corroboration sentence concerns statements tending to expose the declarant to criminal liability and offered to exculpate the accused. The distinct Harrell/Taggart circuit requirement for inculpatory statements remains applicable according to its own terms; it is not displaced or converted into a national holding. Neither corroboration nor Rule 804(a)(2) unavailability substitutes for genuine penal adversity. The expressly disclaimed Rule 801(d)(2)(E) coconspirator ground supplies no alternative basis for admission. No other exception is held satisfied.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

The holding-specific treatment above governs. Bourjaily v. United States, 483 U.S. 171 (1987), remains the source of the ordinary preponderance approach to preliminary admissibility facts under Rule 104(a); it does not establish the substantive exception or restore the expressly disclaimed in-furtherance-of-conspiracy ground. United States v. Harrell, 788 F.2d 1524 (11th Cir. 1986), and United States v. Taggart, 944 F.2d 837 (11th Cir. 1991), identify the circuit's separate inculpatory-corroboration requirement; neither settles the proper unit under today's rule or supplies a harmlessness result for this trial.

Ohio v. Roberts, 448 U.S. 56 (1980), Idaho v. Wright, 497 U.S. 805 (1990), and White v. Illinois, decided January 15, 1992, remain governing confrontation law. The Court leaves the constitutional question open: unavailability or an exception label does not resolve constitutional reliability, and external corroboration does not automatically supply Wright's circumstances-of-making trustworthiness. Bruton v. United States, 391 U.S. 123 (1968), is not converted into a categorical Rule 804(b)(3) accomplice exclusion; Congress removed the proposed constitutional exclusion rather than enacting it. Zobrest v. Catalina Foothills School District, decided June 18, 1993, preserves the distinction between avoiding an unnecessary constitutional issue and automatically reviving an abandoned ground. This remand invents no Government forfeiture and restores no expressly disclaimed ground. No precedent is overruled.

Effective June 27, 1994, the material assertion, evaluated in all surrounding circumstances when made, is the unit of the penal-interest exception. Context may make an apparently neutral detail genuinely incriminating, but a generally adverse narrative cannot provide borrowed admissibility for a neutral or self-serving accusation. The seven-Justice rule resolves the statement unit; it does not abolish Rule 804(a)'s unavailability requirement, Rule 104(a)'s ordinary preliminary-fact procedure or independent applicable corroboration conditions. Harris's refusal despite an order establishes unavailability, not reliability.

The Rule separately covers statements contrary to pecuniary or proprietary interest, tending to impose civil or criminal liability, or tending to invalidate the declarant's claim against another, to the degree specified by the reasonable-person standard at the time of making. Today's penal-interest decision does not rewrite those other categories. Its express exculpatory corroboration sentence and the circuit's inculpatory requirement remain distinct. Courts may use surrounding narrative as context without admitting every part for its truth.

No single application rationale controls the whole Harris account. The seven-Justice general rule governs further proceedings; the four-Justice exclusion conclusion, three-Justice application-remand position, two-Justice affirmative qualification position and two-Justice broader rule retain their stated limits and noncontrolling status. Rule error, constitutional error and judgment consequences remain separate. Other trial evidence may bear on harmlessness without curing absence of adverse interest. Confrontation, firmly rooted status and nationwide inculpatory corroboration remain open.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice O'Connor's Part IV-A, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justice Scalia, would leave particular-assertion assessment to the courts below under the narrow construction. Circumstances when Harris spoke, including cooperation incentives, changing accounts and allocation of responsibility, must be considered; no promised reward is needed for an incentive. The inquiry neither severs isolated words, excludes every mention of another participant, nor approves every accusation accompanying an admission. Part IV-B, joined only by Scalia, goes further in regarding some recounted self-inculpatory portions as qualifying. Stone-Zsela does not join that present classification. Neither portion commands a majority.

Justice Ginsburg, joined by Justices Blackmun, Stevens and Souter, joins the Court's rule but would conclude that all Harris custodial statements recounted by Walton fail it. Police already had Harris and approximately nineteen kilograms of cocaine. His admissions added little to that exposure while successive accounts assigned ownership and direction to Williamson and portrayed Harris as subordinate. In this setting apparently adverse passages remained bound up with efforts to minimize responsibility and obtain favorable treatment. Lee's concern with selective unreliability in accomplice accusations supports this record-specific conclusion. The concurrence neither excludes all police confessions nor holds that cumulative admissions can never be adverse. These four Justices regard the reported account as sufficient to resolve adversity while distinguishing the full trial record needed to assess prejudice. The conditional mistrial statement does not eliminate Government opportunity to argue harmless error based on other evidence. Their preferred remand concerns consequences of established Rule error, rather than another open-ended search for admissible fragments. They decide no confrontation issue and direct no automatic new trial.

Justice Kennedy, joined by Justice Thomas, concurs in the judgment. They would admit some collateral statements related to a genuinely adverse declaration even when those assertions are not independently self-inculpatory. Ordinary narrative usage, the original Advisory Committee note and its reliance on the limited McCormick approach support their construction. They impose two distinct exclusions. A collateral statement must be excluded when it is so self-serving as to be unreliable, including blame allocation tending to reduce the declarant's charges or punishment. Independently, the entire declaration must be excluded when circumstances make significant motivation to obtain favorable treatment likely; an explicit leniency offer is an example, not the exclusive trigger. That second condition may exclude the self-inculpatory words as well as collateral matter. Custody alone does not require exclusion, and absence of an express bargain does not establish admissibility. Rule 104(a) assigns the contextual preliminary assessment to the judge. They would remand for application to Harris's account, with appropriate respect for permissible case-specific determinations. They neither find the whole account admissible nor join the Court's individual-adversity requirement. Their rule preserves other evidentiary conditions, separate confrontation scrutiny if necessary, the distinction between the express corroboration sentence and circuit practice, and properly available harmless-error review.

### Holder v. Hall, No. 91-2012

Merits decision, June 30, 1994

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 4, 1993; decided June 30, 1994. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit, Hall v. Holder, 955 F.2d 1563 (1992), which reversed rejection of the Section 2 claim and remanded for formulation of a remedy. The District Court's decision is reported at 757 F. Supp. 1560 (M.D. Ga. 1991).

The question is whether the size of this county governing body may be challenged as dilutive under Section 2 and whether a reasonable alternative supplies a measure of unequal opportunity for its sole-commissioner structure. Review does not reopen the Court of Appeals' findings of political cohesion and sufficient white bloc voting. The county officials seek reversal; the voters and local NAACP defend liability and a lawful remedy.

The sole commissioner exercises both legislative and executive powers. Black residents constituted approximately 19% of the voting-age population in the census figures used at trial. Georgia's 1985 local act authorized five district-elected commissioners plus an at-large chairman, conditioned on a referendum; voters rejected the proposal in 1986. The school board already uses five districts, and the District Court found that a local district arrangement could contain a compact Black majority. The school-board comparison does not establish identical governmental functions. The Court of Appeals found the remaining Gingles predicates and unequal opportunity under the totality of circumstances, suggested the school-board districts as a possible model, and directed formulation of a remedy. It did not impose a final map or number of commissioners and did not reach the separate constitutional claim.

All nine Justices participate at argument and decision: Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg.

The Court affirms the Eleventh Circuit's Section 2 liability judgment and its existing remand for formulation of a remedy, 5–4.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. The county's governing-body size is within Section 2's coverage

**Controlling proposition:** The number of members of this elected county governing authority is a standard, practice or procedure within Section 2, 42 U.S.C. §1973; its combination of legislative and executive functions and its present single-member form do not categorically exempt it from the statute. Coverage alone establishes neither a usable measure of dilution nor unequal electoral opportunity, and it does not prescribe a remedy.

**Authority:** Blackmun's Opinion of the Court, Part I, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter and Ginsburg; six Justices directly adopt this threshold rule. O'Connor joins this part while opposing statutory liability; Kennedy reserves the affirmative coverage question, and Scalia and Thomas reject coverage of dilution.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the statute reaches the electoral structure of this county governing authority. Section 2 addresses standards, practices and procedures that impair protected citizens' opportunity to participate and elect their chosen representatives. Chisom v. Roemer rejects an artificial exclusion of elected officials from that protection, while Houston Lawyers' Association v. Attorney General of Texas distinguishes statutory coverage from the later consideration of legitimate state interests. The commissioner exercises legislative as well as executive powers; electing one official does not remove that electoral arrangement from the statutory words. Whether a different number of offices supplies a reasonable comparison is a separate question. Neither broad coverage nor the results amendment dispenses with that inquiry or the required proof of unequal opportunity. Section 5's comparison of a proposed change with an existing practice also cannot establish Section 2 liability. The Court therefore rejects a categorical coverage exemption without making every choice about county government unlawful or ordering any particular structure.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Chisom v. Roemer, 501 U.S. 380 (1991): applies its rejection of artificial exclusions from Section 2's electoral coverage; the decision does not itself settle the body-size comparison.
- Houston Lawyers' Association v. Attorney General of Texas, 501 U.S. 419 (1991): follows its separation of coverage, legitimate state interests, liability and remedy; extends no automatic right to additional offices.
- Presley v. Etowah County Commission, January 27, 1992: preserves its Section 5 voting/governing boundary and its reservation of independent Section 2 claims; it supplies no Section 2 size benchmark.
- City of Rome v. United States, 446 U.S. 156 (1980): distinguishes Section 5 review of changes against an existing practice from the proof required for this Section 2 results claim.

##### 2. A reasonable comparison must have legal and practical roots independent of a desired racial tally

**Controlling proposition:** A Section 2 challenge to this sole-commissioner structure may use a reasonable, workable alternative grounded in the actual legal and institutional setting, suitable to the governmental task, and justified independently of the desired number of minority-preferred winners; a uniquely ideal size is not required. The county-specific authorization of five district-elected commissioners plus an at-large chair, the existing school-board districts and the compact potential majority satisfy that comparison requirement here, without making population share, a possible seat, or any proposed enlargement sufficient by itself.

**Authority:** Blackmun's Opinion of the Court, Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg; five Justices directly adopt the complete comparison rule and its application. O'Connor and Kennedy reject that comparison; Thomas and Scalia reject dilution coverage on a different, broader ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a comparison need not identify the only permissible legislature. Gingles requires a meaningful account of the opportunity the challenged arrangement withholds; it does not make an existing electoral structure its own conclusive measure. Georgia authorized an alternative for this very county: five commissioners elected by district and a chairman elected at large. The school board's existing districts give the geographic comparison additional practical grounding, and the District Court found a compact potential Black majority. The school board does not perform identical functions, and the referendum rejected the commission proposal. Those facts deserve consideration, but they do not erase the county-specific evidence of feasibility or immunize a proved federal violation. Houston Lawyers requires attention to genuine institutional interests, and Growe preserves state responsibility for districting. The plaintiffs must establish suitability and unequal opportunity, not merely exhibit a map producing a seat. Multiple reasoned alternatives can exist without arbitrariness. Enlargement of an existing multimember body or fragmentation of an indivisible executive office solely to manufacture seats is not decided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Thornburg v. Gingles, 478 U.S. 30 (1986): extends its comparison inquiry to this locally grounded sole-commissioner challenge while retaining its causal predicates and totality inquiry.
- Houston Lawyers' Association, 501 U.S. 419: applies meaningful consideration of the State's institutional interests; those interests are neither ignored nor converted into categorical structural immunity.
- Growe v. Emison, February 23, 1993: preserves the State's districting responsibility and causal proof requirements; it neither decided nor barred this size comparison.

##### 3. The accepted comparison does not replace the required proof of unequal opportunity

**Controlling proposition:** This size-dilution claim requires a sufficiently large and geographically compact minority to form a district majority, political cohesion, and majority bloc voting ordinarily sufficient to defeat its preferred candidate, absent special circumstances such as an unopposed minority candidate, together with unequal opportunity established under the totality of circumstances; the comparison alone is insufficient and discriminatory purpose is not required. The extent of protected-class electoral success may be considered but creates no right to election in proportion to population, and the preserved appellate findings establish statutory liability here without reopening cohesion or bloc voting outside the limited review question.

**Authority:** Blackmun's Opinion of the Court, Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg; five Justices directly adopt the stated application and preservation of the existing proof requirements. There is no separate holding that the Gingles predicates alone establish liability or shift the ultimate statutory burden.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the voters have established more than an opportunity to draw a favorable district. Gingles supplies the causal predicates, and Growe extends their use beyond multimember submergence while keeping the totality inquiry distinct. The District Court found the potential compact majority. The Court of Appeals corrected its treatment of the evidence of cohesion and majority bloc voting, then considered the totality and found unequal electoral opportunity. Challenges to those appellate cohesion and bloc-voting findings fall outside the question under review. Voinovich requires a proved statutory injury rather than a preference for one districting form; that requirement remains. The statutory results standard does not require a discriminatory-purpose finding, and the District Court's rejection of intentional discrimination therefore does not defeat this claim. Nor does nearly one-fifth of the voting-age population establish an entitlement to office. Electoral success is a circumstance the statute permits consideration of, with its actual context preserved. The Court affirms the existing liability determination, not an automatic rule based on demographics or a hypothetical winning district.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Gingles, 478 U.S. 30: follows the causal prerequisites and independently necessary totality inquiry; no universal statistical method or proportional representation rule is adopted.
- Growe, February 23, 1993: preserves the need for local cohesion and bloc-voting proof, including the stated special-circumstances qualification; coalition cognizability, influence claims and the population denominator remain unresolved.
- Voinovich v. Quilter, March 2, 1993: follows the distinction between unequal-opportunity proof and preference for a district form; no change to its initial burden rule or its influence-claim reservations.
- Shaw v. Reno, June 28, 1993: preserves the independent elements of Section 2; its rejection of the design-only constitutional theory adds no constitutional-injury prerequisite to this statutory claim.

##### 4. The existing remand must provide a lawful remedy for the established violation

**Controlling proposition:** Because Section 2 liability is affirmed, the existing remand for formulation of a workable remedy continues, with genuine county-government interests and the responsible state institutions receiving consideration and relief confined to curing the established violation. The local authorization and district pattern inform that inquiry but compel neither their exact adoption, a particular number of officers, a racial quota nor any candidate's victory; no new liability trial is ordered and the constitutional alternative need not be decided.

**Authority:** Blackmun's Opinion of the Court, Part IV, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg; the five-Justice judgment coalition adopts these remedial limits and the affirmance of the Eleventh Circuit's existing remedy remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that identifying a useful comparison does not enact a replacement government. Houston Lawyers treats the State's genuine institutional interests as relevant to relief as well as liability. Growe recognizes state responsibility for districting, and Voinovich permits federal structural relief only as necessary to remedy an established violation. Here the Court of Appeals found that violation and directed formulation of a remedy; it suggested a local district pattern rather than imposing a final map. Affirmance preserves that procedural distinction. The rejected referendum means that the alternative commission was not adopted, but local rejection cannot bar all relief for the proved federal wrong. The responsible institutions and the courts must address a workable remedy within the statutory limits. This Court supplies no division of governmental duties or mandatory board size that the record does not establish. The constitutional-purpose claim remains unnecessary to the judgment. Neither that unresolved alternative nor the remaining remedial work warrants another trial of the statutory liability already adjudicated.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Houston Lawyers' Association, 501 U.S. 419: follows its instruction to consider genuine state structural interests in fitting relief to a proved violation.
- Growe, February 23, 1993: preserves state responsibility and the lawful federal remedial role; its case-specific dismissal direction is not a universal bar to retaining remedial proceedings.
- Voinovich, March 2, 1993: applies the distinction between state districting choice and a federal remedy justified by an established violation; no mandate to maximize districts follows.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

The holding-specific treatments above govern: Gingles's comparison inquiry is extended to this locally grounded sole-commissioner claim while its predicates and totality remain; Chisom and Houston Lawyers support coverage with separate proof and remedy; Growe and Voinovich retain state responsibility and the requirement of an established violation for federal relief. Presley and City of Rome do not make Section 5's baseline a Section 2 measure. Shaw retains the independent statutory inquiry. No precedent is overruled. Thomas and Scalia's proposal to reject contrary dilution precedents is noncontrolling and does not alter their present force.

Effective June 30, 1994, Section 2 permits this sole-commissioner size challenge when the claimant supplies a reasonable, workable comparison rooted in the actual legal and institutional setting and proves the required causal conditions and unequal opportunity under the totality. The decision keeps four inquiries distinct: statutory coverage, a suitable comparison, proof of the statutory injury, and lawful relief. This decision answers the previously open comparison question on this record; it does not treat a demographic fraction, inability to elect a preferred official, or a map producing one seat as sufficient.

Gingles's ordinary predicates and totality remain; a protected class's electoral success may be considered, and proportional election of protected-class members is not guaranteed. Section 2's protection against racial or color discrimination and its language-minority cross-reference remain unchanged. No discriminatory-purpose requirement is added to this results claim, and no question under the language-minority cross-reference is separately decided. No ruling eliminates ordinary dilution claims or categorically immunizes government size.

The State retains its districting role, and federal structural relief must cure an established violation. Neither the local comparison nor today's judgment requires maximization of opportunity districts, adoption of the school-board map, six officers, or any racial quota. The referendum did not adopt the proposed commission, and the Court does not enact it. Enlargement of an existing multimember body and division of an indivisible executive office solely to manufacture seats remain unresolved. Other counties must establish suitable comparisons and prove their own claims; later applications are not predetermined. Coalition and influence claims and the population denominator remain open as before.

Shaw's actual design-only constitutional boundary remains intact and supplies no extra injury element for Section 2. Section 5's change-based inquiry remains distinct. The constitutional claim in this case is not decided. The four stated holdings rest on direct majorities; there is no need to identify a narrower rationale under Marks, and the incompatible grounds of the two dissents are not combined into Court law.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

##### Stevens's concurrence, joined by Blackmun, Souter and Ginsburg

Stevens joins the Court throughout and gives an additional reason for rejecting the proposal to remove vote dilution from Section 2. The statute has been repeatedly considered and reenacted against the background of decisions protecting effective electoral opportunity. Congress's 1982 amendment deliberately retained protection against discriminatory results while withholding any guarantee of proportional officeholding. Gingles implements that compromise; Chisom and Houston Lawyers preserve broad coverage while requiring the appropriate liability and remedial inquiries. Growe and Voinovich continue those distinctions. Difficulty applying the compromise to electoral structures does not authorize the Court to replace it with a statute confined to registration and ballot administration. The constitutional concerns associated with racial districting do not dispense with the enacted statutory inquiry or establish that every remedy is lawful. The concurrence accepts the Court's concrete comparison and the separate consideration of legitimate county interests. It supplies no right to enlarge any government until a minority can win, no additional remedy, and no disposition of the unreviewed constitutional claim. Its additional reenactment and stare decisis rationale has four named adherents and is not an independently controlling alternative holding.

##### O'Connor's concurrence in part and dissent in part; Kennedy joins Part II only

O'Connor agrees in Part I that governing-body size is a covered standard, practice or procedure. Chisom and Houston Lawyers support that threshold conclusion, and statutory stare decisis weighs against Thomas's proposal to eliminate dilution doctrine. She accordingly joins Part I of the Court's opinion. But coverage does not answer whether electoral opportunity can be measured against an objective alternative. Kennedy reserves that affirmative coverage conclusion and does not join this part of her separate writing.

In Part II, joined by Kennedy, O'Connor would reject this size-dilution claim. Gingles requires an undiluted norm against which impairment can be measured. A different method of electing existing offices may supply such a norm; the statute offers no principled choice of how many offices the government must have. Georgia's authorization, the school-board arrangement and the potential majority district show that an alternative might work. They do not explain why that size supplies the legally relevant baseline for a county government with different responsibilities. The rejected referendum underscores the existence of a governmental choice without establishing immunity from otherwise applicable law. This objection concerns size as the comparison variable, not an invitation simply to propose another feasible number. Section 5's review of change uses an existing practice as its baseline; its coverage cannot cure the different Section 2 comparison problem. The two Justices would reverse statutory liability and remand for appellate consideration of the unresolved constitutional claim, without finding discriminatory purpose, imposing an intent requirement on ordinary results liability, or abolishing other dilution claims.

##### Thomas's dissent, joined by Scalia

Thomas and Scalia would reverse on a broader statutory ground. They read Section 2's qualifications, prerequisites, standards, practices and procedures in the context of the Act's protection of registration, access to the ballot, casting a vote and proper counting. In their view, that language does not reach the allocation of electoral weight among groups through governing-body size or districting. Subsection (b) identifies discriminatory results within the practices covered by subsection (a); it does not enlarge that subject matter into a vote-dilution cause of action. The results test would therefore remain for covered ballot-access practices, without a new requirement to prove purpose.

They regard the lack of a principled body-size benchmark and the difficulty of choosing a theory of representation as further reasons to reject dilution doctrine. The proportional-representation proviso reinforces their objection to making racial electoral success the measure of the statutory right. They would reject Gingles's contrary statutory construction and the incompatible Section 2 propositions in Growe and Voinovich, despite their participation in those decisions. They do not claim that those decisions already adopted their proposed construction. Their agreement that size presents a comparison problem does not join O'Connor and Kennedy's sufficient rationale, which leaves ordinary dilution protection intact.

The dissent preserves its authors' constitutional objection to racial separation expressed in their Shaw dissent, while recognizing that it is not the Court's governing rule. It decides no constitutional-purpose claim, overrules no Section 5 precedent and supplies no alternative map or board size. Its proposed disposition is reversal of statutory liability followed by consideration of the constitutional claim below. Neither its broader statutory theory nor the distinct size-comparison dissent changes the Court's controlling rule.

### International Union, United Mine Workers of America v. Bagwell, No. 92-1625

Merits decision, June 30, 1994

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 29, 1993; decided June 30, 1994. On writ of certiorari to the Supreme Court of Virginia, 244 Va. 463, 423 S.E.2d 349, which on November 6, 1992 reversed the intermediate court and entered final judgment for Bagwell in Record 910634 and affirmed the circuit court in Record 920299. The question is whether the surviving substantial fines for completed out-of-court violations are civil coercion because announced prospectively or criminal punishment requiring a jury.

The 1989 labor injunction prohibited violence, obstruction and specified picketing conduct and required supervision and reporting. The initial $642,000 fines, including a $424,000 suspension, were later vacated and are not under review. The announced schedule for future breaches was $100,000 per violent violation and $20,000 per nonviolent violation. Seven later hearings addressed more than 400 violations and imposed over $64 million, with extensive adversarial process and proof beyond a reasonable doubt but no jury. After settlement the injunction was dissolved and approximately $12 million payable to the Company was vacated. Roughly $52 million for the Commonwealth and two counties remained collectible by Bagwell, preserving a live controversy. No party justified those sums as compensation measured by proved loss; later obedience could not purge the assessed liabilities.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg participated at argument and decision. No case-specific nonparticipation is established.

#### Judgment

The Virginia Supreme Court's judgment sustaining the surviving public fines is reversed, and the case is remanded, 9–0.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Surviving approximately $52 million public assessments | Reverse and remand, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | No Justice | The fines cannot be collected on these civil nonjury adjudications; any further lawful proceeding must respect criminal safeguards and all independent defenses. |

The Court revives neither the initial vacated fines nor the approximately $12 million private assessments, awards no substitute compensation, commands no prosecution and decides no future double-jeopardy defense. It invalidates no injunction wholesale and fixes no new fine or exact mandate date.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|

| Concurrence | Scalia | No other Justice | Joins the Court throughout | Adds a historical-method explanation; reserves a comprehensive future classification test. |

Direct majorities support every holding. Thomas joins no separate Scalia writing; Ginsburg's limited join is not judgment-only agreement with the entire opinion.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The surviving fixed public fines are criminal punishment

**Controlling proposition:** Substantial fixed fines imposed for completed out-of-court violations, unmeasured by proved loss and offering no postimposition compliance-based means to avoid the assessed liability, are criminal punishment in the circumstances presented here. Advance announcement of the schedule does not make these fines civil; genuinely compensatory relief and conditional coercion remain distinct.

**Authority:** Part I of Justice Kennedy's Opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg. All nine directly adopt the classification and application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the fines' operation determines their character. Gompers distinguishes relief benefiting a complainant through performance from punishment for completed disobedience; Hicks requires examination of the substantive relief rather than the label. These public assessments punish completed breaches and cannot be purged through renewed obedience. No party asserts measured compensation for proved loss. Bagwell answers that the Union could have avoided every fine by obeying the announced schedule. But advance notice also accompanies ordinary criminal prohibitions; it does not supply a later means to discharge these liabilities. Mine Workers preserves a fixed fine suspended on a real compliance condition, and Shillitani permits coercion that ends when the contemnor performs the required act. Those mechanisms remain available where their actual conditions exist. Here settlement, dissolution of the injunction and survival of the public assessments underscore the absence of continuing private remedial relief. The Court does not decide that every complex decree, public payee or accrued coercive payment makes a sanction criminal.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Gompers v. Bucks Stove & Range Co., 221 U.S. 418 (1911): applied to distinguish remedial performance from punishment for completed violations.
- United States v. Mine Workers, 330 U.S. 258 (1947): preserved; a suspended fine with a genuine later compliance condition differs from these fixed liabilities.
- Shillitani v. United States, 384 U.S. 364 (1966): preserved; conditional coercion depends on a continuing lawful opportunity to perform the required act.
- Hicks v. Feiock, 485 U.S. 624 (1988): applied; federal constitutional classification follows the proceeding and relief's substance, not its civil label.

**Civil remedies and limits:** Compensation must correspond to proved loss. Genuine coercion may use prospective per-day sanctions avoidable through present compliance, confinement terminable by performance, or a fixed fine suspended on a real compliance condition. The contemnor must have the relevant ability to comply. The Court does not require cancellation of every already accrued per-day burden upon eventual compliance. Wording a command affirmatively or negatively does not itself settle classification; this decree contained both kinds of obligations. No clause-by-clause severance is undertaken. The Court does not displace the distinct classification of legislative civil schemes or resolve every discovery or litigation-management sanction.

##### Necessity for enforcement does not excuse criminal procedure on this record

**Controlling proposition:** The need to enforce this continuing decree does not justify imposing serious punitive sanctions for dispersed out-of-court conduct without the criminal safeguards those sanctions require. The scale of the liabilities, complex obligations and extensive retrospective factfinding reinforce the criminal classification; neither complexity nor amount alone supplies a universal civil/criminal test.

**Authority:** Part II of Justice Kennedy's opinion, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Thomas. Eight Justices adopt this additional bounded procedural explanation. Ginsburg joins the common classification and remedy but not this account.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that enforcement necessity must be assessed in the setting in which the power is used. Immediate courtroom obstruction can require an immediate judicial response under the established limits of direct contempt. Discrete acts that impede adjudication or remain susceptible to performance-based coercion present different needs from a continuing decree regulating many activities outside the court's presence. The present proceedings required extensive evidence and resolution of disputes about hundreds of completed acts. The judge's interest in enforcing the order does not remove the procedural protection that Bloom attaches to serious criminal punishment. Shillitani's power to induce an identifiable performance does not answer that concern. The opposing argument correctly stresses that civil courts must retain effective means of enforcement, but it does not make these final public liabilities conditional or compensatory. This explanation strengthens the classification already established by their operation; it creates no free-form balance permitting a judge to replace a required jury with procedures thought equally reliable.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Bloom v. Illinois, 391 U.S. 194 (1968): applied; the enforcement interest does not dispense with the jury for serious criminal contempt.
- Shillitani v. United States, 384 U.S. 364 (1966): distinguished as continuing coercion directed to an identifiable act the contemnor could perform.

**Scope:** Existing direct-contempt powers remain subject to their own limits; directness alone does not remove the jury right when the punishment is serious. This case involves indirect contempt. No actual judicial bias is found, no mechanical complexity rule is adopted, and no independent ground validates or invalidates the underlying labor injunction.

##### Serious criminal contempt requires a jury and these civil assessments cannot stand

**Controlling proposition:** These approximately $52 million public fines constitute serious criminal contempt and require applicable criminal safeguards, including a jury. Proof beyond a reasonable doubt before a judge does not replace that protection; the judgment sustaining collection through these nonjury civil adjudications is reversed and the case remanded, without directing a new prosecution or reviving any vacated fines.

**Authority:** Part III of Justice Kennedy's opinion, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg. All nine adopt the jury rule and bounded reversal/remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the substantial process furnished here did not include the independent protection the Constitution requires. Bloom establishes a jury right for serious criminal contempt even though a judge has a strong interest in vindicating the court's authority. Notice, discovery, cross-examination and proof beyond a reasonable doubt serve real purposes, but they do not constitute a jury. The approximately $52 million surviving public liabilities are plainly serious without fixing a numerical boundary for petty contempt. The civil character asserted below therefore cannot sustain their collection. This conclusion does not forbid punishment of violent or obstructive conduct through lawful criminal proceedings, confer special immunity on a labor organization, or decide every sanction available under an injunction. It removes the judgment's present legal basis and leaves any further proceeding to its own authority and defenses. Dixon's criminal-jeopardy rule remains applicable in its proper setting, but no hypothetical later charge, offense identity or double-jeopardy defense is adjudicated here.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Bloom v. Illinois, 391 U.S. 194 (1968): applied; serious criminal contempt requires a jury, while the petty-offense qualification remains distinct from the civil/criminal classification.
- United States v. Dixon, June 28, 1993: its rule for completed nonsummary criminal contempt and its retention of Grady remain intact; no future prosecution or defense is decided.

**Remedial boundaries:** No jury waiver or harmlessness finding is made. The vacated initial $642,000 fines, including the former $424,000 suspension, and the vacated approximately $12 million Company assessments remain outside the surviving judgment. No substitute compensation, new fine amount, conviction, prosecution, specific mandate date, substantive-excessiveness ruling or wholesale invalidation of the dissolved injunction is ordered. Petty criminal contempt may lack a jury requirement but remains criminal and retains its applicable safeguards.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Gompers, Mine Workers, Shillitani and Hicks supply the retained relief-based distinctions, and Bloom requires the independent jury safeguard. The precise treatments appear with each holding. Dixon's existing criminal-contempt and successive-prosecution rules, including retained Grady, remain unchanged; no future offense or defense is decided. Kurth Ranch's separate tax classification and Honda's civil punitive-review rule do not replace contempt precedent. No precedent is overruled.

Effective June 30, 1994, the prospectively announced schedule does not convert these fixed, noncompensatory and nonpurgeable public fines into civil coercion. Their serious criminal character requires a jury despite the heightened proof burden used below. The additional necessity/factfinding account reinforces this application without a universal complexity or amount test. Genuine loss compensation and conditional performance-based civil coercion remain available within their predicates. The case creates no special labor exemption, numerical petty-contempt ceiling, automatic prosecution command or prospective jeopardy ruling.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Scalia joins the Court's opinion but separately declines to select a comprehensive test for every civil/criminal boundary. Traditional coercive contempt commonly induced an identifiable act, or cessation of an identifiable continuing act, with confinement or per-day pressure ending through compliance. A continuing judicial code regulating many activities differs: obligations persist and retrospective credibility disputes multiply, concentrating rulemaking, accusation, factfinding and punishment in the same court. Those historical concerns reinforce criminal safeguards on these extreme facts. A discrete supervisory act can remain susceptible to civil coercion; the present record does not authorize separating particular assessments by clause. Ending future per-day pressure does not necessarily erase burdens already accrued. His historical account is additional and noncontrolling; he leaves the exact boundary for other decrees open. No other Justice joins this concurrence.

Justice Ginsburg concurs in part and in the judgment. She joins Parts I and III and relies on the traditional Gompers/Hicks distinction. A contemnor can perform the act required by genuine coercive relief; punishment for a completed forbidden act cannot be undone by promising future obedience. The advance schedule offered only the opportunity to obey before committing the violation, just as criminal law does. Mine Workers's suspended fine provided a later compliance route absent here. Survival of these unpaid public assessments after settlement, withdrawal of the private complainant and appointment of a collection official further reveals vindication of public authority. In this setting, a proceeding retaining only the asserted private civil character would call for vacatur after the parties' settlement and joint request. She announces no payee-only test or rule governing every settlement. She agrees that these serious criminal sanctions require a jury, while declining Part II's additional necessity and factfinding account. No other Justice joins this concurrence.

### Johnson v. De Grandy; De Grandy v. Johnson; United States v. Florida, Nos. 92-519, 92-593 and 92-767, respectively

Merits decision, June 30, 1994

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 4, 1993; decided June 30, 1994. On direct appeals under 28 U.S.C. §1253 from the three-judge District Court for the Northern District of Florida, De Grandy v. Wetherell, 815 F. Supp. 1550. The trial ended and rulings were announced July 1, 1992; House and Senate judgments were entered July 2, the House judgment was supplemented July 6, and the explanatory opinion issued July 17.

The questions are whether Florida's preliminary validation precludes the private Section 2 claims or bars the United States' independent enforcement action, and whether the tried Dade-area House and Senate arrangements deny equal electoral opportunity under Section 2. State officials challenge House liability and the substituted House plan and defend the Senate result; the private Hispanic challengers and the United States seek additional Senate relief. The Black claim and proposed alternative bear on the lower court's asserted remedial conflict, without a separate Black appeal being decided here.

Florida adopted SJR 2-G on April 10, 1992, creating 120 single-member House districts and forty single-member Senate districts. The May 13 state validation, 597 So.2d 276, occurred within a thirty-day timetable and preserved later factual Section 2 challenges. Federal complaints were amended to challenge the new plan. The United States was not a party to the validation. The June 25 Hillsborough adjustment, 601 So.2d 543, responded to a Section 5 objection and did not adjudicate the Dade claims. Dade was not among Florida's five covered counties.

The District Court found continuing effects of official discrimination, substantial polarized voting, and cohesion within Hispanic and Black groups but no general cohesion between them. The House comparison is nine effective Hispanic districts among eighteen primarily-Dade districts against 50 percent Hispanic voting-age population, or nine of twenty districts touching Dade against about 47 percent. These are distinct descriptive frames. Experts identified neighborhood splitting in District 116, concentration in District 114 and fragmentation involving Districts 102 and 109. Those descriptions are not a finding that comparable minority and other neighborhoods were subjected to inconsistent districting standards.

The initial De Grandy House proposal listed eleven Hispanic districts with voting-age percentages of at least 64.5 percent. The imposed Modified De Grandy Plan 268 instead included District 109 at 63.74 percent and District 110 at 62.56 percent. The State's separate ten-district and eleven-district remedial proposals were different plans; the latter included two approximately 61-percent Miami Beach districts whose effectiveness was disputed because of recent noncitizen arrivals. The Court assumes the disputed first Gingles condition favorably and decides no citizenship method, universal percentage minimum or precise remedial-plan effectiveness.

The seven-district Senate area includes substantial Dade voters: Hispanic and Black voting-age shares are 44.8 and 15.8 percent, with three effective Hispanic-majority districts and one Black-majority district, approximately 42.9 and 14.3 percent of seven. Another district supplies Black electoral opportunity with crossover support. In the distinct five wholly-Dade districts, Hispanic and Black voting-age shares are 53.9 and 13.5 percent, with three Hispanic supermajorities and one Black majority. The broader South Florida account includes a Broward/Palm Beach Black-majority district outside the Dade seven. The July 2 Senate judgment stated no violation; the July 17 explanation described violations but conflicting proposed remedies. The Court reviews the operative judgment on the grounds below.

Congressional proceedings, the Escambia settlement, dismissed claims outside the tried area, dismissed constitutional claims and preclearance decisions remain undisturbed. No constitutional challenge to these district lines is decided.

All nine Justices participate at argument and decision: Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg. No recusal or nonparticipation is established.

The Court reverses the House Section 2 liability and dependent remedial judgment and affirms the Senate judgment, 7–2 on each component. The State's procedural objections are rejected, and no additional districting relief is ordered.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. The state validation does not preclude the reserved factual claims

**Controlling proposition:** Under 28 U.S.C. §1738, Florida's validation receives its actual state-law preclusive scope, including its express preservation of later factual Section 2 challenges; it therefore does not bar these private claims. The availability of a return to state court does not require exhaustion of that optional procedure before this preserved federal litigation.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part I-A, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Ginsburg; seven Justices directly adopt this limited rule. Thomas and Scalia do not join the opinion, and their different statutory disposition supplies no additional published joinder.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that respecting a state judgment requires respecting its limits as well as its conclusions. Marrese requires the federal court to begin with the preclusive effect supplied by state law. Allen preserves ordinary preclusion principles without making every federal-rights claim immune from them. Florida's constitutional validation took place on a thirty-day timetable and expressly preserved subsequent factual challenges under Section 2. The private plaintiffs now present the kind of challenge that remained available; their amended complaints concern the new plan. The State's interest in finality cannot convert that limited validation into a completed adjudication of the reserved claims. Growe requires legal effect for an operative state plan, but does not insulate it from a properly pleaded federal defect. Nor does permission to seek further state review make that route mandatory. The Court creates no general Voting Rights Act exception to Section 1738 and decides no preclusion question beyond the judgment's established reservation.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Marrese v. American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, 470 U.S. 373: applies the state-law-first rule for the preclusive effect of a state judgment; no additional federal preclusion is imposed.
- Allen v. McCurry, 449 U.S. 90: preserves ordinary preclusion and its qualifications; creates no categorical federal-forum exemption for Voting Rights Act claims.
- Growe v. Emison, February 23, 1993: follows its requirement to respect the legally operative state plan and distinguishes its obsolete-plan defect; no enlarged party-preclusion rule follows.

##### 2. The United States brings an original enforcement suit

**Controlling proposition:** Rooker and Feldman do not bar the United States' independent Section 2 enforcement suit against this plan: the United States was not a party to the state validation and seeks no lower-federal appellate review of a state judgment entered against it. The holding does not decide every nonparty, privity, intervention or intertwined-claim question or permit review of a preclearance determination.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part I-B, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Ginsburg; seven Justices directly adopt the original-suit distinction on these facts. Thomas and Scalia publish no join in this part.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this enforcement action falls within original federal jurisdiction. Rooker denies federal district courts appellate authority over a state judicial judgment. Feldman preserves that limit while distinguishing an independent challenge from review of a state court's determination. The United States did not participate in Florida's validation proceeding and has no adverse state judgment of its own rights to overturn. It brings its statutory claim against the enacted electoral arrangement. The fact that success could unsettle a court-approved map does not itself transform that original suit into an appeal. The Government's other federal litigation and preclearance responsibilities did not make it a party to the state proceeding. The Court need not classify every aspect of state plan validation as legislative or judicial, nor reconsider ordinary preclusion. Shaw's separate restriction on review of federal preclearance remains intact; no such review is sought here. Jurisdiction permits adjudication of the claim and does not establish its merits.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Rooker v. Fidelity Trust Co., 263 U.S. 413: applies the limit on lower-federal appellate review of state judgments; this independent enforcement action does not invoke that authority.
- District of Columbia Court of Appeals v. Feldman, 460 U.S. 462: preserves the appellate/original distinction without deciding every possible relationship between nonparties and a state judgment.
- Shaw v. Reno, June 28, 1993: preserves the designated channel and nonreviewability limits for federal preclearance; those limits do not immunize a state plan from an independent statutory claim.

##### 3. Necessary voting conditions do not require maximization

**Controlling proposition:** This Section 2 fragmentation claim requires a minority sufficiently large and geographically compact to form a district majority, political cohesion, and majority bloc voting ordinarily sufficient to defeat its preferred candidate, absent special circumstances such as an unopposed minority candidate; the claimant must also establish unequal opportunity to participate and elect chosen representatives under the totality of circumstances, without proving discriminatory purpose. Even when those necessary conditions are satisfied, Section 2 does not require every feasible additional majority-minority district; the disputed first condition is assumed here, leaving citizenship, population-denominator and coalition or influence-claim questions unresolved.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Ginsburg; seven Justices directly adopt the complete necessary-versus-sufficient distinction and rejection of maximization. Thomas and Scalia's statutory noncoverage position is a different rationale and supplies no join.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a possible additional district identifies potential electoral opportunity, not an automatic statutory entitlement. Gingles supplies the conditions connecting the challenged arrangement to the asserted dilution. Growe applies those conditions to single-member fragmentation and requires local voting evidence rather than addition of distinct groups' populations. Voinovich keeps the burden on challengers to establish unequal opportunity and distinguishes state districting choices from judicial relief for a proved violation. Holder likewise separates a workable comparison from proof of injury and from remedy. Those decisions retain the statutory totality inquiry. Here the Court assumes the contested first condition in the challengers' favor and accepts the findings of cohesion within each minority group and polarized voting. Neither those premises nor the feasibility of additional districts dispenses with the remaining inquiry. Congress preserved a results claim while disclaiming proportional officeholding. The Court therefore rejects both an automatic maximization requirement and a new discriminatory-purpose requirement, without selecting a citizenship measure or deciding an influence or coalition entitlement.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Thornburg v. Gingles, 478 U.S. 30: follows its causal conditions, special-circumstances qualification and independent totality inquiry; the conditions do not establish liability by themselves.
- Growe v. Emison, February 23, 1993: preserves local cohesion and majority-bloc proof for single-member fragmentation and the unresolved coalition, influence and population questions.
- Voinovich v. Quilter, March 2, 1993: follows the challengers' burden and the distinction between a State's authority to choose districts and a court's power to remedy a proved violation.
- Holder v. Hall, June 30, 1994: applies its separation of coverage, comparison, proof and remedy; a permissible comparison creates no maximization command.
- Shaw v. Reno, June 28, 1993: preserves the independent statutory elements; its constitutional injury rule adds no further element to Section 2.

##### 4. Proportional opportunity is relevant evidence, not a safe harbor

**Controlling proposition:** The relationship between a group's share of the relevant population and the share of districts in which it has an effective opportunity to elect its preferred candidates is relevant evidence within Section 2's totality inquiry, whose weight depends on the other evidence; it is neither a conclusive safe harbor nor a burden-shifting affirmative defense, and disproportionality alone likewise does not establish liability. The inquiry concerns demonstrated electoral opportunity, not nominal district labels or the race of officeholders; it guarantees no proportional election, racial floor or ceiling, statewide offset, or universal geographic or population denominator, and cannot excuse proved local packing, fragmentation or another barrier merely because opportunities exist elsewhere.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Ginsburg; six Justices directly adopt this contextual rule and its explanation. Kennedy separately accepts the exact limited propositions that proportionality is relevant, that the necessary conditions alone do not establish liability, and that proportionality supplies no safe harbor; his concurrence does not join Part III's full rationale or Parts IV and V's affirmative applications. No combined rationale under Marks is needed.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that actual opportunity matters to the statutory question. Gingles requires an appraisal of the local electoral system under the totality, and Voinovich rejects liability inferred solely from a preferred district arrangement. A relationship between effective districts and population may bear substantially on whether an asserted lack of opportunity exists. But district opportunity, election outcomes and officeholding by racial identity are different facts. The statutory permission to consider protected-class electoral success does not guarantee proportional election or make any numerical relationship decisive. History, polarization and particular barriers retain their force when the aggregate appears favorable. Thus a State cannot defend proved local packing or fragmentation simply by pointing to compensating opportunities elsewhere. Conversely, an arithmetical shortfall does not itself prove the statutory wrong. The State's conclusive-defense proposal fails, as does treating statewide measurement as indispensable to that supposed defense. The Court uses the locally tried record without announcing a general geographic rule or resolving unnecessary preservation questions under Rule 8(c).

**Precedent treatment:**

- Gingles, 478 U.S. 30: follows its contextual inquiry and treatment of electoral success; adopts no conclusive proportional-success presumption.
- Voinovich, March 2, 1993: preserves the need for proof of actual unequal opportunity rather than automatic condemnation or approval of a district form.
- Holder, June 30, 1994: preserves independent totality proof after an adequate comparison; its local institutional comparison supplies neither a quota nor an evidentiary safe harbor.

##### 5. The House liability and dependent remedy cannot stand on this record

**Controlling proposition:** Assuming the disputed first Gingles condition in the challengers' favor and retaining the supported findings of discrimination and polarized voting, the tried House record does not establish unequal opportunity under the totality beyond the erroneous inference that every feasible additional Hispanic district must be created. The liability judgment and dependent substitution of Modified De Grandy Plan 268 are reversed; no new liability trial, Supreme Court replacement map or numerical district minimum is ordered, and implementation must follow lawful election administration.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part IV, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Ginsburg; six Justices adopt this full contextual application. Kennedy supports reversal and the mandate under Parts II and VI on the narrower rejection of maximization, without joining Part IV; the House disposition therefore has seven votes, against Thomas and Scalia's vacatur-and-dismissal alternative.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the House judgment used the wrong measure of injury. The findings of continuing discriminatory effects and polarization remain serious and accepted. Feasibility is assumed favorably. Nine effective Hispanic districts accompany about half the voting-age population in the eighteen primarily-Dade districts; the distinct twenty-district frame yields nine opportunities against about 47 percent Hispanic voting-age population. Those relationships are evidence, not immunity. The Court also considers testimony about split neighborhoods in District 116, concentration in District 114, and fragmentation involving Districts 102 and 109. No finding establishes inconsistent standards for comparable minority and other neighborhoods; such a finding is not a new prerequisite to liability. The credited testimony, history and voting evidence do not establish the asserted unequal opportunity merely because two more districts are feasible. The liability announcement and insistence on eleven districts instead made that possibility obligatory. Gingles requires respect for local findings under Rule 52(a), but does not insulate the legal error defining the ultimate injury. Voinovich and Holder require a proved wrong before federal relief. The dependent remedy consequently falls without adjudicating its precise boundaries or reopening liability.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Gingles, 478 U.S. 30: follows the totality standard and Rule 52(a)'s protection of supported findings; corrects a legally infected liability determination without creating a ratio presumption or new intent requirement.
- Voinovich, March 2, 1993: applies the need for an established federal violation before judicial imposition of remedial districts.
- Holder, June 30, 1994: distinguishes a sufficient comparison from the separate proof of unequal opportunity; its established liability and remedy remand do not supply missing liability here.

##### 6. The Senate judgment stands because no violation was proved

**Controlling proposition:** On the tried Senate record, the asserted unequal opportunity is not established under the totality merely because an additional Hispanic district and a different additional Black district are each feasible; the judgment leaving the State's Senate plan operative is affirmed for lack of a proved violation, not because a proved violation may remain when proposed remedies conflict. The existing Black electoral opportunity with crossover support is relevant evidence, not a second Black-majority district, proof of general cohesion between Hispanic and Black voters, or a legal entitlement to a crossover district.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part V, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Ginsburg; six Justices adopt this full contextual application. Kennedy supports affirmance under Parts II and VI on the narrower no-maximization ground without joining Part V; the Senate disposition has seven votes, against Thomas and Scalia's vacatur-and-dismissal alternative.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Senate result rests on absence of statutory injury. The July 2 judgment found no violation, while the July 17 explanation described violations but competing remedies. The latter reasoning cannot excuse a proved wrong. Gingles requires examination of actual opportunity, and Holder requires lawful relief once injury is established. Here the seven-district area has three effective Hispanic-majority districts and one Black-majority district against respective voting-age shares of 44.8 and 15.8 percent. A further district supplies demonstrated Black electoral opportunity with crossover support. The Court considers those opportunities with the accepted discrimination and polarization findings; no ratio alone controls. The five wholly-Dade districts and broader South Florida account remain distinct. Separate cohesion within the two groups does not establish a joint coalition. The possibility of maximizing each group's districts separately does not first prove two injuries and then create a conflict of federal rights. Voinovich preserves the difference between feasible arrangements and required remedies. No additional Senate district follows, and no separate Black appeal or influence-district entitlement is decided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Gingles, 478 U.S. 30: applies contextual unequal-opportunity proof without inferring liability from separately feasible district increases.
- Growe, February 23, 1993: preserves the need for proved common cohesion before distinct groups can be treated as one electoral coalition.
- Voinovich, March 2, 1993: preserves the unresolved influence-claim question and ties compelled relief to an established violation.
- Holder, June 30, 1994: preserves the obligation to formulate lawful relief for an established wrong; no competing-remedies exception to liability or relief is recognized.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

The holding-specific treatments govern. Gingles's causal conditions, independent totality inquiry and protection of supported local findings remain; the Court rejects neither dilution coverage nor the 1982 results standard. Growe and Voinovich retain state responsibility, local proof requirements, the challengers' burden and the distinction between a permissible state district choice and a judicial remedy for a proved wrong. Holder's coverage, reasonable-comparison, proof and remedy holdings remain distinct; none creates maximization.

Allen and Marrese govern the actual preclusive scope of the state validation. Rooker and Feldman retain their lower-federal appellate limit. Shaw's constitutional boundary and its separate federal-preclearance channel remain unchanged. Presley v. Etowah County Commission, January 27, 1992, preserves the Section 5 voting/governing boundary and independent Section 2 claims; it supplies no nonretrogression defense to these Dade claims. No precedent is overruled. The dissent's contrary statutory construction is noncontrolling.

Effective June 30, 1994, the ordinary Section 2 fragmentation predicates remain necessary but do not establish liability without unequal opportunity under the totality. The Court expressly rejects maximization and a conclusive proportionality defense. Relevant proportionality concerns effective electoral opportunity and must be assessed with the complete local evidence; it does not guarantee proportional election, shift the claimant's burden or excuse proved local injury. The full contextual House and Senate applications have six direct adherents; the mandate and the necessary-versus-sufficient rule have seven. No fractured-opinion combination is required.

The disputed first Gingles condition is assumed for decision. Citizenship methods, the population denominator, a universal geographic scale, coalition cognizability, influence claims and adaptation of the majority-size condition remain unresolved. The Court neither decides a general Rule 8(c) preservation rule nor replaces the tried local claim with an untried statewide claim. Section 2's race, color and language-minority coverage and its permissive consideration of protected-class electoral success remain distinct from the disclaimer of proportional officeholding; no question under the language-minority cross-reference is separately decided.

Supported findings of history and polarization remain. Inconsistent treatment of comparable neighborhoods is not made a mandatory element, discriminatory purpose is not required, and no statutory violation may be excused because proposed remedies conflict. Constitutional claims retain their own elements, and preclearance supplies no general immunity. These appeals determine the presented statutory claims, not the legality of every racial district configuration or a future electoral record.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

##### O'Connor's concurrence

O'Connor joins the Court throughout and emphasizes the proper evidentiary role of proportionality. The relationship between effective opportunity districts and population is relevant to the ultimate statutory inquiry and must be considered when the record supplies it. Its relevance does not depend on a presumption that a particular racial ratio defines the right. Gingles's local appraisal remains necessary, and other evidence can show unequal opportunity despite aggregate proportionality. Conversely, a shortfall does not itself establish dilution. Her concurrence creates no affirmative-defense burden shift, district quota, statewide denominator or constitutional approval. It explains why neither ignoring the evidence nor making it conclusive states the Court's rule.

##### Kennedy's concurrence in part and in the judgment

Kennedy joins the limited procedural holdings, the necessary-versus-sufficient distinction, the rejection of maximization and the mandate. Voinovich separates a possible districting arrangement from proved injury; Holder's comparison dispute likewise illustrates why a desired alternative does not itself measure the statutory right. The lower court's treatment of feasible additional districts as required supplies his ground for reversing the House judgment and affirming the Senate result.

Kennedy separately accepts that proportionality has limited relevance, that the Gingles conditions do not automatically establish liability and that proportionality cannot provide a safe harbor. He withholds the Court's full affirmative application of proportionality and any suggestion that this statutory disposition constitutionally validates race-conscious districting. His constitutional concern is a reservation consistent with his dissent in Shaw, not the governing rule of that case. Shaw's rejection of the design-only theory remains the Court's law, and no constitutional claim is presented here. His concurrence supplies no racial floor or ceiling, statewide offset, requirement to merge minority groups, or order to create an additional district. The six-Justice full application controls without expanding his joins.

##### Thomas's dissent, joined by Scalia

Thomas and Scalia would vacate the appealed judgments and remand with instructions to dismiss the consolidated Section 2 actions for failure to state a covered claim. They maintain the statutory construction stated in their Holder dissent: the Act protects registration, ballot access, casting and proper counting, rather than the allocation of electoral weight through district boundaries. In their view, subsection (b)'s results inquiry operates within the practices covered by subsection (a); it does not create a districting-dilution cause of action. They would preserve a results test for practices they regard as covered, without requiring discriminatory intent for those practices.

Their construction would reject Gingles's contrary dilution rule and the incompatible portions of Growe and Voinovich, despite their earlier joins. They recognize that current majority law takes the other view. The difficulty of selecting a theory of group representation reinforces their objection, but does not turn their broader construction into a join in the Court's no-maximization or proportionality analysis. Nor do they merely affirm the Senate judgment on a different measure of statutory injury: their proposed vacatur and dismissal is a different formal mandate. They adjudicate no constitutional claim and would not revive dismissed claims, reopen congressional proceedings or disturb the Escambia settlement. Their disagreement changes no controlling precedent.

### Madsen v. Women's Health Center, Inc., No. 93-880

Merits decision, June 30, 1994

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 28, 1994; merits decision June 30, 1994. On writ of certiorari to the Supreme Court of Florida, *Operation Rescue v. Women's Health Center, Inc.*, 626 So. 2d 664 (October 28, 1993), No. 81905. The Florida court approved an April 8, 1993 amended permanent injunction entered after three days of evidence in Seminole County No. 91-2811-CA-16-K; the Fifth District certified immediate review. The amendment followed the September 30, 1992 permanent injunction. The demonstrators seek reversal of the challenged restrictions; the clinic seeks affirmance.

The three petition questions concern whether the 36-foot clinic zone is an unconstitutional content-based restriction; whether the 300-foot clinic-approach and residential restrictions are reasonable time, place and manner controls or unconstitutional prior restraints; and whether restrictions on named demonstrators and persons acting in concert violate speech and association. Petitioners' description of residential consent does not replace the order: paragraph (6) contains territorial and obstruction prohibitions, while a separate invited-contact paragraph governs willing residential contact. Geographic tailoring of the clinic zone and review of paragraph (4)'s noise and images provisions receive the distinct scope treatment stated below. Both parties argued paragraphs (7)–(9), whose independent substantive validity is nevertheless left undecided.

The findings describe continued interference with driveway access and traffic, unwanted approaches to vehicles, sound reaching patients during surgery and recovery, fence-side intrusion and residential misconduct. The prior order had prohibited trespass, obstruction and physical abuse while preserving lawful signs, singing and prayer. Not every crowd member or sound source was attributed to these defendants. Petitioners did not supply a complete certified evidentiary record and assumed a factual basis for their legal appeal. The Court accepts the supported findings while reviewing the legal sufficiency and breadth of the remedy; it makes no new site, volume or individual-concert finding.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg participated at argument and decision. No recusal or nonparticipation is established.

#### Judgment

The Florida judgment is affirmed in part and reversed in part, and the case is remanded. The qualified public access arrangement and medical-noise restriction survive; the north/west private-property extension, images prohibition, initial-consent approach condition and 300-foot residential territorial ban do not. The Court does not order a replacement personal-separation injunction.

The public zone's southern boundary is the south edge of Dixie Way's pavement; the south unpaved shoulder remains usable subject to other lawful restrictions. On the east, defendants may use the U.S. Highway One shoulder, may not ascend the embankment, and must stay at least five feet from the clinic's east line. The north/west owners and invitees had an exception from entry prohibition, with other restrictions applicable when acting in concert; reversal of that extension does not adjudicate their property rights. Pending painted lines, the crossing is an eight-foot strip running directly from the south edge to the north edge of Dixie Way, with its western line coextensive with the western right-of-way line of U.S. Highway One. Once marked, the crosswalk governs. Within the buffer no other pavement entry is allowed. Crossing must be prompt, without stopping, hesitation, picketing, congregation, demonstration or impediment to traffic. This existing crosswalk dimension is distinct from the separate opinion's proposed personal separation.

The noise restriction applies from 7:30 a.m. through noon, Monday through Saturday, during surgical procedures and recovery periods, to patients inside the clinic. It covers singing, chanting, whistling, shouting, yelling, bullhorns, auto horns, sound amplification equipment and other sounds within those patients' earshot, in the lower court's medical-operation context. The Court adds no decibel, intent or volume threshold. The Court rejects suppression because of a message's viewpoint or its mere disagreeableness.

Paragraph (5) concerns physical approach, not every word or sign within 300 feet. Its invitation provision permitted nonthreatening conversation and literature when a service-seeker approached or inquired, but never inside the 36-foot zone. Its withdrawal protections prohibit accompanying, encircling, surrounding, harassment, threats and physical or verbal abuse of a declining person. Reversal of the advance-invitation requirement does not invalidate every independently lawful conduct protection or approve every disputed use of those labels.

Paragraph (6)'s separate bans on blocking entrances, exits or driveways and impeding a street providing sole access to the residences' street are not the territorial ban. The separate invited-contact definition permits willing contact outside the clinic buffer; residential invitation must be communicated from a distance and before contact, cannot be obtained by an uninvited knock or doorbell, and may be revoked, requiring immediate termination and departure. Removing the territorial speech exclusion does not create a right to trespass or compel residential contact. The remand must conform any retained invitation or conduct language to the reversal, rather than recreate a territorial exclusion through it.

Paragraph (7) addresses physical abuse, grabbing, intimidation, harassment, touching, pushing, shoving, crowding and assault of persons entering, leaving, working at or using the clinic, or seeking access to specified homes; it has its own invited-contact qualification. Paragraph (8) addresses harassment, intimidation, physical abuse, assault and threats against present or former clinicians, staff, employees or volunteers, without that invited-contact text. Paragraph (9) addresses encouraging, inciting or securing another's prohibited act. Their different terms remain distinct. Paragraphs (1) and (2), concerning clinic-property entry and access obstruction, are unchallenged; the predecessor remains effective except as lawfully modified. The Court enters no contempt judgment and decides no stranger's liability or the separate *Cheffer* litigation.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The limited scope of geographic and paragraph (4) review

**Controlling proposition:** Geographic tailoring of the 36-foot zone is fairly included in the presented challenge to exclusion from peaceful expression in designated zones. Paragraph (4)'s separate noise and images questions receive an exceptional prudential review because their continuing categorical restraint, direct connection to the clinic remedy, existing medical record, adversarial presentation and severable legal correction together justify that limited step; briefing alone does not enlarge the petition.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Part I, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg: eight. Stevens declines these distinct merits questions.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the scope inquiry must distinguish a subsidiary argument from a separate question. Izumi preserves a strong prudential presumption against reaching questions neither stated nor fairly included, while retaining exceptional consideration. The third petition question challenges exclusions from peaceful expression within the designated zones; deciding whether the clinic zone's geography fits its stated justification is part of that challenge. Paragraph (4) is different: the questions do not expressly identify its separate audible and visible expression restrictions. The Court nevertheless addresses those continuing restraints at the clinic whose access remedy is before it. The medical findings already supply the asserted justification, and the written clause permits severable legal correction without new findings or reversal of a separate fact-dependent antecedent order. The parties' identified arguments provide notice, but are not sufficient alone. This combination warrants a confined exception, not a general exception for speech litigation or an enlargement to every provision of the decree.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Izumi Seimitsu Kogyo Kabushiki Kaisha v. U.S. Philips Corp.*, November 30, 1993: applies its strong prudential presumption and exercises its retained exceptional route on the stated combination of circumstances.
- *Yee v. City of Escondido*, question-presented portion: preserves the distinction between a subsidiary argument and a related independent question; usefulness alone does not establish fair inclusion.

##### An injunction binds responsible parties and actual concert, not a viewpoint

**Controlling proposition:** An injunction may bind named responsible defendants and persons legally shown to act in concert or participation with them who have notice; notice, presence, sympathy, organizational affinity or a shared viewpoint alone does not establish that relation. This principle does not determine any absent person's concert, contempt or individual defenses, or itself establish the neutral operation of this decree.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Part II-A(1), joined by all eight other Justices: nine. The three dissenters on operational neutrality join only this distinct coverage principle.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an injunction's identification of parties does not dispense with individual responsibility. Regal Knitwear permits enforcement against persons who actually aid bound parties, while preventing an order from reaching independent strangers merely through expansive wording. Claiborne Hardware likewise protects lawful advocacy and association from liability inferred from others' misconduct. A person cannot become an actor in concert merely by receiving a copy of this injunction or opposing the clinic. Conversely, actual participation with a bound defendant does not gain immunity because the participants also share political beliefs. The named petitioners' categorical association objection therefore cannot invalidate the legal category of actual concert with notice. Whether a particular nonparty falls within that category requires its own lawful determination and defenses. The preliminary appearance exchanges did not adjudicate every individual's responsibility. This limited common principle neither resolves the disputed operational-neutrality inference nor grants relief in the separate Cheffer litigation.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Regal Knitwear Co. v. NLRB*, 324 U.S. 9: applies actual-concert coverage and the protection of independent strangers; injunction wording does not prejudge individual status.
- *NAACP v. Claiborne Hardware Co.*, 458 U.S. 886: applies protection against responsibility based merely on advocacy, association or another person's unlawful acts.

##### The decree as confined is not categorically content based

**Controlling proposition:** As authoritatively confined to named responsible parties and actual concert with notice, this injunction addresses established interference with access, traffic, medical care and residential privacy rather than abortion advocacy as such; defendant-specific selection does not make it categorically content or viewpoint based. Each restraint still requires independent constitutional scrutiny, and a materially different viewpoint-based application receives no approval.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Part II-A(2), joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg: six. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas reject this application while accepting the separate actual-concert principle.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that identifying defendants because of the conduct being remedied differs from selecting expression because of its message. Ward directs attention to justification without reference to content. Turner requires examination of operation as well as justification: benign purpose is not conclusive, and different treatment of speakers is not conclusive in the other direction. The Florida court's limiting construction confines this order to parties and actual participants with notice, not everyone who opposes abortion. The troubling preliminary statements about opposition must be considered alongside the written order, the authoritative construction and the reservation of individual defenses. They do not establish a general lawful power to enjoin a viewpoint. The remedial interests are independently legitimate, but that conclusion cannot sustain a provision broader than its harm requires. The Court therefore rejects categorical invalidity and examines each reached restriction. It leaves any concrete contrary application to proper proceedings and grants no blanket approval to all remaining language.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781: applies justification without reference to content; defendant-specific relief still requires scrutiny.
- *Turner Broadcasting System, Inc. v. FCC*, June 27, 1994: applies its operation-and-justification distinction without importing its cable-specific result or plurality evidentiary application.
- *Cincinnati v. Discovery Network, Inc.*, March 24, 1993: preserves the rule that a benign objective alone does not cure a speech-selective burden.

##### A neutral speech injunction must burden no more speech than necessary

**Controlling proposition:** A content-neutral injunction burdening protected expression must burden no more speech than necessary to remedy the established conduct and serve the corresponding significant governmental interest. Its precise terms require a closer fit than ordinary review of generally applicable neutral time, place and manner rules; neither an injunction label nor past misconduct permits suppression of unrelated lawful advocacy.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Part II-B, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Souter and Ginsburg: five. Stevens's individualized equitable account and the strict-restraint dissent are not joins in this standard.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an order directed at identified speakers demands particular care about the expression it forbids. Ward allows neutral general regulation without requiring the least restrictive imaginable means and forbids substantially excessive burdens. That formulation does not fully answer an injunction's special risk: selected persons face a continuing judicial command backed by contempt. Carroll requires precision in orders burdening speech, while Claiborne Hardware connects permissible relief to established unlawful conduct and individual responsibility. Together those protections require each challenged term to burden no more protected speech than the remedial interest needs. Yamasaki's limitation of equitable relief to what is necessary reinforces the wrong-and-remedy relationship. This closer inquiry does not make every injunction content based or impose strict scrutiny on every conduct order. Nor does it excuse obstruction because advocacy accompanies it. The Court applies the standard separately to access, medical noise, images, personal approach and residential territory, without converting any distance or medical setting into a universal rule.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*: distinguishes generally applicable neutral regulation from this direct injunction; its ordinary rule remains intact.
- *Carroll v. President and Commissioners of Princess Anne*, 393 U.S. 175: applies its demand for precision in speech-burdening injunctive relief.
- *NAACP v. Claiborne Hardware Co.*: applies the relation between unlawful conduct, responsible persons and permissible relief.
- *Califano v. Yamasaki*, 442 U.S. 682: applies the limitation of equitable relief to what the established violation requires.

##### The qualified public access arrangement survives

**Controlling proposition:** The public portion of the 36-foot clinic clearance, with its retained south shoulder, east shoulder subject to the five-foot setback and no-embankment rule, and prompt-crossing exception, burdens no more speech than necessary on this record to secure driveway access and traffic after narrower obstruction commands failed. It remains enforceable at that qualified scope, without creating a generally permissible clinic-buffer distance.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Part III-A, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Souter and Ginsburg: five on geographic fit. Stevens adds a vote to leave the arrangement intact only against the categorical challenge; Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would reverse the expressive exclusion.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that protecting actual passage sometimes requires a defined clear area when a command simply forbidding obstruction has failed. The accepted findings concern the two driveway approaches, traffic movements and recurring interference under the predecessor. The question is not whether all advocacy obstructs, but whether this qualified access arrangement is necessary to secure passage in that setting. Carroll's precision requirement directs attention to the boundaries and exceptions, and Claiborne Hardware prevents using individual wrongdoing to suppress expression unconnected with the remedy. Speakers retain the unpaved south shoulder, the restricted east shoulder and prompt crossing, as well as lawful expression beyond the cleared area. Ladue requires practical attention to those channels, not their mere nominal existence. Repeating the earlier unsuccessful prohibition need not be the only remedy. The Court sustains the public arrangement at its actual scope, including the crossing limitations, without finding an ideal distance for other sites or approving the unsupported private-side extension.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Carroll*: applies precision to the access boundary and actual crossing and shoulder permissions.
- *Claiborne Hardware*: confines the expressive burden to supported access relief.
- *City of Ladue v. Gilleo*, June 13, 1994: applies attention to usable alternatives without creating an entitlement to every preferred location.

##### The private-property extension lacks the necessary remedial connection

**Controlling proposition:** The north/west extension of the 36-foot exclusion cannot suppress otherwise lawful expression on adjoining private land without a demonstrated connection between that full exclusion and the access or operational harm to be remedied. Florida's approval of that extension is reversed; removing the government's expressive restriction neither authorizes trespass nor adjudicates owners' property rights.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Part III-B, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Souter and Ginsburg: five. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas concur in reversal on their stricter grounds; Stevens declines distinct geographic review.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a justified public-access clearance does not automatically justify extending an expressive exclusion across neighboring private property. The findings concerning persons using ladders near the north and west fences identify intrusive conduct and noise, but do not establish why every otherwise lawful expressive presence throughout the private-side area must be prohibited. Claiborne Hardware requires the remedy to answer the supported wrong; Carroll requires precision in the restriction itself. Noise and particular intrusion can be addressed on their own lawful grounds without treating all speech in that area as obstruction of the driveways. The owners and invitees' exception from entry prohibition does not cure the remaining expressive restrictions when they act in concert. Ladue's attention to practical channels also prevents assuming that another place always replaces a lost lawful location. The Court removes this governmental exclusion while leaving trespass and other property protections intact. It neither finds permission to enter nor resolves any private boundary dispute.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Claiborne Hardware*: applies the requirement that relief correspond to the established wrong.
- *Carroll*: applies precision to the unsupported geographic extension.
- *Ladue*: preserves practical channel analysis; it supplies no right to use another person's property.

##### The qualified medical-noise restriction survives

**Controlling proposition:** Paragraph (4)'s noise restriction is valid in its medical-operation context from 7:30 a.m. through noon, Monday–Saturday, during surgical procedures and recovery periods, as to the listed and other sounds within earshot of patients inside the clinic. Its affirmance rests on the treatment interference established here, not disagreement with a message, and adds no decibel, intent or newly drafted volume threshold.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Part III-C, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Souter and Ginsburg: five. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would reverse on strict direct-restraint and adequate-unlawful-predicate grounds, despite accepting fit under the Court's assumed standard; Stevens declines merits.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the established medical interference supports this treatment-specific restriction on sound. Ward recognizes that controlling sound can serve a physical interest independent of what the speaker says. The accepted medical findings concern noise reaching patients during surgery and recovery, with consequences for anxiety, sedation and treatment. Both the stated morning window and the surgery-or-recovery condition limit the command; neither may be dropped. It protects patients inside, not everyone who encounters protest in the neighborhood. The lower court's medical-operation context supplies the basis for reading the listed singing, chanting, whistles, shouting, horns and amplification as the sound intrusion addressed by the order. The Court does not replace the text with an invented decibel line or make offensive ideas an injury sufficient for suppression. The contrary breadth argument receives weight, but does not overcome the relation between this qualified command and the found treatment harm. The distinct visual prohibition lacks the same demonstrated necessity and is separately removed.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*: applies the distinction between controlling sound's physical effects and suppressing its content; the heightened injunction rule governs this remedy.
- *Forsyth County v. Nationalist Movement*, June 19, 1992: preserves the distinction between a neutral harm and burdens resting on hostility to a message.
- *Carroll*: applies precision through the time, treatment and patient-location limits.

##### The prohibition on observable images is too broad

**Controlling proposition:** The prohibition on all images observable to patients inside the clinic, even within the stated morning surgery-and-recovery periods, burdens more speech than necessary on this record and must be removed. Patient vulnerability or distress at a message does not establish a general power to prevent visible advocacy; independently lawful threat or intrusion relief remains available.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Part III-D, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Souter and Ginsburg: five. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas concur in reversal on stricter grounds; Stevens declines merits.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an interest in undisturbed medical care does not justify treating every observable image as a harmful intrusion. The time and inside-patient limits narrow paragraph (4), but still leave a categorical visual prohibition. Erznoznik protects expression against broad suppression based on its visibility to unwilling viewers, and Boos distinguishes emotional reaction to a message from a neutral physical injury. The record does not establish that every visible image disrupts medical care or that shielding and other narrower measures would inadequately protect patients. Respondents' account of particular patient-name signs is an argument, not a new finding that supplies those missing conclusions. Patients undergoing care have substantial interests, but naming those interests cannot prove the necessity of this full restriction. The Court therefore removes the images term while preserving the independently justified noise command. It orders no particular window treatment and does not immunize threats, trespass or other unlawful conduct because an image accompanies it.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Erznoznik v. City of Jacksonville*, 422 U.S. 205: applies protection against broad suppression merely because images are visible to unwilling viewers.
- *Boos v. Barry*, 485 U.S. 312: applies the distinction between communicative emotional effects and neutral physical harms.
- *Carroll*: requires precision beyond a categorical visual prohibition unsupported by the demonstrated need.

##### Peaceful initial approach cannot depend on advance invitation throughout 300 feet

**Controlling proposition:** Paragraph (5)'s requirement of an indication of interest before any physical approach to a person seeking clinic services within 300 feet burdens more speech than necessary and must be removed. This holding protects peaceful initial advocacy, not a right to accompany, encircle, surround, threaten, obstruct or force communication upon a person who declines it, and approves no universal application of disputed harassment or abuse terms.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Part III-E, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Souter and Ginsburg: five. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas concur in reversal on stricter grounds; Stevens dissents from reversal of this physical-approach condition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that requiring advance invitation throughout this area forecloses peaceful initiation before the threatened conduct has occurred. The provision regulates physical approach, rather than every word or displayed sign; that distinction makes its burden more precise, but does not make the burden immaterial. Claiborne Hardware protects persuasion as well as public declarations and requires a connection between unlawful conduct and relief. Boos likewise separates protected political communication from genuine coercion or threats. The found unwanted approaches and pursuit justify attention to that conduct, but do not show that every peaceful initial approach throughout 300 feet requires permission. The injunction already distinguishes invited conversation, withdrawal and accompanying or surrounding a declining person. Those distinctions show why refusal and actual interference matter. Removing the initial condition therefore does not abolish lawful protection against pursuit or obstruction. The Court leaves any new conduct-specific remedy to lawful consideration on remand; it adopts no personal separation distance or mandatory replacement order.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Claiborne Hardware*: applies protection of persuasion and the connection between misconduct and relief.
- *Boos*: preserves political approach while allowing properly grounded protection against threats and coercion.
- *Carroll*: requires the restriction to distinguish protected initiation from the conduct justifying restraint.

##### Residential privacy does not justify this territorial exclusion

**Controlling proposition:** The 300-foot residential prohibition on approaching, congregating, picketing, patrolling, demonstrating and amplification sweeps beyond the supported protection of a targeted home and must be removed. Its advance-invitation qualification does not restore general neighborhood expression; distinct lawful obstruction, trespass, threat and properly focused residential protections are preserved without selecting a replacement distance.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Part III-F, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg: six on this bounded ground. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas concur in reversal under their stricter direct-restraint account; the judgment is unanimous.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that protecting a resident against focused intrusion differs from excluding expressive activity throughout a surrounding territory. Frisby sustained a prohibition confined to picketing directed at a particular home, with general neighborhood communication retained. The present radius also captures approaching, congregation, marching and other activity not sufficiently connected to that focused harm. The reported residential misconduct supports real protective interests but does not establish the necessity of the whole territory. The separate invitation clause permits willing contact, yet requires an invitation communicated from a distance beforehand and excludes an uninvited knock or doorbell as the means of obtaining it. That exception does not restore general neighborhood activity or cure the geographical breadth. Ladue reinforces attention to usable expression without creating a right to enter someone else's home. The Court reverses the territorial ban and preserves separate lawful commands against blocked driveways and sole-access streets. Any renewed focused restriction must receive its own lawful justification; no substitute radius is prescribed.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Frisby v. Schultz*, 487 U.S. 474: applies its distinction between focused home picketing and general neighborhood expression; its narrow residential protection remains.
- *City of Ladue v. Gilleo*: applies practical attention to lost channels while preserving the distinction between one's own home and intrusion on another's.

##### The independent conduct-language claims remain undecided

**Controlling proposition:** The Court declines the independent facial and separately framed as-applied challenges to the disputed substantive terms in paragraphs (7), (8) and (9), despite actual adversarial merits briefing, because those additional contextual constructions are neither fairly included in the designated-zone and party-coverage questions nor necessary to their resolution, and no further prudential exception is warranted. This nonreach neither denies the named parties' standing nor approves the provisions' disputed outer applications.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Part IV-A, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg: eight on the Rule 14 ground. Stone-Zsela agrees only in the nonreach disposition for these independently disputed terms and applications and adds no ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that deciding the designated zones and lawful party coverage does not require construing every additional conduct term. Izumi distinguishes an independent question from an argument fairly included in the petition and preserves a strong prudential presumption against expansion. The parties did argue harassment, intimidation, crowding and encouragement; absence of argument is no basis for the Court's decision. The strongest reason to proceed is that broad conduct labels may chill protected persuasion just as paragraph (4) does. But that paragraph imposes a categorical audible-and-visible-expression rule whose medical justification and severable legal correction are already before the Court. The further clauses require distinct contextual constructions unnecessary to resolving the selected questions. The Court declines that additional exceptional step. It does not require a contempt conviction before any facial claim can be heard, find universal waiver, or deny the named parties' own interest. Paragraph (7)'s invited-contact qualification remains distinct from paragraph (8), which lacks it, and no general incitement rule is announced.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Izumi*: applies the strong prudential presumption and declines a further exceptional extension; briefing is not petition presentation.
- *R. A. V. v. City of St. Paul*, June 22, 1992, and *Claiborne Hardware*: their protections for expression and facial challenge remain available, without deciding these omitted independent claims.

##### Relief removes invalidated predicates

**Controlling proposition:** Paragraph (9) cannot support enforcement for encouraging, inciting or securing conduct prohibited only by a restriction set aside here, and the invalidated exclusion cannot be reinstated simply by relabeling that protected activity under another clause. The private-extension, images, initial-consent and residential-territory predicates must be removed without deciding paragraph (9)'s independent substantive validity.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Part IV-B, joined by all eight other Justices: nine at the invalidated-predicate and no-evasion level. This agreement does not settle the permissible content of a new decree.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that effective reversal must remove a prohibited act's dependent use as well as its direct enforcement. An order cannot retain the invalid private extension, images rule, initial-consent condition or residential territory by punishing assistance in violating those same restrictions. Paragraph (9) itself depends on an act prohibited elsewhere in the decree. Once this judgment removes that prohibition, its dependent use cannot survive as though the judgment had never issued. Claiborne Hardware reinforces the distinction between lawful advocacy and responsibility for an established unlawful act. The same protection prevents evading the judgment merely by applying another label to the expressive activity the invalid provision excluded. This is relief on the reached components, not adjudication of every possible encouragement, intimidation or harassment claim. Independently unlawful threats, assault and obstruction remain distinct. No contempt conviction is before the Court, and the remand supplies neither a general incitement test nor immunity from independently valid conduct law.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Claiborne Hardware*: applies the connection among unlawful conduct, responsibility and effective relief; no general incitement holding follows.

##### Remand permits lawful relief without requiring a personal zone

**Controlling proposition:** The lower court must conform the decree to this judgment and may consider independently lawful, precisely limited conduct relief supported by the record and the governing constitutional rules. This remand commands neither a replacement post-refusal injunction nor any personal-separation distance; the existing independently lawful restrictions are not automatically dissolved.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Part IV-C, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg: eight. Stone-Zsela dissents from the refusal to require his bounded post-refusal replacement.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that correcting the invalid provisions does not require selecting a new personal zone. Yamasaki limits relief to the established violation, and Claiborne Hardware requires attention to individual responsibility and lawful advocacy when conduct relief affects expression. The state court may consider proved obstruction, threats, disruptive sound, focused residential intrusion or actual unlawful assistance under the governing constitutional standards. But the record does not establish the necessity or workable geography of a particular moving separation measure, and this Court does not prescribe one. Safeguards protecting stationary speakers, willing listeners and unavoidable proximity would be relevant to a proposal's lawfulness, but their desirability does not itself make that proposal compulsory. The lower court must remove the invalid restrictions and their dependent operation. Permission to consider further precise relief remains permission, not an obligation to enter another injunction. The Court neither fixes a distance nor determines an unproved alternative's sufficiency on this site's geometry.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Califano v. Yamasaki*: applies remedial limits; it does not supply missing findings or compel a particular replacement injunction.
- *Claiborne Hardware*: preserves the relation between conduct relief, responsibility and protected advocacy.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

The provision-specific precedent treatments above govern. No precedent is overruled. Ward's generally applicable neutral-regulation test remains; the Court develops closer tailoring for a direct neutral speech injunction. Carroll, Claiborne Hardware, Yamasaki and Regal Knitwear supply precision, remedial connection and individual responsibility. Frisby's focused-home protection remains distinct from territorial exclusion. Izumi retains its strong prudential presumption and confined exceptional route. Turner supplies classification principles, not a majority holding of evidentiary insufficiency applicable here. Ladue requires usable alternative channels. Casey and Bray remain within their state-action and independently grounded-remedy limits; neither authorizes suppression of otherwise protected private advocacy.

Effective June 30, 1994, the no-more-speech-than-necessary rule governs this content-neutral speech injunction, with five direct supporters. The public clearance and exact medical-noise application each have five merits supporters. Private extension, images and initial consent are invalid under five-Justice controlling applications despite broader judgment coalitions. The residential fit holding has six supporters and a unanimous reversal. No Marks aggregation is needed or made: Stevens's equitable theory and the strict-restraint dissent are distinct, and a nonreach or assumed-standard concession cannot become a merits join. The nine-Justice actual-concert principle does not become nine votes for operational neutrality, which has six.

Stone-Zsela's post-refusal replacement, including the proposed eight feet and its conditions, is noncontrolling. The Court neither mandates that relief nor establishes a universal fixed or moving clinic zone. The independently disputed conduct-language merits remain open. The Court's derivative remedy prevents use of an invalidated restriction as a predicate and supplies no general incitement rule. Private property, valid traffic and access law, and independently lawful conduct remedies retain their force.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela concurs in part and dissents in part. He joins the closer injunction-specific standard and the Court's component merits dispositions. He would require a replacement conduct-based post-refusal protection on remand, proposing eight feet subject to site-specific proof and a narrower supported implementation if that measure cannot be justified. This preferred mandatory remedy differs from the Court's permission to consider lawful relief. He agrees only in the disposition leaving the independent validity of the particular disputed terms and applications of paragraphs (7)–(9) undecided; that disposition-only agreement supplies no additional rationale.

Outside the fixed access zone, an enjoined person must remain free to make a peaceful initial approach, offer literature or request conversation. Protection begins only after words or clear conduct convey that further individualized contact is unwanted, a reasonable person would understand that refusal, and the enjoined speaker has notice. Silence, disagreement with the viewpoint and entry into the vicinity do not automatically trigger it. Once triggered, that speaker must stop following, shadowing, accompanying, repeated reapproaches, surrounding and close-range individualized speech, including coordinated substitution of another participant to continue the same pursuit. His proposed order would require at least eight feet of separation while the person proceeds toward or away from the facility within a finite, ascertainable, route-related clinic vicinity.

The refusal ends a particular unwanted encounter, not all exposure to a viewpoint. Lawful stationary signs, speech to the public, offers to willing listeners and communication from outside the separation remain protected. An unavoidable passing encounter, a patient voluntarily approaching a stationary protester and momentary inability to separate safely must not be punished. The speaker must stop targeted pursuit and take reasonable safe steps once able to comply; a listener cannot force a speaker from an otherwise lawful stationary location. Renewed voluntary engagement can reopen communication, but repeated unwanted approaches cannot be used to solicit that permission.

Carroll's precision requirement, Claiborne Hardware's protection of advocacy and association, Yamasaki's remedial limitation, Ward's content distinction and Frisby's focused protection support his proposed conduct-based response. The practical aim is to make the termination of a close encounter observable after conduct that defeated earlier protection. Yet a moving personal zone may burden leafleting, be difficult to measure on a narrow sidewalk, or turn aversion to speech into suppression. The stated safeguards answer those dangers without proving the number necessary. The state court would have to define the vicinity, support space and necessity with record-specific findings, and consider concrete less burdensome means. If eight feet cannot lawfully be supported, it must implement a supported narrower protection, rather than an unsupported numerical floor. Eight feet is a proposed operational measure, not a constitutional number or an already established rule.

Legally bound defendants and persons shown to act in concert with notice are the only subjects of that order. Touching, grabbing, intentional obstruction, threats, vehicle interference, surrounding and coordinated physical intimidation may independently be prohibited when the record and governing law support them, without waiting for refusal. These express conduct positions are not blanket approval of every disputed term in paragraphs (7)–(9). Casey protects choice against state interference and creates no direct private-liability right here; Bray preserves independently grounded state relief without weakening speech protection. Contempt requires notice and the process appropriate to the actual sanction. The separate writing decides no contempt-classification question and rests on no later personal-zone authority.

Justice Stevens concurs in part and dissents in part. He rejects categorical content discrimination and the association challenge, joins the residential reversal, and agrees with the Court's nonreach and limited remand. He declines separate geographic tailoring of the 36-foot zone and the noise and images merits. Under Izumi, later argument does not alone enlarge the petition. His earlier willingness to reach an indispensable intervention issue does not require resolving additional provisions unnecessary to these selected questions. Leaving those provisions intact on that limited review is not a merits endorsement.

His injunction standard is individualized equitable relief, not the Court's heightened speech-injunction formula or a universal adoption of ordinary Ward review. Yamasaki requires no more burden than necessary to give complete relief; Professional Engineers, Hudson and Paradise explain why a remedy for identified wrongdoers can reach beyond the precise past act when needed to prevent recurrence or eliminate its effects. The trial court's familiarity matters. Those principles do not insulate every remedy or presume that the entire decree is necessary.

He would sustain paragraph (5)'s physical-approach restraint for the bound actors in this established setting. It is not a prohibition on every unsolicited word or all advocacy within 300 feet. Distant expression, offered verbal or written counsel that does not become prohibited approach, and invited nonthreatening conversation remain distinct. The documented approaches and interference support this individualized constraint even though a similar general rule for all speakers would pose a different issue. He therefore disagrees with the reversal of the initial-invitation condition, but does not endorse a general right to suppress unwelcome ideas.

He joins the narrower Frisby-based conclusion that the residential radius exceeds supported protection. Advance invitation does not cure suppression of general neighborhood activity. His agreement there does not join the Court's distinct standard or the private-geography, noise and images merits. He would permit lawful consideration of further precise conduct relief on remand; he does not require a new post-refusal injunction or endorse a numerical substitute. His equitable theory and clinic-approach position remain noncontrolling.

Justice Souter concurs. He emphasizes that actual concert is a matter for individual determination, not a synonym for opposition to the clinic. Regal Knitwear allows an injunction to reach actual assistance to a bound party while preserving a stranger's right to deny that relation. The preliminary appearance exchanges included troubling references to abortion opposition, but also reserved nonassociation and concert defenses for later proceedings and distinguished arrest reports from formal charges. They cannot be treated as a final finding against every absent person.

His concurrence accepts the written order and Florida's authoritative limiting construction without declaring every later enforcement neutral. A person must have notice and must actually fall within the lawful bound category; receiving the order alone supplies neither concert nor guilt. His position differs from the dissent's operational inference, yet grants no immunity to an unconstitutional application. The named petitioners' categorical challenge and Cheffer's independent federal preliminary-relief litigation remain separate. He joins the provision-specific speech review and derivative relief; his additional explanation neither approves a new distance nor imposes a mandatory replacement decree.

Justice Scalia concurs in part and dissents in part, joined throughout by Justices Kennedy and Thomas. They would also reverse the public expressive exclusion and the noise restriction. Their first sufficient objection is that a judicial command directly forbidding selected persons' future protected advocacy demands the rigorous protection for prior restraints even if nominally content neutral. Carroll, Keefe, Vance and Claiborne Hardware support that distinction. Ordinary conduct orders and incidental consequences of neutral sanctions are different. Alexander's distinction between postconviction effects and a direct future-speech prohibition does not shelter this order.

They separately reject the decree's asserted neutral operation. Turner requires attention to what a rule does, not merely a benign description of its aims, while preserving the proposition that speaker identity alone is not content discrimination. Here the already reported preliminary exchanges connecting coverage to opposition to the clinic supply the additional operational concern. The three accept the abstract legality of actual concert with notice and the bar on ideological guilt; they do not treat that legal category as proof of neutral implementation. They determine no absent person's concert or contempt.

The public-zone objection also concerns the legal predicate for this expansion. They accept the facts found but dispute whether incidental slowing and momentary hesitation, without the necessary relation to obstruction forbidden by the predecessor, legally justify the whole exclusion of peaceful expression. This is a legal sufficiency objection, not a retrial through selected video. Precisely directed road, driveway or conduct controls may address genuine interference; none is found sufficient or ordered as a compulsory substitute. The crossing and shoulder permissions do not themselves establish the necessity of the full exclusion.

For noise, they accept that the qualified clause fits the Court's test if that test is assumed. That concession does not affirm it or join the governing standard. The separate objection is that medically desirable restraint is not an identified adequate prior or threatened violation of statute, common law or the predecessor justifying this personalized speech order. They do not find that Florida has no applicable noise law, deny medical harm or invent a decibel threshold. Strict direct-restraint review independently remains applicable. They concur in reversing private-side exclusion, images, initial-consent approach and residential territory under their stricter grounds, without a right to trespass, threaten, pursue a declining person or invade a home.

They join the confined review-scope ruling and decline independent paragraphs (7)–(9) merits despite actual briefing. Every predicate they would reverse must fall from dependent enforcement; at minimum they join removal of the predicates this judgment actually sets aside. Independently lawful obstruction, assault, threats and conduct remedies survive. Their position does not command a replacement personal zone, approve Stone-Zsela's proposed distance, decide an unreported contempt or dissolve the entire predecessor injunction.

### McFarland v. Scott, No. 93-6497

Merits decision, June 30, 1994

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 29, 1994; decided June 30, 1994. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit, 7 F.3d 47. The questions are whether an indigent capital prisoner may obtain §848(q)(4)(B) counsel before a formal federal habeas application and whether the qualifying request permits consideration of a §2251 stay.

The reviewed proceeding began with McFarland's October 22, 1993 request expressly identifying a contemplated §2254 challenge and seeking statutory counsel and a stay. The resource center had been unable to obtain volunteer counsel. The district court denied relief October 25; on October 26 the Fifth Circuit granted leave to proceed without prepayment of fees but denied a certificate of probable cause, counsel and a stay. It also relied on the absence of a general constitutional state-collateral-counsel right, the absence of a shown substantial constitutional claim and hypothetical dismissal for nonexhaustion if the motion were treated as a petition. A skeletal petition filed by a located attorney on October 26 began a separate proceeding; it is not this motion. The earlier October 27 execution setting supplies history, not a present execution date.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg participated at argument and decision. No case-specific nonparticipation is established.

#### Judgment

The Fifth Circuit's judgment is reversed to permit qualified appointment and lawful consideration of a stay, and the case is remanded. Counsel is sustained by seven Justices; stay authority and its discretionary exercise by six.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Statutory appointment before the formal merits application | Reverse, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg | Scalia, Thomas | Appoint qualified counsel under the statutory conditions. |
| Categorical denial of preapplication §2251 stay authority | Reverse, 6–3 | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg | O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas | Consider a necessary stay through individualized discretion; no present automatic or indefinite stay. |

The certificate denial is removed only insofar as it prevents the statutory consideration supported by each component coalition. It is not a grant for an unidentified constitutional claim or a general holding about §2253. O'Connor would reverse appointment denial but affirm stay denial. Scalia and Thomas would affirm both. Leave to proceed without prepayment remains granted; the conviction, sentence and distinct October 26 proceeding are not adjudicated.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Qualified capital counsel may be appointed before the merits application

**Controlling proposition:** A defendant who is or becomes financially unable to obtain adequate representation or reasonably necessary investigative, expert or other services may initiate the postconviction proceeding contemplated by 21 U.S.C. §848(q)(4)(B) through a request for counsel to prepare an identified federal §2254 or §2255 challenge seeking to vacate or set aside a death sentence. The statute entitles the qualifying defendant to counsel before a formal merits application, subject to the qualifications and service conditions stated below; a request unrelated to that federal capital challenge does not qualify.

**Authority:** Part I of Justice Stevens's opinion, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg. Seven Justices directly adopt the statutory rule and its application to the October 22 motion.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress provided assistance in preparing the pleading, not merely assistance after the prisoner has prepared it alone. Section 848(q) supplies qualified representation and reasonably necessary services in the capital postconviction proceeding. Habeas Rules 2(c) and 4 require factual pleading and permit early dismissal; investigation therefore has substantial work before the first adequate application. The statutory word proceeding encompasses the express request that starts this authorized preparatory stage. Texas's contrary reading gives insufficient effect to that sequence, although assistance would also perform work after filing. Murray v. Giarratano concerns a general constitutional entitlement to state collateral counsel and does not defeat this enacted federal entitlement. McFarland's October 22 motion expressly identified the federal challenge and requested the statutory assistance. The later October 26 skeletal petition is a different proceeding and supplies neither the basis nor an obstacle to this ruling. The Court decides no underlying claim, general constitutional counsel right, or complete scope of representation in state exhaustion proceedings.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Murray v. Giarratano, 492 U.S. 1 (1989): distinguished; its constitutional collateral-counsel question does not restrict the express federal statutory appointment mechanism.
- Herrera v. Collins, January 25, 1993: its record-specific rejection of the asserted innocence showing remains intact; no general freestanding-innocence standard is imported into this appointment question.

**Statutory qualifications:** Section 848(q)(6), governing appointment after judgment, requires at least one appointed attorney admitted to the relevant court of appeals for five years with three years of experience handling felony appeals there. Paragraph (5)'s separate pre-judgment rule requires five years' admission in the trial court and three years' actual felony-trial experience there; it does not replace paragraph (6). Under paragraph (7), good cause permits another attorney whose background, knowledge or experience enables proper representation, with due consideration of the possible penalty's seriousness and the litigation's unique and complex nature. Under paragraph (8), representation continues through subsequent available judicial proceedings, all available postconviction process, stay applications and other appropriate motions and procedures, and available competency and clemency proceedings unless similarly qualified counsel replaces the attorney on the attorney's or defendant's motion. The full reach of that provision into later state proceedings is not decided.

Under paragraph (9), an ex parte finding that investigative, expert or other services are reasonably necessary for representation concerning guilt or sentence requires authorization and payment under paragraph (10). If timely procurement could not practicably await prior authorization, the court may authorize the services and payment retroactively; it is not required to do so. Paragraph (10) requires compensation and service fees and expenses at rates or amounts the court determines reasonably necessary, notwithstanding generally applicable criminal-case rates and maxima. These conditions do not authorize unconditional payment for unidentified services.

##### The statutory preparation proceeding satisfies §2251 pendency

**Controlling proposition:** A qualifying request invoking §848(q)(4)(B) counsel to prepare the identified federal capital challenge commences a habeas proceeding for §2251's limited preparatory purpose. The federal justice or judge before whom it is pending therefore has authority to consider a stay of covered state proceedings although the formal merits application has not yet been filed.

**Authority:** Part II of Justice Stevens's opinion, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg. Six Justices adopt this construction; O'Connor does not join it.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the authorized preparation stage is a pending proceeding within the stay statute. Section 2251 is the source of this power; the counsel entitlement does not itself authorize an injunction. Congress's provision for counsel to investigate and prepare a capital application identifies a stage of the federal habeas undertaking to which §2251's existing language can apply. Requiring the completed application before that stage can receive protection would allow execution to defeat the work Congress made available. The contrary view properly insists that funding and jurisdiction are separate questions, but it reads proceeding as though Congress had said merits application. The Court gives each statute independent work while recognizing the same limited proceeding. Barefoot v. Estelle's treatment of capital habeas review does not require an already developed claim as the price of preparing it with the assistance Congress supplied. The Court neither determines the separate October 26 petition's sufficiency nor excuses any requirement governing the eventual merits application.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Barefoot v. Estelle, 463 U.S. 880 (1983): its capital merits and successive-review principles are preserved in their settings; they do not eliminate the statutory preparatory stage.
- Herrera v. Collins, January 25, 1993: distinguished as adjudication of an actual substantive submission, not the initiation of this statutory preparation proceeding.

**Section 2251's remaining terms:** The judge may act before final judgment, after final judgment of discharge, or pending appeal. The covered state proceeding must be against the detained person in a state court or under state authority and concern a matter involved in the habeas proceeding. After a stay is granted, covered subsequent state proceedings are void; without a stay, they remain valid as though no federal habeas proceeding or appeal were pending.

##### Section 2251 supplies the express statutory exception to §2283

**Controlling proposition:** For a covered state-court proceeding within the pending habeas matter described above, §2251 expressly authorizes a stay and thus satisfies §2283's exception for an Act of Congress expressly authorizing an injunction. This ruling does not independently find §2283's distinct exceptions for aid of jurisdiction or protection or effectuation of judgments satisfied.

**Authority:** Part III of Justice Stevens's opinion, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg. Six Justices directly adopt this statutory conclusion; it is distinct from the appointment entitlement and from the exercise of stay discretion.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Anti-Injunction Act does not defeat the authority Congress expressly supplies through §2251. Section 2283 forbids a federal injunction staying state-court proceedings unless one of its stated exceptions applies. Section 2251 authorizes a judge with a pending habeas proceeding to stay the covered state action. Once the qualifying preparation motion satisfies that pendency requirement, the express congressional authorization is present. The lower court's contrary conclusion depended on its rejected view that no habeas proceeding existed. Recognizing the exception therefore requires no general equitable power and no inference that every statutory right to counsel carries an injunction remedy. The competing reading would deny this exception because it denies pendency, not because §2251 lacks express stay language. The Court preserves the other two exceptions as separate provisions and does not decide that either supplies an alternative basis here. Barefoot's distinct review principles continue to govern their proper stages; statutory authority remains separate from entitlement to its discretionary exercise.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Barefoot v. Estelle, 463 U.S. 880 (1983): preserved; this statutory authorization ruling neither displaces its applicable review principles nor creates an automatic capital stay.

**Independent statutory steps:** Financial eligibility and a covered counsel request establish the appointment entitlement. Section 2251 separately supplies stay power after the Court's pendency construction. Section 2283's express-authorization exception answers the prohibition on staying state-court proceedings. None of those conclusions alone requires a stay.

##### Reasonable preparation and individualized stay discretion govern the remand

**Controlling proposition:** The district court must appoint qualified counsel and afford a reasonable opportunity for meaningful investigation and presentation of available habeas claims, considering a stay when necessary to protect that work. A stay is neither automatic nor indefinite: the court must assess the actual circumstances and may deny relief where the prisoner inexcusably ignores an available opportunity and obstructs the process; no such finding is made here.

**Authority:** Part IV of Justice Stevens's opinion, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg. Six adopt the stay-discretion rule and common remand; O'Connor separately joins the appointment direction under Part I only.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that authority must be exercised to make the statutory opportunity meaningful without conferring unlimited delay. Counsel needs a reasonable chance to investigate and prepare the first application. Execution timing, realistic preparation needs, prior opportunities for assistance and demonstrated obstruction bear on that assessment; no rigid schedule or presumption of abuse follows. Barefoot preserves substantive review standards where developed claims or successive applications are before the court, but requiring that finished showing before preparatory counsel can work confuses the stages. The lower court's constitutional-counsel observation does not answer §848, and its hypothetical conversion of this motion into an unexhausted petition does not decide the relief actually requested. Exhaustion and other collateral limitations govern the eventual petition at their proper stage. The certificate denial falls only insofar as it obstructs the statutory consideration ordered here. The Court grants no certificate for an unidentified constitutional claim, issues no present execution stay, and decides no general rule for every ancillary order under §2253.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Barefoot v. Estelle, 463 U.S. 880 (1983): preserved for merits and successive-review questions; distinguished from reasonable preparation under the enacted counsel right.
- Murray v. Giarratano, 492 U.S. 1 (1989): does not sustain denial of the statutory federal assistance recognized here.
- Reed v. Farley, June 20, 1994: collateral-relief and exhaustion rules remain separate; exhaustion retains exceptions where state corrective process is absent or circumstances make it ineffective to protect the prisoner's rights.

**Limits:** No conviction or death sentence is vacated, no release or merits writ is ordered, no new execution date is found, and no decision concerns the separate October 26 action's dismissal or exhaustion. The grant of leave to proceed without prepayment of fees remains undisturbed.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Giarratano's constitutional question, Barefoot's applicable merits and successive-review principles, Herrera's record-specific innocence disposition, Custis's distinct ACCA channel and Reed's collateral and exhaustion limits retain their force. The holdings above identify the precise distinctions. No precedent is overruled; no general constitutional collateral-counsel right is announced.

Effective June 30, 1994, the qualifying federal capital-counsel request begins the statutory preparation proceeding before the formal merits application. Section 2251 separately permits a stay of covered state action and supplies the express congressional exception to §2283. Appointment entitlement and stay discretion remain different decisions. The court must provide a reasonable preparation opportunity while managing proved delay or obstruction; later pleading, exhaustion, default and abuse rules retain their proper operation. The decision establishes no universal §2253 rule or entitlement to merits relief.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice O'Connor joins the counsel holding because Congress may provide assistance before a merits application. She nevertheless regards §2251 as requiring an actual habeas application and would affirm the stay denial in this proceeding. Section 848's reference to stay applications describes counsel's work rather than amending the separate pendency requirement; practical need cannot supply the missing authority on her reading.

Justice Thomas, joined by Justice Scalia, would affirm both denials. They read the appointment statute's reference to a proceeding under §§2254 or 2255 and §2251's pendency requirement to demand an actual application. Investigative services still perform work after filing, and practical usefulness does not remove either textual predicate. They make no finding of deliberate delay, adequate alternative assistance or any defect in the distinct October 26 action.

### Tuilaepa v. California and Proctor v. California, Nos. 93-5131 and 93-5161, respectively

Merits decision, June 30, 1994

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 22, 1994; decided June 30, 1994. On writs of certiorari to the Supreme Court of California, 4 Cal.4th 569, 842 P.2d 1142, and 4 Cal.4th 499, 842 P.2d 1100, respectively, affirming the convictions and death sentences. Both petitioners challenge §190.3(a); only Tuilaepa challenges (b) and (i), on vagueness and inadequate-guidance theories at penalty selection. The §190.2 eligibility findings are unchallenged.

Tuilaepa was convicted of the 1986 bar-robbery murder, with the robbery special circumstance found true. His relevant-factor charge prohibited mechanical counting and arbitrary weights, allowed moral and sympathetic valuation and required aggravation sufficiently substantial compared with mitigation to warrant death. His other-violent-act instruction required proof beyond a reasonable doubt before aggravating use; the conduct included custody and trial misconduct. Proctor was convicted of the 1982 murder with rape, burglary and torture special circumstances. After a penalty mistrial and his requested venue change, a new jury received the full factor list and older mandatory-language instruction, with nonmechanical-weighing and mitigation directions permitting pity, sympathy and mercy and excluding conjecture, prejudice and public opinion. The two charges and questions remain distinct.

Section 190.3 requires consideration of listed factors if relevant: (a) the circumstances of the present crime and the existence of special circumstances found true under §190.1; (b) the presence or absence of criminal activity involving use or attempted use of force or violence, or an express or implied threat to use force or violence; and (i) the defendant's age at the time of the crime. The statute's weighing directive, the state courts' constructions and the actual instructions are separate matters.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg participated at argument and decision. No case-specific nonparticipation is established.

Both California judgments are affirmed against the presented selection-factor challenges, 8–1 in each docket. No new penalty hearing is ordered on those grounds.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Relevant individualized selection does not require fixed labels or numerical weights

**Controlling proposition:** On the unchallenged eligibility premise presented here, the Eighth and Fourteenth Amendments permit consideration of understandable, relevant subjects for individualized penalty selection without requiring every consideration to be a yes-or-no factual element, exclusively aggravating or mitigating, or assigned a fixed numerical weight. This rule does not authorize forbidden or wholly irrelevant adverse considerations, exclusion of effective mitigation, or disregard of a separately established selection error.

**Authority:** Part II of Justice O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg. Eight directly adopt this bounded rule; Stevens and Ginsburg do not join Part I's additional framework discussion.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that individual sentencing judgment cannot be reduced to a required arithmetic formula. Zant permits consideration of relevant characteristics of the crime and offender once eligibility has been lawfully narrowed, while Lockett and Eddings protect the defendant's opportunity to give mitigation meaningful effect. The present eligibility findings are unchallenged; the Court assumes their sufficiency for these questions. Petitioners argue that flexible subjects allow almost any fact to be urged toward death. That possibility does not make every relevant subject vague or require the State to assign it an exclusive direction. It also does not license an unconstitutional adverse inference. Graham and Johnson v. Texas require an effective route for independent culpability mitigation; these California mechanisms permit that assessment without forcing a false answer to another factual question. Boyde requires attention to the instructions in context, not favorable weight for every item. The Court sustains the presented factors within those limits and decides no actual forbidden use unsupported by these records.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Zant v. Stephens, 462 U.S. 862 (1983): applied to relevant individualized selection after unchallenged narrowing; it supplies no exemption for forbidden adverse considerations.
- Lockett v. Ohio, 438 U.S. 586 (1978), and Eddings v. Oklahoma, 455 U.S. 104 (1982): preserved; relevant mitigation must have effective consideration, without guaranteed favorable weight.
- Boyde v. California, 494 U.S. 370 (1990): applied to contextual review and individualized moral judgment, not mechanical factor counting.
- Graham v. Collins, January 25, 1993, and Johnson v. Texas, June 24, 1993: distinguished in application; their effective independent-mitigation requirement remains fully operative.

**Framework and continuing safeguards:** Lawful eligibility narrowing and individualized selection perform distinct functions under Gregg, Woodson and Lowenfield. Arave's authoritative limitation on an eligibility aggravator remains necessary in its setting. Eligibility does not cure a later unconstitutional weighing factor under Stringer and Espinosa. Romano's separate-death-decision responsibility rule and Simmons's future-danger/parole-ineligibility rebuttal rule retain their distinct operation. This decision neither extends nor retreats from those rules and does not approve the entire California capital scheme.

##### The circumstances-of-the-crime factor survives both presented challenges

**Controlling proposition:** Section 190.3(a), directing consideration when relevant of the circumstances of the crime of conviction and the existence of special circumstances found true under §190.1, identifies an understandable and relevant selection subject and is not unconstitutional on the facial and instructional theories presented by either petitioner. The Court affirms each judgment on that ground without adjudicating §190.2 eligibility or any independently preserved claim of actual forbidden use.

**Authority:** Part III of Justice O'Connor's opinion, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg. Eight find no error on each docket's factor-(a) challenge; none of these joins is a harmlessness vote.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the circumstances of this crime identify relevant subject matter rather than an undefined judgment that a murder is especially heinous. Godfrey and Maynard require meaningful limits on the vague aggravating labels at issue there; their cautions do not automatically invalidate this direction to consider the actual offense. Zant's individualized inquiry permits facts that can carry different significance in different cases. Tuilaepa's jury received relevant factors, nonmechanical weighing and a requirement that aggravation be sufficiently substantial to warrant death. Proctor's separate jury received the full list and older mandatory language, together with nonmechanical weighing and directions permitting mitigation, pity, sympathy and mercy while excluding prejudice and conjecture. The Court preserves that difference and evaluates the presented objection in each setting. Proctor's venue-requested new jury establishes neither bias nor a surrender of responsibility. Petitioners' concern about possible impermissible uses remains reviewable on a proper record, but broad relevance alone establishes neither those uses nor constitutional error here.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Godfrey v. Georgia, 446 U.S. 420 (1980), and Maynard v. Cartwright, 486 U.S. 356 (1988): distinguished; their vague pejorative aggravators differ from identification of the actual crime's circumstances.
- Zant v. Stephens, 462 U.S. 862 (1983): applied; relevant individualized consideration does not require an exclusive aggravating or mitigating label for every circumstance.
- Boyde v. California, 494 U.S. 370 (1990): applied to each complete reported charge; no ordinary word or boilerplate direction automatically cures an actual contradiction.

**Application and nonreach:** Both factor-(a) challenges fail without harmless-error review. The judgment does not decide unrelated guilt-phase comments, every other factor, racial discrimination, the lawfulness of treating absence of a minor-accomplice role as aggravation, or the whole California eligibility scheme. No actual juror mental state is found.

##### The violent-criminal-activity factor is sufficiently definite in Tuilaepa

**Controlling proposition:** Section 190.3(b), when relevant, permits consideration of the presence or absence of criminal activity involving use or attempted use of force or violence, or an express or implied threat to use force or violence. As construed to concern punishable offenses involving force or threatened force against a person, this is an understandable selection subject and defeats Tuilaepa's presented vagueness challenge; Proctor presents no factor-(b) question.

**Authority:** Part IV of Justice O'Connor's opinion, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg. Eight directly sustain factor (b) on no-error grounds; Blackmun dissents on this factor as well as the others.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that criminal acts involving violence or threatened violence identify a discernible subject for sentencing judgment. The direction concerns conduct, including the conduct in custody and during trial reported here, rather than only acts preceding the capital offense. Zant permits relevant information about the offender, and Boyde does not require the State to assign a numerical moral value to each proved act. Tuilaepa's jury was instructed to find the acts beyond a reasonable doubt before considering them in aggravation. That reported safeguard addresses whether the conduct occurred; the Court does not convert it into a constitutional burden for every sentencing evaluation. The competing argument correctly distinguishes proof from sentencing weight, but the factor's defined violent subject and the individualized assessment do not become vague merely because weight remains for the jury. The state court's no-request and no-prejudice reasoning about instructions defining particular offenses is a separate matter. The Court resolves no independent elements-instruction claim or categorical objection to all unadjudicated conduct.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Zant v. Stephens, 462 U.S. 862 (1983): applied to relevant offender conduct without requiring a uniform assigned weight.
- Boyde v. California, 494 U.S. 370 (1990): applied; individualized sentencing is not mechanical factual counting.

**Qualification:** The trial instruction's proof burden is a record fact, not a new statutory or universal constitutional rule. This holding supplies no finding about an unreported act, no general permission to use unreliable or forbidden evidence, and no factor-(b) ruling in Proctor.

##### Age is a relevant contextual subject, not an automatic aggravator

**Controlling proposition:** Section 190.3(i)'s direction to consider, when relevant, the defendant's age at the time of the crime is sufficiently understandable on Tuilaepa's presented challenge and requires no universal numerical weight or exclusive aggravating or mitigating label. The absence of youth does not automatically aggravate the crime, and the possibility of improper argument does not establish a facial defect or a proved as-applied violation; Proctor presents no age-factor question.

**Authority:** Part V of Justice O'Connor's opinion, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg. Eight adopt the rule and no-error application; Part VI gives the corresponding affirmances without a new penalty hearing.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that age has intelligible but contextual significance in assessing the person who committed the crime. Zant permits relevant individual consideration without demanding that every ambiguous circumstance favor only one side. Lockett and Eddings, as implemented by Graham and Johnson v. Texas, still require a genuine route for youth's independent mitigating significance. California's selection mechanism permits that moral assessment rather than making a finding of deliberateness or future danger the exclusive expression of culpability. The objection that a prosecutor might characterize any possible age as aggravating raises a real boundary, not a reason to presume that every use of the subject is invalid. Absence of youth is not automatically a reason for death. An argument treating every age as independently sufficient aggravation would need examination on its own preserved record. The facts before the Court establish no such adjudicated misuse here. The Court therefore affirms on no-error grounds and reaches neither the lower separate harmlessness theories nor a general approval of prosecutorial age arguments.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Zant v. Stephens, 462 U.S. 862 (1983): applied; contextual relevance does not require an exclusive label or numerical weight.
- Lockett v. Ohio, 438 U.S. 586 (1978), and Eddings v. Oklahoma, 455 U.S. 104 (1982): preserved as protection for meaningful mitigation.
- Graham v. Collins, January 25, 1993, and Johnson v. Texas, June 24, 1993: their independent-culpability-mitigation rules remain binding; the different California mechanism supports a different no-error application.

**Limits and remedies:** The Court finds no constitutional error on any presented factor challenge and orders no new penalty hearing. It makes no harmlessness finding and adds no alternative cure holding. If an independent properly presented claim establishes a weighing error, Stringer and Espinosa still require genuine state-authorized reweighing or constitutional harmless-error review with the State carrying Chapman's beyond-a-reasonable-doubt burden, and a new sentencing proceeding if no lawful cure exists. Nothing here resolves Romano's distinct further-remedy disagreement or alters Simmons's case-specific mandate. Guilt remains outside these sentencing questions.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

The holding-specific treatments govern. Gregg, Woodson and Lowenfield retain the distinct requirements of lawful narrowing and individualized selection. Zant, Lockett, Eddings and Boyde permit these relevant subjects within the stated constraints. Godfrey and Maynard are distinguished; Arave's limited eligibility holding is preserved. Graham and Johnson's actual independent-mitigation requirements, Stringer and Espinosa's weighing-error correction, Victor/Sandoval's contextual instruction review, Romano's responsibility rule and Simmons's rebuttal rule remain in force. No precedent is overruled.

Effective June 30, 1994, the challenged crime-circumstances, violent-criminal-activity and age subjects survive the presented selection challenges. Relevant individualized assessment need not use fixed numerical weights, universally exclusive labels or only factual yes-or-no elements. The ruling is no-error affirmance, not harmless-error approval. Existing mitigation, responsibility, rebuttal and weighing-error safeguards continue independently; no general exemption from constitutional selection review, automatic absence-of-youth aggravation or comprehensive approval of California's scheme follows.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Stevens, joined by Justice Ginsburg, concurs in part and in the judgments. They join Parts II–VI and rely on the narrower Zant/Lockett ground: with eligibility unchallenged, the State may identify relevant matters that inform an individual sentencing decision without labeling every ambiguous item exclusively aggravating or mitigating. Identifying those subjects may reduce the risk that prejudice substitutes for individual consideration. They reject authority to rely adversely on constitutionally forbidden or wholly irrelevant matters and preserve effective mitigation. Their votes find no error; they do not use Zant as a harmlessness shortcut for an actually invalid California weighing factor. Their limited joins do not adopt Part I's full framework discussion.

Justice Scalia concurs, joining the Court's opinion while reserving his broader objection to distinctive Eighth Amendment regulation of weighing after lawful eligibility narrowing. His present join accepts the narrow sustaining result and leaves existing selection holdings intact; it does not expand them or make his broader view controlling. Neither Stringer and Espinosa nor Graham and Johnson is overruled by implication. No other Justice joins this separate position, including Justice Thomas.

Justice Souter concurs, joining the Court throughout. Factor (b) is essentially factual in form but sufficiently clear, while the broader crime-circumstances and age subjects can guide individual judgment without becoming yes-or-no eligibility elements. The distinction is functional: nonpropositional phrasing is not immunity from review, and factual phrasing is not itself a defect. The present no-error conclusions preserve meaningful mitigation, sentencing responsibility and review of actual impermissible use. No other Justice joins the additional explanation.

Justice Blackmun dissents from both judgments and from rejection of each challenged factor. His concern is the guidance governing aggravating use on a legally consequential weighing scale. Proof that violent conduct occurred does not determine the principles controlling its weight; understandable references to crime circumstances, violent activity and age can still permit arbitrary selection. His Arave dissent demanded a coherent limiting formulation, and Stringer and Espinosa establish that eligibility does not cure a later weighing defect. He regards California's nonmechanical directions and the violence proof instruction as insufficient answers to that different problem. He does not demand numerical weights, find any unreported act or forbidden argument proved, or announce a general ban on unadjudicated conduct. Independently, he retains his published Romano and Simmons position that capital punishment as presently administered fails to reconcile consistent selection, individualized fairness and reliability. His preferred relief is vacatur of the death-sustaining judgments and lawful noncapital disposition through the state courts, without disturbing guilt. As minimum case-specific relief he accepts vacatur and correction of the factor errors through genuine state-authorized reweighing or constitutional harmless-error review with the State bearing Chapman's burden; a new sentencing proceeding follows if no lawful cure exists. He makes no current harmlessness finding and does not accept another capital proceeding as an answer to his independent broader objection. No other Justice joins his dissent.
