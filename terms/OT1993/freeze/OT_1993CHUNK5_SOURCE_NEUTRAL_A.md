## BFP v. Resolution Trust Corp.

Official report: https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep511/usrep511531/usrep511531.pdf ; entire 40-page PDF and text recovered under `tmp/ot1993_chunk5_sources/bfp`. The primary Archive record is https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385013_0383 . Its petitioner merits brief has been recovered as `bfp_pet.txt`; it refers to the amended petition appendix pp. 69–74 for the complete operative statutory text. That appendix is not yet extracted. The report quotes the relevant statutory clauses on pp.535–536. Judgment below independently reproduced at https://openjurist.org/974/f2d/1144 . This reproduction confirms an important alternative ground: the bankruptcy court thought BFP lacked an interest because the antecedent conveyance had been rescinded; the Ninth Circuit rejected that ground, reading the state quiet-title judgment as giving BFP and the Foremans fee co-ownership. It nevertheless affirmed on reasonably equivalent value. Do not carry the rejected no-interest premise as a finding controlling this appeal.

Verified material facts: foreclosure on July 12, 1989; price $433,000; asserted value over $725,000 is an allegation, not an adjudicated appraisal. BFP's Chapter 11 petition was October 25, 1989. Osborne bought in good faith without notice of the title dispute. The operative federal claim is constructive fraudulent transfer; procedural regularity, lack of collusion, and adequate state-law notice were upheld below. The Circuit affirmed two consolidated routes: the district court's affirmance of dismissal of the private buyers, and the Bankruptcy Appellate Panel's affirmance of judgment for Imperial. It expressly held BFP had a property interest.

Section 548(a) permits the trustee to avoid “any transfer of an interest of the debtor in property, or any obligation incurred by the debtor, that was made or incurred on or within one year before the date of the filing of the petition, if the debtor voluntarily or involuntarily” either acts with the intent described in (a)(1), or “(2)(A) received less than a reasonably equivalent value in exchange for such transfer or obligation; and (B)(i) was insolvent on the date that such transfer was made or such obligation was incurred, or became insolvent as a result of such transfer or obligation.” The statutory alternatives in (B)(ii) and (iii) concern unreasonably small capital and incurring debts beyond ability to pay; they are not asserted grounds here and their exact full text is not quoted from a verified edition in this supplement. Section 101(54)'s transfer definition expressly includes foreclosure of the debtor's equity of redemption; the 1984 amendments also added “voluntarily or involuntarily” to §548(a). Section 548(d)(2)(A) defines value as property, or satisfaction or securing of a present or antecedent debt of the debtor, excluding an unperformed promise to furnish support to the debtor or a relative. These amendments settle inclusion of foreclosure transfers, not their valuation.

The parties' competing positions remain distinct: BFP seeks a federal substantive assessment rather than conclusive validation from procedural compliance; respondents invoke foreclosure conditions, title stability, state law, and the absence of a congressional minimum-price formula. BFP need not establish that the full fair-market price is always required. Durrett v. Washington National Insurance Co., 621 F.2d 201 (5th Cir. 1980), invalidated a 57%-of-market transfer and discussed 70%; In re Bundles, 856 F.2d 815 (7th Cir. 1988), uses all circumstances with a rebuttable presumption; In re Madrid, 21 B.R. 424 (BAP9 1982), and Winshall Settler's Trust, 758 F.2d 1136 (6th Cir. 1985), supply the competing conclusive approach. No rule in this supplement automatically extends to tax sales, collusive sales, personal property, or noncompliant foreclosure procedures.

Surviving pre-divergence authorities directly bearing on the fork include Butner v. United States, 440 U.S. 48 (1979), state-defined property interests absent countervailing federal law; United Savings Assn. v. Timbers, 484 U.S. 365 (1988), no adequate protection of a right to immediate foreclosure; Owen v. Owen, 500 U.S. 305 (1991), text governs avoidance despite state exemptions; United States v. Whiting Pools, 462 U.S. 198 (1983), bankruptcy recovery of seized assets subject to adequate protection; United States v. Ron Pair Enterprises, 489 U.S. 235 (1989), coherent plain Code text; Gregory v. Ashcroft, 501 U.S. 452 (1991), clear statement in a different sovereign governmental setting; Gelfert v. National City Bank, 313 U.S. 221 (1941), forced sale price and deficiency protections. Earlier simulated holdings must control their own modified propositions; post-divergence historical Code decisions are not imported here.

## Custis v. United States

Official report: https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep511/usrep511485/usrep511485.pdf ; entire 28-page source read. Record at pp.487–490; actual petitioner merits brief, with Appendix 1a–3a specifying 1993 Supplement text, recovered as `custis_pet.txt` from https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385013_0521 . The lower court is 988 F.2d 1355 (4th Cir. 1993).

Custis's July 1, 1991 arrest led to firearm and drug charges. The jury acquitted of possession with intent to distribute and using a gun during drug trafficking; it convicted of felon possession and simple cocaine possession. The three enhancement predicates were 1985 Pennsylvania robbery, 1985 Maryland burglary, and 1989 Maryland attempted burglary. He attacks only the two Maryland predicates: ineffective advice and unknowing plea in 1985 (failure to advise of voluntary intoxication); failure to advise of rights in a stipulated-facts proceeding tantamount to a plea in 1989; and ineffective assistance because the facts allegedly established attempted breaking and entering, not attempted burglary. These are not allegations of complete absence of counsel.

The district court initially assessed and rejected his substantive claims but later rested on inability to entertain them under ACCA apart from complete absence of counsel. It imposed 235 months, offense level 33/category VI. The Fourth Circuit affirmed on statutory silence, uniqueness of appointment-of-counsel failures, finality, and practical burdens of stale state records. Custis was still in state custody for purposes of challenging those sentences when federally sentenced. Do not treat a possible later reopening application as guaranteed relief.

Exact §924(e)(1), as printed in the predecision brief appendix: “In the case of a person who violates section 922(g) of this title and has three previous convictions by any court referred to in section 922(g)(1) of this title for a violent felony or a serious drug offense, or both, committed on occasions different from one another, such person shall be fined not more than $25,000 and imprisoned not less than fifteen years, and, notwithstanding any other provision of law, the court shall not suspend the sentence of, or grant a probationary sentence to, such person with respect to the conviction under section 922(g), and such person shall not be eligible for parole with respect to the sentence imposed under this subsection.” Section 924(e)(2)(C) includes a finding of juvenile delinquency involving a violent felony. The classification of these predicates as violent felonies is distinct from their constitutional validity.

Exact §921(a)(20) relevant text: the defined crime excludes federal or state antitrust, unfair-trade, restraint-of-trade or similar business-regulation offenses, and state misdemeanors punishable by two years or less. “What constitutes a conviction of such a crime shall be determined in accordance with the law of the jurisdiction in which the proceedings were held. Any conviction which has been expunged, or set aside or for which a person has been pardoned or has had civil rights restored shall not be considered a conviction for purposes of this chapter, unless such pardon, expungement, or restoration of civil rights expressly provides that the person may not ship, transport, possess, or receive firearms.” The firearms reservation must not be dropped when stating the exemption. Beecham's effective simulated rule supplies the jurisdictional referent.

The comparator statute, 21 U.S.C. §851(c), expressly permits a written validity challenge, requires particular facts for a constitutional challenge, places the factual burden on the claimant by a preponderance, and treats an unraised challenge as waived absent good cause for failure to make a timely challenge. Its existence supports competing inferences; it is not ACCA's enacted procedure. Custis contends Congress legislated against Burgett/Tucker and the appellate acceptance of challenges and that avoidance/lenity support reading “convictions” as lawful ones. The Government invokes the fact of conviction, §921's set-aside language, Congress's express authorization elsewhere, and the difference between a current sentencing forum and review in the convicting jurisdiction.

Pre-divergence law: Gideon, 372 U.S.335; Burgett, 389 U.S.109 (facially uncounseled prior conviction and enhancement); Tucker, 404 U.S.443 (sentence relying on constitutionally invalid priors); Lewis, 445 U.S.55 (felon gun disability, distinguished in the opinion from reliance on unreliable convictions to support guilt/enhance punishment); Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S.458; Strickland, 466 U.S.668; Boykin, 395 U.S.238; Maleng, 490 U.S.488 (custody and a later enhanced sentence, without resolving scope of attack on the expired predicate); Timmreck, 441 U.S.780 (collateral attack and finality); DeBartolo, 485 U.S.568 (constitutional avoidance); Albernaz, 450 U.S.333 (lenity applies to penalties). Apply simulated Parke within its recorded counseled-plea/proof-allocation scope. Neither Parke nor a fact-intensive allegation proves that the underlying conviction is invalid.

## NLRB v. Health Care & Retirement Corp.

Official report: https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep511/usrep511571/usrep511571.pdf ; entire 29-page source read. Underlying Board/ALJ decisions: 306 N.L.R.B.63,68 (1992); Sixth Circuit: 987 F.2d1256 (1993). Quoted Board findings and reported submissions below are recoverable public facts, not present commitments.

Complete §2(11), 29 U.S.C.§152(11): “The term ‘supervisor’ means any individual having authority, in the interest of the employer, to hire, transfer, suspend, lay off, recall, promote, discharge, assign, reward, or discipline other employees, or responsibly to direct them, or to adjust their grievances, or effectively to recommend such action, if in connection with the foregoing the exercise of such authority is not of a merely routine or clerical nature, but requires the use of independent judgment.” All three statutory elements matter: listed authority; independent judgment rather than routine/clerical action; and exercise in the employer's interest. Section 2(3) excludes supervisors. Section 2(12) includes professional work with intellectual variation, consistent judgment, nonstandardizable output, and advanced specialized learning; subsection (b) includes trainees completing such study under professional supervision. Section 9(b) limits compulsory grouping of professionals with nonprofessionals.

Four licensed practical nurses, classified as technical rather than registered professional employees, had duties virtually identical to registered nurses. Heartland had a director, assistant director, 9–11 staff nurses and 50–55 aides. Staff nurses were senior on duty after 5 p.m. and weekends, about 75% of operating time. The employer emphasized staffing, daily assignments, monitoring, discipline/counseling, grievances, evaluations, and reports. The ALJ found limited time spent directing aides, established assignment patterns, aides often selecting assignments themselves, administrators always on call for nonroutine matters, and nurses chiefly furnishing patient care. The Board affirmed employee status. The Sixth Circuit rejected the patient-care rationale as inconsistent with the statute and found the nurses supervisors.

The Board defends its professional/technical patient-care distinction as reconciling covered professional work with exclusion of front-line management; it relies on professional norms, the limited managerial authority in the record, its leadperson cases, and the 1974 committee history. Respondent contends patient care is the employer's business, the statutory list cannot be reduced to hiring/pay decisions, and a committee report cannot amend §2(11). The case as presented focuses on “in the interest of the employer”; the Board did not separately petition for affirmance of its order under an independent-judgment alternative. A proposed remand on that alternative requires the preserved-question/agency-ground barrier to be addressed, not ignored.

The Board's argument at oral argument must be stated completely: asked whether only nurses do not serve the employer's interest while attending customers, counsel answered no; counsel then said the theory had not been phrased in the same terms for other professions, and explained the analogy to other minor supervisory employees. Do not turn the second statement alone into a categorical concession of an unprecedented nurse-only exemption.

Eligible authorities: NLRB v. Yeshiva University, 444 U.S.672,682–690 & n.30 (1980), managerial university faculty and the boundary for routine professional duties, expressly discussing incidental patient-care direction; NLRB v. Bell Aerospace, 416 U.S.267 (1974), managerial employees; Packard Motor Car, 330 U.S.485 (1947), pre-Taft-Hartley coverage and the phrase employer's interest; Fall River Dyeing, 482 U.S.27,42 (1987), rational/consistent Board interpretation; NLRB v. Curtin Matheson, 494 U.S.775 (1990), Board interpretive discretion; American Hospital Assn., 499 U.S.606 (1991), regulation of hospital units and the legal status of the 1974 report; Northcrest Nursing Home, 313 N.L.R.B.491 (1993), current Board elaboration. The 1947 Congress adopted a supervisor exclusion after Packard; the 1974 amendments extended health-care coverage without rewriting §2(11).

## Staples statutory completion from actual merits brief

The United States' brief in the Archive item quotes §5845(a) completely: firearm includes (1) shotgun barrel(s) under 18 inches; (2) modified shotgun under 26 inches overall or barrel(s) under 18 inches; (3) rifle barrel(s) under 16 inches; (4) modified rifle under 26 inches overall or barrel(s) under 16 inches; (5) any other weapon as subsection(e) defines; (6) machinegun; (7) silencer under 18 U.S.C.§921; (8) destructive device. It excludes an antique firearm or a device, other than a machinegun or destructive device, which the Secretary finds from manufacture date, value, design and other characteristics primarily a collector's item unlikely to be used as a weapon.

Complete §5845(b) from that brief: “The term ‘machinegun’ means any weapon which shoots, is designed to shoot, or can be readily restored to shoot, automatically more than one shot, without manual reloading, by a single function of the trigger. The term shall also include the frame or receiver of any such weapon, any part designed and intended solely and exclusively, or combination of parts designed and intended, for use in converting a weapon into a machinegun, and any combination of parts from which a machinegun can be assembled if such parts are in the possession or under the control of a person.” Those extra definition branches are not additional charged counts established here.
