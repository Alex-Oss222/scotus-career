## 87. Turner Broadcasting System, Inc. v. FCC, No. 93-44

### Verified record and lower posture

January 12, 1994 argument; direct appeal from the three-judge District Court for the District of Columbia, 819 F. Supp. 32 (1993). Section 23 of the 1992 Cable Act provides a three-judge court for challenges to §§4 and 5 and direct Supreme Court appeal. Five consolidated actions challenged the carriage requirements. All plaintiffs sought summary judgment; several intervenor-defendants cross-moved for summary judgment; the federal defendants moved to dismiss. The district court nevertheless entered summary judgment for federal defendants and intervening defenders. Do not describe all defendants as having moved for summary judgment.

The lower majority characterized the Act as a response to cable market dysfunction, considered the must-carry provisions content neutral in form and intent, applied O'Brien, and found the local-broadcast preservation interest important and the carriage provisions sufficiently tailored. It acknowledged cable's dominance and Congress's predictive assessment. Judge Williams dissented: he accepted a genuine communications bottleneck problem but disputed preferring broadcast speakers, identified Congress's statements about local programming as content-related, and found no demonstrated jeopardy to access to free television. These are the lower court's competing grounds, not Supreme Court holdings.

The jurisdictional statement recovered from the Internet Archive asks, in substance, whether Congress may require cable operators to devote channels to Congress-favored local broadcast programmers regardless of operators' preferences and the effect on other programmers. Its OCR is damaged; no exact quotation is authenticated here. The statement and appendix are available in IA item `micro_IA40385013_0440`, document 02. A recovered petition-stage document is not itself an in-world Court order.

Congress's enacted findings include: more than sixty percent of television households subscribe to cable; most subscribers do not or cannot maintain antennas, do not have selector switches, or cannot otherwise receive broadcast service; local franchising and construction expense contribute to local cable monopoly; operators can refuse, delete, or reposition broadcast signals; operators and broadcasters compete for advertising; vertical affiliations give operators incentives to favor affiliated programming. Congress also invoked local news, public affairs, educational/informational programming, diversity, and free television for people unable to afford other delivery. Keep these as legislative findings, not independently adjudicated facts about every system or station.

The evidentiary disputes include the meaning of a 1988 FCC survey reporting that about twenty percent of cable systems had dropped or refused at least one local station and about twenty-three percent had repositioned at least one local station; the survey's time frame and duration of drops; effects of drop/repositioning on station finances; cable programming displaced; existing unused capacity; future capacity; and alternatives. The report of the proceedings supplies no established finding that every uncarrried station is financially threatened. Conversely, absence of proven station failure is not a concession that threatened future harm is impossible. The government relies on predictive judgments; operators/programmers contest both the prediction and the burden's fit.

### Statutory mechanics and corrections to report shorthand

Authority: Pub. L. 102-385, §§2, 4–6, 23, 106 Stat. 1460, 1460–1463, 1471–1483, 1498 (October 5, 1992). The official original Act is recovered locally as `freeze/chunk8-sources/B/Cable_Act_1992.pdf`. The following is a source-based description, not a judicial construction. Preserve differences between commercial §614 (47 U.S.C. §534) and noncommercial §615 (47 U.S.C. §535).

- **Commercial quantity and small-system exception.** Section 614(b)(1)(A) requires a system with twelve or fewer usable activated channels to carry at least three local commercial stations, but a system in that category with **300 or fewer subscribers** has no requirements under that section **so long as it does not delete any broadcast station signal**. Section 614(b)(1)(B) requires a system with more than twelve usable activated channels to carry local commercial stations up to one-third of its aggregate usable activated channels. The text does not make 300 subscribers a freestanding exemption for every system. The statutory small-system exemption is also not a blanket exemption from §615.
- **Selection and duplicate signals.** Where eligible local commercial stations exceed required capacity, the operator chooses, but may never choose a qualified low-power station instead of a local commercial full-power station. If it elects to carry a network affiliate, the selected affiliate must have the city-of-license reference point closest to the principal headend. A station substantially duplicating a carried local commercial station need not be carried, nor need more than one local commercial affiliate of a network; duplicates voluntarily carried count toward the obligation. Selection is therefore discretionary within stated limits.
- **Commercial content and technical duties.** Primary video, accompanying audio, line-21 closed captions, and, to the extent technically feasible, program-related vertical-blanking/subcarrier material travel with the station. Other/nonprogram-related material is discretionary. Appropriate feasible removal of signal enhancement with its use at the headend is allowed. The entire schedule is carried unless specified FCC rules prohibit particular programming and authorize substitution. Carriage is without material degradation, with technically feasible nondiscriminatory processing standards. This does not require operator endorsement of the programs.
- **Commercial channel position.** At the station's election: over-the-air channel, July 19, 1985 carriage channel, or January 1, 1992 carriage channel; alternatively a channel mutually agreed by operator and station. The FCC resolves disputes. A summary saying only the over-the-air channel is required omits statutory alternatives.
- **Commercial payment exceptions.** Ordinarily no payment or valuable consideration for required carriage or position. The station may bear good-signal/baseband delivery costs; distant-signal copyright indemnification may be paid; a payment agreement entered before June 26, 1990 may continue through its expiration, but no further. Commercial eligibility excludes a distant signal if its station refuses increased-copyright indemnification and excludes inadequate headend signals if the station refuses responsibility for good-quality/baseband delivery. Low-power/translator/passive repeater signals are not ordinary full-power local commercial stations.
- **Commercial availability, notice, and complaint.** Required signals reach every subscriber and connected receiver; particular self-installed extra receiver arrangements carry notification and converter-box offer duties. Written deletion/reposition notice is at least thirty days; deletion/reposition cannot occur during major audience-measurement periods. Notice cannot evade substantive carriage rights. A station first notifies the operator of the alleged violation; within thirty days the operator carries as requested or states its reasons. The station may complain to the FCC; the operator gets an opportunity to respond. FCC determination is due within 120 days; a carriage order continues at least twelve months. An operator meeting its obligations receives dismissal. Section 614(e) abolishes duties to provide or publicize selector switches, not the possibility of using them as an argued alternative.
- **Market adjustments and low-power qualifications.** Section 614(h)(1)(C) permits adding/excluding communities upon written request and directs particular attention to localism: historical carriage; local service/coverage; whether another eligible station supplies local news or sports/events; cable and noncable viewing patterns. A signal cannot be deleted while that market proceeding is pending. Section 614(c) requires one qualified low-power station on systems with no more than thirty-five channels, or two above that size, only if full-power signals do not fill the commercial allotment. Additional low-power carriage may use otherwise unused PEG channels with franchising approval. Qualification requires minimum broadcast hours; compliance with specified nonentertainment, political/candidate, controversy, editorial, personal-attack, children's-programming, and employment obligations; an FCC finding of unmet local news/information needs because full-power stations are geographically distant; interference compliance; location within thirty-five miles of the headend and a good signal; both relevant areas outside the 160 largest June 30, 1990 metropolitan statistical areas; a community population no greater than 35,000 then; and no full-power station licensed in the cable-served county or political subdivision. Secondary status survives. The lower court did not separately analyze the classification of these low-power and market-adjustment provisions; treatment of them must not silently follow an undifferentiated full-power description.
- **Noncommercial quantity.** Section 615 begins with carriage of every qualified local noncommercial station requesting it, subject to the express exceptions. Systems of twelve or fewer activated channels carry one (additional carriage is discretionary); systems of thirteen to thirty-six carry at least one and need not carry more than three (additional carriage discretionary); larger systems are governed by the general carriage requirement and duplication exception. A system expanding above thirty-six after March 29, 1990 falls under that larger-system treatment.
- **Noncommercial importation and existing carriage.** A system without a local qualifying station imports one, selected by the operator. A twelve-or-fewer-channel system need not remove programming actually provided March 29, 1990 to do this, but must use the first available channel. All systems continue carrying qualifying local noncommercial signals carried March 29, 1990; a particular operator and station may waive this by mutual written consent. Additional signals may use unused PEG capacity with franchising approval.
- **Noncommercial duplication.** For thirteen-to-thirty-six-channel systems carrying an affiliate of a state public-television network, an additional affiliate of that network need not be carried if its programming is substantially duplicated. A system above thirty-six channels required to carry three qualifying stations need not carry additional substantially duplicative stations; the FCC defines duplication to promote access to distinctive services. A carried station cannot use its network-nonduplication right to delete another carried qualifying noncommercial station's programming.
- **Noncommercial technical, channel, and payment conditions.** Primary video/audio/captions and technically feasible related accessibility/educational/language material must be transmitted; other material is discretionary. Equal technical capacity and no material degradation are required, but a good-quality or baseband headend signal is necessary. Channel choices are over-the-air, July 19, 1985, or mutual agreement; **January 1, 1992 is not separately listed here**. Thirty-day notice to station and subscribers precedes repositioning/deletion. Required signals go in the lowest-priced tier including local commercial broadcasts. Payment is forbidden except delivery costs; a newly added distant copyright signal need not be carried without station indemnification, while existing §615(c) carriage remains distinct. FCC complaints carry an opportunity to respond and a 120-day determination/remedial-order or dismissal rule.
- **Noncommercial definition.** Qualification covers nonprofit/public-agency stations licensed under the specified FCC rules with licensees eligible for CPB community-service grants, or municipal stations predominantly transmitting noncommercial educational programming; specified translators and reserved-channel stations are included. Local status is either a principal-community reference point within fifty miles of the headend or a Grade B contour encompassing it. This differs from commercial television-market boundaries.
- **Sales-programming transition and retransmission consent.** Section 614(g) initially made home-shopping carriage discretionary pending an FCC public-interest proceeding; the FCC was directed to consider viewing, competing spectrum demands, and competition with nonbroadcast services and then qualify public-interest stations or permit a reasonable programming-transition period for others. That deadline preceded this case, so the original interim clause alone does not establish June 1994 treatment. The actual FCC implementing determination must be consulted if material. Section 6 supplies a distinct election between must-carry and retransmission consent; neither should be represented as a right to demand both simultaneously. Exact disputed election/transition administration is not independently resolved here.

## 88. Board of Education of Kiryas Joel Village School District v. Grumet

### Verified record and lower posture

Nos. 93-517, 93-527, 93-539; argued March 30, 1994. Review of 81 N.Y.2d 518, 618 N.E.2d 94 (1993). Trial court granted respondents summary judgment under all three Lemon criteria and both federal/state constitutions; divided Appellate Division affirmed on primary effect; New York Court of Appeals affirmed under the federal Establishment Clause and **expressly reserved the state constitutional question**. Chief Judge Kaye's concurrence used strict review for a particular religious group's special benefit and found a full district unnecessarily broad compared with providing a local school. Judge Hancock's concurrence also found an impermissible purpose. Judge Bellacosa dissented based on secular public special education and accommodation of religious/cultural differences. No separate adequate state-law affirmance ground should be invented.

The initial plaintiffs included the State School Boards Association and Grumet and Hawk in official/individual capacities. Appellate Division denied the Association and its officers standing; individual respondents proceed as citizen taxpayers under New York State Finance Law §123. Original state defendants left through stipulation after the two school districts intervened; the Attorney General continued defending the statute under Executive Law §71. These details qualify the supplied packet's generic standing description without deciding a new federal objection.

The village incorporated in 1977 under general village law after disputes about zoning and negotiations with neighbors opposed to inclusion. Its 320 acres were inhabited/owned by Satmars; it had about 8,500 residents in the relevant record. It lay in Monroe-Woodbury. Its private religious schools did not offer the needed special education. Monroe-Woodbury began providing public special services at a religious-school annex in 1984 and stopped after Aguilar/Ball in 1985; parents later reported panic, fear, and trauma with outside placements and withdrew most children. By 1989 only one child attended the outside public school. Some others had privately financed special services; others went without. Emotional distress is a reported parental basis, not a finding that interaction violates Satmar doctrine.

**Wieder, 72 N.Y.2d 174, 527 N.E.2d 767 (1988):** the Court of Appeals rejected Monroe-Woodbury's claimed state-law inability to establish a separate public program within the village; state law provided educational authorities broad discretion. It did not hold that free exercise required a separate school: the parents relied on emotional trauma rather than inconsistency with religious doctrine/practice. Full Wieder review is not claimed in this handoff. Neither “permitted” nor “within discretion” becomes “required.”

Chapter 748 grants general district powers, although the district operates a secular special-education program. Its board can open/close schools, hire teachers, prescribe textbooks/disciplinary rules, and raise property taxes under Education Law §1709. Other village children attend private schools, using transportation, remedial and health/welfare services. A child seeking regular public instruction would be tuitioned to a neighboring district. Several neighboring districts send special-education pupils into Kiryas Joel; the report gives just over forty full-time pupils, about two-thirds from outside, and two or three times as many part-time pupils. There is no statutory exclusion of residents by faith, no stated rabbinical appointment, and no established religious instruction in the public program. A non-Hasidic superintendent and teachers/therapists from outside, coeducational classrooms, absence of religious symbols, and public-school regulatory compliance are reported record circumstances. These do not decide the constitutional characterization of the district's creation.

### Challenged statute in full

1989 N.Y. Laws, chapter 748, as reproduced at 512 U.S. 693 n.1:

> Section 1. The territory of the village of Kiryas Joel in the town of Monroe, Orange county, on the date when this act shall take effect, shall be and hereby is constituted a separate school district, and shall be known as the Kiryas Joel village school district and shall have and enjoy all the powers and duties of a union free school district under the provisions of the education law.
>
> § 2. Such district shall be under the control of a board of education, which shall be composed of from five to nine members elected by the qualified voters of the village of Kiryas Joel, said members to serve for terms not exceeding five years.
>
> § 3. This act shall take effect on the first day of July next succeeding the date on which it shall have become a law.

The Governor's approving memorandum is dated July 24, 1989. The text supplies no sunset, religious-membership requirement, or limitation of district power to special education. General public-school law still applies; the special act's broad allocation and current limited program are separate facts.

### Date-eligible authority and individual joins verified

**Larkin v. Grendel's Den, 459 U.S. 116 (December 13, 1982), full report read.** Burger's opinion was joined by Brennan, White, Marshall, Blackmun, Powell, Stevens, and O'Connor; Rehnquist dissented. The challenged provision gave churches/schools a standardless veto over liquor permits within 500 feet. It distinguished reasonable zoning and an opportunity to present views from transfer of discretionary governmental decisionmaking to religious institutions. Valid secular protection did not alone cure the delegation, and equal-protection/other due-process boundaries remained distinct. Blackmun, Stevens, and O'Connor thus have actual joins; no later-appointed Justice is assigned one.

**Corporation of Presiding Bishop v. Amos, 483 U.S. 327 (June 24, 1987), full report read.** White's opinion was joined by Rehnquist, Powell, Stevens, and Scalia. It sustained Title VII's religious-employer exemption as applied to nonprofit activity, treating relief from interference in religious mission as permissible and distinguishing denominational discrimination from a general religious exemption. It did not decide the for-profit boundary or hold free exercise compelled the exemption. Brennan, joined by Marshall, concurred in judgment based on nonprofit institutional autonomy and the intrusive/chilling consequences of case-by-case religiosity inquiries. O'Connor separately concurred in judgment, requiring a real burden being alleviated and an objective observer's distinction between accommodation and endorsement; she rejected a simple “government permits/church advances” formula and reserved for-profit application. Blackmun separately concurred essentially for O'Connor's reasons, expressly preserving the for-profit question. These are distinct rationales, not interchangeable direct joins.
