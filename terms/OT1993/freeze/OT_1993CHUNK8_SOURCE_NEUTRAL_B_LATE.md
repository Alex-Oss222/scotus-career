### Review scope and qualifications requiring fresh consideration

The petition questions, reproduced at petition page i in the official Report, concern (1) whether the 36-foot zone is an unconstitutional content-based restriction on speech/association, (2) whether a consent requirement in 300-foot clinic and residential areas is a reasonable time/place/manner restriction or unconstitutional prior restraint, and (3) whether binding named demonstrators and persons acting in concert violates speech/association. These descriptions report what petitioners asked; they do not decide the questions' legal reach.

Paragraph (6) itself contains no consent proviso. Nevertheless, the **separate invited-contact paragraph** applies to protected persons and expressly addresses contact at residences: invitation must be transmitted from a distance and before the contact; an uninvited doorbell or knock is insufficient. The protected person may revoke invitation, requiring immediate termination and departure. Thus neither “all residential contact is forbidden regardless of invitation” nor “paragraph (6) repeats the clinic consent clause” accurately gives the full text. This supplement flags the source qualification for independent neutral review; it does not revise the supplied packet's analytical framing.

Paragraph (3) contains three distinct exceptions/permissions: the east shoulder with no ascent of the embankment and a five-foot setback; the northern/western record owners and invitees' entry exception (other prohibitions still apply if acting in concert); and a precisely located crosswalk, limited to prompt crossing, without stopping, hesitation, picketing, congregation, demonstration, or impediment to traffic. The south shoulder remains usable subject to the other restrictions. Paragraph (5)'s invitation allows nonthreatening conversation and delivery of literature within 300 feet but never within the 36-foot zone; it also preserves immediate withdrawal and prohibits accompanying, encircling, surrounding, harassment, threats, or physical/verbal abuse of a declining person. Do not transform restrictions on physical approach into an unqualified ban on all speaking within 300 feet without independently construing the operative words.

Paragraph (4) states both the 7:30 a.m.–noon Monday–Saturday period and the surgery/recovery qualification. Its words cover sounds or images observable to or within earshot of patients **inside** the clinic. The record does not justify dropping either temporal phrase. Paragraph (7) also has an invited-contact exception. Actual notice and concert/participation are distinct predicates; notice alone is not the textual definition of concert.

The petition formulation presents a material review-scope issue for separate tailoring attacks on the north/west portion of the 36-foot zone and for the noise/images provision, which is not expressly named in the three questions. Do not import a resolution of what is fairly included. The official record reports that petitioners challenged neither (1)–(2) nor (7)–(9); the full text above does not convert those into granted components. The lower appeal raised free speech, association, equal protection, and free exercise, but those lower claims do not establish that all were granted here. The Florida opinion says petitioners mentioned Florida constitutional speech protection without a separate state-constitutional argument. No claim for damages was before that court.

Petitioners did not certify a complete evidentiary transcript and opposed requiring the full transcript/record in their Florida appeal, describing the appeal as presenting legal error on the order's face and assuming a factual basis for injunctive relief for purposes of appeal. Selected transcript extracts and the edited video therefore are not the complete evidentiary record. An assertion that all facts were independently retried or that selected images negate unchallenged findings would be unsupported. The Florida court stated that competent substantial evidence supported the trial findings and that petitioners did not seriously contest those findings or the technical validity of the injunction. Whether the found facts legally establish violation of the earlier order is distinct from contesting the evidence supporting the findings.

### Earlier order, findings, and lower reasoning

The September 30, 1992 order barred trespassing on, sitting in, blocking, impeding, or obstructing ingress/egress at abortion facilities in Brevard and Seminole Counties; physically abusing persons entering, leaving, working at, or using them; and attempting or directing others to do those acts. It expressly preserved legitimate First Amendment activities, including signs, singing, and praying, conducted without violating those prohibitions. The April amendment left the earlier order effective as to all persons, entities, and places except as modified.

The April findings describe the clinic at the northwest corner of U.S. 1 and Dixie Way, with driveways across the public pedestrian area to two parking lots and fences on its west and north. Crowds ranged from a handful to about 400, one to three days weekly, usually largest Saturday. They included named respondents, loosely associated demonstrators, clinic supporters, media, and five to fifty officers. Clinic supporters ordinarily occupied clinic property but sometimes shared the sidewalk. Noise came from both partisan groups, exterior clinic loudspeakers, portable radios, and occasional bullhorns; persons sometimes shouted in one another's faces. Police sometimes erected separating barricades and on one occasion closed the north lane to give people more room, leaving two-way traffic in the south lane. These qualifications matter: not all persons or all noise in the findings were attributed to the enjoined groups.

The findings also describe a circular picket line crossing driveways, cars slowing and sometimes momentarily stopping until people moved, and sidewalk counselors exploiting that interval to approach vehicles. A doctor testified that he had to wait while Cadle and others took their time moving and shouted at him; he saw protesters beside/in front of patients' vehicles pushing pamphlets through windows without indicated interest. He testified to anxiety, hypertension, more sedation and associated surgical risk, noise through the clinic walls during surgery/recovery, and risks from postponed treatment when patients turned away. Persons sometimes used ladders outside the north/west fence to shout at persons entering the clinic. A prayer that God strike a staff member dead was reported. A person associated with the respondents followed the physician's vehicle and mimed shooting him. The physician later left employment after those activities and the killing of another physician at a different north-Florida clinic. The latter killing is not a finding that these petitioners committed it.

Residential conduct included picketing, neighbor doorbells and leaflets, communications with minor children home alone, and a motel demonstration: Cadle remained off premises while others entered the lobby shouting, delaying a doctor's departure by half an hour. Findings additionally concern tracing patient license plates to addresses and contacting them, multiple calls jamming clinic phone lines and preventing emergency ambulance calls, and stalking-like following. The trial court concluded that ingress remained obstructed, noise interfered with clinic operation/patient well-being, and residential intrusion, telephone interference, and uninvited following required restraint; it separately recognized the demonstrators' rights to speak.

Florida's Supreme Court treated the surrounding streets, sidewalks and rights-of-way as a traditional public forum and the order as content neutral because it regulated location, timing, volume and harmful conduct rather than abortion views. It applied significant-interest/narrow-tailoring/alternative-channels analysis, with de novo review of legal matters and deference to fact-based equitable choices. The interests it identified were access to lawful medical care, traffic, public safety/order, property, residential privacy, and protection of patients unable to avoid communications while receiving care. It regarded the restrictions as responses to specific abuses and declined incremental adjustments to distances. It identified remaining expression from the south side of Dixie Way, the east/west edges of U.S. 1, invited counseling outside the inner zones, and residential-neighborhood activity outside 300 feet. It described Dixie Way as 21 feet wide near the clinic.

The Florida court rejected prior-restraint, vagueness and overbreadth objections, construed the injunction with Florida Rule of Civil Procedure 1.610(c)'s actual-notice and active-concert limits, and rejected the remaining constitutional claims without extended analysis. It treated the noise/images language as giving notice against audible noise and ladder displays visible to patients during surgery/recovery. These are **lower-court holdings and interpretations**, not the result to be reached here.

The separate federal litigation is **Cheffer v. McGregor, 6 F.3d 705 (11th Cir. Oct. 20, 1993), No. 93-2407**. [CAP complete opinion](https://static.case.law/f3d/6/html/0705-01.html); local `Cheffer_lower.html` / `Cheffer_lower.txt`, read in full. Cheffer was neither a party to the state injunction litigation nor an arrested person, and alleged independent intended counseling chilled by threatened enforcement. The district court denied temporary/preliminary relief and deferred permanent relief. The panel **vacated the denial and remanded for reconsideration of preliminary relief**; it did not itself enter a final judgment invalidating the state injunction. Tjoflat and Dubina treated the injunction's operation against nonparties as analogous to a viewpoint-based local criminal prohibition, recognized standing, rejected Younger abstention absent an ongoing proceeding in which Cheffer could raise her rights, and directed the district court to reconsider the usual four preliminary-injunction factors. Paine dissented from that approach and proposed immediate relief limited to Cheffer as an independent nonparty, under ordinary injunction law rather than treating the order as legislation. No federal final judgment after that remand is established by the sources used here.

### Application to nonparties: complete qualification, not a factual shortcut

The written injunction names organizations, individual defendants and those acting in concert/participation or on their behalf with notice. The April 12 appearance-hearing excerpts contain both statements connecting the injunction's coverage to persons opposed to the clinic and statements leaving independent action/concert to later individual trials. One exchange said that antiabortion position was, in effect, within the order's coverage; others said claimed nonassociation was a defense to present with counsel at trial. Another answer denied that opposition to abortion alone explained the arrest. The judge distinguished an arrest report from a formal charge and said charging rested with the prosecutor. The complete qualification is that these are preliminary appearance statements, not an adjudication that every arrestee actually acted in concert. Neither quoted side of these exchanges should erase the other. The complete hearing transcript has not been independently read here; the Report reproduces substantial relevant extracts, and Cheffer reports an additional exchange. Madsen's named petitioners and Cheffer's alleged independent nonparty posture must remain distinct.

## 94. International Union, United Mine Workers of America v. Bagwell, No. 92-1625

### Sources and precise posture

The full lower judgment is **Bagwell v. International Union, United Mine Workers of America, 244 Va. 463, 423 S.E.2d 349 (Nov. 6, 1992), consolidated Record Nos. 910634 and 920299**. [CAP complete judicial text](https://static.case.law/va/244/html/0463-01.html); local `Bagwell_lower.html` and `Bagwell_lower.txt`, read in full. The complete [official Report, 512 U.S. 821](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep512/usrep512821/usrep512821.pdf) was also read; the predecision record and procedural discussion at 823–826 and record details at 830–838 supply corroboration. Fresh neutral contexts should use this handoff rather than the complete same-matter Report. Argument here occurred November 29, 1993.

The case involves an International Union and its District 28, collectively called the Union. Clinchfield Coal Company and Sea “B” Mining Company, collectively the Company, brought an April 12, 1989 complaint in Russell County Circuit Court after a strike following expiration of their collective bargaining agreement and use of replacement employees. An April 13 injunction followed evidence; it was amended April 21 in response to continuing mass and roving pickets, threats, violence, and property destruction. The appealed orders comprised eight contempt orders: five in Record 910634 and three in Record 920299.

The underlying injunction prohibited obstructing ingress/egress, throwing objects at and threatening Company employees, placing jackrocks on roads, and exceeding specified numbers of picketers; affirmative provisions required steps to ensure compliance, picket-site supervisors, and violation reports. The exact full order and numerical picket limits are not reproduced in the sources independently read here. The official Report locates them at petition appendix 114a–116a. This is a **source limit**, not authority to invent the missing limits or to describe a purely prohibitory decree. No party requested an appellate clause-by-clause separation of sanctions based on affirmative versus negative commands. If such a separation becomes material, the actual order and violation-by-violation assessments are needed before making it.

### Sanctions, beneficiaries, procedure, and settlement

The May 18 first contempt order found 72 violations, 15 violent, and imposed $642,000, with $424,000 suspended on future compliance. Those initial fines were subsequently vacated as criminal; they are **not** the surviving public fines being reviewed. In that first order the judge announced a prospective schedule: $100,000 for each violent violation and $20,000 for each nonviolent violation. Advance compliance would avoid future scheduled assessments. Once the later fines were imposed for completed violations, there was no opportunity to erase them by later compliance. Do not equate the first order's suspended portion with a purge clause for the surviving later fines.

Seven further contempt hearings from June through December 1989 addressed more than 400 separate later violations, many violent. They were conducted as civil proceedings before the judge, with discovery, evidence and cross-examination. The court required proof beyond a reasonable doubt but afforded **no jury**. The Virginia opinion describes approximately 260 witnesses and many exhibits and says the Union did not challenge evidentiary sufficiency in those appeals. Its reference to more than 500 violations across the litigation should not be substituted for the Report's more than 400 later violations under the schedule. The different counting periods must be preserved.

Later assessments totaled over $64 million. Approximately $12 million were directed to the Company; the Union objected to making the private Company the beneficiary. Roughly $52 million were directed to the Commonwealth and Russell and Dickenson Counties, associated in the trial judge's explanation with policing burdens and the public's suffering. These approximate totals are the official Report's descriptions; the itemized lower orders should not be turned into an exact aggregate without accounting for each Union defendant and order.

While appeals were pending, the Union and Company settled the strike and jointly sought vacatur of the contempt fines. A special mediator acting for the Secretary of Labor and the county governments supported that request. The circuit court dismissed the underlying case, dissolved the injunction, and vacated the Company fines, but refused to vacate the public fines. The lower opinion notes that the record did not clearly show express vacatur of every Company assessment, while **both parties agreed that those fines had been vacated**. Do not describe the private sums as still collectible merely because of that record qualification.

The Company withdrew and declined enforcement. After local Commonwealth Attorneys sought disqualification, John L. Bagwell was appointed special commissioner to collect for the governments. The appointment included docketing judgments, interrogatories, execution, levy, garnishment, attachment and creditor proceedings where property could be found; employment of counsel/accountants required court leave, and collections went to the clerk for court-ordered distribution. This was an enforcement representative for public beneficiaries, not a newly injured compensatory claimant.

**No party defended the surviving public fines as compensation for proved loss.** No governmental damages request or proof supplied their amount, the governmental beneficiaries were not parties/intervenors when assessed, and the assessments were not calibrated to actual loss. Mention of extraordinary public enforcement costs in a judge's explanation is not a finding of quantified compensable damage. Conversely, the dissolved injunction does not make the unpaid judgments disappear by itself; whether settlement extinguishes them depends on their legal character and the governing remedial law.

The Virginia Court of Appeals, **United Mine Workers v. Clinchfield Coal Co., 12 Va. App. 123, 402 S.E.2d 899 (1991)**, assumed civil character and ruled that settlement required vacating civil contempt fines arising from a private action. Its full separate text was not independently obtained; that account is verified in the full Virginia Supreme Court judgment. The Court of Appeals had denied Bagwell intervention but allowed an amicus brief. Virginia's Supreme Court allowed him to defend the public judgments on appeal, finding his broad appointment, the private Company's withdrawal, absence of prejudice, and timely appeal sufficient; accepting amicus participation did not waive the effort to intervene. The court granted review in 910634 and certified the three-order appeal in 920299 from the intermediate court.

On the merits the Virginia Supreme Court classified the fines as prospective civil coercion because the schedule was announced in advance and could be avoided by compliance. It rejected the argument that their prohibitory rather than affirmative focus required criminal treatment, and held that settlement did not compel vacatur under Virginia law. It also rejected excessiveness, labor-law preemption and recusal objections. The recusal allegation involved the judge's father's defeat by a Union official in an election and a separate confrontation prosecution; the court found no abuse of discretion and observed that significant adverse rulings preceded those events. The judgment reversed the intermediate court and entered final judgment for Bagwell in 910634, and affirmed the circuit court in 920299. These are the **judgments below**, not a proposed Supreme Court disposition.

### Question and parties' competing positions

The question established by the official procedural account is whether these substantial fixed fines, imposed without a jury for completed out-of-court violations after advance announcement, were civil coercion or criminal punishment. The Union seeks relief from the surviving fines; Bagwell seeks collection. The Union stresses completed violations, absence of a later purge, lack of compensation, and public beneficiaries. Bagwell and the Solicitor General stress prospective announcement and avoidability through obedience, distinguishing fines announced only after misconduct and defending courts' ability to enforce decrees.

A separately authenticated petition question and full grant wording remain unavailable. Do not enlarge this issue to every lower-court objection, a wholesale attack on the original injunction, or a numerical maximum for every civil or criminal fine. The civil label, proof beyond reasonable doubt, jury entitlement, purge opportunity and beneficiary are separate facts and questions. The materials do not establish waiver of a jury. Exact statutory or common-law authority for every injunction clause has not been independently transcribed; the classification issue does not itself authorize adding new claims about those clauses.

### Ward v. Rock Against Racism, 491 U.S. 781 (June 22, 1989)

[Official Report](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep491/usrep491781/usrep491781.pdf), local `Ward.pdf` / `Ward.txt`, entire report read. Kennedy wrote for Rehnquist, White, O'Connor and Scalia; Blackmun concurred in the result without a separate explanatory writing. Marshall dissented with Brennan and Stevens. Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg were not members of that Court.

Kennedy's opinion concerns a generally applicable sound-equipment/technician requirement for a municipal bandshell. Content neutrality turns on justification independent of regulated content, including the government's purpose; incidental differential effects alone are insufficient. A neutral time/place/manner regulation must serve a significant interest, be narrowly tailored and leave ample alternatives. It need not be the least restrictive imaginable option, but must not burden substantially more speech than necessary, and a substantial part of the speech burden cannot fail to advance the asserted goals. The opinion relied on facts that sponsors retained artistic control over mix, the city consulted over volume, the equipment met performers' needs, and music remained permitted. Its prior-restraint discussion distinguished authority to suppress speech from control of volume. The facial discretion question was assumed rather than definitively expanded. See 791–803, particularly 795 n.5 and 798–800. The joins establish O'Connor's and Scalia's agreement with those propositions **there**; Blackmun's result-only concurrence does not establish agreement with every formulation.

Stevens joined Marshall's dissent, 803–812, which accepted content neutrality and significant noise interests but required comparison with workable less intrusive controls; it objected that control of amplification and mix before expression presented censorship risks absent adequate standards and prompt review. That dissent distinguished regulated sound from the city's control of the means of communicating. These are narrower, documented positions than any general inference about how Stevens must treat injunctions.

### Frisby v. Schultz, 487 U.S. 474 (June 27, 1988)

[Official Report](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep487/usrep487474/usrep487474.pdf), local `Frisby.pdf` / `Frisby.txt`, entire report read. O'Connor wrote for Rehnquist, Blackmun, Scalia and Kennedy. White concurred in the judgment; Brennan dissented with Marshall; Stevens dissented separately. Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg were not members.

O'Connor's opinion sustained a facially challenged residential-picketing ordinance **as narrowly construed to focused picketing solely before a particular residence**, not general marching in residential neighborhoods or along a block. Residential streets remained traditional public forums. Protection of an unwilling captive home audience supported the limited ban, including a solitary targeted picket, while door-to-door dissemination, mail and nonharassing telephone communication remained available. A complete prohibition can be narrow only if every activity within its scope is an appropriately targeted evil. Applications to invited picketers or homes used for business/public meetings were expressly left open. See 480–488. The opinion does not itself establish that a 300-foot area-wide exclusion or a medical-facility restriction is the same measure.

Stevens's own dissent, 496–499, distinguished offensive manner/context from disagreement with ideas, accepted regulation of repetitive hostile picketing amounting to a siege, and accepted application to the conduct giving rise to that ordinance. He objected to the text's coverage of willing/indifferent listeners, brief communication and friendly signs, and to the resulting enforcement discretion; he proposed confining the law to conduct unreasonably invading home privacy without a reasonable communicative purpose. This writing simultaneously supplies recognition of a privacy interest and limits on an indiscriminate ban.

### Hicks v. Feiock, 485 U.S. 624 (April 27, 1988)

[Official Report](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep485/usrep485624/usrep485624.pdf), local `Hicks.pdf` / `Hicks.txt`, entire report read. White wrote for Brennan, Marshall, Blackmun and Stevens. O'Connor dissented with Rehnquist and Scalia. Kennedy took no part. Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg were not members.

The opinion joined by Blackmun and Stevens treats classification for constitutional protections as federal law; state labels are informative but not decisive. The character of relief matters: conditional compliance-based confinement is civil; unconditional determinate confinement is criminal. A court-payable fine may be civil when the contemnor can avoid it by doing the ordered affirmative act; relief compensating the complainant is distinct from punishment for the court. Purposes overlap, so a court's intended coercion does not alone settle classification. Suspension/probation does not by itself transform a fixed sentence into civil relief; what remained to be determined there was whether satisfaction of arrears would purge the determinate sentence. Criminal contempt requires proof beyond reasonable doubt; serious criminal contempt also requires a jury, with the petty-offense qualification preserved. Civil coercion cannot be imposed when inability to comply is clearly established. See 630–641 and nn.5, 7, 9–11. The opinion's references to Gompers, Penfield, Shillitani, Bloom and related decisions are verified as its treatment of those authorities; their complete standalone Reports were not independently read for this supplement.

O'Connor's dissent, joined by Scalia, 641–651, also used substantive features rather than labels and recognized that compensation proportional to loss is civil while a fixed court-payable fine is punitive. She treated the suspended child-support sentence as civil because the private beneficiary initiated enforcement, the prosecutor acted on that beneficiary's behalf, and avoiding jail required current compliance and curing the arrears. She did not regard uncertainty about release after eventual incarceration as requiring resolution at that stage. That documented dispute concerns a continuing private support order and conditional suspension; it does not establish an answer about the public fines, settlement and lack of later purge here.
