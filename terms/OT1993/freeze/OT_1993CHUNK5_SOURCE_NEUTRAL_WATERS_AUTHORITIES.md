## Pickering — baseline and express reservations

[Official complete report, 391 U.S. 563–584](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep391/usrep391563/usrep391563.pdf), 22 pages. At 568 the Court balances the employee's citizen interest in public-concern speech against the employer's interest in effective public service. At 569–570 it refuses an all-purpose rule for the many different employment settings. Pickering did not involve daily working contact with the criticized officials, disruption of close relations needing personal loyalty, or disruption of actual duties. The employer could not identify its members' personal sensitivity with the schools' institutional interests. At 570 n.3 it reserves especially confidential or intimate superior-subordinate relationships; at 572 and n.4 it reserves circumstances in which an employee's special factual access or a narrowly drawn internal grievance procedure might matter.

At 572–575, erroneous public criticism on the record presented could not support discharge absent knowing or reckless falsity. The Court did not decide whether a knowingly or recklessly false statement nevertheless remains protected when neither actual nor reasonably presumed harm exists (574 n.6). It also reserves remarks demonstrating general unfitness for assigned work, as distinguished from dismissal for the remarks as such (573 n.5). The appendix independently examines the evidence because the Board combined target, prosecutor, and factfinder roles and the state courts had reviewed only for substantial evidence; the separate due-process objection had not been preserved (578–579 n.2). None of these facts constitutes a general rule resolving every disputed account of an employee conversation.

## Mt. Healthy — causation remains distinct

[Official complete report, 429 U.S. 274–287](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep429/usrep429274/usrep429274.pdf), 14 pages. Blackmun and Stevens joined. At 283–287, the absence of tenure or a contractual entitlement to reemployment does not permit denial of employment because of constitutionally protected speech. But an employee must first show protected conduct was a substantial or motivating factor in the challenged decision. The employer may then prove by a preponderance that it would have made the same decision without the protected conduct. A protected episode cannot put an employee in a better position than the employee would otherwise occupy; conversely, the existence of other grounds does not itself prove the same decision would actually have occurred. This supplies a verified individual commitment of both Blackmun and Stevens to separating protection from causation and a proved same-decision defense.

## Givhan — private delivery and manner are different questions

[Official complete report, 439 U.S. 410–418](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep439/usrep439410/usrep439410.pdf), 9 pages. Blackmun and Stevens joined. At 413–416, speech does not lose First Amendment protection merely because the employee communicates privately with the employer. At 415 n.4, a personal confrontation may make manner, time, and place relevant to the institutional-efficiency side of the balance in addition to content. Protection for private delivery is therefore not immunity for every manner of delivering otherwise protected criticism.

At 416–417 the Court requires a factual determination of the Mt. Healthy defense: proof that discharge would have occurred absent the protected encounters, rather than a conclusion that other grounds could have justified it. Stevens's separate concurrence at 417–418 thought findings that dismissal rested primarily or almost entirely on the speech would ordinarily support rejecting a same-decision claim, but agreed with remand because the trial predated Mt. Healthy. He explained that the trial judge could supplement the existing findings without receiving more evidence if the existing record sufficed. Do not turn that possibility into a mandatory new hearing or a blanket entitlement to judgment.

## Connick — directly opposed personal grounds

[Official complete report, 461 U.S. 138–170](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep461/usrep461138/usrep461138.pdf), 33 pages.

**O'Connor's majority join (147–154):** content, form, and context, viewed in the entire record, determine whether speech concerns the public. Most questionnaire topics in an assistant prosecutor's transfer dispute were personal employment grievances; a question about pressure to participate in political campaigns was public concern. The employer's justification burden varies with the speech's nature and importance. Where close working relationships require trust, substantial weight goes to the employer's reasonable judgment about disruption; the office need not await a breakdown before acting. The majority relied on the questionnaire's limited public significance, distribution at work, and transfer-dispute context, and reserved the possibility that speech of greater public importance would require a stronger showing. The public-concern question remains legal and subject to independent review (148 n.7). A reasonable disruption prediction is not automatically proof of what words were spoken or of the employer's actual motive.

**Blackmun and Stevens's join in Brennan's dissent (156–170):** criticism of how a government office operates may concern the public even when voiced internally, motivated by a personal dispute, or directed at management. The dissent opposed treating that context as sufficient to exclude public significance and then using the same context again to diminish the speech in the balance. It stressed public interest in informed employee criticism and the trial record's lack of demonstrated disruption. Judicial appraisal should test the claimed institutional harm rather than accept a supervisor's fear without adequate record support. The dissent nevertheless used Pickering balancing; it did not deny the government's legitimate interest in functioning services or eliminate the employee's causation burden.

## Rankin — competing assessments of role and institutional risk

[Official complete report, 483 U.S. 378–401](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep483/usrep483378/usrep483378.pdf), 24 pages. This decision is eligible for 1994 merits analysis, subject to the notice-date limitation above.

**Blackmun and Stevens's majority join (384–392):** content, form, and context made the employee's remark public-concern speech; its offensiveness did not by itself defeat that characterization. A true threat would be different. The employer must justify discharge through the balance, which considers discipline, coworker harmony, close relationships needing confidence, performance of duties, and regular operations. The majority focused on the employee's actual clerical duties, negligible relationship with the constable, lack of a confidential or policymaking function, private setting, and absence of demonstrated operational harm. The opinion did not immunize clerical speech that truly injures effective functioning (391 n.18). The majority and dissent disputed what the record established about the employee's meaning and role; do not convert either side's characterization into an uncontested finding.

**Scalia's dissent, joined by O'Connor (394–401):** he disputed both public concern and balancing. On his reading, an expressed wish for a successful assassination was not converted into core political criticism merely by the preceding political discussion. Alternatively, a law-enforcement office had substantial interests in employee cohesion and public confidence when employees endorsed violent crime. Actual interruption at that moment was not required; the likely effect on agency mission and relations could suffice. He rejected any categorical immunity for nonpolicymaking employees and disputed the majority's factual characterization of the employee's public contacts. His analysis separated whether the employer could suppress the speech in that work setting from whether dismissal was an excessive response: the First Amendment did not require the mildest sanction for speech the employer could constitutionally prohibit. This dissent does not establish that any report of disruptive words must be accepted without inquiry or that retaliatory motive is irrelevant.

## Live-record boundary preserved

The separate Waters source packet preserves competing accounts of what was said, the pre-discharge interviews, the subsequent grievance inquiry, and allegations of other protected criticism as the actual cause. This addendum makes no new finding that an inquiry was adequate, that a disputed account was true, or that a retaliatory motive was absent. Any such commitment must identify the evidence and summary-judgment inference that supports it. Whether the employer could act on a mistaken account, what reasonable inquiry requires, what motive caused discharge, and individual damages immunity are separate questions. No same-case later solution enters this supplement.
