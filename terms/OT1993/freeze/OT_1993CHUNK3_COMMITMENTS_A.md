## 25. American Dredging Co. v. Miller — No. 91-1950

### Neutral preflight and entering-law conclusions

Merits review of the Louisiana Supreme Court's March 2, 1992 judgment, 595 So. 2d 615, following the intermediate decision at 580 So. 2d 1091. Argument November 9, 1993; event February 23, 1994. Research cutoff is immediately before the event. Other uncoordinated February 23 decisions are excluded from this event's entering law. Cowart's LHWCA settlement conditions and Gizoni's seaman-status/voluntary-benefits holdings do not settle this forum question. The selected current-term holdings supply no controlling change to the saving-to-suitors or forum non conveniens rules.

Section 1333(1) reserves admiralty and maritime original jurisdiction to the federal district courts while saving other remedies to which suitors are otherwise entitled. An ordinary in personam Jones Act and general maritime action can be heard in a competent state court. The reviewed judgment concerns which forum hears it, not ultimate seaman status, negligence, unseaworthiness, wages, maintenance, cure, or damages. The Louisiana trial court rejected personal-jurisdiction dismissal; the state appellate issue and present review concern forum non conveniens. Neither the motion's alternative jurisdictional ground nor the absence of Louisiana injury contacts supplies an independently adjudicated basis to dismiss here.

The lower opinion, read in full, supplies the text of Article 123. Paragraph A permits a Louisiana district-to-district transfer for convenience and justice, upon a contradictory motion or hearing, but protects a suit filed in the plaintiff's domicile in a competent court of proper venue. Paragraph B permits dismissal without prejudice of a claim predicated solely on federal statute and acts or omissions outside Louisiana when a more appropriate out-of-state forum is shown, considering the event's location, convenience, and justice. It excludes a Louisiana-domiciled plaintiff's suit in a competent court of proper venue, and requires defendants to waive prescription if the same claim is filed in a competent court within sixty days of dismissal. Paragraph C says that Paragraph B does not apply to Jones Act or federal maritime claims. The state high court treats the statute as the exclusive authority for forum non conveniens dismissal and C as excluding this action. The Supreme Court need not reinterpret state law or hold these paragraphs universally valid to decide the maritime-preemption issue presented.

Louisiana cannot transfer this case to Pennsylvania through §1404(a); that federal mechanism is distinct from dismissing a state suit. Miller's Mississippi residence, the employer's Pennsylvania incorporation/New Jersey principal office, and the Delaware River injury and northeastern treatment establish a substantial inconvenience argument. They do not themselves decide the legal authority for dismissal. The employer retained a Louisiana service agent; the merits of its jurisdictional objection are outside this issue.

Applicable surviving law: Jensen's restrictions on material prejudice to characteristic maritime features and interference with harmony and uniformity; Mayfield's state procedural autonomy in FELA forum decisions, subject to its antidiscrimination qualifications; Bainbridge's state venue rule for state-court Jones Act actions; Piper and Gulf Oil's discretionary federal forum doctrine; and Miles v. Apex Marine's statute-bounded uniformity of substantive seamen's remedies. Sun Oil's recognition that substance/procedure classifications depend on the governing legal context is useful by analogy, not a maritime-preemption holding. No freestanding outcome-determinative formula is imported from Erie.

### Enhanced alternative-path comparison

**State-retention path.** Affirm because declining a competent forum for convenience is a rule governing the forum's administration, not a substantive maritime defense or a historically distinctive maritime liability rule. Mayfield and Bainbridge show that federal causes and seamen's claims do not automatically federalize state forum administration. Jensen remains enforceable where state procedure actually impairs a protected maritime feature or substantive uniformity. The strongest contrary argument is that forum non conveniens has a long admiralty history and materially protects maritime defendants from litigation disconnected from the dispute. The answer is that long federal use and practical importance do not alone establish an entitlement every state court must supply; the saving clause contemplates procedural differences and the substantive governing law is unchanged here. This path does not find Louisiana most convenient or authorize displacement of substantive maritime law.

**Federal-rule path.** Reverse the state high court's refusal to permit the federal inquiry because uniform maritime adjudication includes the established power to decline an inappropriate forum; Jensen's protected features need not be exclusive to admiralty. The general maritime obligation should not turn on a special state exception directing retention of maritime cases while allowing dismissal of some other federal cases. Piper's recognition of the doctrine's admiralty history and protection against serious litigation burdens supports this view; Miles reinforces substantive uniformity but does not itself decide procedure. The strongest contrary argument is that Mayfield and Bainbridge already preserve state choices over forum administration. The response distinguishes ordinary venue or generally applicable FELA practice from complete elimination of a longstanding maritime forum safeguard. This position would require a lawful federal assessment, not pronounce the underlying injury claims defeated. It must retain the adequate-alternative-forum condition, consideration of relevant private and public interests, appropriate weight to the chosen forum, and abuse-of-discretion review of a properly conducted weighing.

**Additional narrower path considered.** Confine affirmance to this domestic dispute without adopting a universal rule for foreign maritime cases or choice-of-law displacement. Such narrowing is compatible with the affirming commitments if needed, but no unresolved foreign-law question prevents reaching the presented state-rule question. Conversely, mere inconvenience cannot justify affirming the trial dismissal before resolving whether Louisiana has a duty to employ federal doctrine.

## 28. Liteky v. United States — No. 92-6921

### Neutral preflight and entering-law conclusions

Merits review of the Eleventh Circuit judgment cited at 973 F.2d 910 (1992), affirming convictions under 18 U.S.C. §1361 and rejecting recusal. Argument November 3, 1993; event March 7, 1994. Research cutoff immediately before the event. Campbell is an uncoordinated same-day matter and supplies no entering law. The official predecision facts transmitted in the separate sanitized extract are compatible with the original packet and add no Supreme Court outcome or rationale.

The challenged acts arose in the 1983 Fort Benning protest prosecution of Bourgeois and the 1991 prosecution of these petitioners. The earlier complaints concern keeping argument from becoming a political forum, insisting that openings describe anticipated proof, limiting cross-examination, questioning witnesses, keeping testimony responsive and material, restricting closing argument to evidence already introduced, and a sentence petitioners regarded as excessive. The later judge allowed defendants to state and testify about political purposes, while limiting extended discussion of El Salvador and Government policy; counsel's opening drew a prosecution objection and a direction to discuss expected evidence. The renewed recusal motion added public admonishment concerning that opening and unspecified admonishments of others, including the two pro se codefendants. These allegations must be evaluated together. Neither outside information nor a financial/relational interest is established. A belief that punishment was excessive does not establish bias; no sentence error is independently decided here.

Section 455(a) requires disqualification in any proceeding in which impartiality might reasonably be questioned. Section 455(b)(1) addresses personal bias/prejudice or personal knowledge of disputed evidentiary facts. Section 144 has its own affidavit procedure; Grinnell addressed personal bias under that provision. The Constitution's impartial-tribunal guarantee is not coextensive with every statutory appearance claim. Liljeberg establishes an objective statutory protection and distinguishes violation from remedy; its Rule 60(b)(6) civil reopening procedure is not automatically transplanted into a criminal direct appeal. Grinnell shows why a judge's case-based assessments, even tersely expressed, ordinarily do not establish bias, but the statutory text contains no universal external-source prerequisite.

The opening Standards and Tests rule **Impartiality of balanced bar-dues arbitration panels**, present operation effective Gibson, December 4, 1991, rejects a membership-only facial objection while preserving actual bias, direct interest, delay, and operational ineffectiveness. It supplies no categorical §455(a) source rule. Martin's November 2, 1992 particular-frivolousness determination is likewise not such a rule. The current-term Weiss decision, January 19, 1994, preserves challenges to an intolerable structure without actual bias while finding the military system facially sufficient; its independent constitutional review and safeguards-based rationale reinforce keeping the statutory appearance question separate. No current simulated holding compels disqualification on these incidents.

### Enhanced alternative-path comparison by component

**Interpretive path preserving a judicial-source ordinary rule.** Judicial rulings and opinions formed from the proceedings ordinarily are not grounds for disqualification because adjudication requires the judge to assess evidence and control presentation. Grinnell supplies that distinction. An external source is a significant reason to question whether a view is legitimately judicial, but is neither necessary nor sufficient in itself. Courtroom-origin words or conduct can require disqualification if, viewed objectively and in context, they reveal such deep-seated favoritism or antagonism that fair adjudication appears impossible. This is a formulation of what the conduct reveals, not a requirement to prove a judge's subjective state of mind. Routine impatience, annoyance, limits on irrelevant material, and ordinary adverse rulings do not cross that line.

The strongest objection is that an exceptional-intensity formulation can become a near-actual-bias threshold that shrinks the independent appearance protection enacted in §455(a). The formulation is acceptable to its supporters only if it expressly preserves objective assessment, cumulative context, and judicial-source disqualification. A truly absolute source bar fails the text and cannot account for courtroom conduct that plainly demonstrates prejudgment.

**Interpretive path applying the ordinary appearance standard without an elevated judicial-source threshold.** Apply §455(a)'s reasonable-observer inquiry to all relevant evidence. The source matters to what an informed observer would infer, but it does not create a distinct heightened element. Liljeberg's objective rule, including its refusal to demand proof of actual knowledge, counsels against imposing a substitute for the enacted standard. Grinnell is fully consistent with treating ordinary adjudication as ordinarily insufficient evidence. The strongest objection is that a source-neutral slogan could make every disappointed litigant's account of trial management a recusal claim. The answer requires an informed observer, the full context, the ordinary functions of judging, and an objective basis beyond adverse rulings or subjective offense; the statute is not satisfied by a litigant's dissatisfaction.

**Application and remedy paths.** Affirm under either lawful interpretation because the specified incidents, including the most serious claimed restriction on new material in closing argument, concern ordinary control of relevance and presentation and reveal no reasonable basis for questioning impartiality on this record. Cumulative examination adds context but does not turn several ordinary management decisions into evidence of an inability or apparent unwillingness to adjudicate fairly. The strongest case for vacatur is that both courts used a categorical legal rule and therefore may not have evaluated appearance at all. A remand would be appropriate if the record contained a concrete potentially disqualifying incident whose meaning or factual foundation needed resolution. The complete set of incidents supplied here permits the Court to reject the recusal claim under the proper standard without endorsing the categorical language. Directly ordering a new trial is unsupported: no statutory violation is established, and a remedy inquiry cannot substitute for finding one.
