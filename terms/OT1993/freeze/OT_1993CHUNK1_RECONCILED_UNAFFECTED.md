## 3. Harris v. Forklift Systems — November 9, 1993

**Final common commitment.** Reverse and remand. Under Title VII and Meritor, actionable harassment must be sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter employment conditions, creating an environment that a reasonable person would find hostile or abusive and that the employee actually perceives as abusive. Serious psychological injury is unnecessary. All circumstances matter, including frequency, severity, physical threat or humiliation rather than a merely offensive utterance, and unreasonable interference with work; no listed consideration is independently indispensable. Psychological effects may inform subjective abuse but are not a required injury. Sex-based offense alone does not establish altered employment conditions.

The trial court's close-case determination materially used the erroneous injury standard. Forklift's alternative claim that independent findings sustain judgment does not remove that dependence. Remand permits the proper factual assessment; it does not establish liability or damages. Hicks retains its distinct persuasion and circumstantial-proof rules. The proposed EEOC regulations are not decided.

## 7. United States v. James Daniel Good Real Property — December 13, 1993

**Pre-seizure process.** Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, and Ginsburg affirm the hearing ruling. Absent demonstrated exigency, the Government must give notice and a meaningful opportunity to be heard before taking real property for civil forfeiture. This rule covers real property generally, not only owner-occupied homes. Under Mathews, control and rents are substantial property interests; an adversarial opportunity can expose defenses not answered by probable cause or the prior conviction; immovable land can ordinarily be secured without immediate seizure. To establish exigency the Government must show that less restrictive measures such as a lis pendens, restraining order, or bond would not prevent sale, destruction, or continued unlawful use. No such showing is established here. Criminal forfeiture is reserved.

O'Connor, Scalia, and Thomas would reverse the pre-seizure ruling, but do not share every ground. O'Connor accepts Mathews as a proper framework and considers the warrant, conviction, continued occupancy, and public enforcement interests sufficient on these facts; she also reads Calero-Toledo and the forfeiture tradition as supporting delayed process. Scalia relies principally on historically established sovereign forfeiture procedures and the protection afforded by the warrant, with Gerstein and the traditional in rem cases supporting sufficiency in this context. This is not a proposition that the Fourth Amendment automatically displaces every due-process claim; Soldal remains binding. Thomas's sufficient ground is expressly confined to the conviction, warrant, and absence of physical dispossession on this record. He does not treat the historical fiction as an unlimited justification for the breadth of section 881(a)(7). The last two limitations preserve the frozen narrower positions rather than inventing a collective dissent rationale.

**Administrative timing.** All eight reverse the statutory-timeliness ground. Sections 1602, 1603, and 1604 impose distinct promptness duties; they remain commands, not optional advice. Their breach does not create a dismissal sanction for an action timely under section 1621's five-year period. Brock and Montalvo-Murillo distinguish a mandatory duty from an unexpressed extinguishment of public authority. Separate constitutional delay and otherwise authorized relief remain distinct.

## 8. John Hancock Mutual Life Insurance v. Harris Trust — December 13, 1993

**Reconciled division.** Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Souter, and Ginsburg affirm the fiduciary-status ruling. O'Connor, Kennedy, and Thomas would remand for consideration of the operation of the 1977 amendment under a different construction of the guaranteed-benefit exception. The provisional broad whole-contract exemption for O'Connor and Kennedy is narrowed; Thomas changes from affirmance to that remand. These are reconciliation revisions, not post-freeze circulation changes.

**Affirming rule.** Section 401(b)(2) excludes an insurer's underlying assets only to the extent the policy guarantees the amount of benefits. Components that supply genuine guarantees are distinguished from free funds for which the insurer does not guarantee the aggregate benefit amount. The free funds therefore remain plan assets, and discretionary authority or control over their management supplies fiduciary status under section 3(21)(A). General-account location and the possibility of buying annuities later do not confer a blanket exclusion. The actual contract contains guarantees and a residual floor; it is inaccurate to say it guarantees nothing. A reasonable guaranteed return and contract-fixed conversion protection are significant indicators, not an exhaustive formula that every qualifying contract must reproduce. United Benefit's allocation-of-risk distinction is informative; the ERISA text governs.

**Insurance and agency grounds.** ERISA expressly addresses insurance in this provision; McCarran-Ferguson does not displace that federal direction. Compatible state insurance regulation survives and an actual conflicting state obligation yields. Fabe's different federal-priority conflict remains separate. The 1975 prohibited-transaction bulletin and later litigation interpretation cannot exclude all general-account assets contrary to section 401(b)(2)'s limitation. The affirmers reject the interpretation because it exceeds the statutory range, not because inconsistency or the litigation setting always defeats deference. The general degree of deference owed a litigation position remains open.

**Remanding rule.** The phrase provides for can cover a policy making provision for future guaranteed payments to participants before each amount vests; it need not require a guaranteed aggregate investment return at every stage. But to the extent still limits the exclusion, and not every general-account contract qualifies. The 1977 amendment, already disclosed in the neutral packet, stopped automatic new guarantees and permitted nonguaranteed benefits. Its operation matters under this interpretation, so the proper result is remand for that assessment, not a judicial finding that every free fund is excluded. These three also reject the agency's categorical exclusion and claimed Chevron entitlement; that shared negative proposition does not establish agreement with the affirmers' investment-risk analysis. Potential state-insurance complications and reliance warrant care but do not create an independent McCarran-Ferguson immunity.

## 11. Ratzlaf v. United States — January 11, 1994

**Final division.** Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Ginsburg reverse and remand. Blackmun, O'Connor, and Thomas affirm. Blackmun and Ginsburg change their provisional dispositions after reconciliation; the others retain them. No historical departure survives this reconciliation.

**Reversing rule.** For the charged section 5322(a)/5324(3) offense, the Government must prove knowledge that the structuring was unlawful, beyond knowledge of the bank's reporting duty and an intent to evade that duty. Section 5324 supplies the evasion purpose; the separate willfulness term must receive effect within the reporting scheme. Spies makes willfulness context-dependent, while Cheek and Liparota demonstrate that Congress can require knowledge of legal duty in a particular scheme. The same omnibus penalty term operates in related reporting provisions. Structuring to avoid reporting is not invariably conduct a person necessarily knows is unlawful. The text supplies the rule; if material ambiguity remains, date-eligible lenity principles supply an independently sufficient alternative favoring the accused.

Knowledge can be proved circumstantially; no section-number knowledge, Code reading, or special direct-evidence category is required. This is not a general ignorance-of-law defense, a finding that the funds were unlawful proceeds, or an acquittal. The erroneous instruction requires reversal of the reviewed judgment and remand for lawful further proceedings, retaining docket/count distinctions and any independent grounds. If reproducing the entire section 5322(a) provision, preserve the exception for section 5315 and regulations prescribed under it. Subsections 5324(1) and (2) concern failure to file and inaccurate reports; they are not collapsed into subsection (3)'s structuring offense. Later legislation does not define the charged conduct.

**Affirming rule.** In this statutory context, knowledge of the reporting duty plus intentional structuring for the purpose of evading it supplies willfulness. An omnibus culpability provision need not add a new element to every substantive prohibition, especially one already framed in terms of an evasion purpose. Cheek's tax context and Liparota's concern with broadly innocent conduct do not require the same knowledge element here. Purposeful defeat of a known reporting duty supplies the culpability the text demands. This ground does not impose strict liability, dispense with knowledge of reporting, or punish genuinely separate ordinary transactions merely because each is below a threshold. These three regard the text as sufficiently clear against the additional element; lenity therefore does not change their result.

## 12. Thunder Basin Coal Co. v. Reich — January 19, 1994

**Shared statutory commitment.** All eight affirm the district court's lack of jurisdiction over this anticipatory Mine Act challenge. Congress's intent to place initial review in the administrative channel is fairly discernible from the Act's text, structure, and purposes: citation; a timely contest; independent Commission review; and eventual exclusive appellate review, alongside specifically allocated district-court enforcement authority. The claims concern Mine Act representation/posting duties and can be addressed or preserved for Article III review. This is a scheme-specific determination, not a mandatory three-condition test or a presumption that every administrative statute displaces section 1331. MCorp's express bar supports respecting the actual channel but does not mechanically decide implied preclusion here. Darby forbids extra exhaustion conditions in an otherwise reviewable APA action, not this statute's allocation of original review.

**Six-Justice non-Stone process scope.** Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, and Ginsburg find no demonstrated constitutionally intolerable loss of meaningful review on this record. Compliance has not been shown to cause the asserted serious irreversible organizing injury; particular abuse remains addressable. Noncompliance can cause potentially onerous penalty accrual, but payment follows Commission and appellate review, with expedition and available interim relief materially protecting access. The conclusion is record-specific, not an assurance of immunity from penalties or a holding that every delayed payment makes review adequate. Their statutory analysis permits confirming legislative history, without treating it as an independent source of jurisdiction. They reserve final agency action, cause of action, ripeness, and exhaustion in resolving the statutory preclusion issue.

**Scalia/Thomas qualification.** Both join the statutory text/structure and claim-channel analysis, but not a legislative-history rationale, an unqualified premise of harmless compliance, or reservation of prerequisites that are necessary to decide a distinct constitutional attack on preclusion itself. The district court retains section 1331 jurisdiction for the limited question whether preclusion is unconstitutional and therefore ineffective. That constitutional claim fails here because the operator can comply and still challenge, or obtain judicial review before noncompliance penalties are imposed in the constitutionally material sense; the alternative is sufficient despite possible nonrecoverable compliance costs. Ex parte Young addresses a choice that effectively foreclosed judicial access through severe coercive penalties; this record supplies a review route that avoids that defect. No rule makes every irreparable compliance expense sufficient for a pre-enforcement action.
