## Final supplemental commitment

Blackmun retains the newly modeled, independently sufficient objection that capital punishment **as presently administered** cannot satisfy the Eighth Amendment's combined demands of consistency, individualized fairness and reliability. He would set aside the Sarfaty death sentence even if the specific evidence error were absent. His preferred disposition on this broader ground is reversal of the reviewed judgment insofar as it sustains death, vacatur of the death sentence, and remand for a lawful noncapital disposition through appropriate state proceedings. Guilt and the independent Thompson proceedings remain untouched. No precise noncapital punishment, acquittal, immediate release or relief in Thompson is selected.

The ground is an individual Justice's objection to sustaining death, not an established party-presented categorical claim or a new Court-wide holding within the limited grant. The supplied material does not establish preservation, briefing or inclusion of a general challenge to capital punishment. That uncertainty does not prevent Blackmun from expressing the individual ground, but prevents treating it as an adjudicated party claim or using this supplement alone to expand the Court's question.

The base handoff's shorthand description of the reserved ground as categorical must be read with this qualification: the personal source concerns the administered system and leaves theoretically open procedures that genuinely provide consistency, fairness and reliability, although Blackmun doubts they can be devised. This is not a conclusion that constitutional text forbids death under every imaginable future arrangement. No prior simulated adoption of the position is asserted.

## Individual historical comparison

The official historical Romano report, 512 U.S. 1, 15, contains two explicit choices by Blackmun: agreement with the case-specific responsibility dissent, and an independent wish to vacate death and remand for resentencing because death cannot be fairly imposed within constitutional constraints. His reference there to Callins is evidence of his personal legal basis, not admission of an in-world Callins action. The historical case-specific dissent concludes at page 25 with sentence vacatur and a new sentencing hearing. His historically combining those positions is strong evidence that support for minimum case-specific relief need not surrender his broader objection or its preferred noncapital consequence.

The fresh supplemental model independently reaches that broader objection without receiving Romano's historical outcome or writings. Reconciliation accepts the ground after testing the actual difference in legal setting. The official Callins writing, 510 U.S. 1141, 1143–1159, supplies two distinguishable lines of reasoning: an objection to arbitrary selection even in a system with objective eligibility and full individualized mitigation, and criticism of this Court's asserted retreat from safeguards and review. The first is stated directly at 1151–1155, especially 1152–1153; the latter includes historical accounts of Graham and Johnson that cannot describe this Court's actual decisions.

The actual January 25, 1993 Graham decision granted relief on youth and background and required an effective route for independent culpability mitigation. The June 24, 1993 Johnson decision applied that protection on direct review. Blackmun joined both controlling positions and their permission for adequate capital resentencing or lawful noncapital punishment. Those earlier remedial joins affirmatively show his acceptance then of correction within the system; they cannot be retrospectively relabeled as a general prohibition. They also remove the proposed examples of judicial abandonment of effective mitigation.

That changed premise is substantial, but it does not exhaust his later personal argument. Callins expressly considers objective narrowing followed by broad mitigation and concludes that narrowing eligibility leaves arbitrary selection among the remaining eligible defendants. Effective mitigation answers the loss-of-individualized-consideration problem; it does not, on Blackmun's stated view, remove the separate difficulty in reliably and consistently choosing who dies. His structural objection thus addresses the strongest version of the actual Graham/Johnson framework. This supports the model's retention of the broader ground despite the weakened institutional-retreat account. It is a Justice-specific legal conclusion grounded in his own writing, not an assumption that every historical view survives unchanged or that a favorable mitigation ruling could never affect him.

The public Herrera entry supports continuity of concern about capital review and Blackmun's proposed innocence claim, but supplies no earlier general prohibition. Its actual record-insufficiency disposition leaves the existence and general burden of a freestanding claim unresolved; Callins's broader historical characterization is not imported. Schiro, Victor/Sandoval and the June 6 public update remain limited to the subjects stated in the neutral/model supplements. Nothing in them admits Callins or silently resolves this new individual ground.

**Historical departure:** The supporting explanation is narrowed from the institutional account incorporated through historical Callins: this Court's actual Graham and Johnson decisions enforced the mitigation protection Blackmun supported, so those cases cannot serve as examples of abandoning it. Blackmun nevertheless retains the broader administered-system objection because his own date-eligible Callins argument at 1152–1153 expressly rejects the adequacy of eligibility narrowing combined with full mitigation. There is no departure in his vote to set aside the existing death sentence or in the independently sufficient objection to imposing death under the existing system.

The strongest contrary argument remains that the Constitution contemplates capital punishment, Gregg and Jurek permit regulated systems, and the actual Court has made meaningful corrective intervention. Blackmun's response concerns whether administration can meet the constitutional conditions, not whether the text contains the word death or whether every mitigation rule has been abandoned. The competing Scalia writing in the complete Callins report supplies the textual and doctrinal objection; it does not determine Scalia's present vote in this supplement. The broad argument creates no new finding about Romano's guilt, race, counsel, juror thought, discrimination in this prosecution or Oklahoma's entire system.
