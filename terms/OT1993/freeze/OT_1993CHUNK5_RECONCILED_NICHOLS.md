## A. Four-Associate broad rule and precedent treatment

**O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas:** An uncounseled misdemeanor conviction valid under Scott because no imprisonment was imposed may be used to enhance punishment for a later, counseled offense, including its criminal-history score. A later sentence punishes the new offense rather than retroactively changing the punishment imposed for the misdemeanor. Scott's actual-imprisonment boundary governs the original proceeding; it does not permanently disable its valid judgment as sentencing information. The rule does not validate an originally unconstitutional conviction, a conviction after an invalid waiver where counsel was constitutionally required, or an unlawful scoring method. States remain free to provide broader counsel guarantees by their own constitutions or policy. Historical support: 746–749 and n.12.

The four rely on the traditional breadth of lawful sentencing information, including prior conduct that did not produce a conviction. Williams v. New York supplies the traditional information principle; McMillan supplies the permissibility of the particular sentencing-fact procedure it approved, including proof by a preponderance in that setting; Tucker's general description of sentencing breadth does not erase its separate exclusion of unconstitutional predicates. The Court need not invent proof of the underlying DUI conduct in this case: the actual basis is the valid conviction. Nichols's current counsel and procedural opportunities matter, but no holding permits inaccurate information, denies required procedural safeguards, or extends the rule to every later sentencing-factor controversy. Historical reasoning: 747–748.

Actual Wisconsin v. Mitchell is consistent at its own level: the term-opening public holding permits enhancement of an independently criminal battery for proved intentional racial selection, while preserving relevance, evidentiary and abstract-belief limits. It supplies an illustration of lawful consideration of motive, not a holding on counsel or a blanket authorization for every sentencing fact. The actual entry in `state/holdings/first-amendment.md` was read for this check. No historical counterpart displaces the actual rule.

**Stare decisis:** The four confront Baldasar rather than pretend it never produced a judgment. They would overrule it insofar as it bars later enhancement with a Scott-valid uncounseled misdemeanor. Their grounds are the incoherent relation between its fractured reasoning and Scott's validity boundary, persistent competing lower-court accounts of its reach, and the traditional new-offense rationale. Confusion is a reason for reexamination, not a universal rule that every plurality case may be discarded. Absence of legitimate reliance on committing another crime at a fixed sentencing rate does not alone suffice to overturn precedent. The strongest contrary argument is that Scott's no-jail protection loses practical effect when a valid fine conviction becomes the legal cause of more imprisonment; the four answer that Scott determines the initial counsel entitlement and that the later counseled prosecution supplies a distinct punishment framework. Historical support: 743–749. Payne's and Miller's reexamination references are limited to the reasoning actually invoked; no general new stare-decisis test is adopted.

**Separate warning claim:** Due process does not require a court imposing this Scott-valid misdemeanor conviction to warn that a future conviction may receive an enhanced sentence because of it. The four reject an obligatory forecast of other jurisdictions' and federal sentencing rules; the validity of an old fine conviction does not depend on such a warning. Historical support: 748. This does not decide whether Nichols actually waived counsel, whether any waiver was informed in the legally relevant sense, or whether he was indigent. It is not permission to omit warnings otherwise required for the actual plea or present punishment.

## B. Souter's narrower sufficient ground

**Souter affirms this Guidelines use only.** He regards Baldasar as providing no controlling resolution of the valid-prior automatic-enhancement question because four Justices accepted such use, four rejected it, and Blackmun rejected the premise of original validity. He does not join a general overruling of a rule he thinks never obtained a controlling rationale on that question. Nor does he treat the divided precedent as irrelevant: its opposed reasoning demonstrates a substantial constitutional issue. Historical support: 749–751.

He finds that issue unnecessary to resolve here because the Guidelines use the conviction as presumptive evidence of criminal history, with a genuine opportunity to contest its reliability, relevance, and the category's significant overrepresentation of seriousness or recidivism risk. Section 4A1.3 permits an appropriate downward departure; it does not make the arithmetic point optional, automatically remove the point, or mandate a departure. The opportunity to answer sentencing information is material to his constitutional ground. A defendant may attempt to show that an uncounseled plea reflected expedience, inability to fund a contest, or a poor indicator of actual conduct. That is not a newly created constitutional right to relitigate every final conviction or a decision expanding Custis's ACCA collateral-attack channel. Historical support: 751–754.

The official November 1990 source supports the pertinent scoring, exclusion and departure mechanisms; later publication of the same operative provision in a 1993 manual is not new law entering this case. No actual §4A1.3 departure is invented. Nichols did not present the significant-overrepresentation contention; Souter therefore need not decide whether a particular showing would merit a departure. He reserves a scheme that automatically makes the conviction conclusive and requires enhanced incarceration. His actual Custis dissent seeks a meaningful opportunity to contest invalid predicates; this distinct point concerns how a Scott-valid conviction reflects conduct, and does not force either abandonment of that dissent or broad overruling here.

The strongest objection, maintained by all three dissenters, is that discretionary departure offers an unreliable safeguard against the usual pressures of uncounseled misdemeanor proceedings, leaves the score presumptively binding, and may be unavailable in an ordinary rather than atypical case. Souter accepts the need for real reliability protection but regards the Guidelines framework as sufficient in the first instance. He does not call an unexercised departure harmless-error relief for an otherwise unconstitutional score. Preserve this reason, not merely a narrower verbal formulation of the four's categorical rule.

## C. Common three-Associate dissent; Blackmun's additional premise

**Blackmun, Stevens and Ginsburg:** The uncounseled conviction, accepted as valid to support the fine under Scott, should not furnish the legal basis for additional imprisonment for the later offense. Their concern is the reliability needed before liberty may be taken, not a double-jeopardy theory that the later sentence literally repunishes the DUI. Counsel in the new proceeding cannot recreate the lost assistance that would have tested the prior conviction. A record of conviction that formally increases the Guidelines range differs from independently proving earlier conduct in the counseled sentencing proceeding. Historical common reasoning: 754–765, express Blackmun explanation at 755–756 n.1.

Argersinger and Scott supply the liberty-based counsel protection; Burgett and Tucker show why an uncounseled predicate cannot supply imprisonment when constitutionally unavailable. The dissent would carry their reliability principle to this later use rather than regard the original fine's validity as conclusive for every purpose. Lewis's distinction between a status-based firearms disability and punishment relying on a conviction's reliability remains consistent with that position; Stevens joined Blackmun's Lewis opinion. The three reject using Williams v. New York, McMillan or actual Mitchell as if any had resolved uncounseled-misdemeanor use. They also reject §4A1.3 as a sufficient substitute for the lost counsel, while accepting that independently proved conduct and an appropriate current sentencing proceeding raise different questions. Historical support: 757–764, especially 761–762 n.4.

**Blackmun additionally retains his original-invalidity premise.** His Scott dissent would require appointed counsel for an indigent defendant when more than six months' imprisonment is authorized, or whenever imprisonment is actually imposed. The DUI's one-year exposure meets the first branch if entitlement and lack of valid waiver are established. That rule was his express ground in Baldasar and remains distinct from Scott's controlling rule. He does not falsely label it the Court's 1979 holding. At the present event he also fully endorses the alternative later-use objection even on Scott's premise, as stated above. His actual Parke concurrence's procedural allocation in sentencing neither abandons that substantive objection nor supplies a finding of valid waiver. His Custis dissent is published individual continuity, not authority overriding Custis's controlling channel holding.

**Ginsburg's additional distinction:** Custis decides the available forum for specified collateral attacks on operative state convictions; Nichols seeks to limit the later incarceration consequence of a conviction he does not attack as a fine judgment. Her historical explanation at 765–766 directly confirms that her Custis majority join and this dissent coexist. Actual Custis retains that distinction: its Gideon exception is an unconstitutional complete denial of appointed counsel when entitled and without valid waiver, not a declaration that every uncounseled misdemeanor is invalid. No premise in actual Granderson converts its statutory limit into a counsel holding; it remains useful to show why a real Guidelines increase and an invented alternative sentence cannot be dismissed as immaterial.
