# Cavanaugh v. Roller, No. 92-1510 — recovered predecision materials

This source extract contains only publicly documented information available before November 30, 1993. It supplies no adjudication or proposed disposition. Counsel's statements are attributed as representations or arguments, not converted into findings. Questions at oral argument do not establish any Justice's eventual position.

## Official argument transcript

Source: [Supreme Court official transcript, November 8, 1993](https://www.supremecourt.gov/pdfs/transcripts/1993/92-1510_11-08-1993.pdf). Complete PDF and extracted text: `../sources/chunk1/cavanaugh_92_1510_argument_1993_11_08.pdf` and `.txt`. The transcript's numbered pages 1–48 correspond to PDF pages 2–49. It has been read in full, including both arguments and rebuttal.

- **Pages 3–7:** At the outset, the Court requested discussion of possible mootness raised by a statutory amendment counsel had called to its attention. Petitioners' counsel, Carl N. Lundberg, stated that the law had passed in June but that he, his colleagues, and respondent's counsel became aware of the pertinent provision only the preceding Friday. He identified the retroactive-effect provision in section 266 and the new subsection 16-1-60(B). He represented that Roller had not received a parole hearing in 1993.
- **Pages 5–6 and 17:** Lundberg maintained that the issues were still live on the argument date, but said the new legislation, effective January 1, 1994, would make the hearing-rescheduling issue moot then. On page 17 he expressly conditioned his statement on no intervening legislative change. His suggestion that the legislature might change the provision was a possibility he advanced, not evidence of an enacted reversal.
- **Pages 7–8:** Lundberg described the two presented questions as the ex post facto validity of changing the reconsideration interval from one year to two years and whether the claim belongs under section 1983 or habeas corpus.
- **Pages 11–12, 17–20:** Counsel discussed an intervening South Carolina Supreme Court decision, Griffin. Lundberg represented that the department was scheduling hearings for persons affected by Griffin and acknowledged an obligation to give Roller a 1993 hearing under the outstanding rulings, while describing resource and scheduling problems. Do not treat these representations as proof of the date a later hearing actually occurred. His oral description of the state decision as occurring in August is corrected by the published decision's July 19 decision date and August 17 rehearing-denial date, below.
- **Pages 20–21:** Lundberg stated that Roller's original pleading invoked both constitutions and said he did not know whether Griffin's pleading had done so. He argued that the state decision rested on federal law. That characterization was disputed by respondent's counsel; the state opinion itself is preserved below.
- **Pages 21–22:** Asked whether extraordinary circumstances allowed an earlier hearing, Lundberg answered that the Board was fixed into one- or two-year intervals following rejection and represented that there was no provision for expediting hearings in the Board manual. His answer also distinguished parole revocation, for which the legislature had not prescribed the procedure and the Board set its own. This is an actual recorded representation relevant to interim review; it is not an independently recovered complete Board manual or an adjudicated finding about all possible remedies.
- **Pages 22–26:** Respondent's counsel, W. Gaston Fairey, discussed the legislation and Griffin. He argued that the State's own actions should preclude routine vacatur under Munsingwear and disputed the characterization of the state court as merely compelled by the Fourth Circuit. These are arguments about the effect and remedy of mootness, not a ruling.
- **Pages 36–38:** Fairey maintained that the requested relief was a hearing or opportunity for consideration, not an order of release, and acknowledged the discretionary nature of the ultimate parole decision.
- **Page 44:** Fairey agreed that the case would be moot January 1 if not decided by then, while asking that the Court preserve the lower judgment rather than apply the usual vacatur remedy.
- **Pages 46–48:** Lundberg requested Munsingwear vacatur if mootness occurred before decision; the parties disputed the consequences for the Fourth Circuit judgment and Griffin. No final resolution appears in this argument transcript.

## Official South Carolina enactment

Source: [1993 Act No. 184, H.3151, official legislative history and enacted text](https://www.scstatehouse.gov/sess110_1993-1994/bills/3151.htm). Saved as `../sources/chunk1/sc_1993_act184_bill3151.html` and `.txt`. The official page identifies approval June 21, 1993, after passage June 14 and ratification June 15. Relevant provisions below are transcribed in full, with line wrapping normalized. Sections 8, 266, 268, and 269 were read; the unrelated provisions of this omnibus act are not summarized here.

**Section 8:**

> Section 16-1-60 of the 1976 Code is amended to read:
>
> “Section 16-1-60. (A) For purposes of definition under South Carolina law a violent crime includes the offenses of murder (Section 16-3-10); criminal sexual conduct in the first and second degree (Sections 16-3-652 and 16-3-653); criminal sexual conduct with minors (Section 16-3-655); assault with intent to commit criminal sexual conduct (Section 16-3-656); assault and battery with intent to kill (Section 16-3-620); kidnapping (Section 16-3-910); voluntary manslaughter (Section 16-3-50); armed robbery (Section 16-11-330); drug trafficking as defined in Section 44-53-370(e); arson in the first degree (Section 16-11-110(A)); burglary in the first degree (Section 16-11-311); and burglary in the second degree (Section 16-11-312(B)).
>
> (B) For a person to be considered guilty of a violent crime, the offense must be defined as a violent crime pursuant to subsection (A) at the time of the commission of the crime.”

The enacted-text page displays `16-11-110(A)` for arson; the June 14 Senate Journal conference text displays `16-11-310(A)`. That collateral citation discrepancy is preserved rather than silently repaired. The new subsection (B) and the effective-date language agree.

**Section 266:**

> All proceedings pending and all rights and liabilities existing, acquired, or incurred at the time this act takes effect are saved. The provisions of this act other than Section 16-1-60(B) apply prospectively to crimes and offenses committed after the effective date of the act. The provisions of subsection 16-1-60(B) apply retroactively to all persons convicted under the laws of this State. All sentences pronounced on or after the effective date of this act must comply with the classification system, except where a penalty greater than the one in effect on the date the offense was committed would be required.

**Section 268:**

> Section 267 of this act takes effect July 1, 1993.

**Section 269:**

> This act, other than Sections 267 and 268, takes effect January 1, 1994.

These provisions establish the enactment and its specified dates. The Court must independently assess their application, the savings sentence, and jurisdiction as of the relevant event date. Counsel's agreement about future mootness does not itself adjudicate present mootness.

## Intervening state decision

[Griffin v. State, 315 S.C. 285, 433 S.E.2d 862 (1993), full CAP text](https://static.case.law/sc/315/cases/0285-01.json), preserved as `../sources/chunk1/griffin_315sc285.json`, was read in full. Decided July 19, 1993; rehearing denied August 17, 1993. The court affirmed postconviction relief, adopted the Fourth Circuit's holding in Roller, and overruled Gunter's contrary holding. Griffin's application alleged both state and federal ex post facto clauses. The opinion described the Fourth Circuit's reasoning as compelling, reasoned that a sufficiently intrusive procedural change can substantively affect parole review, and declined to reach the separate voluntariness-of-plea issue. The complete text, rather than either advocate's characterization, is the source for deciding whether it provides an independently sufficient state-law basis.
