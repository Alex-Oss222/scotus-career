## 69. Livadas v. Bradshaw

### Reach, components, and proposed disposition

Argued April 26, 1994. Review concerns the Ninth Circuit's amended 987 F.2d 552 opinion: originally filed September 11, 1991 and amended on rehearing denial March 8, 1993. The federal issue is interference with protected bargaining, not appellate correction of state-law eligibility as such. Each Justice would reach the presented federal issues and reverse the Ninth Circuit judgment, with a bounded remand. That does not reinstate every part of the district court's relief automatically.

| Component | Frozen common proposition and judgment consequence |
|---|---|
| L1 — §301 defense | This independent wage-payment/penalty claim is not preempted merely because calculating the penalty uses the CBA's undisputed wage rate. Resolving the claim requires no disputed contract interpretation. Reject the Commissioner's §301 justification; preserve federal interpretation rules for a genuinely contract-dependent claim. |
| L2 — protected bargaining | A State that offers enforcement of independent wage rights may not withdraw it from an otherwise eligible employee merely because the employee is covered by collectively bargained arbitration terms, where the claim needs no contract interpretation. This policy burdens the exercise of the employee's NLRA-protected collective-bargaining right. The existence of a private wage suit and generalized resource or neutrality rationales do not remove that burden. No general federal duty to operate an enforcement agency is created. |
| L3 — §1983 channel | The definite federal bargaining right, rather than the Supremacy Clause alone, supports this §1983 challenge to state interference. No exclusive remedial arrangement forecloses this challenge: the NLRB's private labor-relations machinery does not provide a substitute remedy for this state policy. This is not a rule making every preemption claim or state-law error actionable under §1983. |
| L4 — relief and matters left open | Reverse and remand for relief consistent with L1–L3 against the official-capacity successor. Preserve prospective declaratory/injunctive relief within ordinary requirements and lawful processing eligibility. Do not award the penalty, decide employer willfulness, settle the effect of reinstatement/backpay, or direct official-capacity monetary payment. The Eleventh Amendment issue and precise lawful relief remain distinct; there is no personal-capacity claim to which an invented immunity analysis could attach. |

The operative state qualifications remain part of this commitment. Section 201 requires immediate payment of earned unpaid wages on discharge. Section 203 requires **willful** failure and continues wages as a penalty until payment or commencement of an action, capped at 30 days; the claim is for three days, not automatic liability from delay alone. Section 219 forbids agreements setting aside these protections but permits more frequent, larger, or earlier payments. The distinct §204.2 rules for designated executive, administrative, and professional employees' pay periods and §227.3 collective-agreement vacation terms are not exceptions in this §201/203 claim. Section 229 permits individual wage actions notwithstanding arbitration, except claims involving a dispute over interpretation or application of a CBA containing arbitration. The Commissioner's broader automatic refusal is an administrative interpretation, not a quoted statutory command. Contract §18.8 itself allows direct wage claims needing no interpretation to use an authorized tribunal or agency.

The agency's court route under §98.3(a) retains financial-inability and valid/enforceable-claim judgments; administrative hearings under §§98(a), 98.1 are distinct. The policy operated across both. Section 218's individual suit and §218.5's fee provision do not establish identical practical access or negate discrimination. Neither this table nor the remand finds every claimant otherwise eligible.

## 70. United States v. Carlton

### Reach, components, and proposed disposition

Argued February 28, 1994. Review is of the Ninth Circuit's August 10, 1992 judgment, 972 F.2d 1051, directing judgment for the executor on the retroactive ownership amendment. Each Justice would reach due process, reverse that judgment, and remand for proceedings consistent with constitutional application of the ownership requirement. The parties narrowed the refund case so that this question resolves entitlement on the challenged ground; no new assessment calculation or refund amount is ordered. Contract Clause and Takings theories were not pursued in the Ninth Circuit and are not decided.

| Component | Frozen common proposition and judgment consequence |
|---|---|
| C1 — statutory temporal reach | Section 10411(b) expressly makes the 1987 amendment effective as if included in §1172 of the 1986 Act. Apply that temporal command subject to constitutional review. *Landgraf*'s presumption does not override it, and later statements of original purpose do not rewrite the original ownership-free text. |
| C2 — constitutional rule and application | The retroactive operation of this economic tax amendment must itself be rationally related to a legitimate legislative purpose. Restricting a newly enacted deduction to securities the decedent owned, and preventing the unintended revenue consequences of the broader deduction during a limited corrective interval, meets that requirement here. Legislative authority to change the deduction is distinct from a claim that ownership was already required. The amendment does not violate due process as applied to this transaction. |
| C3 — reliance, notice, and limits | Specific detrimental reliance and the absence of advance notice of this ownership condition do not by themselves make the application unconstitutional. Accept the stipulated reliance, original eligibility, interim market risk, and $631,000 loss as real. The enactment-to-amendment interval of approximately fourteen months, prompt 1987 notice/proposals, and correction of this recent deduction support the application; this decision sets no universal maximum period and licenses no arbitrary or unlimited lookback. |
| C4 — disposition and distinct qualifications | Reverse and remand; reject the asserted due-process basis for the refund. Decide the ownership condition only. The amendment's allocation requirement, including its bona fide business-transaction substitution exception, is distinct and was not contested as a failure here. Do not reach the abandoned theories or treat the amendment as reopening a completed federal judgment. |

The original deduction permitted 50 percent of qualifying sale proceeds, subject to receipt before the extended estate-return deadline, the qualifying ESOP/cooperative and employer-securities definitions, the exclusions for specified plan distributions or stock-option/right acquisitions, the verified consent concerning §4979A, and the December 31, 1991 sale cutoff. The government concedes original eligibility; none of those conditions supplies a hidden ownership restriction. The amendment separately requires direct ownership immediately before death and allocation to participants or future allocation connected with a §4975 exempt loan or §4980(c)(3) asset transfer. Substituting for already allocated or future-held securities ordinarily fails the allocation condition, except a bona fide business transaction such as an employer merger. That allocation exception does not excuse the estate's admitted ownership failure.

The decedent died September 29, 1985. The estate bought stock December 10, 1986 for $11,206,000 and sold it December 12 for $10,575,000 before the December 29 extended return deadline. The ESOP had expressed interest without a binding purchase agreement at acquisition. January 5, 1987 advance notice was formally published January 26; bills followed February 26; enactment was December 22. No ownership bill was introduced between original passage and the prior October 18 adjournment; the October 22, 1986 enactment cannot be represented as supplying notice of the later condition. The deduction amounts differ across the reported sources: $5,287,500 in the lower stipulated account and $5,287,000 in the official account. The $2,501,161 tax reduction and $631,000 loss are consistent. The disposition does not depend on choosing one inconsistent deduction figure silently.

## 71. Department of Taxation and Finance v. Milhelm Attea & Bros.

### Reach, components, and proposed disposition

Argued March 23, 1994. Each Justice would reach the presented facial federal-preemption question and reverse the June 10, 1993 New York Court of Appeals judgment, 81 N.Y.2d 417, with remand. That court both accepted categorical Indian-Trader-Act preemption and alternatively found the burdens significant; the commitments below address both grounds. The Court does not import a historical earlier Supreme Court remand, adjudicate unimplemented applications, or settle the distinct treaty arguments not decided below.

| Component | Frozen common proposition and judgment consequence |

Tax Law §471(1) excludes cigarettes beyond the State's taxing power and §471(2) places ultimate incidence/liability on the consumer. Licensed agents pre-affix stamps; the identified rate was 56 cents per pack. The evidence that untaxed deliveries would represent 20 and 32 times statewide per-capita consumption in the respective years supports a collection problem; it is not an adjudication of each retailer or purchaser's conduct.

The qualifications are not collapsed. A qualified consumer is an enrolled member of an exempt New York nation or tribe purchasing within a qualified reservation for that individual's own use or consumption within that reservation, not resale. Untaxed wholesale deliveries require a valid exemption certificate, intended distribution to exempt consumers, and reservation delivery. The wholesale buyer shows the certificate first and signs subsequent invoices; individual consumers supply certification on the first purchase and identity evidence later. The tribe may enter a §336.7(c)(1) regulatory agreement with tribal delivery approval. Otherwise, the Department calculates probable demand: evidence submitted by a tribe regulating, licensing, or controlling cigarette distribution, or, absent that arrangement, New York per-capita consumption multiplied by enrollment. Trade territory is determined in consultation with a regulating tribe or from Department information otherwise. Valid orders and quantities reasonably related to exempt demand condition approval; monthly coupons implement allotments. The Department may withhold approval from past or current violators and cancel noncompliers' certificates. Those powers must remain tied to lawful collection and cannot be used to destroy the protected exemption. There is no numerical quota on stamped sales.

The principally invoked §261 grants the Commissioner sole appointment authority and authority over the kind, quantity, and prices of goods sold to Indians. This decision does not deny that text; it distinguishes reasonable burdens incident to collecting a valid nonexempt tax from federal control of protected Indian trade. The complete qualifications of §§262–264 were not reproduced in the packet and no new holding turns on an unprovided exception in them. The official report identifies the 1992 edition of §§336.6–336.7 while the lower reproduction uses §335. The shared described mechanics suffice for this bounded facial ruling; no provision-specific effective-date or numbering dispute is invented or silently resolved.

## 72. Howlett v. Birkdale Shipping Co.

### Reach, components, and proposed disposition

Argued April 20, 1994. Each Justice would reach the turnover-warning question, vacate the Third Circuit's opinionless affirmance, 998 F.2d 1003, and remand for application of the proper duty and summary-judgment standards. No reasoned appellate opinion is invented. The claim is confined to turnover warning; active control during unloading, intervention, and independent stevedore negligence are not alternative claims being decided here.

| Component | Frozen common proposition and judgment consequence |
|---|---|
| H1 — negligence and allocated duties | Section 905(b) permits a vessel-negligence action, not liability from injury, ownership, or the abolished warranty of seaworthiness. Apply *Scindia*: ordinary care for safe turnover to a competent stevedore and warning of qualifying hazards are distinct from the stevedore's primary cargo-work responsibility. A stevedore's negligence is not automatically the vessel's negligence. |
| H2 — knowledge, reasonable care, and cargo inspection | The warning duty covers a hazard known to the vessel or discoverable through its own exercise of reasonable care, likely to be encountered, and not known, obvious, or reasonably anticipated by a competent unloading stevedore. Reasonable care does not ordinarily require supervising the independent stevedore's loading or inspecting its completed cargo stow to discover hazards it created. Contract, positive law, or custom can alter the ordinary allocation. Reject Howlett's proposed general contractor-inspection rule, while preserving knowledge obtained through ordinary vessel operations and any separately established vessel duty; no qualifying contractual, legal, or customary enlargement is found on this record. |
| H3 — relevant obviousness and proof | Visibility to the loading stevedore or crew during loading does not itself establish that the condition was known, obvious, or reasonably anticipated by the competent unloading stevedore at turnover. Conversely, visibility during unloading can defeat a warning claim; Howlett's own adverse witness descriptions must be assessed. Supplying plastic and crew presence are evidence, not conclusive knowledge, and alleged dirt/debris concealment is not a finding. Neither the plaintiff's nor the vessel's disputed factual account is adopted. |
| H4 — bounded vacatur/remand | Vacate and remand because the lower reasoning does not properly preserve those distinctions. Reassess the material knowledge and unloading-visibility evidence under the correct rule. Do not order trial unless a genuine material dispute survives proper summary-judgment analysis; do not find vessel liability or damages. Renewed summary judgment remains possible. |

The material facts retained are specific. Howlett unloaded cocoa for Northern Shipping at Philadelphia. Removing a draft exposed approximately eight square feet of tween-deck; he jumped about three feet from surrounding bags and slipped on clear plastic, which he alleges dirt and debris concealed. Independent Guayaquil loaders placed it. The vessel supplied plastic together with paper, plywood, and dunnage, and crew were present on the upper deck during aspects of loading. The parties agreed paper and plywood were customary condensation protection and plastic was improper because it worsened condensation. None of that establishes the crew's actual awareness of the particular stow hazard or what competent unloaders would perceive when work began or the area became exposed. His witnesses' descriptions of visible plastic during discharge, including from the upper deck, are adverse record evidence, not omitted because inconvenient to the remand.

The material statutory text also bars direct or indirect employer liability to the vessel for §905(b) damages and contrary agreements or warranties. Rejecting a general vessel duty to inspect the foreign stevedore's work does not reintroduce indemnity or seaworthiness. Nor is *Scindia*'s intervention analysis about known equipment problems imported into this turnover-only claim. Section 412's proposed reasonable periodic/after-completion inspection analogy is assessed as actually urged, not recast as a demand for continuous surveillance.
