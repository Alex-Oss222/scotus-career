## What respondents actually addressed

Respondents' own questions at printed p.i frame the matter broadly as the validity of a medical-access time/place/manner injunction, whether overbreadth follows from hypothetical protected applications, and whether the injunction is vague in context. Their formulation is advocacy, not substituted certiorari questions.

**Noise, paragraph (4):** At p.23 respondents expressly defend the prohibition on loud noises heard inside the clinic while medical procedures occur, citing Ward, Grayned and Kovacs. At p.33 they argue that the sights/sounds provision is confined to surgery or recovery, leaving singing, chanting, whistling, shouting, yelling and bullhorn use at other times subject to local nuisance ordinances. At pp.35–36 they expressly describe the prohibition on intrusive sights and sounds as applying only during surgery/recovery hours. At pp.39–41 they defend the provision against overbreadth, discuss harmless detectable noise as a possible hypothetical application, and use Grayned's antinoise rule as an analogy. They therefore address noise independently and within the combined paragraph (4) defense.

**Images, paragraph (4):** At p.24 respondents separately defend the restriction on images observable to patients inside the clinic. They rely on their account of signs naming patients and companions displayed over privacy fencing, assert that the signs could be seen inside, and argue that their placement during medical treatment could traumatize patients. They describe the order as restricting location/circumstances rather than banning the images altogether, invoking Grayned; footnote23 additionally invokes Taxpayers for Vincent and argues that this individualized visual harm justifies a more limited location-based measure. These are respondents' factual/legal assertions, not new findings supplied by this handoff.

At pp.33–34 respondents expressly dispute petitioners' account of a flat ban on singing, chanting and images. At pp.39–40 they acknowledge as a theoretical possibility, while denying it is realistically within the order's operation, that some sign or audible sound could reach patients without harming treatment; they argue the legitimate applications overwhelmingly exceed any protected marginal applications. At pp.40–41 and notes31–32 they answer petitioners' examples of bumper stickers, lapel pins and picket signs, invoking surgery hours, visibility **inside** the clinic, hedges and privacy fences. They suggest narrow construction or case-specific correction rather than complete invalidation. Thus images received a distinct defense; they were not merely an incidental word in a quotation about sound.

**Important printed-text qualification:** The original PDF at p.34 literally says the terms “permit singing, chanting, and images observable during the hours in which surgical procedures or recovery takes place,” then describes current procedures as occurring Wednesdays and Saturdays and denies a flat ban. That sentence conflicts with the brief's own descriptions at pp.23–24,33 and35–36 and with the injunction. It is present in the scan, not merely an OCR error. This supplement does not silently change “during” to “outside,” treat the sentence as an amendment of the order, or turn the brief's two-day operational assertion into a lower-court finding. The actual written injunction retains its stated Monday–Saturday 7:30 a.m.–noon and surgery/recovery qualifications.

## Correction concerning paragraphs (7)–(9)

The earlier source supplement's statement that petitioners did not challenge paragraphs (7), (8) and (9) must **not be used as a statement that their merits brief made no arguments against those provisions**. The completed petitioner-brief reading receipt identifies those arguments, and respondents' own full brief confirms the adversarial dispute:

- At pp.19–20 respondents characterize (3)–(9) as specific means implementing the general prohibitions of (1)–(2), whose legitimacy they say petitioners concede.
- At pp.26–27 n.25 respondents expressly defend the threats, harassment and intimidation portions of (5), (7) and (8), and acknowledge petitioners' objections to those provisions, citing Petitioners' Brief pp.12 and29. They argue threats are unprotected and that context shows fear and medical effects rather than intellectual disagreement.
- At p.33 respondents again specifically defend (5), (7) and (8) as protections from individualized threats and harassment.
- At p.38 respondents state that petitioners seek to engage in activity forbidden by (3)–(9), citing Petitioners' Brief pp.27–30; they argue this presents an as-applied claim rather than a basis for facial overbreadth.
- At pp.39–40 respondents specifically address “intimidating,” “crowding,” “harassing” and “encouraging” violations of the order, citing Petitioners' Brief p.29. They allow arguendo that some such applications could be protected but argue that the order's legitimate coverage dwarfs them. This reaches (9)'s encouragement language, as well as (7)–(8).
- At p.44 respondents answer the contention that the in-concert language is unconstitutional when applied to the asserted nonobjective speech in (3)–(9), citing Petitioners' Brief p.27 n.19. They invoke ordinary injunction practice and necessity for controlling aiders acting with the parties.

The supported factual correction is **arguments were made and answered**. It does not establish that all those challenges were properly presented in the petition, granted, preserved at every earlier stage, or required resolution on the merits. If a later neutral record leaves (7)–(9) undecided, its stated reason must be the independently determined limit on review or need for decision, not the erroneous assertion that no merits challenge existed. The textual provisions and their qualifications remain those reproduced in the earlier source supplement.

Respondents at pp.39 and10 n.15 identify different unchallenged matters: entry on clinic property, obstructing clinic ingress/egress, and identification of persons jamming clinic telephone lines. Their concession account cannot be expanded to (7)–(9), which they expressly discuss as contested.

## Other boundaries material to a reach inquiry

Respondents' overbreadth discussion at pp.37–45 distinguishes their asserted as-applied challenge from third-party facial invalidation and addresses standing to contest the in-concert clause at p.43 n.34. At p.45 n.36 they argue alternatively for appellate modification rather than total invalidation even if substantial overbreadth is found. Their vagueness discussion at pp.45–49 invokes contextual fair warning, chilling effects and enforcement discretion, giving examples concerning crosswalk speed, approaching persons, counseling, residences, agents, and threatening behavior. At p.48 n.37 they again favor narrowing construction or modification over invalidating the whole order. This is a general defense of the disputed terms; it should not be described as a separately developed answer to every conceivable noise/images vagueness theory.

The precise supported briefing statement is: petitioners made the identified noise/images breadth/vagueness arguments, and respondents expressly defended **both** noise and images through conduct/content, tailoring, alternatives and overbreadth arguments, accompanied by a general vagueness defense. The sources do not justify the broader assertion that both sides fully briefed every possible subissue or that briefing alone settled reviewability. Respondents did not separately discuss Rule14 or the “fairly included” formula in the full brief read here. Their own reformulated questions and merits discussion do not supply a judicial enlargement of the petition questions.
