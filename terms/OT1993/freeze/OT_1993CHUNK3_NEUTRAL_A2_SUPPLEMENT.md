## Fogerty — validated record and scope

The Ninth Circuit affirmed three different district-court rulings: striking stale counterclaim allegations; summary judgment rejecting rescission and dependent infringement counterclaims; and denial of the successful infringement defendant's fees. Only the last ruling is within this packet's Supreme Court question. A correction of the fee standard neither reopens the jury's defense verdict nor restores Fogerty's contract counterclaims.

The lower court found neither frivolousness nor bad faith. Its rule was disjunctive: either could permit a defendant's award. The Ninth Circuit rejected Fogerty's argument that the trial court had mistakenly required both. It also held that the evidence did not compel a bad-faith finding. Those findings must not be silently changed merely because the prevailing-defendant legal prerequisite is under review. Fogerty requested evenhanded treatment below; the supplied neutral packet additionally identifies his automatic-award alternative at Supreme Court review. The latter is treated as a supplied litigation position, not falsely attributed to the lower opinion.

The exact operative text of 17 U.S.C. §505 is:

> In any civil action under this title, the court in its discretion may allow the recovery of full costs by or against any party other than the United States or an officer thereof. Except as otherwise provided by this title, the court may also award a reasonable attorney's fee to the prevailing party as part of the costs.

The costs sentence, the fee sentence and the title's other limitations are distinct. Neither the federal-party exclusion nor the meaning of full costs is litigated here. No holding about §412 or any other unpresented limitation is needed. Permissive recovery is not a mandatory award.

Surviving pre-divergence authority includes Christiansburg Garment Co. v. EEOC, 434 U.S. 412 (1978), whose Title VII rule allows a prevailing defendant's fees for a frivolous, unreasonable or groundless action, including one continued after becoming so, without requiring subjective bad faith. Mere defeat is insufficient. That rule follows Title VII's enforcement structure; whether to carry it to copyright remains the question. Blackmun did not participate in Christiansburg and must not be described as joining it. Hensley v. Eckerhart, 461 U.S. 424 (1983), separates prevailing status from a reasonable fee and relates compensation to successful legal work. Its special civil-rights policies do not independently establish a §505 presumption.

Entering simulated standards: **No contingency enhancement under the environmental fee provisions**, present operation effective Dague, June 24, 1992, excludes contingency-risk enhancements under 42 U.S.C. §6972(e) and 33 U.S.C. §1365(d), with ordinary lodestar proof and no double counting; it leaves differently worded schemes and other listed limits undecided. **Nominal civil-rights relief and reasonable fees**, present operation effective Farrar, December 14, 1992, establishes eligibility from enforceable nominal relief while requiring an explained Hensley assessment of actual success and unrelated unsuccessful work; zero remains possible without mechanical damages proportionality. Neither rule decides plaintiff/defendant parity under §505.

## Campbell — corrections and complete scope

**Release and permission chronology is disputed.** The neutral packet's statement that release followed a request and refusal is too definite. The Sixth Circuit majority used a June 1989 release; the district court had used July 15. Linda Fine's request was dated July 5. The lower opinion expressly records the parties' disagreement and their different supporting materials. The refusal letter and continued distribution are supported. This supplement makes no finding about the disputed initial sequence and draws no bad-faith inference from it. A request or refusal does not alone determine statutory fair use.

The suit included state interference claims. The district court found them preempted under §301, and Acuff-Rose did not challenge that determination. The district court also denied leave to add a Lanham Act claim; that denial was not challenged. They are outside this copyright fair-use review. The deposited funds, calculated by reference to royalty rates and returned by the district court, do not establish a concession of infringement or an adjudicated royalty obligation. The record reports a claimed possibility of sound sampling, not a conclusive sampling finding; no separate sound-recording infringement holding is supplied.

The Sixth Circuit reversed the defendants' summary judgment and concluded that the use was not fair. Its majority accepted parody for the analysis while expressing reservations in a footnote. It made commerciality decisive in the first factor, treated borrowing the musical core as adverse, and presumed market harm. Nelson would affirm the district court: he considered the new work an evident criticism of the original's romantic narrative, distinguished transformation from copying for the original's purpose, and credited the unrebutted different-audience evidence. His account of what the song criticizes is a lower-court dissenting position; this stage did not listen to authenticated audio or inspect the original affidavits independently.

The lower record supports two distinct market concerns. Acuff-Rose had earned income from covers and other derivatives; its licensing affidavits also discussed licensing parodies. Defense affidavits addressed the original song's audience and popularity. Their assertions cannot be enlarged into conclusive proof about every possible derivative market. A market for licenses to criticism itself and a substitutive market for an ordinary rap adaptation are analytically distinct. The actual existence, likelihood and impairment of the latter must be assessed on evidence; the neutral packet's identification of it is an unresolved application issue, not an established finding of injury.

Section 107 directs a case-specific inquiry, with nonexclusive examples of purposes and four required considerations: purpose and character including commercial or nonprofit educational nature; nature of the copyrighted work; amount and substantiality used in relation to that work as a whole; and effect on its potential market or value. The original work is published and creative. Artistic character supplies protection but does not itself eliminate parody from fair use. Meaningful comparison of music and lyrics remains needed when assessing how much borrowing the criticism reasonably requires.

The operative first sentence exempts fair use notwithstanding §§106 and 106A and includes reproduction or other means specified by that section. The current text also provides: “The fact that a work is unpublished shall not itself bar a finding of fair use if such finding is made upon consideration of all the above factors.” Congress added that last sentence on October 24, 1992, Pub. L. 102-492, 106 Stat. 3145. The published composition here does not turn on it; it prevents an overbroad statement of current entering law based only on the earlier Harper & Row wording. No retrospective unpublished-work dispute is decided.

Sony Corp. v. Universal City Studios, 464 U.S. 417 (1984), protects the statutory balance and expressly rejects a wholly determinative productive/unproductive-use dichotomy; its commerciality and harm language arose in examining reproduction and noncommercial home time shifting. Harper & Row v. Nation Enterprises, 471 U.S. 539 (1985), requires all four factors and treats fair use as an affirmative defense. It protects against substitution for a copyright owner's valuable market, including derivative markets, while rejecting a categorical public-figure exception. The commercial and market passages are material contrary authority, not language to omit. The choice is whether those passages require a presumption for every sold parody or permit examination of the new work's actual critical function and substitutive effect.
