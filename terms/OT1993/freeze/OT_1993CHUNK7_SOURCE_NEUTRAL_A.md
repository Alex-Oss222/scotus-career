## 73. Simmons v. South Carolina

### Posture, participation and material record

No. 92-9059, certiorari to the South Carolina Supreme Court, 310 S.C. 439, 427 S.E.2d 175 (1993). The state appeal was heard October 13, 1992; decided February 1, 1993; rehearing denied March 10, 1993. Supreme Court argument was January 18, 1994. All nine members of this Court participate; no additional nonparticipation is established. The event is scheduled for June 17, 1994. [S1–S2]

The reviewed state judgment upheld convictions for the July 17, 1990 murder of Josie Lamb, first-degree burglary and petit larceny, with death, a consecutive life sentence and thirty days respectively. The prior assaults against elderly women occurred in August 1989, May 1990 and June 1990. During the week before the murder trial, Simmons pleaded guilty to first-degree burglary and two criminal-sexual-conduct counts arising from other attacks. His prior violent-crime convictions and separate sentencing, followed by this conviction, made him ineligible for parole under the repeat-violent-offender provision discussed below. The single statutory aggravating circumstance submitted at the murder sentencing was first-degree burglary. Evidence of other attacks also appeared at guilt to prove intent; a state dissent questioned that use. That guilt-stage controversy is not an additional federal question established by this parole-information review. [S1, pp. 156–160; S2]

Simmons presented childhood abuse, mental-disorder and adaptation evidence. Witnesses described his particular danger to elderly women. Defense jail witnesses and an expert supported good prison adjustment or low risk outside that victim class. That is evidence to evaluate, not a conclusive finding that no person in prison could face danger. Outside the jury's presence, attorneys from the correctional and parole departments testified that Simmons would not be eligible for parole. The State did not contest that eligibility conclusion. Questions about work release and furlough were also explored; department regulations prohibited those programs for a life-without-parole prisoner, and no such convicted murderer was known to have been furloughed. The regulation-based release restrictions must not be attributed to the text of §24-21-640 itself. [S1, pp. 156–160, record descriptions across the report]

The trial court barred references to parole in voir dire, evidence before the jury and argument. Simmons requested a parole-ineligibility instruction and, alternatively, an instruction explaining life as imprisonment for his actual natural life. He also sought to restrict the State's future-danger argument. The judge allowed the State to argue dangerous propensity but prohibited implying a period of confinement shorter than the law provided. The State used a future-danger and community-protection theme. The context matters: its statement that the defendant was in the community answered mitigation about his origins; its self-defense theme came at the end of a closing that disclaimed anger, emotional catharsis, reaction to a child's testimony and reaction to gruesome facts as the basis for sentencing. The prosecutor did not expressly tell the jury that Simmons would be paroled. Whether those arguments nonetheless created a materially incomplete account of the sentencing alternatives remains a question for adjudication. [S1–S2]

After about ninety minutes the jury asked: “Does the imposition of a life sentence carry with it the possibility of parole?” The court told it not to consider parole and to understand life imprisonment and death in their plain and ordinary meaning. About twenty-five minutes later the jury returned its penalty verdict. A survey admitted without objection before trial reported that only 7.1 percent of respondents firmly understood life as the remainder of life; almost half expected parole within twenty years, almost three-quarters within thirty, and over three-quarters regarded parole availability as important. The survey is not proof of any actual juror's thoughts. [S1–S2]

### State law, including qualifications and chronology

The operative ground was repeat-violent-offender parole ineligibility, not a rule that every South Carolina life sentence was without parole. Section 24-21-640 (Supp. 1993), as quoted in the judicial record, provides:

> The board must not grant parole nor is parole authorized to any prisoner serving a sentence for a second or subsequent conviction, following a separate sentencing from a prior conviction, for violent crimes as defined in Section 16-1-60.

The older state opinion quotes “following a separate sentencing **for** a prior conviction.” This minor quotation variant is reported rather than silently resolved. The material condition is a subsequent conviction following separate sentencing. **McKay v. State**, 300 S.C. 113, 386 S.E.2d 623 (1989), quotes the further proviso:

> Provided that where more than one included offense shall be committed within a one-day period or pursuant to one continuous course of conduct, such multiple offenses shall be treated for purposes of this section as one offense.

McKay assigns the eligibility/continuous-course determination to the parole board, rather than treating every sentencing-court characterization as binding on the board. The quoted exception must accompany any reusable description of the repeat-offender rule. The separately dated attacks here and the uncontested department testimony are the record basis for ineligibility; this supplement does not independently adjudicate a continuous-course question. First-degree burglary, first- and second-degree criminal sexual conduct and murder were qualifying violent crimes under the provision applicable to this record. [S1–S3]

South Carolina Act No. 184, approved June 21, 1993, H.3151, is a later **offense-classification** enactment, not a parole-instruction enactment. Section 8 states §16-1-60 as follows:

> (A) For purposes of definition under South Carolina law a violent crime includes the offenses of murder (Section 16-3-10); criminal sexual conduct in the first and second degree (Sections 16-3-652 and 16-3-653); criminal sexual conduct with minors (Section 16-3-655); assault with intent to commit criminal sexual conduct (Section 16-3-656); assault and battery with intent to kill (Section 16-3-620); kidnapping (Section 16-3-910); voluntary manslaughter (Section 16-3-50); armed robbery (Section 16-11-330); drug trafficking as defined in Section 44-53-370(e); arson in the first degree (Section 16-11-110(A)); burglary in the first degree (Section 16-11-311); and burglary in the second degree (Section 16-11-312(B)).
>
> (B) For a person to be considered guilty of a violent crime, the offense must be defined as a violent crime pursuant to subsection (A) at the time of the commission of the crime.

The complete material savings and effective-date provisions state:

> SECTION 266. All proceedings pending and all rights and liabilities existing, acquired, or incurred at the time this act takes effect are saved. The provisions of this act other than Section 16-1-60(B) apply prospectively to crimes and offenses committed after the effective date of the act. The provisions of subsection 16-1-60(B) apply retroactively to all persons convicted under the laws of this State. All sentences pronounced on or after the effective date of this act must comply with the classification system, except where a penalty greater than the one in effect on the date the offense was committed would be required.
>
> SECTION 268. Section 267 of this act takes effect July 1, 1993.
>
> SECTION 269. This act, other than Sections 267 and 268, takes effect January 1, 1994.

Section 267 concerns the legislative criminal-law study committee. Do not collapse enactment and effective dates, or the retrospective subsection (B) exception and the general prospective rule. The pertinent official Act text is available in S4; this supplement does not represent a full independent legislative-history search. [S4]

The Court's actual **Cavanaugh v. Roller** public decision of November 30, 1993, restored annual parole reconsideration for Roller's 1982 offenses under the earlier law. Its §1983 holding concerns a better opportunity for release without an entitlement to release, and its ex post facto holding concerns the loss of alternate mandatory annual reconsideration under §§24-21-645 and -650. It did not hold all South Carolina prisoners eligible for parole, displace §24-21-640's separate repeat-violent-offender bar, or decide all consequences of Act 184 after January 1, 1994. At that November decision, the relevant new Act provisions were not yet effective. Cavanaugh's chronology and reservation must be preserved; the historical disposition of that matter supplies no substitute rule. [S5]

### Preserved theories and live alternatives

The record presents both due-process rebuttal and Eighth Amendment mitigation/reliability theories. The reconstructed neutral question's focus on due process should not erase the Eighth Amendment theory. The parties' exact petition questions were not independently authenticated; no broader grant is asserted. [S1–S2]

Simmons argues that the State's use of future dangerousness creates a right to answer with the legally fixed noncapital alternative; the jurors' specific inquiry makes a generic life/death instruction inadequate. He invokes the opportunity to rebut adverse sentencing information under **Gardner**, the admissibility of prison-adaptation mitigation under **Skipper/Lockett/Eddings**, and accurate capital choice under **Beck**. The narrower rebuttal theory can be considered separately from a rule requiring parole instructions in every capital prosecution.

The State invokes **California v. Ramos**, 463 U.S. 992 (1983), and traditional state authority over sentencing information: it made no explicit parole assertion, danger might exist inside prison, both parties were subject to the parole restriction, and a direction not to speculate could suffice. Clemency or future legal change raises a distinct objection to describing imprisonment as literally certain until death; it does not establish eligibility for ordinary parole. A precise instruction about present parole law and the requested natural-life formulation are not identical alternatives. These arguments remain open for independent evaluation.

Relief concerns the death-sustaining portion of the judgment and further penalty proceedings; neither the presented information claim nor the requested relief establishes innocence, acquittal, release or invalidity of the separate convictions. Whether a new hearing must be ordered immediately, or any constitutional harmless-error inquiry remains available, requires an independently supported remedial determination. No automatic remedial conclusion is imported from the later history of this matter.

### Date-eligible writings and actual earlier public law

**Skipper v. South Carolina**, 476 U.S. 1 (1986), is a fully read earlier source. White's opinion, joined by Brennan, Marshall, Blackmun, Stevens and O'Connor, treats good prison conduct and adaptability as relevant mitigation under Lockett/Eddings; on that record independent jail-witness testimony was not merely cumulative of defendant/family testimony. Its footnote 1 additionally identifies due-process rebuttal when the prosecution relies on future danger. Powell, joined by Burger and Rehnquist, concurred in the judgment on the narrower due-process response to the prosecution's express predicted prison misconduct, while resisting a general extension of mitigation beyond character, record and offense. The former Justices' positions are authority, not votes attributed to the current roster. The distinction between factual adaptation evidence, legal sentencing alternatives and a responsive prosecutorial theme remains to be tested. [S6]

The actual earlier **Romano v. Oklahoma**, June 13, 1994, is supplied by the bounded public-authority projection, not by that matter's historical opinion. Souter's responsibility holding, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens and Ginsburg, forbids the State to present an irrelevant death sentence imposed by another jury in circumstances substantially inviting the present jury to treat its own death choice as already made elsewhere. It extends Caldwell beyond an inaccurate statement of legal responsibility, requires more than speculative influence, preserves independently admissible prior-conduct evidence and leaves focused corrective instructions open in another record. The same five hold that removal of an aggravator and reweighing do not themselves resolve that responsibility error. They vacate the death-sustaining appellate judgment and remand, without a controlling choice between an immediate new penalty hearing and a first constitutional harmlessness inquiry. No independently sufficient ordinary-due-process holding or general evidence code was adopted. [S7]

The public Romano writings give individualized contrary paths. Souter, Blackmun, Stevens and Ginsburg require a new penalty hearing if death is pursued on that record; Blackmun also states his broader objection to capital punishment and preference for noncapital relief. O'Connor, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, limits Caldwell to inaccurate or misleading descriptions of legal sentencing responsibility and finds no fundamental unfairness under Donnelly/Darden; this dissent does not declare that truth or general instructions cure every defect. Stone's public separate remedy position is distinct from current-chunk instructions. Romano involved affirmative disclosure of another jury's death selection; Simmons concerns exclusion of accurate information about this jury's noncapital alternative. Neither resemblance nor distinction mechanically determines a vote. Skipper supplies an additional prior direct join by Blackmun, Stevens and O'Connor; Romano supplies current public writings or joins for each current non-Stone Justice.

## 74. MCI Telecommunications Corp. v. AT&T

### Posture and participation

No. 93-356 and consolidated No. 93-521, United States et al. v. AT&T; certiorari to the D.C. Circuit. Argument March 21, 1994; event June 17, 1994. **O'Connor took no part in consideration or decision.** No reason is supplied. The participating Court is Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg. The packet's roster-only language must yield to this case-specific qualification. [M1, participation notation]

The judgment directly reviewed is the D.C. Circuit's unpublished summary reversal of the FCC's November 1992 permissive-detariffing rule, **7 FCC Rcd 8072**, based on its earlier published decision, **AT&T v. FCC**, 978 F.2d 727 (D.C. Cir. 1992). The rule's operation had been stayed at **7 FCC Rcd 7989**. The 978 F.2d decision is a material antecedent, not the unpublished judgment itself. Its panel was Wald, Silberman and Sentelle; Silberman wrote; decision November 13, 1992, rehearing denied January 21, 1993. The unpublished order's full standalone text and precise issuance date were not independently recovered. [M1–M3]

The FCC began distinguishing dominant carriers possessing market power and nondominant carriers in 1980; AT&T was the only dominant carrier in this record. The Second Report (1982) made filing optional for resellers, the Fourth (1983) extended that treatment to specialized common carriers including MCI, and the Fifth (1984) broadened the nondominant class. The Sixth (1985) prohibited nondominant filings. The challenged later rule makes filing optional, **not prohibited**. Sections 201 and 202's substantive just-and-reasonable and nondiscrimination obligations remain, as do complaint processes and potential changes of regulatory classification. [M1–M3]

On August 7, 1989, AT&T filed a §208 complaint alleging MCI charged unfiled rates and seeking damages and cease-and-desist relief. The FCC's January 28, 1992 disposition treated the Fourth Report as a substantive rule and declined the requested relief while directing future rulemaking. The 978 F.2d court held that prospective rulemaking did not justify failing to decide the complaint, and rejected the permissive exemption under §203; it remanded for appropriate relief without itself computing damages or issuing AT&T's requested specific cease-and-desist command. The rule-review matter here does not independently quantify those damages. [M3]

### Complete §203 text and surrounding provisions

The official 1994 Code prints §203 with amendment history through September 28, 1990. Thus its relevant text precedes the cutoff. Do not use the older ninety-day notice language quoted in the 1985 case. The operative notice ceiling is **120 days**. [M4, printed p. 39]

> **(a) Filing; public display.** Every common carrier, except connecting carriers, shall, within such reasonable time as the Commission shall designate, file with the Commission and print and keep open for public inspection schedules showing all charges for itself and its connecting carriers for interstate and foreign wire or radio communication between the different points on its own system, and between points on its own system and points on the system of its connecting carriers or points on the system of any other carrier subject to this chapter when a through route has been established, whether such charges are joint or separate, and showing the classifications, practices, and regulations affecting such charges. Such schedules shall contain such other information, and be printed in such form, and be posted and kept open for public inspection in such places, as the Commission may by regulation require, and each such schedule shall give notice of its effective date; and such common carrier shall furnish such schedules to each of its connecting carriers, and such connecting carriers shall keep such schedules open for inspection in such public places as the Commission may require.
>
> **(b) Changes in schedule; discretion of Commission to modify requirements.** (1) No change shall be made in the charges, classifications, regulations, or practices which have been so filed and published except after one hundred and twenty days notice to the Commission and to the public, which shall be published in such form and contain such information as the Commission may by regulations prescribe.
>
> (2) The Commission may, in its discretion and for good cause shown, modify any requirement made by or under the authority of this section either in particular instances or by general order applicable to special circumstances or conditions except that the Commission may not require the notice period specified in paragraph (1) to be more than one hundred and twenty days.
>
> **(c) Overcharges and rebates.** No carrier, unless otherwise provided by or under authority of this chapter, shall engage or participate in such communication unless schedules have been filed and published in accordance with the provisions of this chapter and with the regulations made thereunder; and no carrier shall (1) charge, demand, collect, or receive a greater or less or different compensation for such communication, or for any service in connection therewith, between the points named in any such schedule than the charges specified in the schedule then in effect, or (2) refund or remit by any means or device any portion of the charges so specified, or (3) extend to any person any privileges or facilities in such communication, or employ or enforce any classifications, regulations, or practices affecting such charges, except as specified in such schedule.
>
> **(d) Rejection or refusal.** The Commission may reject and refuse to file any schedule entered for filing which does not provide and give lawful notice of its effective date. Any schedule so rejected by the Commission shall be void and its use shall be unlawful.
>
> **(e) Penalty for violations.** In case of failure or refusal on the part of any carrier to comply with the provisions of this section or of any regulation or order made by the Commission thereunder, such carrier shall forfeit to the United States the sum of $6,000 for each such offense, and $300 for each and every day of the continuance of such offense.

The connecting-carrier exception in (a), good-cause and special-circumstances language in (b)(2), and otherwise-authorized proviso in (c) are distinct. Section 201(b) requires just and reasonable charges, practices, classifications and regulations; §202(a) forbids unjust or unreasonable discrimination and undue or unreasonable preferences or disadvantages. Sections 206–208 provide liability, choice of damages forum and Commission complaint/investigation machinery. Their survival is part of the FCC's argument that nondominant rates remain controlled; it does not settle whether Congress required an additional filed-tariff mechanism. [M1–M4]

### Competing arguments and narrower paths

MCI and the federal petitioners read “modify any requirement,” the general-order authority and “special circumstances or conditions” to allow a class-based exemption for carriers without market power. They emphasize the unchanged substantive rate duties, reimposition of filing if conditions change, permissive rather than mandatory nonfiling, and competitive harm from costly filing and public advance price information. The FCC reports a market growing from twelve carriers in 1982 to 482 in 1992, with nondominant firms together providing approximately forty percent; those aggregate figures are agency-reported context, not a finding that an individual nondominant carrier possesses market power. They seek deference to an expert agency's construction within actual statutory delegation. [M1–M3]

AT&T emphasizes “every,” “shall,” the expressly named connecting-carrier exception, filed-rate enforcement and customer information, and the distinction between modifying a filing requirement and eliminating its obligatory force for a large class. It contests treating optional filing as a minor change merely because the substantive duties continue. The dictionary dispute must be assessed with the full section and Act, rather than treated as a freestanding choice of one definition. The FCC's statutory-authority explanation must be the agency's own; policy desirability does not itself enact a delegation. Conversely, economic importance alone is not a free-standing 1994 major-questions test. [M1–M3]

Later enactments offer competing contextual inferences: the Telephone Operator Consumer Services Improvement Act of 1990, §226(h), separately provided informational tariffs and a phaseout mechanism, while the 1993 mobile-services provisions in §332(c)(1)(A) expressly permit specified forbearance but preserve identified substantive duties. Neither legislative inaction nor an inference from those different provisions should be represented as an express enactment ratifying or prohibiting this precise FCC rule. If an independently sufficient construction of those provisions is proposed, retrieve their full operative text first; they are contextual arguments here. [M1]

Available merits paths include holding the optional rule outside §203's delegation, or sustaining a reasonable construction that treats nondominance as a qualifying special condition. The Court may distinguish permissive from compulsory detariffing or limit its analysis to the statutory architecture without announcing a general ban on substantial administrative change. No judgment here should silently award unproved damages in the separate complaint.

### Date-eligible individualized sources

It rejected the Sixth Report's compulsory nonfiling regime as inconsistent with the mandatory statute. Crucially, it expressly reserved the validity of the earlier **permissive** orders, including the possible enforcement-discretion distinction associated with Heckler v. Chaney. Its discussion of limited modification authority is relevant to the present statutory challenge, but the reserved permissive issue is not a prior holding by Ginsburg resolving this case. It also uses the then-operative ninety-day notice version; the amended current text above governs. [M2, especially pp. 1195–1196 and n.5]

Kennedy's dissent, joined by O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas, finds the employer-interest limitation inconsistent with the statute while reserving other grounds. These sources give concrete writings or joins for all participating non-Stone Justices. They do not establish universal deference or universal hostility to regulation, and they do not repeal Chevron. ## 75. West Lynn Creamery, Inc. v. Healy

### Posture, participation and procedural limits

No. 93-141; certiorari to the Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court, **415 Mass. 8, 611 N.E.2d 239 (1993)**. The state case was heard January 6, 1993 and decided April 15, 1993. Supreme Court argument March 2, 1994; event June 17, 1994. All nine members participate; no additional nonparticipation is established. [W1–W2]

Only the **amended February 26, 1992 pricing order** is under review; the original February 18 order is not the operative challenged instrument. The dealers filed their constitutional action July 24, 1992 seeking declaratory relief, damages and preliminary/permanent injunctions. Preliminary relief was denied July 31, renewed August 7 and denied August 14. Following September 14 administrative hearings, the department conditionally revoked licenses November 16 for nonpayment; separate review actions followed November 18. Further preliminary relief/stays were denied November 24–25, then an appellate single justice stayed revocation November 25. A December 1 extension was conditioned on payment of specified past premiums; the cases were reported and consolidated in December. The SJC sustained the order's constitutionality and remanded for Superior Court action consistent with its opinion. This was not an adjudicated federal refund award. The federal disposition must identify the judgment and relief it actually reaches; it should not invent a refund amount, restitution from farmers or a final damages judgment. [W2]

### Material operation of the order and findings

Massachusetts General Laws c.94A, §§10–12, supply the state pricing authority identified below; §§6–7 concern license consequences and §§8 and 21 judicial review. Federal milk marketing regulation under the Agricultural Marketing Agreement Act and 7 C.F.R. pt.1001 establishes minimum, not maximum, prices. Class I is fluid milk; other classes cover other uses. Dealers pay class-dependent prices into the federal system, and producers receive the applicable blend price. The Commonwealth's program uses that federal blend as its calculation baseline. [W1–W2]

Every licensed milk dealer selling Class I milk to Massachusetts retailers owes the same assessment regardless of where the milk was produced. The dealer definition includes persons receiving, purchasing, pasteurizing, bottling, processing, distributing or otherwise handling milk and specified consignor/agent, producer-dealer, dealer-retailer and subdealer roles. A producer produces milk from dairy cattle. West Lynn obtains approximately 97 percent of its milk from outside Massachusetts, processes it and sells about sixty percent within Massachusetts; LeComte purchases its milk from West Lynn and sells at retail. Roughly two-thirds of Massachusetts milk consumption comes from outside the State. These estimates do not make the charge facially unequal at the dealer-collection step. [W1–W2]

The monthly premium per hundredweight equals **one-third of the difference between $15 per hundredweight and the federal blend price**, multiplied by the dealer's covered sales measured in corresponding hundredweight units. A hundredweight is 100 pounds; do not multiply a per-hundredweight premium by unconverted pounds. All premiums go to the Dairy Equalization Fund. Monthly distributions go only to Massachusetts producers, proportionately to their preceding month's production, with these qualifications:

- No producer receives credit for more than **200,000 pounds per month**.
- No payment may bring the producer's return, including the federal minimum and fund payment, above **$15 per hundredweight**.
- Surplus funds are returned to dealers.

These caps limit the program and must accompany any account of its production incentives. The assessment and distribution are components of one designated fund; the State neither purchases nor sells the assessed milk. The legal payors and recipients occupy different stages of the market. The claims about combined market effects should be analyzed with those facts intact. [W1, pp. 188–192; W2]

The January 28, 1992 emergency declaration reported the federal blend price declining from $14.67 per hundredweight in 1990 to $12.64 in 1991 against estimated average production costs of $15.50. It described severe local-farm losses and risk of losing about half the remaining farms within a year; maintaining a local fresh-milk supply and open land were asserted benefits. The department found production had declined after the order. The SJC treated predictions of increased local output as speculative in view of the payment cap, herd-expansion expense and short duration. Those findings do not prove that insulating otherwise declining local production has no competitive effect; equally, no inevitable future production increase may be manufactured as a record fact. [W2]

### Competing characterizations and remedies

The dealers attack the integrated program: an even collection rule can burden interstate production differently when its proceeds return exclusively to competing local farmers. They argue that preserving local market share or offsetting local costs can be protectionist even if sales volumes do not expand, the direct tax recipient is a dealer and local consumers bear part of the incidence. Baldwin's external minimum-price barrier, Bacchus's local-product exemption and New Energy's discriminatory tax credit are offered as analogies. A ruling on this designated fund need not determine the validity of every appropriation for local industry. [W1–W2]

The Commissioner characterizes the collection as origin-neutral and the distributions as local subsidies of a kind governments commonly make; local dealers and consumers pay, dealers retain the incentive to buy the least expensive milk, and the capped emergency support stabilizes an endangered local supply without a demonstrated excessive interstate burden. The State invokes the local-producer minimum-price arrangement in **Milk Control Board v. Eisenberg Farm Products**, 306 U.S. 346 (1939), and the distinction between ordinary subsidy and discriminatory taxation. The SJC assumed without deciding standing to assert the producers' competitive objections, and relied on both its evenhanded characterization and Pike analysis. Do not turn that assumption into a separate adjudicated Supreme Court standing holding. [W2]

The decisive classifications remain open: whether the whole linked arrangement discriminates, whether a nondiscriminatory-tax/direct-subsidy distinction answers this arrangement, and whether the asserted benefits justify the applicable burden. If discrimination is found, the eligible strict justification rule differs from Pike's test for genuinely evenhanded regulation. If a narrower invalidity is found, relief can address the February 26 order and remand without invalidating all subsidies, all milk-price regulation, or the federal minimum-price program. Recovery of particular payments and state administrative consequences require the appropriate further proceedings rather than an invented immediate mandate.

### Earlier eligible writings and actual public decisions

**New Energy Co. of Indiana v. Limbach**, 486 U.S. 269 (1988), is fully read. Scalia wrote for a unanimous Court, including Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Kennedy. It invalidates unequal fuel-tax treatment based on ethanol origin/reciprocity; limited numbers of beneficiaries and harmed competitors do not defeat patent discrimination. Tax assessment is governmental regulation rather than proprietary purchase merely because it subsidizes industry. It distinguishes ordinary direct subsidization, leaves legitimate purposes without adequate reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives available as a demanding justification, and does not decide the legality of every direct subsidy. That ordinary-subsidy distinction supplies a substantial argument to confront as well as the discriminatory-tax rule. [W3]

The actual **Oregon Waste**, April 4, 1994, opinion by Thomas was joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg. It applies origin-discrimination scrutiny and requires substantially equivalent events for a compensatory-tax defense; general local tax contributions do not themselves supply the necessary matching event. Blackmun's dissent gives weight to a comprehensive environmental program and cost-directed statutory design against a facial challenge without finding exact incremental costs or proprietary status. [W4]

The actual **Carbone**, May 16, 1994, opinion by Kennedy was joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Thomas and Ginsburg. It treats the reservation of a presently private processing market as discriminatory notwithstanding exclusion of other local processors and a public financing objective. O'Connor concurs in the judgment under Pike while rejecting that discrimination classification. Souter, joined by Blackmun, dissents on the municipal-service characterization and insufficient proof of a clearly excessive interstate burden, without categorical proprietary immunity. This is a concrete prior disagreement about classifying an integrated public program, not an automatic answer about the dairy fund. [W4]

The actual **Associated Industries v. Lohman**, May 23, 1994, opinion by Thomas was joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg; Blackmun concurred in the judgment without a separate opinion. It requires comparing equivalent transactions in the relevant locality rather than masking a local discriminatory rate by statewide averaging. Do not invent a Blackmun rationale from his unexplained judgment agreement. The three current public cases and New Energy provide writings/joins for every current non-Stone Justice while preserving their distinct grounds. [W4]
