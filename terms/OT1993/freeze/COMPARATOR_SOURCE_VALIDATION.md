## Findings by matter

1. **In re Day, 510 U.S. 1 (October 12, 1993).** Eight dockets: 92-8788, 92-8792, 92-8888, 92-8905, 92-8906, 92-9018, 92-9101, 93-5430. The per curiam denies leave to proceed in forma pauperis, allows until November 2 to pay fees and submit Rule 33 petitions, and imposes prospective fee/form restrictions on noncriminal certiorari petitions. It recites 27 earlier petitions and a June 1993 Rule 39.8 refusal. Its description of current petitions as demonstrably frivolous is the historical Court's adjudicative characterization, not a substitute for the missing petition texts. Stevens alone dissents: he would deny the petitions without reaching the fee motions and announces that, absent exceptional circumstances, he will no longer note disagreement with similar orders. That announced practice matters when assessing silence in later orders. No financial affidavit or substantive petition grounds are reproduced.

2. **In re Sassower, 510 U.S. 4 (October 12, 1993).** Ten dockets: 92-8933, 92-8934, 92-9228, 93-5045, 93-5127, 93-5128, 93-5129, 93-5252, 93-5358, 93-5596. Thomas and Ginsburg take no part only in No. 93-5252; no reason is supplied. The order denies fee relief, permits the November 2 fee/form cure, and restricts future noncriminal certiorari petitions and extraordinary-writ petitions. Criminal sanctions remain outside that restriction. It recites eleven earlier filings in three years, ten pending in four months, and no previous Rule 39.8 refusal. No dissent is recorded; that is not an individual affirmative poll. Its current-frivolousness characterization cannot establish missing petition grounds or financial eligibility.

3. **Harris v. Forklift Systems, Inc., 510 U.S. 17 (November 9, 1993), argued October 13.** Unanimous reversal and remand, O'Connor for the Court. Scalia and Ginsburg each write separately and each joins the Court's opinion in full. Objective severe-or-pervasive discriminatory hostility and subjective perception of abuse are both required; psychological injury is not. All circumstances matter, including frequency, severity, physical threat or humiliation rather than a mere offensive utterance, and unreasonable interference with work. No listed factor is mandatory. Psychological effects may be relevant to subjective abuse. The erroneous legal standard requires reconsideration of this close case, not a final liability determination. The proposed EEOC regulations are not adjudicated. Scalia does not adopt a mandatory productivity-loss element; Ginsburg's broader equality observations remain separate reasoning.

4. **Florence County School District Four v. Carter, 510 U.S. 7 (November 9, 1993), argued October 6.** Unanimous affirmance, O'Connor for the Court, no separate writing. The public placement's inadequacy and private placement's appropriateness are settled. Section 1401(a)(18)'s four requirements concern public expense/supervision/direction and no charge; state educational standards; appropriate preschool, elementary, or secondary education; and conformity with an individualized education program. A parent's unilateral private placement need not satisfy the public-school requirements, but must be appropriate. Parents act at financial risk; reimbursement remains equitable, and unreasonable cost may limit full reimbursement. The absence of an approved-school list in South Carolina is not an essential condition of the rule.

5. **Izumi Seimitsu Kogyo Kabushiki Kaisha v. U.S. Philips Corp., 510 U.S. 27 (November 30, 1993), argued October 12.** Certiorari dismissed as improvidently granted, per curiam; Stevens dissents, joined by Blackmun (7–2 disposition). The denial-of-intervention question was not presented as Rule 14.1(a) requires. A Rule 14.1(a) presumption is prudential, strong, and subject to exceptional circumstances; an antecedent issue is not invariably fairly included. A denied intervenor may seek review of that denial under Scofield. The majority does not decide routine settlement vacatur. The dissent would reach intervention and reject routine vacatur, emphasizing public and third-party interests. Any use of post-divergence Yee, Williams, or Cardinal Chemical as entering law must consult the simulated authority rather than automatically importing their historical forms.

6. **Cavanaugh v. Roller, 510 U.S. 42 (November 30, 1993), argued November 8.** One-sentence dismissal as improvidently granted; no reasons, separate writing, or individual roll is supplied. No merits doctrine follows from it. The judgment below, Roller v. Cavanaugh, 984 F.2d 120, is preserved at `sources-neutral/Roller-below.txt` and originates at `https://static.case.law/f2d/984/cases/0120-01.json`. The 1982 crimes preceded the March 25, 1983 sentence. Former section 24-21-620 supplied annual reconsideration; the 1986 enactment supplied biennial reconsideration for violent offenders without changing initial eligibility. After a 1990 denial, the 1992 hearing occurred and parole was again denied. The prospective section 1983 claim remained distinct from an unappealed damages ruling and from release through habeas corpus. The mandate called for declaratory relief and, if necessary, injunctive relief. The opinion does not establish the absence of every possible interim review mechanism. Do not infer mootness or a Supreme Court dismissal rationale from the later hearing.

7. **United States v. James Daniel Good Real Property, 510 U.S. 43 (December 13, 1993), argued October 6.** Kennedy writes for all nine in Parts I and III and for Kennedy, Blackmun, Stevens, Souter, and Ginsburg in Parts II and IV. Rehnquist's separate opinion is joined throughout by Scalia; O'Connor joins only Parts II and III of that separate opinion. O'Connor and Thomas also write separately. O'Connor accepts the suitability of Mathews but disputes its application. Thomas's separate rationale includes the conviction, warrant, lack of physical dispossession, and concerns over the forfeiture statute's scope. The notice/hearing rule concerns real property generally, not solely owner-occupied homes. Exigency requires showing that less restrictive measures, such as a lis pendens, restraining order, or bond, would not prevent sale, destruction, or continued unlawful use. Criminal forfeiture is reserved. Good had already rented the premises; seizure diverted the tenants' $900 monthly rent. Sections 1602, 1603, and 1604 impose distinct promptness duties, but their breach does not create a dismissal remedy where section 1621's five-year limit is satisfied. Promptness is not optional. The judgment affirms in part, reverses in part, and remands; it neither awards damages/accounting nor automatically dismisses the forfeiture.

8. **John Hancock Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Harris Trust & Savings Bank, 510 U.S. 86 (December 13, 1993), argued October 12.** Affirmed 6–3. Ginsburg is joined by Rehnquist, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, and Souter. Thomas dissents with O'Connor and Kennedy; the dissent seeks remand to assess the 1977 amendment's actual operation. The guaranteed-benefit exception applies to policy components genuinely guaranteeing the amount of aggregate benefits, not categorically to all general-account assets. Guaranteed reasonable return and contract-fixed conversion terms are significant indicators, not an exhaustive formula every qualifying contract must copy. The old minimum floor does not itself guarantee the present return on free funds. Previously guaranteed benefits are distinguished from free funds. Plan-asset status does not resolve breach, causation, or remedy. ERISA's express relation to insurance defeats the asserted McCarran-Ferguson displacement; actual conflicts yield to federal law. The majority finds the agency construction beyond the text's permissible range and reserves the general deference owed agency litigation positions; inconsistency alone is not a universal bar to deference. The dissent also rejects categorical exclusion of all general-account assets and rejects the agency's claimed Chevron deference (page 118 note 5); do not equate that overlap with adoption of the majority's risk analysis. A 1994 law-review citation appears in the printed report; commentary is not independent in-world authority for the December 1993 event.

9. **Tennessee v. Middlebrooks, 510 U.S. 124 (December 13, 1993), argued November 1.** Dismissed as improvidently granted; Blackmun notes a dissent without explanation. The report supplies no jurisdictional or merits rationale. The relevant state opinion, 840 S.W.2d 317, is at `sources-neutral/Middlebrooks-below.txt`, from `https://static.case.law/sw2d/840/cases/0317-01.json`. Its operative Part III expressly grounds the narrowing requirement in article I, section 16 of the Tennessee Constitution as well as the Eighth Amendment, explains why Lowenfield does not supply Tennessee's constitutional rationale, and overrules State v. Smith and progeny on the duplication point. The guilt judgment remains intact; another valid aggravator can support seeking death again. The torture aggravator was supported, but the error was not harmless beyond a reasonable doubt, requiring resentencing. An adequate-and-independent-state-ground determination requires legal analysis of this language, not merely counting state citations; it is not the historical Supreme Court's published reason for dismissal.

10. **Burden v. Zant, 510 U.S. 132 (January 10, 1994).** No argument. The per curiam grants in forma pauperis leave and certiorari, reverses, and remands; no dissent or individual roll is recorded. The court of appeals attributed a nonexistent factual finding to the district court. The remand calls for analysis of actual conflict and adverse effect under Sullivan on the correct record; it does not establish an automatic constitutional violation, order release, or resolve every section 2254(d) exception. The earlier Supreme Court remand, 498 U.S. 433, was decided **February 19, 1991**, not January 22. The full lower-court majority, dissent, and notes were read in `sources-neutral/Burden-below.txt`.

11. **Ratzlaf v. United States, 510 U.S. 135 (January 11, 1994), argued November 1.** Reversed and remanded, 5–4. Ginsburg is joined by Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter; Blackmun dissents with Rehnquist, O'Connor, and Thomas. The caption includes the wife as co-petitioner. For the charged sections 5322(a)/5324(3) offense, the Government must prove knowledge that the structuring was unlawful, beyond knowledge of the bank's reporting duty and an intent to evade it; circumstantial proof remains permissible. This is statute-specific, not a general ignorance-of-law defense. If reciting the complete section 5322(a) penalty provision, preserve its exception for section 5315 and regulations under that section. Section 5324(1) and (2) address failure to file and inaccurate reports; section 5324(3) addresses structuring. The 1992 recodification and international-transfer addition do not alter the charged conduct's law. The Court orders remand, not acquittal. Lenity supplies an alternative if ambiguity remains; the majority first finds the text sufficient. Do not introduce later harmless-error doctrine.

12. **Thunder Basin Coal Co. v. Reich, 510 U.S. 200 (January 19, 1994), argued October 5.** All nine affirm. Blackmun is joined throughout by Rehnquist, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, and Ginsburg. Scalia and Thomas join except Parts III-B, IV, and V; Scalia's separate concurrence is joined by Thomas. The question is whether preclusion is fairly discernible from the review scheme, considering meaningful review; the discussion is not three mechanically indispensable conditions. A mine operator may contest within thirty days; an ALJ decision becomes final after forty days if unreviewed; expedited review/interim relief and eventual appellate review are material. Penalties may accrue while contested, and potentially onerous accrual is acknowledged, even though payment awaits review. The majority finds no present deprivation on this record and leaves the union/NLRA merits unresolved. Finality, cause of action, ripeness, and exhaustion are reserved. Scalia and Thomas do not accept an unqualified harmless-compliance premise: due process depends on a means to comply and challenge, or to obtain review before sanctions constitutionally coerce compliance; district court jurisdiction remains for testing the constitutionality of preclusion itself. The separate writing occupies pages 219–221, not 219–222.
