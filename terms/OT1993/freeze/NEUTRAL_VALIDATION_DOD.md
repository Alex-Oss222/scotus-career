## Neutral corrections and distinctions required downstream

1. The Navy request concerns the Navy Exchange at Gulfport, Mississippi. The Army and Air Force Exchange Service is headquartered in Dallas, but the request in No. 90-4775 concerns bargaining-unit employees at the Post Exchange at Lowry Air Force Base, Colorado. Headquarters must not be mistaken for the requested employees' work location. This specificity is supported by the judgment below and changes no legal path.
2. Necessity under §7114(b)(4)(B) is expressly uncontested; the judgment below records the agencies' concession. The availability of workplace communications therefore cannot become a new ruling that the data were unnecessary. Reasonable availability likewise is not disputed in the supplied Supreme Court-stage facts. The case concerns the separate limitation that furnishing the data must not be prohibited by law.
3. FOIA Exemption 6 removes an obligation to disclose under FOIA when its requirements are met. It is not itself the prohibition on these disclosures. The Privacy Act's §552a(b) supplies that prohibition for covered records absent written request or prior written consent of the subject or an applicable enumerated exception. The asserted exception here, §552a(b)(2), turns on disclosure being required under FOIA.
4. The separate §552a(b)(3) routine-use exception was argued below but not reached there; the sanitized Supreme Court-stage handoff states it is not pressed here. The earlier 1989 Treasury case did resolve a routine-use issue under its own notices and record. That resolution must not be imported into this case. Written consent is a separate route and is not the asserted basis of the present orders.
5. Exemption 6 requires a disclosure that would constitute a clearly unwarranted invasion of privacy. Exemption 7(C), involved in Reporters Committee, concerns qualifying law-enforcement material and uses the different, broader privacy language could reasonably be expected to constitute an unwarranted invasion. Carrying across the kind of public interest relevant to FOIA does not erase those differences.
6. The Fifth Circuit's conclusion that these address disclosures would not meaningfully expose agency action is a conclusion about this record. It is not statutory language or a rule that no personnel information, address, or contact lead can ever contribute to public understanding.
7. No adjudicated finding establishes harassment, unwanted visits, hostility toward unions, or why a particular employee did not provide an address. The absence of those findings does not settle whether disclosure itself invades an interest in controlling dissemination of one's residence. Possible later contacts must remain risks or arguments rather than historical facts.
8. The strongest enforcement path is the Fifth Circuit's statutory-context argument: Congress declared collective bargaining in the public interest, imposed fair representation of nonmembers, and made necessary-data access part of that scheme. It claims a statute-specific disclosure entitlement, not merely favored treatment because the requester is a union. The contrary path follows the unmodified cross-references and ordinary FOIA public-interest inquiry. Both paths belong in independent modeling.
9. Ray supplies a concrete Exemption 6 balance and preserves possible derivative public benefits; its controlling rule does not require wrongdoing evidence. Scalia and Kennedy's narrower position on derivative investigation is individual continuity, not the holding. Thomas was not a participant. The facts of Ray cannot be treated as a requirement that every privacy interest involve equivalent sensitive interviews or assurances of confidentiality.
