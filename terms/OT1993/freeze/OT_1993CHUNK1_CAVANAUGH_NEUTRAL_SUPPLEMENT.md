## Material corrections and additions

The original packet's statement that no later governing-rule development was established is superseded. Before the proposed November 30 event, an official enactment and an intervening South Carolina decision had materially changed the setting for prospective relief. Neither establishes that Roller had already received his 1993 hearing or that the controversy was presently moot.

1. **The 1993 enactment.** South Carolina Act No. 184, H.3151, was approved June 21, 1993. Section 8 adds a crime-date condition to the definition of a violent crime. Sections 266 and 269 make that condition retroactive when the relevant part of the act takes effect January 1, 1994. Enactment and operative dates are distinct. Section 266 also saves pending proceedings and existing rights and liabilities. The act is not a present November 30 repeal of the challenged hearing rule.
2. **Griffin.** Griffin v. State, 315 S.C. 285, 433 S.E.2d 862, was decided July 19, 1993; rehearing was denied August 17. The argument's description of a decision in August conflates these dates. Griffin adopted the Fourth Circuit's Roller holding and overruled Gunter's contrary holding. This is an intervening state-court decision, not a decision of the simulated Supreme Court.
3. **Uncompleted 1993 consideration.** At argument, petitioners' counsel represented that Roller had not received a hearing in 1993. Counsel acknowledged the State's obligation under the outstanding rulings to provide one and described efforts to schedule the affected prisoners, together with resource constraints. An undertaking to comply does not prove completed compliance.
4. **The parties' future-mootness positions.** Petitioners' counsel maintained that the case remained live at argument but that the legislation would moot the hearing claim on January 1, absent another legislative change. Respondent's counsel agreed that the case would be moot then if undecided, while opposing routine vacatur. These are recorded positions about future legal consequences, not jurisdictional findings binding the Court.
5. **Interim hearing authority.** Petitioners' counsel represented that the Board was fixed into annual or biennial intervals after denial and that its manual supplied no expedited-hearing provision. The answer separately distinguished revocation, for which the Board prescribed its own procedure. This is stronger record support than silence about an intervening hearing, but it is counsel's representation; the complete manual has not been recovered.
6. **Petitioners' merits position is broader than the lower-court description.** At argument counsel maintained that the Ex Post Facto Clause did not apply to parole-eligibility or reconsideration changes, even in the longer-interval hypotheticals posed, and alternatively defended the one-to-two-year change as preserving a meaningful opportunity for parole. The Fourth Circuit had described a concession that a seven-year increase would be substantive. Preserve the distinction between that lower-court account and the position actually advanced at this argument; do not erase either.

## Updated neutral paths and limits

**Retain review and resolve the live controversy.** The strongest ground is that the new statute has not taken effect, Roller still lacked a 1993 hearing when counsel last addressed the matter, and a direction enforcing annual consideration could supply effectual relief. Church of Scientology, as adopted November 16, 1992, makes meaningful partial relief sufficient. The national legal disagreement identified below also persists. The strongest objection is prudential: the enactment, Griffin, and the officials' acknowledged annual-hearing obligation have substantially altered this case as a vehicle for resolving continuing statewide enforcement of the biennial rule.

**Dismiss the writ as improvidently granted without deciding mootness.** Discretionary review need not produce constitutional or §1983 holdings after material developments undermine the vehicle. This course leaves the Fourth Circuit's judgment and its instructions for declaratory and, if necessary, injunctive relief undisturbed. Its strongest counterargument is that the case remains capable of yielding present relief and has already been fully argued. The availability of present relief defeats a premature mootness holding, but does not itself require the Court to retain discretionary review. A dismissal cannot be represented as endorsing the Fourth Circuit's merits, declaring the January 1 legal effect, or ordering a new remand.

**Present mootness followed by Munsingwear vacatur.** This path lacks an established present mooting event. January 1 has not arrived; actual receipt of the outstanding hearing or release has not been verified; damages are not preserved to keep the case alive, but their absence does not eliminate existing prospective relief. Munsingwear addresses a controversy that has become moot, not an anticipated future change. The parties' separate dispute about responsibility for mootness and preservation of the judgment would also need resolution. Karcher shows that vacatur does not automatically follow every end of appellate review. No later vacatur doctrine is imported.

**Hold for the statute's effective date.** This is a procedural option, not present mootness and not a terminal event. If chosen, the matter must remain open pending a verified dated action, including review of the savings clause and any effectual relief that remains. No future hearing, repeal, order, or automatic dismissal may be invented to close it.

**Conditional merits landscape, without frozen merits votes.** If review is retained, the preserved §1983 dispute concerns an opportunity for discretionary consideration, not a judicial order of release. Preiser, Patsy, and the lower court's account supply the competing remedial positions; the immunity and damages rulings are outside this appeal. On ex post facto merits, the hearing obligation and ultimate parole discretion remain distinct. Weaver and Miller support scrutiny of increased punishment despite an unchanged maximum sentence; Collins and Dobbert preserve the distinction between punishment and genuinely procedural alterations. The recorded lack of an expedited-hearing mechanism matters to actual opportunity, but is not a finding that release would have occurred. Later Morales/Garner formulations are excluded. The procedural commitments do not silently decide these questions.
