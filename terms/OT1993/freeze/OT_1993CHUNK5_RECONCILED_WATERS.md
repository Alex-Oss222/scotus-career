## Component A — objective factual care, three full-framework Associates

**O'Connor, Souter and Ginsburg:** The public-employer speech balance may use a genuinely held, reasonably reached account even if later adjudication shows that account mistaken. When discipline is based on supposed speech and a reasonable supervisor would recognize a substantial likelihood that the actual speech was protected, the employer must use the care a reasonable manager would use for that employment decision in that setting. Good faith is necessary but is not sufficient. Strong contrary evidence known to be available cannot simply be replaced by an unsupported or extremely weak account. The rule demands neither a trial, judicial evidence rules, confrontation, an interview of every witness, nor perfect accuracy. Multiple reasonable investigative choices may exist. Historical rule: 677–678; the earlier discussion at 675–677 explains the legitimacy of ordinary managerial credibility judgments and hearsay.

Their constitutional basis is protection against erroneous suppression of employee speech within the balance applicable to government as employer. It is not a property-based entitlement to a hearing and does not transplant Health Care's agency-law analysis. Pickering supplies the balance; Connick gives substantial weight to reasonable prospective disruption assessments while making the speech's public value and context important; Givhan keeps private expression within the protection and permits attention to manner/time/place. Mt. Healthy did not decide mistaken accounts and therefore does not foreclose this rule; it still supplies the distinct causal inquiry and same-decision defense.

**Application:** The completed process here falls within the reasonable range if the managers genuinely relied on the reports. Hopper's opportunity to hear Churchill, corroborating reports and repeated checks support that legal assessment. It does not require an appellate credibility determination that Ballew or Perkins-Graham told the truth. The clean allegations of bias and retaliation remain relevant to actual belief and motive; no established knowledge of bias changes the objective application. A new holding about a separately pleaded interim injury, a universal post-termination cure, or every manager's independent process duty is not supplied.

The reported discouragement of a transfer, attack on supervisory authority and claimed impossibility of repairing the working relationship support a sufficient prediction of disruption on that account. The test concerns the speech for which the employee was actually disciplined. An unprotected disruptive portion does not become immune merely because it is surrounded by protected discussion. Conversely, a permissible portion does not justify discharge actually caused by protected patient-care criticism. A factfinder could conclude that management acted because of nondisruptive cross-training comments believed to have occurred in this conversation or earlier statements. That record-supported dispute prevents defendants' summary judgment. Historical support: 680–682.

## Component B — genuine ground, three Associates refusing objective-inquiry duty

**Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas:** The First Amendment prohibits discharge in retaliation for protected speech. A genuine adverse decision based on an account that describes constitutionally disciplinable speech does not violate that right merely because the account was mistaken. They refuse a distinct constitutional investigation requirement. Selective or implausible investigation may prove pretext, but absence of reasonable inquiry is not itself their constitutional wrong. Honest belief does not make legally protected speech disciplinable; the legal balance and the actual reason remain judicially reviewable. Historical evidence: express joins at 686, substantive grounds 686–694.

Their central contrary argument is that responsible factual care protects against chilled speech. Their answer is that Pickering, Perry and Connick protect against retaliation; Mt. Healthy already permits testing actual causation and requires the employer to prove it would have made the same decision without protected conduct when that defense is raised. Recognizing a new inquiry right here is unnecessary because the case requires the same pretext remand even on the objective-inquiry approach. They object to uncertainty over triggers, procedures, decisionmaker and remedy and the lack of an established hearing entitlement for this at-will employee. Scalia's own Rankin dissent supports institutional-function concerns without waiting for actual breakdown, but it is not falsely presented as already deciding mistaken reports. Kennedy and Thomas's position is confirmed by their exact same-case historical joins because no changed relevant premise has been established.

They support further proceedings to decide whether the asserted permissible report was genuinely the basis for the action. They do not endorse an automatic employer victory upon claiming honest belief, an actual-fact finding about the conversation, or final immunity for any defendant.

## Component C — actual protected speech, two Associates

**Blackmun and Stevens:** Affirm the appellate reversal and remand. The First Amendment protection depends on the speech actually uttered and its causal connection to discharge; an employer's reasonable factual mistake does not extinguish the substantive right. At this summary-judgment stage Churchill's supported account must be taken favorably to her rather than displaced by competing witness accounts. On that account, patient-care policy criticism without the reported personal attack is protected for purposes of reviewing the employer's motion. That is a procedural assumption, not a trial finding. Historical support: 694–699, especially 695 n.1.

Their verified Connick dissent and Rankin majority joins protect the public significance of employee criticism while preserving institutional balancing. Their Mt. Healthy and Givhan joins preserve causation, private speech protection and actual same-decision proof; Stevens's Givhan concurrence reinforces the distinction between a potentially valid reason and a finding it actually would have produced discharge. Here they regard the disputed speech itself as the admitted cause, rather than assume an established unrelated ground that would independently produce dismissal (699 n.6). They therefore do not require bad faith as an added element of the actual-speech claim. Do not narrow their position to intentional pretext only.

The strongest objection is the burden of responsible factual mistakes on public management. They answer that the substantive balance already protects legitimate efficiency interests and that personal damages immunity addresses some mistake risks without shrinking the right. Burnup & Sims supplies an analogy concerning protected labor activity and mistaken reports, not a holding that the NLRA directly governs this constitutional claim. They agree specifically that disciplining an employee on an **unreasonable, incorrect report of actually protected speech** violates the First Amendment (697–698 n.4). That agreement does not make them full-framework supporters of the opposing reasonable-error safe harbor. They would not order relief without adjudication of the disputed material facts and applicable defenses.

## Souter's additional explanation and majority boundaries

Souter fully supports component A and emphasizes genuine belief as an independent requirement. Even a reasonable investigation cannot protect an employer who does not actually credit the report or uses it to conceal punishment for other protected speech. Genuine doubt about the asserted disciplinable portion may bear on that inquiry. He also explains that the employer must believe its selected discipline appropriate rather than excessive, while recognizing no separate pretext-of-excessiveness claim was raised here (684 n.*). This personal explanation does not add a Court-wide mildest-sanction requirement or decide an unpresented claim; Scalia's contrary Rankin position remains intact.
