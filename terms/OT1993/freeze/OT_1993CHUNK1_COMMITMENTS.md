## 3. Harris v. Forklift Systems — November 9, 1993

**Reach and sufficient ground.** Reach the preserved Title VII standard under §1254(1). Serious psychological injury is not an element. Harassment must be sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter employment conditions and create an environment objectively hostile or abusive, and the employee must actually perceive it as abusive. Consider the circumstances as a whole: frequency, severity, physical threat or humiliation as opposed to a merely offensive utterance, and unreasonable interference with work. Psychological effects are relevant evidence when present, not a prerequisite; no single listed consideration is indispensable. Sex-based offense alone is not enough. The ultimate question remains discriminatory alteration of employment conditions.

**Remedy.** Reverse the affirmance and remand for application of the correct standard. The trial court's close-case assessment materially used the wrong injury requirement; this Court cannot find the error harmless by making new factual findings. No liability finding or damages award. Hicks leaves the ordinary persuasion allocation intact and is not a replacement hostile-environment test.

**Alternative test.** Affirmance despite the erroneous component would be lawful if independent findings conclusively resolved objective and subjective abuse under the correct rule. Its strongest support is Forklift's claim that the result rested on the entire environment. It fails on the packet's express dependence on psychological well-being and close-case characterization. Conversely, remand cannot presume liability simply because several incidents are offensive. Meritor, 477 U.S. 57, supplies severe-or-pervasive alteration rather than an injury threshold.

## 7. United States v. James Daniel Good Real Property — December 13, 1993

**Component I, pre-seizure process.** Path A affirms the hearing ruling: absent demonstrated exigent circumstances, the Government must provide notice and a meaningful opportunity to be heard before taking control of real property for civil forfeiture. Mathews weighs the substantial possessory/rental interest, risk and value of an adversarial opportunity, and the Government's need. Doehr demonstrates that even interference short of eviction matters. Calero-Toledo's movable property and exceptional need do not automatically justify this real-property seizure. The Government bears the showing that less intrusive measures, such as notice of lis pendens, restraining orders or bond, cannot protect its legitimate interest. No exigency is established here. An ex parte probable-cause warrant and an earlier conviction do not resolve every forfeiture defense.

**Strongest counterargument and Path B.** Traditional sovereign in rem seizure, unlike a private creditor's attachment, historically precedes the hearing and secures forfeiture jurisdiction and public enforcement. A judicial warrant and the drug conviction reduce error. The narrower rejection of Path A would sustain the seizure in this setting under Calero-Toledo and its historical practice, not hold all procedural due process displaced by the Fourth Amendment. Soldal forecloses that latter automatic displacement. Against Path B, land cannot abscond, enforcement was delayed for years, and rents and control were taken notwithstanding the tenants' continued presence.

**Component II, administrative timing.** All eight would reverse the statutory-dismissal ruling. Brock and Montalvo-Murillo distinguish a mandatory command from an unstated loss of government power. The strongest contrary argument is that a command requires an effective remedy; it does not establish this particular remedy, especially where §1621 separately governs time to sue. Constitutional delay and any otherwise authorized remedy remain separate.

## 8. John Hancock Mutual Life Insurance v. Harris Trust — December 13, 1993

**Path A: affirm relevant fiduciary-status ruling.** ERISA §401(b)(2) excludes an insurer's underlying assets only to the extent a policy guarantees the amount of benefits. Divide the guaranteed annuity obligation from free funds whose investment experience and present benefit amount are not guaranteed. The latter remain plan assets, making discretionary control or management fiduciary activity under §3(21)(A). General-account placement does not create a blanket exemption. SEC v. United Benefit supplies a useful distinction between investment and insured guarantees; the enacted ERISA language controls. The contract's residual floor and ability to purchase an annuity later do not guarantee the amount attributable to all present free funds. The 1977 amendment stopped automatic new benefit guarantees; no later request supplied them.

A later litigation reading cannot override the limited exclusion's text; no broad revision of Chevron or categorical denial of all deference to interpretive material follows.

A narrower possible remand would require an actually unresolved contract feature; none may be invented merely because the result is difficult.

## 9. Tennessee v. Middlebrooks — December 13, 1993

**Threshold disposition.** Dismiss review because an adequate independent Tennessee constitutional ground sustains the resentencing judgment. The state court expressly applies Article I, §16 as well as the Eighth Amendment and states that Lowenfield supplies no rationale for constitutionality under the Tennessee Constitution. Its holding and remedy repeatedly state both grounds. Overturning its federal reasoning would not remove the state ground. Long's federal presumption resolves ambiguity; it does not disregard an independently sufficient state holding simply because the reasoning also discusses federal authority. ## 11. Ratzlaf v. United States — January 11, 1994

**Statutory alternatives.** Section 5324(3) prohibits structuring for the purpose of evading §5313(a)'s reporting requirements; §5322(a) separately punishes willful violation of the subchapter. Path A requires knowledge that structuring itself is unlawful. It gives independent content to willfulness and fits the same penalty term used for neighboring reporting duties. Cheek recognizes contextual knowledge of legal duty; Liparota avoids criminalizing conduct without the knowledge the particular statutory structure requires. Avoiding reporting may serve a lawful aim and is not invariably self-evidently criminal. Knowledge may be proved circumstantially; the government need not prove that the defendant knew a section number or read the Code.

**Path B** reads willfulness in this context as intentional structuring with knowledge of the reporting duty and purpose to defeat it. Its strongest ground is the express evasion purpose and the ordinary rule that ignorance of law does not excuse purposeful regulated conduct. Cheek's special tax-law construction does not mechanically apply to every statute. Against Path A, it is implausible that Congress created a specific structuring offense yet required proof that a deliberate evader knew the new prohibition. Against Path B, §5324 already requires that purpose, making §5322's added willfulness do no independent work for this offense; rejecting an element as inconvenient is not statutory interpretation. No later amendment enters either path.
