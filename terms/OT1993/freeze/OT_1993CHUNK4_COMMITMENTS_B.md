## 41. Stansbury v. California — April 26, 1994

### Result and sufficient ground

Vacate the California Supreme Court judgment and remand for reconsideration under the correct objective Miranda custody standard. A person's custody depends on formal arrest or restraint on freedom of movement to the degree associated with formal arrest, evaluated from the objective circumstances and how a reasonable person in the interviewee's position would understand them. An officer's uncommunicated belief that the person is, or is not, a suspect has no independent effect. A belief manifested through words or conduct may matter insofar as that communication changes the objectively perceived restraint; even communicated suspicion does not automatically establish custody. The interviewee's undisclosed subjective view does not replace the objective inquiry.

The lower court's explicit reliance on when suspicion focused on Stansbury leaves uncertainty whether the same judgment follows under this standard. The Court need not decide whether the initial interview was custodial, whether each objective circumstance was communicated or perceived, whether any admissibility error was harmless, or what derivative evidence has its own admissibility basis. Vacatur corrects the governing legal premise without ordering suppression or a new trial. No general rule makes every station interview, locked room, seizure, or suspect interview custodial.

## 43. United States v. Alvarez-Sanchez — May 2, 1994

### Baseline result and sufficient ground

Reverse the Ninth Circuit's judgment insofar as it aggregates state-only detention into the §3501(c) period without a federal/D.C. presentment obligation, and remand for further proceedings consistent with the federal-trigger rule. Section 3501(c) addresses delay in bringing the defendant before a magistrate or other officer empowered to commit persons charged with federal or D.C. offenses. Arrest or detention solely for a state offense does not itself start that federal period. “Any law-enforcement officer” includes a state officer effecting relevant federal detention; it does not eliminate the statute's federal-presentment premise. The nature of the detention controls, not merely an officer's title or a label. Federal questioning of a person still held solely on state charges and the possibility of eventual federal prosecution do not alone establish earlier federal arrest or detention.

The reported sequence is state narcotics arrest, Monday confession during state custody, later federal custody, and Tuesday federal presentment. No improper federal-state holding arrangement is found in the authorized record. The holding must reserve the application of Anderson v. United States where a working arrangement uses state custody improperly to obtain a confession and evade the governing presentment requirement. It must not approve sham detention or decide the status of a federal hold merely from the absence of a formally announced arrest.

The six-hour protection remains a qualified safe harbor against exclusion **solely for presentment delay**, with voluntariness, jury weight, and the transportation/distance proviso preserved. The Court need not resolve the entire relationship of subsections (a), (b), and (c), whether every confession outside six hours is excludable, or a separate constitutional voluntariness, Miranda, Fourth Amendment, or prompt-probable-cause-review claim. The Ninth Circuit's broader suppression analysis and its alternative subsection (b) balance used the erroneous aggregate-delay premise; a distinct preserved ground must be considered under its own rule on remand. No automatic new trial, acquittal, categorical admission, or reinstatement of conviction is selected.
