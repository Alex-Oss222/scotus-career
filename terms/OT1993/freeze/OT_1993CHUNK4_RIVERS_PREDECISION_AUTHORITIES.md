## Patterson: source and scope distinctions

Kennedy's opinion, joined by O'Connor and Scalia among current associates, preserves Runyon's private-contract coverage but construes the old statutory making/enforcement language to exclude ordinary postformation working-condition harassment. The making protection addresses refusal to contract or discriminatory terms at formation. Enforcement protects access to judicial and nonjudicial legal processes addressing binding contract obligations, including private obstruction and a union's racially discriminatory failure to enforce the contract. Breach alone does not establish unequal access to enforcement. A promotion may qualify if it creates a new and distinct relation; the discriminatory-promotion proof instruction was separately set aside. Those latter issues do not enlarge Rivers' grant.

Patterson's jury had rejected a discriminatory-discharge claim, but the Supreme Court's reviewed merits issues were harassment and the promotion instruction, not a separate holding about every discharge. In Rivers, the Sixth Circuit supplied the particular extension of Patterson to its discharge record while preserving a distinct retaliatory contract-enforcement claim. Do not turn Patterson's posture into a discharge ruling it did not separately make.

The majority's distinction between interpretation and legislative amendment matters to the later temporal inquiry: it identifies the governing old text while acknowledging Congress's power to change statutory law. The source itself does not answer §101's temporal reach, because §101 did not yet exist. Title VII and §1981 remain distinct, with different procedures and remedies; Patterson does not make Title VII an exclusive remedy wherever old §1981 actually applies.

Blackmun joined all of Brennan's partial concurrence/dissent. Brennan treated the old text and Reconstruction history as protecting against severe or pervasive postformation racial harassment evidencing unequal contracting; he opposed using later Title VII procedure to restrict the older statute and emphasized independently available damages and jury remedies. Stevens joined Parts II-B, II-C, and III, not the entire Brennan opinion, and wrote separately. His own opinion treats a contract as an ongoing relationship, particularly an at-will contract repeatedly remade or amended as duties change, and regards the narrower harassment rule as inconsistent with the established Runyon/Goodman structure. These are direct personal substantive positions relevant to a restoration argument. They are not themselves positions on the temporal operation of a later enactment. A final commitment must test that additional question against actual Landgraf rather than infer temporal assent from substantive disagreement with Patterson.

Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg were not on the Patterson Court. No participation or personal position is imputed. The retired Justices' opinions remain authority or recorded arguments at their proper level, not votes on Rivers.

## Lytle: existing legal claim and jury remedy

The unanimous Court distinguishes Parklane's later separate action from legal and equitable claims properly joined in one suit. An erroneous dismissal of the legal claim cannot let the later bench findings defeat the jury right on common issues. Beacon Theatres and Dairy Queen preserve jury priority; conserving judicial resources cannot legitimize the consequence of the erroneous dismissal. A bench dismissal under Rule 41(b), where the judge weighs facts, is not the same as a Rule 50 determination drawing permissible inferences for the nonmovant. No automatic directed verdict follows merely because the judge found against the plaintiff.

Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, and Kennedy joined the full Lytle opinion. O'Connor, joined by Scalia, separately stresses that whether the pleaded discharge and retaliation claims actually survive Patterson must first be considered below on an adequate record. The Court did not resolve that issue initially, and its footnote assumes, without deciding, the absence of a Title VII jury entitlement on the arguments presented. Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg did not participate.

The Sixth Circuit in Rivers has already recognized a narrow old-law retaliation-for-contract-enforcement theory and preserved a jury remand against collateral estoppel from the adverse Title VII findings. Lytle supports that procedural distinction; it does not independently expand Rivers' Supreme Court temporal-review scope, reopen the separate union/LMRA issues, prove discrimination, or authorize adding new §1981(b) liability. Denying retrospective §101 application must not be rendered as affirming total dismissal or eliminating the old-law jury remand. Conversely, applying §101 would change statutory coverage without itself resolving liability or damages.
