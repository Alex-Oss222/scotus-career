## Record and judgment below

Pacific Mutual sued in March 1991 over approximately $8 million in InterFirst securities bought in July and September 1987 following the June 1987 InterFirst/RepublicBank merger. The defendants included Morgan Stanley, Goldman Sachs, Ernst & Young, and Salomon Brothers. Then-governing Fifth Circuit law borrowed Texas's four-year fraud limitation period, and defendants initially did not contest timeliness.

After Lampf and James B. Beam, the district court dismissed the §10(b) claims with prejudice in August 1991 because the complaint alleged transactions more than three years before filing. **Pacific Mutual did not appeal.** Its judgment was therefore final and nonappealable when Congress enacted §27A on December 19, 1991. The original dismissal is not simply a still-pending direct-review case. Pacific Mutual filed its reinstatement motion January 31, 1992, within the sixty-day statutory period. The United States intervened to defend the statute. The district court rejected reinstatement on due-process/final-judgment and judicial-retroactivity grounds. The Fifth Circuit reversed and remanded for further proceedings, treating §27A(b) as a lawful change of limitation law, not a command to decide securities fraud liability for a designated party.

The Fifth Circuit opinion also addressed Simmons/TGX, but that other plaintiff's voluntary-dismissal, without-prejudice, relation-back, and waived claim-preclusion issues are not Pacific Mutual facts and are not separate matters in this docket. Do not transfer them here.

## Exact statutory mechanism

Section27A(a), 15 U.S.C.§78aa-1(a): “The limitation period for any private civil action implied under section 10(b) of this Act that was commenced on or before June 19, 1991, shall be the limitation period provided by the laws applicable in the jurisdiction, including principles of retroactivity, as such laws existed on June 19, 1991.”

Section27A(b): “Any private civil action implied under section 10(b) of this Act that was commenced on or before June 19, 1991—(1) which was dismissed as time barred subsequent to June 19, 1991, and (2) which would have been timely filed under the limitation period provided by the laws applicable in the jurisdiction, including principles of retroactivity, as such laws existed on June 19, 1991, shall be reinstated on motion by the plaintiff not later than 60 days after [December 19, 1991].” The bracketed date identifies enactment, as in the lower-court quotation. These conditions are cumulative. The statute does not itself find fraud or direct damages. Subsection(a)'s treatment of pending actions must remain distinct from subsection(b)'s reopened judgments. The isolated opening reference in the circuit opinion to enactment in “November” is superseded by its accurate statutory citation and date of December19.

## Competing grounds and eligible sources

Defendants argue that Article III protects the conclusive judgment once ordinary review ends and forbids Congress to act as a superior appellate tribunal; amending substantive law for pending cases does not reopen completed private litigation. They distinguish judgments subject to continuing equitable supervision, cases still on appeal/rehearing, and the United States' waiver of its own res judicata defense. They separately argue due-process protection for judgment rights and a statute of repose, and contend that §27A evades the judicial retroactivity rule without a genuine prospective change of law.

Pacific Mutual and the United States argue that Congress changed the governing limitation rule for a defined category rather than dictating findings or liability, that adjudication of the reinstated claims remains with courts, and that rational restoration of the filing expectations existing before Lampf does not unconstitutionally disturb protected property. They rely on power to legislate retroactively in civil matters, to alter limitations, and to allow reopening. The Fifth Circuit accepted these propositions, relying on CFTC v. Schor's attention to practical intrusion on the judiciary, United States v. Sioux Nation's reopening legislation, and the Court's civil-retroactivity authorities. These are the lower court's holdings and litigating grounds; they do not resolve this Court's inquiry.
