### Boca Grande: correction and effective dependency

The packet describes the judgment below as a reversal. The actual short appellate opinion says **vacated and remanded**. The district court had dismissed Florida Power & Light's contribution claim on summary judgment under the circuit's former settlement-bar rule. The intervening Great Lakes rule caused appellate vacatur. The appellate court expressly declined Boca Grande's alternative contention that Florida Power & Light was not entitled to contribution on the record. That alternative was not adjudicated and is not independently resolved here.

The supplied McDermott public holding is now effective under the required same-day sequence. It reduces the claim against nonsettlers by settling tortfeasors' equitable shares; the plaintiff and settlers bear a settlement bargain's shortfall or benefit. It bars contribution from a settler merely to shift that voluntary settlement shortfall back to the settler. Reliable determination of fault remains necessary even when a settler is absent. Ordinary fraud or invalidity challenges remain available. It preserves ordinary joint and several liability for shortfalls unrelated to settlement, including the distinct statutory setting of Edmonds. All eight associates joined that specific public holding. Thus the packet's former live choice between pro tanto credit with contribution and proportionate credit with settlement finality has been resolved by actual entering law. No settlement amount, percentage, or damage total is needed to decide this legal issue, and none is invented.

### Irvine: the regulation is a real fork, not an assumed premise

The lower opinion establishes an irrevocable 1917 inter vivos trust, knowledge by 1931 at age twenty-one, termination in June 1979, and a partial disclaimer in August 1979. The neutral packet's fraction is retained: five-sixteenths of Irvine's share, one-sixteenth to each of five children. Minnesota treated the disclaimer as timely because its six-month period ran from final ascertainment and indefeasible vesting; federal timeliness does not follow automatically. The refund dispute includes the 1979 assessment and the resulting unified-credit adjustment to the third quarter of 1980. Reversal must encompass the legal premise of both, while leaving calculation and remaining issues for remand.

Three separate texts must remain separate:

1. Sections 2501(a)(1) and 2511(a) tax transfers of property by gift and reach direct and indirect transfers, in trust or otherwise, of real or personal, tangible or intangible property, subject to statutory limitations. The claimed taxable event is Irvine's 1979 disposition, not her grandfather's 1917 settlement.
2. Revenue Act of 1932 §501(b) excludes transfers on or before enactment. It does not say that property acquired before enactment can never be transferred by gift afterward.
3. The 1986 form of Treasury Regulation §25.2511-1(c)(2) speaks of **taxable transfers creating an interest before January 1, 1977**, and requires local-law effectiveness, an unequivocal refusal, a reasonable time after knowledge, and no prior acceptance. The 1958 provision operative when Irvine disclaimed referred to property **transferred from a decedent**, although this trust was inter vivos. The Government conceded below that the later version superseded the earlier version and governed the dispute. That concession is not a concession that the statute independently exempts the disposition.

The en banc majority read the later regulation's “taxable transfers” limitation to exclude the 1917 creation and then treated state relation back as controlling. The concurrence also relied on the older decedent limitation and rejected treating its scope as a drafting accident. The dissent treated a completed original gift as a transfer within the regulation even if the gift tax was not yet enacted, and would reverse with valuation left for remand. Those are substantive alternatives; no commitment may silently replace the older wording with the broader later text.

The minimum reversal ground is statutory: exclusion from a regulatory exception is not exclusion from §§2501 and 2511; a state-law refusal long after knowledge can constitute a later gratuitous disposition for federal purposes. Jewett supplies both the federal nature of the question and the rejection of a possession-date clock under the disclaimer regulation. Dickman confirms that the broad statutory transfer rule is not limited to forms previously enumerated or enforced by Treasury. A commitment on that ground need not determine that the 1986 regulation has retroactive rulemaking force or that the 1958 text covered an inter vivos transfer. If the estate invokes the regulatory exclusion, local effectiveness alone is insufficient and the delay measured from knowledge does not satisfy Jewett. No mechanical extension of §2518's later nine-month rules to this pre-1977 interest is permitted.

### Landgraf: enacted text, distinct forms of relief, and scope

The primary appellate report was read in full. Trial occurred in February 1991; the Civil Rights Act became law on November 21, 1991 during appellate proceedings. The alleged harassment and resignation occurred in 1984–1986. The lower court's findings on constructive discharge and the consequent retaliation theory do not become new questions merely because damages may become available. Harris changes no relevant temporal-remedy premise and does not authorize reopening those findings. The reconstructed question is not represented as the verbatim certiorari grant.

The complete thirty-page enacted Act, Pub. L. 102-166, 105 Stat. 1071, was downloaded from govinfo and its extracted text read in full. The following operative distinctions govern modeling:

- Section 102(a)(1) authorizes compensation and punitive damages, in addition to §706(g) relief, for specified intentional Title VII discrimination, excluding disparate impact, when the complaining party cannot recover under §1981. It does not replace every existing equitable remedy.
- Section 102(b)(1) permits punitive damages against a respondent other than a government, government agency, or political subdivision only upon malice or reckless indifference to federally protected rights. Compensation and punishment therefore require separate temporal analysis.
- Section 102(b)(2) excludes backpay, interest on backpay, and other §706(g) relief from the new compensatory category. Section 102(b)(3) caps, per complaining party, the combined specified future pecuniary and nonpecuniary compensation and punitive damages at $50,000, $100,000, $200,000, or $300,000 according to the statute's employee bands and twenty-calendar-week condition. No damages figure or employer band is established here. Section 102(b)(4) leaves §1981's scope intact.
- Under §102(c), **if** the complaining party seeks compensation or punitive damages under this section, **any party may demand** a jury; the court **shall not inform** the jury of the statutory caps. The availability of a jury is conditional and elective. It is not a freestanding jury command for every pending Title VII case.
- Section 402(a) makes the Act and its amendments effective upon enactment except as specifically otherwise provided. This identifies an effective date; whether it also directs liability for earlier conduct is contested.
- Section 109(c)'s express prospective rule belongs to that section's extraterritorial amendments. Section 402(b) separately excludes any disparate-impact case whose complaint was filed before March 1, 1975 **and** whose initial decision was rendered after October 30, 1983. Neither is the other's exception. The inference that all other provisions govern past conduct is considered, not silently presumed.

The principal paths are: (A) deny §102 damages for conduct completed before enactment because those damages add substantive liability and Congress has not directed that reach, with no consequent §102 jury election; (B) apply compensatory relief to this still-pending action under Bradley's current-law rule while withholding punishment and allowing a jury election for authorized compensation on remand; or (C) apply the whole new regime to pending cases. Path C faces the strongest new-obligation objection for punitive damages. A separate procedural path cannot obtain a jury under this statute when neither statutory damages category is available. Prospective application of a procedural amendment to an uncompleted future trial is not the same as reopening a completed bench trial under a conditional jury provision.
