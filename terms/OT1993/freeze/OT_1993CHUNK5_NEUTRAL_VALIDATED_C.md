## 60. Nichols v. United States

Completed Custis public law has now been read and enters within the precise limits stated above. The questions are distinct: Custis concerns whether the new federal sentencing forum permits collateral invalidation of an existing predicate; Nichols concerns the permissible use of a conviction assumed valid under Scott because no imprisonment was imposed. The published Custis dissent and the Court's exceptions remain public continuity material without fixing any Justice's Nichols commitment.

The life statutory ceiling does not make the Guidelines increase immaterial or establish that the same sentence would have been imposed lawfully on the lower range. A court may not invent an alternative departure or independently proved prior conduct to sustain the actual sentence.

Scott's actual-imprisonment boundary and Baldasar's judgment and separate rationales must be stated at their own levels. A Marks claim requires the actual rationales and logical relationship rather than an assumed general rule from a shared judgment. The principal paths include applying or extending Baldasar's protection to this Guidelines use, limiting its judgment to the distinct repeat-misdemeanor setting, or overruling the relevant proposition with a supported stare-decisis account. The government also preserves a waiver/burden objection; it cannot disappear merely because the principal constitutional construction would favor Nichols.

Parke's actual counseled-plea proof rule is not a holding that every old uncounseled misdemeanor has a valid waiver. Godinez, effective June 24, 1993, distinguishes competence from an actual knowing, intelligent, voluntary waiver; no competence dispute is established here, and that distinction alone does not allocate Nichols's contested collateral proof burden. A result concerning later use need not invalidate the original fine conviction or establish a general duty to warn every misdemeanant of all possible future enhancement.

If Guidelines commentary concerning uncounseled convictions is invoked, identify the governing version and the date and scope of any amendment. Stinson, effective May 3, 1993, treats interpretive commentary as authoritative unless unconstitutional, contrary to statute, inconsistent with the Guideline, or plainly erroneous; it cannot itself decide the constitutional challenge and did not settle temporal applicability of every commentary amendment. Williams, effective March 9, 1992, requires an actual supported same-sentence basis and retains its separate limits for nonconstitutional departure error; do not convert that holding into an unqualified harmlessness rule for a Sixth Amendment violation.
