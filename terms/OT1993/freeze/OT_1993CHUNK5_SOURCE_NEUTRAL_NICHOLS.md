## Record and Guidelines

Nichols pleaded guilty in1990 to cocaine conspiracy under21U.S.C.§846. His criminal history included three points for a1983 federal drug felony and one point for a1983 Georgia DUI, on a nolo contendere plea, resulting in a$250 fine and no imprisonment. The DUI carried up to one year imprisonment; the petitioner's brief quotes then Georgia§40-6-391(c): imprisonment not less than ten days nor more than one year, **or** fine$100–$1,000, **or both**. This is not a finding that Georgia imposed imprisonment here. The conviction was also accompanied by DUI school.

At offense level34, including the DUI moved Nichols from categoryII (168–210months) toIII (188–235months). He received235months and eight years supervised release. The extra25months describes how far his actual sentence exceeded the previous category's ceiling; it is not the statutory maximum, which was life with a ten-year minimum. The current felony prosecution was counseled.

The petitioner quotes the Guidelines commentary expressly as **November1990**, not1993. Section4A1.1 provides (a) three points for each prior imprisonment sentence exceeding one year and one month; (b) two points for each prior imprisonment sentence of at least sixty days not counted in(a); and (c): “Add1 point for each prior sentence not counted in(a) or(b), up to a total of4 points for this item.” The typography here normalizes spaces, not the rule. His one DUI point arose under(c). No premise that all uncounseled misdemeanors yield the same score is justified.

The §4A1.2 background commentary, effective November1,1990 (amendment353), reads: “Prior sentences, not otherwise excluded, are to be counted in the criminal history score, including uncounseled misdemeanor sentences where imprisonment was not imposed.” Earlier November1989 Application Note6 spoke of valid convictions and excluded uncounseled misdemeanors resulting in imprisonment under circumstances violating the Constitution. The government's brief explains amendment353's clarifying purpose and quotes its effective date; its citation to a1993 manual's accumulated amendment appendix does not mean the case applied a newly adopted1993 counting rule. No ex post facto issue based merely on that publication date is supplied.

Section4A1.3 separately allows a downward departure where reliable information shows the criminal-history category significantly overrepresents the seriousness of the defendant's criminal history or the likelihood of further crimes. That is a discretionary sentencing safety valve, not an automatic constitutional exclusion or mandatory deduction of a point. The record does not show Nichols sought departure on that overrepresentation ground. If a proposed holding turns on the complete list of misdemeanor exceptions or departure mechanics beyond these propositions, retrieve the applicable complete manual rather than infer them. No party contests that this DUI otherwise falls within the counting rule.

## Waiver, indigency, and procedural alternatives

It was uncontested below that Nichols did not have counsel at the DUI proceeding. The district court treated waiver as unclear and declined to presume it from a silent record; petitioner characterizes this as a no-waiver finding. The Sixth Circuit expressed reservations about waiver but upheld counting by limiting Baldasar to use that converts a subsequent misdemeanor into a felony. Judge Jones dissented from that limitation. Do not turn this sequence into an undisputed affirmative finding of valid waiver.

The government preserves an alternative: collateral review carries a presumption of regularity and Nichols bears the burden of proving no knowing waiver; his reported consultation with an attorney who advised that representation was unnecessary for a nolo plea is relevant. It invokes Johnson v.Zerbst and Parke v.Raley. It also disputes proof of indigency, pointing to unrebutted reported contracting income. These are government grounds, not resolved findings that Nichols validly waived counsel or was nonindigent. A separate warning argument concerns whether he needed notice at the DUI plea that it could enhance a future crime's punishment. Keep right to counsel, waiver, indigency, and notice of future consequences distinct. Use the actual simulated Parke holding and any effective Custis outcome, not historical counterparts.

Petitioner argues Baldasar prevents additional incarceration attributable to a prior uncounseled misdemeanor; the Guidelines' increase cannot be distinguished by calling it felony sentencing rather than elevating a misdemeanor. Government argues a valid Scott conviction is available in conventional recidivist sentencing, punishment is for the new offense, sentencing may consider broad reliable conduct, Baldasar supplies no governing majority rationale or is distinguishable, and waiver/presumption supplies an alternative. Burgett and Tucker involve constitutionally invalid prior convictions and disputed reliability; Lewis addresses a statutory firearms disability and distinguished those punishment cases. Resolve whether that distinction applies here rather than merely importing a result.

## Justice-specific eligible writings: Scott and Baldasar

**Complete official reports read**, not later summaries: Scott v.Illinois,440U.S.367–390 (24pages), https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep440/usrep440367/usrep440367.pdf ; Baldasar v.Illinois,446U.S.222–235 (14pages), https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep446/usrep446222/usrep446222.pdf . Both predate the simulation opening and are eligible authority.

**Scott.** The Court's actual-imprisonment rule permits a fine-only uncounseled misdemeanor conviction without appointed counsel, even where imprisonment was authorized. Brennan dissented with Marshall and Stevens, favoring the authorized-imprisonment line. **Blackmun wrote a dissent**,440U.S.389–390: counsel where the offense is nonpetty (more than six months authorized), or where actual imprisonment occurs. He rejected the Court's actual-imprisonment-only threshold and also declined the broader authorized-imprisonment rule for every petty offense. The prior DUI's one-year exposure therefore matters to his own framework even though no jail was imposed. Do not call his Scott writing a concurrence.

**Baldasar.** A prior fine/probation uncounseled misdemeanor was used to elevate the later theft to a felony with imprisonment. A per curiam reversal was accompanied by three different concurrences, with no opinion receiving five votes. Stewart concurred with Brennan and Stevens: accepting Scott, increased imprisonment attributable to the uncounseled prior is impermissible. Marshall concurred with Brennan and Stevens: lack of counsel and reliability restrict using the conviction to deprive liberty, even though the fine-only conviction can stand; the point is not that enhancement imposes punishment twice. Blackmun concurred alone, invoking his Scott nonpetty-offense test: the prior charge carried more than six months, so he thought that conviction invalid from the outset. He **did not expressly adopt** the other four's rule governing a prior conviction conceded valid under Scott. Powell dissented with Burger, White, and Rehnquist, treating the valid prior as available for increased punishment of the new offense and criticizing uncertainty.

Only Blackmun and Stevens among the current eight non-Stone Justices participated in Scott/Baldasar. Their own writings/joins give direct competing premises. Neither concurrences' existence nor the later Court's personnel permits attributing those views to O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, or Ginsburg. A Marks claim must demonstrate a logical subset of rationales, not merely label Blackmun's vote the narrowest. The existing source-authority addendum for Custis verifies **Stevens joined Blackmun's Lewis opinion**; he did not dissent in Lewis. Blackmun's actual simulated Parke concurrence distinguishes increased sentence from a new separate crime; assess its interaction with his Scott view expressly rather than treating the Parke distinction as silently overruling it.
