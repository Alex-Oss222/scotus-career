## Lower record and judgment

Bleckley County, created in 1912, covers approximately 219 square miles. The trial used 1980 census figures: population 10,767; approximately 22% Black, 77% white, 1% other; voting-age population approximately 19% Black, 80% white, 1% other. Black and white registration were each approximately 70% of their respective eligible populations. The 1990 census later put total population at 10,430; the appellate opinion notes a slightly increased Black population percentage but supplies no replacement voting-age percentage.

The sole commissioner is the county governing authority under Georgia law, exercising executive and legislative powers. Election is countywide, with a majority requirement since 1964. No Black person had run for commissioner. At trial there was one polling place for the entire county, in the Jaycee Barn in Cochran; it belonged to an all-white civic club. That fact is part of the political setting, not a separately adjudicated challenge to polling-place law. The school board's five single-member districts used different assigned voting machines within the single precinct; one district's electorate was approximately 65% Black, the other districts no more than 12% Black.

The 1985 Georgia local act, 1985 Ga. Laws 4406, authorized five district-elected commissioners **plus a chairman elected at large**. The referendum rejected the proposal, 1,156 to 887. A similar 1982 referendum had approved the school-board district system. Do not describe the local act as simply creating a five-member commission; it authorized six officers, five district seats and a chair. The separate benchmark argument often refers to five district seats. The full local act has not been retrieved; details beyond the reported structure and referendum condition remain unverified.

The plaintiffs also initially challenged school-board and Cochran City Council elections. Those claims were settled before trial. Only the county governing structure was tried. The district court rejected both the statutory dilution and constitutional intentional-discrimination claims. It found a sufficiently numerous and compact Black electorate to constitute a majority in a school-board-like district if the structure changed, but held that cohesion and sufficient white bloc voting had not been proved. It also found no purpose to discriminate in creation or maintenance of the structure, no candidate-slating barrier, no proven official unresponsiveness, and no tenuous policy explanation for the structure. Those findings must be distinguished from its extensive findings of past exclusion and continued socioeconomic disadvantages affecting participation.

The Eleventh Circuit reversed on §2 and remanded **for formulation of a remedy**. It suggested the school-board districts as a possible model, taking account of county-specific concerns and the Act. It did not impose a final number of commissioners or a completed district map. It did not reach the constitutional claim. It applied Rule 52(a)'s clear-error standard to ultimate dilution findings, while correcting legal errors that shaped the fact assessment.

The appellate court's stated reasons were that the district judge wrongly confined the polarization inquiry largely to election statistics and treated its negative Gingles conclusions as a premise for the totality inquiry. The available presidential-primary regression and exit-poll evidence, city elections, community testimony, and socioeconomic history could be considered together insofar as they bore on cohesion and bloc voting. The court did **not** say that the mere existence of other Senate factors substitutes for the predicates: its footnote 19 expressly ties those factors to their bearing on the Gingles inquiries. It found the predicates satisfied, no meaningful rebuttal evidence, and a statutory violation under the totality. It acknowledged an Eleventh Circuit division over whether satisfying Gingles alone establishes liability or instead shifts a burden to defendants; its own decision also considered the totality.

Record qualifications: Willie Basby's electoral success occurred in city elections, not a countywide commissioner contest. The appellate court treated it as exceptional because of crossover support, earlier plurality rules, narrow early wins, and later unopposed campaigns. The Reverend Hall lost a countywide school-board race in 1984 but won in the majority-Black school-board district in 1986. These are evidence bearing on opportunity, not a legal guarantee that a candidate of a particular race must win.

## Operative federal statute

42 U.S.C. §1973, as amended June 29, 1982 and operative for this event:

> (a) No voting qualification or prerequisite to voting or standard, practice, or procedure shall be imposed or applied by any State or political subdivision in a manner which results in a denial or abridgement of the right of any citizen of the United States to vote on account of race or color, or in contravention of the guarantees set forth in section 1973b(f)(2) of this title, as provided in subsection (b) of this section.
>
> (b) A violation of subsection (a) of this section is established if, based on the totality of circumstances, it is shown that the political processes leading to nomination or election in the State or political subdivision are not equally open to participation by members of a class of citizens protected by subsection (a) of this section in that its members have less opportunity than other members of the electorate to participate in the political process and to elect representatives of their choice. The extent to which members of a protected class have been elected to office in the State or political subdivision is one circumstance which may be considered: Provided, That nothing in this section establishes a right to have members of a protected class elected in numbers equal to their proportion in the population.

## Competing submissions, with attribution and limits

The United States supports the voters' statutory position. It argues that Houston Lawyers rejects an automatic single-member-office exemption; that §§2 and 5 cover changes in electoral structure; and that the single commissioner combines legislative and executive functions. Its proposed comparator is contextual: five district seats are common under Georgia law, the legislature specifically authorized such a structure for this county, and the county already uses five school-board districts. It rejects an entitlement to arbitrarily enlarging a body until any small group can win; it says a tiny group's proposed 25- or 50-member body could be rejected as eccentric or impracticable. It expressly distinguishes ordinary single executive offices such as governor, sheriff, mayor, probate judge, or tax collector, and states that the case does not present enlarging an existing multimember body. These are positions advanced in the brief, not conclusions of this Court.

The United States also reports the officials' opposing arguments, with citations to their merits brief: inability to win is caused by the minority's size rather than race; the Gingles comparator should retain the existing number of offices; otherwise a virtually unlimited expansion could evade the first predicate and create proportional representation; restructuring a county's government intrudes upon state policy; the sole commissioner is economical and efficient; and Gregory v. Ashcroft's clear-statement principle protects the state choice. The government's response invokes the Act's results language, contextually reasonable comparators, the Civil War Amendments enforcement basis, and consideration of genuine state interests under totality and at remedy. ## Johnson v. De Grandy — predecision facts and scope

The consolidated Supreme Court dockets are Nos. 92-519 (Johnson v. De Grandy), 92-593 (De Grandy v. Johnson), and 92-767 (United States v. Florida). Argument: October 4, 1993. Direct appeal from the three-judge Northern District of Florida in De Grandy v. Wetherell, 815 F. Supp. 1550 (July 17, 1992). The district court announced its ruling July 1, entered judgment July 2, and supplied the reported explanatory opinion July 17. Preserve these distinct dates.

Florida's legislature adopted Senate Joint Resolution 2-G on April 10, 1992, using 1990 census figures to create 40 single-member Senate and 120 single-member House districts. Under article III, §16(c) of the Florida Constitution, the attorney general sought state Supreme Court review. The state court's May 13 validation, 597 So.2d 276, occurred within a 30-day timetable, using briefs and evidentiary submissions without a trial. It expressly preserved a later factual Voting Rights Act challenge. The private federal complaints had initially attacked the obsolete 1982 districts and were amended to challenge the new plan. ### District findings and appealed components

The district court found political cohesion within the Hispanic and Black groups, none between the groups, substantial non-Hispanic-white bloc voting, and continuing effects of past official discrimination. Do not combine the groups merely by adding their populations.

The House ruling found §2 dilution and substituted a plan with eleven majority-Hispanic districts for the State's nine. The alternative districts each had at least a 64% Hispanic voting-age population. The State disputed whether this translated into enough citizen voters to elect preferred candidates: many Hispanic voting-age residents were not citizens. The district court treated the supermajority as compensating for noncitizenship and participation levels. Voting-age population, citizen voting-age population, total population, and minority officeholders are distinct measures.

Reported record comparisons: Hispanics were 50% of Dade County's voting-age population and had nine of eighteen House districts located primarily within Dade; in the twenty districts lying at least partly within Dade, Hispanics were about 47% of voting-age population and had nine effective-majority districts (45%). These are demographic/composition facts, not a source-collector's conclusion that §2 is satisfied. Plaintiffs' expert described neighborhoods split by District 116's line, concentration in District 114, and fragmentation involving Districts 102 and 109. Distinguish recited expert testimony from an express finding of inconsistent districting standards between minority and other neighborhoods.

For the Senate, the district court left the State's plan operative. Its July 2 judgment said the plan did not violate §2; its July 17 opinion described independent Black and Hispanic claims but said a different remedy need not be imposed because proposed additional districts conflicted. The alternatives could create a fourth Hispanic effective-majority district or an additional Black-majority district, but the court found it could not create both. Preserve this tension in the lower reasons; the operative result was no substituted Senate plan.

The seven-district Senate area including substantial Dade voters had a voting-age population 44.8% Hispanic and 15.8% Black. Three districts had Hispanic effective majorities (42.9% of seven) and one a Black majority (14.3%); another district enabled Black voters to elect their preferred candidate with crossover support despite their being well short of a majority. In the five districts wholly within Dade, three had Hispanic supermajorities of at least 64% and one a clear Black majority; the voting-age shares there were 53.9% Hispanic and 13.5% Black. These alternative frames are descriptive. This handoff does not choose the legally proper denominator or geography.

The State challenges House liability and substitution, and supports its Senate plan. The private Hispanic plaintiffs and United States challenge the absence of additional Senate relief. The State's res-judicata defense addresses the private plaintiffs; its Rooker/Feldman theory concerns the United States' independent federal suit. The State proposes a conclusive defense based on proportional electoral opportunity. Plaintiffs emphasize polarization, discriminatory effects, and feasible additional districts. The United States would consider proportionality as an affirmative defense measured statewide and contests its preservation below. These are competing submissions, not adopted rules.

## Tuilaepa v. California / Proctor v. California — distinct records

Nos. 93-5131 and 93-5161; argument March 22, 1994. Certiorari to separate California Supreme Court judgments: People v. Tuilaepa, 4 Cal.4th 569, 842 P.2d 1142 (December 28, 1992), and People v. Proctor, 4 Cal.4th 499, 842 P.2d 1100 (1992). Both affirmed convictions and death sentences. Both petitioners challenge section 190.3(a); Tuilaepa also challenges (b) and (i). These questions concern penalty selection; the sufficiency of the section 190.2 special circumstances to narrow eligibility is unchallenged here.

Tuilaepa's murder occurred during a bar robbery in Long Beach on October 6, 1986. The jury found the robbery special circumstance. The prosecution introduced other misconduct committed in custody, including a 1987 assault and robbery during the capital trial. Thus factor (b) evidence should not be described as exclusively conduct preceding the capital offense. The State's highest court upheld the convictions and penalty. It rejected the vagueness challenge, assuming without deciding that section 190.3 was subject to that analysis. It understood factor (b) as punishable offenses involving force or threatened force against a person and age as supplying inferences concerning relative youth or maturity, rather than as a numerical rule for punishment.

Tuilaepa's jury received only relevant section 190.3 factors. For other violent criminal acts, it was told it must be satisfied beyond a reasonable doubt that he committed the acts before considering them as aggravation. That trial instruction is distinct from a statutory obligation to prove every sentencing judgment beyond reasonable doubt. The state court held that an instruction defining each underlying offense's elements was not required without a defense request; it identified potential tactical reasons not to highlight unadjudicated conduct and also found no prejudice. Tuilaepa's charge expressly rejected mechanical factor counting and arbitrary assignment of weights, permitted moral or sympathetic valuation of applicable evidence, and required each juror to be persuaded that aggravation was so substantial compared with mitigation that death rather than life without parole was warranted. The written verdict said aggravation substantially outweighed mitigation.

Mosk concurred in Tuilaepa's judgment but disputed the majority's vagueness approach, emphasizing jurors' understanding and Zant's prohibition of constitutionally impermissible considerations. He regarded any error as harmless on this record. Kennard joined except on the age-vagueness question, which she found unnecessary to decide because age played little part in evidence or argument and any error was harmless under any standard. These are lower-court alternative positions, not Supreme Court commitments.

Proctor was convicted of murder with rape, burglary, and torture special circumstances. The first penalty jury could not reach a verdict. After his successful venue motion, another jury in Sacramento County imposed death. It received the full statutory factor list and the older mandatory-language CALJIC instruction. It also received a requested instruction rejecting mechanical counting, instructions permitting pity, sympathy, and mercy while excluding conjecture, prejudice, and public opinion, and a beyond-reasonable-doubt instruction for an asserted aggravating circumstance. The state majority assessed the whole charge and advocacy and rejected his claim that jurors were misled about personal responsibility or compelled to count factors. Its factor (a) analysis relied on the relevance of offense circumstances; it rejected his contention that a new penalty jury necessarily had only death sentencing as an outlet for outrage. His own venue motion supplied the new jury; no finding of actual bias followed.

The Proctor dissent by Mosk criticized the trial judge's guilt-phase comments and would reverse on that ground. Its additional discussion questioned whether factor (a) and the general penalty charge guided lay jurors, using the distinction between eligibility and weighing. Kennard's concurrence also objected to treating absence of a minor-accomplice role as aggravation. Those additional lower-court issues do not automatically enter the Supreme Court question presented. ### Statutory factors and qualifications


The complete factor list, including the relevance condition, is quoted from the event-effective statutory text as reproduced at 512 U.S. 969 n.*. This is enacted text, not a Supreme Court rationale. Original line wrapping is retained.

```text
*Section 190.3 provides in part:

   "In determining the penalty, the trier of fact shall take into account any
of the following factors if relevant:

   "(a) The circumstances of the crime of which the defendant was con-
victed in the present proceeding and the existence of any special circum-
stances found to be true pursuant to Section 190.1.

   "(b) The presence or absence of criminal activity by the defendant
which involved the use or attempted use of force or violence or the express
or implied threat to use force or violence.

   "(c) The presence or absence of any prior felony conviction.
   "(d) Whether or not the offense was committed while the defendant was
under the influence of extreme mental or emotional disturbance.
   "(e) Whether or not the victim was a participant in the defendant's hom-
icidal conduct or consented to the homicidal act.
   "(f) Whether or not the offense was committed under circumstances
which the defendant reasonably believed to be a moral justification or
extenuation for his conduct.
   "(g) Whether or not defendant acted under extreme duress or under
the substantial domination of another person.
    "(h) Whether or not at the time of the offense the capacity of the de-
 fendant to appreciate the criminality of his conduct or to conform his con-
 duct to the requirements of law was impaired as a result of mental disease
 or defect, or the [e]ffects of intoxication.
    "(i) The age of the defendant at the time of the crime.
    "(j) Whether or not the defendant was an accomplice to the offense and
 his participation in the commission of the offense was relatively minor.
    "(k) Any other circumstance which extenuates the gravity of the crime
 even though it is not a legal excuse for the crime."
```

### Competing contentions and earlier authority

The petitioners contend that broad subjects such as circumstances, criminal activity, and age permit nearly any fact to be counted toward death, and that jurors need clearer propositions or directions for their use. The State invokes the subjects' intelligibility, relevance to individualized sentencing, and the separate special-circumstance eligibility findings. The lower dissents raise the concern that an understandable label may still allow an impermissible adverse inference or turn absence of mitigation into aggravation. Whether such a defect occurred in either actual charge is distinct from a facial objection. A conclusion that an instruction is invalid also requires a separate remedy analysis; the lower Tuilaepa concurrences supply record-specific harmlessness positions, not a holding that all such defects are harmless.
