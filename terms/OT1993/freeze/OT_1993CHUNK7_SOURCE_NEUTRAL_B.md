## 76. Hawaiian Airlines, Inc. v. Norris — No. 92-2058

**Argument:** April 28, 1994. The petition also brings up the individual-officer action under Rule 12.2. The limited question is whether the Hawaii Supreme Court erred in concluding that Norris's state wrongful-discharge claims were not preempted by the Railway Labor Act (RLA). Contractual grievance merits, an implied FAA cause of action and ultimate liability on the state torts are different questions.

**Posture and record.** Norris was an FAA-licensed aircraft mechanic employed by Hawaiian Airlines beginning February 1987. In July 1987 he objected to the condition of an aircraft axle sleeve, wanted replacement, and disputed instructions concerning repair and signing a maintenance record. He alleged that signing would certify an unsafe condition. The employer contended he was asked to sign a record only for the work he actually performed. He was suspended, reported the condition to the FAA, and was discharged for insubordination after a contractual hearing. Later grievance correspondence offered mitigation to a suspension and return to work, subject to future-discipline terms; he did not pursue that proposed resolution through the full contractual machinery. The discharge/suspension characterization and the content of the requested certification were disputed. Do not turn either party's account into an established merits finding.

Norris brought one suit against the airline and another against officers Paul Finazzo, Howard Ogden and Hatsuo Honma. The contract claim in the airline litigation was dismissed in federal court after removal; the independent state claims were remanded. That contract disposition is not challenged here. The state counts assert discharge contrary to Hawaii public policy and retaliation under Hawaii's Whistleblowers' Protection Act, including his FAA report. They seek ordinary state-law relief subject to proving their elements, not enforcement of the collective-bargaining agreement (CBA) itself.

The full reported Hawaii opinion in **Norris v. Hawaiian Airlines, Inc., 74 Haw. 235, 842 P.2d 634 (December 16, 1992), No. 15022**, concerns the action against the individual officers. It reverses the order dismissing Counts I and II for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction, vacates the certified final judgment and remands for further proceedings. The later airline judgment is **74 Haw. 648, 847 P.2d 263 (1993)**. The federal reports identify both judgments and link the airline result to the published earlier reasoning; the separate full text of 847 P.2d 263 was not recovered. Do not describe 842 P.2d 634 as the sole judgment or pretend the later order was separately read. The earlier airline appeal had initially failed for want of an effective remand to state court; that procedural detour does not decide the RLA issue presented now.

The CBA provides a grievance process and just-cause protection. Article IV.D.4(a) says an employee “may be required to sign work records in connection with the work he performs.” Its safety provision protects refusal to work in circumstances violating specified health and safety requirements. The parties dispute whether that workplace provision determines the distinct public-air-safety objection. Reference to the agreement, overlap in evidence, and actual construction of disputed contract terms must be kept distinct.

**Relevant enacted terms.** The complete airline adjustment provision, 45 U.S.C. §184, is reproduced in the statutory appendix below. Its first paragraph encompasses disputes “growing out of grievances, or out of the interpretation or application of agreements concerning rates of pay, rules, or working conditions,” with the full procedural qualifications preserved. Section 153 First (i), governing railroad adjustment machinery, uses closely related language; do not attribute the railroad board itself to airlines.

Hawaii's contemporaneous court quotes HRS §378-62(1)'s operative protection against discharge, threats or discrimination in employment because an employee reports or is about to report to a public body, verbally or in writing, a violation or suspected violation of a law or rule of Hawaii, a political subdivision or the United States, **unless the employee knows the report is false**. It also quotes §378-66(b): “Where a collective bargaining agreement provides inferior rights and remedies to those provided in [the HWPA], the provisions of [the HWPA] shall supersede and take precedence over the rights, remedies, and procedures provided in collective bargaining agreements.” The bracketed abbreviation is the state court's quotation convention. The common-law public-policy count and statutory retaliation count remain separate; the lower court treats union membership as no categorical bar to the common-law count. The complete contemporaneous state enactment was not independently obtained; do not assert extra statutory elements or an automatic punitive-damages entitlement from this quotation.

**Strongest competing paths.** The carrier relies on the disjunctive “grievances” language, the RLA's compulsory machinery and the danger of relabeling disputes over contractual discipline as torts. Its best record argument is that whether Norris could refuse the signature demand necessarily requires resolving the CBA's work-record and discipline provisions. Norris answers that state law supplies the duties and that his conduct, the nature of the requested certification, employer motive and FAA reporting can be determined without interpreting the CBA. A contractual justification for discharge does not by itself resolve whether discharge violated an independent statutory or public-policy obligation. The legal fork is the actual dependence of these counts on contract interpretation, not whether a CBA could also afford relief.

**Earlier writing map.** Lingle v. Norge Division of Magic Chef, Inc., 486 U.S. 399 (1988), O'Connor's unanimous opinion, separates retaliatory-discharge facts and motive from CBA interpretation. Atchison, Topeka & Santa Fe Railway v. Buell, 480 U.S. 557 (1987), Stevens's unanimous opinion, preserves an independent federal injury remedy despite potential RLA grievances. Consolidated Rail Corp. v. Railway Labor Executives' Assn., 491 U.S. 299 (1989), distinguishes major/minor contractual disputes using arguable contractual justification; whether that test governs a genuinely independent tort is a separate step. Andrews v. Louisville & Nashville Railroad, 406 U.S. 320 (1972), supplies the employer's mandatory-arbitration precedent where the claimed right depends on the agreement. Treat authorship/joins not specified here as requiring verification, not as inferred support.

**Actual earlier-term constraint:** Livadas, June 13, 1994, in the prepared Public Authority, has all nine Justices joining Stevens on the antecedent §301 rule: an independent wage claim is not displaced by consulting an undisputed wage rate for arithmetic; genuinely disputed contract interpretation remains different. Its NLRA governmental-interference and §1983 holdings are distinct and do not mechanically decide RLA scope or Norris's damages. The model must address Livadas's factual/interpretive distinction for every Justice while evaluating the different statute.

## 77. Director, OWCP v. Greenwich Collieries — No. 93-744, including Maher Terminals

**Argument:** April 25, 1994. The Director petitions from two Third Circuit benefits-review judgments. The two statutory programs and two lower rationales must remain distinct.

**Greenwich record and exact disposition.** Stanley Ondecko worked about 31 years in coal mining. Under the Black Lung Benefits Act (BLBA), the ALJ found competing evidence equally probative on pneumoconiosis and total disability, and applied the true-doubt rule to resolve both in his favor. A separate ten-year-employment presumption bore on the connection to coal-mine employment; it was not a presumption that supplied every disability element. The Benefits Review Board affirmed. **990 F.2d 730 (3d Cir. March 23, 1993), No. 92-3270**, vacated the Board's decision and remanded to the ALJ to determine whether the evidence preponderated for Ondecko. The court observed that the ALJ might have stopped after finding equally strong opposing evidence without completing the required weighing. Its holding rested on its reading of Mullins Coal, the preponderance requirement and 20 C.F.R. §718.403. Footnote 4 expressly declined to determine whether the APA's first sentence itself allocates the burden of persuasion because the BLBA regulation already allocated the burden. This was not a final award to the employer on disputed medical facts.

**Maher record and exact disposition.** Michael Santoro, a longshore employee aged 62, strained his neck while avoiding a swinging shackle on July 23, 1985. A spinal astrocytoma was later diagnosed; he died in March 1986. His widow sought lifetime disability and death benefits. The disputed causal question was whether the work injury aggravated the condition and hastened death, not a finding that the accident originally caused the cancer. The ALJ found the initial conditions for the Longshore Act's §20(a) presumption, found that employer evidence rebutted it, then found the record in equipoise and awarded benefits through true doubt. The presumption had dropped from the case; it was not evidence independently carrying the final decision. The Board affirmed. **992 F.2d 1277 (3d Cir. April 19, 1993), No. 92-3222**, **vacated** the Board's order with directions to remand to the ALJ for a preponderance determination. Its discussion of APA §556(d)'s first sentence allocates persuasion to the claimant. Descriptive references to a reversal must not replace the lower opinion's formal vacatur and remand.

**Texts and distinctions.** APA §556(d), Longshore §§919(d), 923(a), and BLBA §932(a) appear in the appendix. The first APA sentence says “Except as otherwise provided by statute, the proponent of a rule or order has the burden of proof.” The third sentence's whole-record and reliable/probative/substantial-evidence requirement is different from that allocation clause. The dispute over production versus persuasion is distinct from the degree of persuasion. Neither equals appellate substantial-evidence review.

The contemporaneous regulatory passage invoked by the Director is 20 C.F.R. §718.3(c) (1993): “In enacting [the BLBA], Congress intended that claimants be given the benefit of all reasonable doubt as to the existence of total or partial disability or death due to pneumoconiosis.” Section 718.403 supplies: “Except as provided in this subchapter, the burden of proving a fact alleged in connection with any provision of this part shall rest with the party making such allegation.” These are the full operative quoted sentences, not a claim that the whole 1993 regulatory volume was obtained. Do not silently replace “reasonable doubt” with a regulation explicitly naming the true-doubt rule or expressly allocating persuasion. Section 932(a)'s regulatory-exception language and whether an actual regulation exercises any such power are separate questions.

**Strongest competing paths.** The Director's best argument distinguishes an initial production burden from ultimate risk of nonpersuasion; invokes the explicit treatment of APA burden language in NLRB v. Transportation Management Corp., 462 U.S. 393, 404 n.7 (1983); emphasizes longstanding administration, benefits legislation and the BLBA's express regulatory exception. Employers counter with the 1946 legal meaning of “burden of proof,” the rule/order proponent's position, the APA's common framework and Steadman's preponderance analysis. The Director can answer that Steadman concerns standard rather than allocation; employers can distinguish Transportation Management's affirmative defense and argue that a general welfare purpose does not change enacted burden language. Any disposition must separately explain each statute's APA incorporation and asserted exemption. An answer about §932(a)'s power need not be broader than necessary to resolve the actual regulation.

**Earlier writing map and actual law.** Steadman v. SEC, 450 U.S. 91 (1981), is **Brennan's** opinion (not Blackmun's); its operative inquiry is APA §7(c)'s evidentiary standard in SEC adjudication. Transportation Management, 462 U.S. 393 (1983), White's opinion, sustains the Wright Line framework with the General Counsel first proving prohibited motivation and an employer same-decision affirmative defense; footnote 7's production-language treatment must be squarely addressed. Mullins Coal Co. v. Director, OWCP, 484 U.S. 135 (1987), concerns invocation of a specified interim presumption and weighing qualifying evidence; its regulatory setting cannot be collapsed into the present regulations. Full earlier-opinion joins are not newly certified in this supplement.

Actual John Hancock, NLRB v. Health Care & Retirement Corp. (May 23), and PUD No. 1 (May 31), as supplied in Current Public Authority, are the available current-term statutory/deference authorities. Health Care's permissible-construction holding is Ginsburg's, joined by Blackmun, Stevens and Souter as well as Stone-Zsela; the contrary Justices' objections must be drawn from that actual public writing. It preserves the agency's scheme-consistent distinction between skilled direction and genuine supervision, not permission to disregard statutory terms. PUD's scheme-specific construction does not announce deference to an agency on all cross-cutting APA questions. No same-day Greenwich result enters this handoff.

## 78. Barclays Bank PLC / Colgate-Palmolive Co. v. Franchise Tax Board — Nos. 92-1384, 92-1839

**Argument:** March 28, 1994. Separate taxpayers and lower judgments present California's worldwide combined reporting/formula apportionment. The bank group has a foreign parent; Colgate has a domestic parent and foreign subsidiaries. These are refund suits about past tax years, not prospective adjudication of every amended water's-edge election.

**Record.** Barclays' parent was based in the United Kingdom; the unitary group contained more than 220 corporations in roughly 60 nations. The petitioning taxpayers were Barclays Bank International (BBI), based in the United Kingdom and operating in many nations, and its California banking subsidiary (Barcal). The disputed 1977 additional taxes were $1,678 for BBI and $152,420 for Barcal. Their unitary relationship was accepted for this litigation. Colgate was a Delaware corporation headquartered in New York with 75 foreign subsidiaries; its challenged 1970–1973 additional tax was $604,765. Although it disputed unitary status at trial, it did not dispute that status on appeal. Do not decide a new unitary-business issue from the foreign affiliations alone.

California applied an equally weighted three-factor average of California-to-worldwide property, payroll and sales to the worldwide combined income of the unitary business. Worldwide income inclusion and factor apportionment are distinct steps; California did not purport to tax every dollar of worldwide income. Later double-weighting of sales is not the formula for these tax years.

**Exact judgments.** In Barclays, the California Supreme Court, **2 Cal.4th 708, 829 P.2d 279 (1992)**, rejected the one-voice challenge and remanded remaining issues. The resulting **10 Cal.App.4th 1742, 13 Cal.Rptr.2d 377 (November 20, 1992)** reversed the taxpayer's trial-court refund judgment and directed entry of judgment for the FTB; each side bore its own appellate costs. Rehearing was denied with modification December 18, 1992; review was denied February 18, 1993. In Colgate, **10 Cal.App.4th 1768, 13 Cal.Rptr.2d 761 (November 20, 1992)** reversed the portion of the judgment concerning foreign commerce, affirmed the constitutional and statutory distortion rulings and the denial of discovery sanctions, and awarded the Board costs on appeal. Review was denied February 18, 1993. The sanctions component concerns the Board's untimely motion after trial and is not a new federal tax question. Keep these distinct lower dispositions available for a precise mandate.

**Compliance facts.** Barclays' estimated cost of literal compliance was $5 million initially and $2 million annually. Its reported historical BBI-return preparation cost of $900–$1,250 was for BBI alone, representing about a third of the group, not a proven cost of complete worldwide compliance for the entire group. The lower court nevertheless found the FTB had accepted approximations and no identified reasonable approximation had been rejected unreasonably. Taxpayers challenge whether accommodating practice cures an excessive legal burden; the Board invokes binding regulation and its administration. Do not convert a finding of this practice into a statutory command excusing all burdensome information.

**Complete relevant adjustment terms.** California Revenue and Taxation Code §25137 provides: “If the allocation and apportionment provisions of this act do not fairly represent the extent of the taxpayer's business activity in this state, the taxpayer may petition for or the Franchise Tax Board may require, in respect to all or any part of the taxpayer's business activity, if reasonable: (a) Separate accounting; (b) The exclusion of any one or more of the factors; (c) The inclusion of one or more additional factors which will fairly represent the taxpayer's business activity in this state; or (d) The employment of any other method to effectuate an equitable allocation and apportionment of the taxpayer's income.” These alternatives depend on the opening predicate; there is no unconditional election of separate accounting under this provision.

18 Cal. Code Regs. §25137-6(e)(1) provides: “In computing the income and any of the factors required for a combined report, the Franchise Tax Board shall consider the effort and expense required to obtain the necessary information. In appropriate cases, such as when the necessary data cannot be developed from financial records maintained in the regular course of business, the Franchise Tax Board may accept reasonable approximations.” The **duty to consider** effort/expense and **permission to accept** reasonable approximations are distinct. Subsection (e)(2) permits requests to the Legal Division for advance determinations under the regulation, makes those determinations individual and limited to the submitted facts, preserves later factual review, and expressly says failure to request or obtain a favorable advance determination does not preclude considering requested variances later. Do not turn optional advance guidance into exhaustion.

Colgate's 70% comparison with separate accounting was litigated. The lower court held this comparison did not capture unitary flows of value and did not meet either the constitutional clear-and-cogent distortion burden or the state statutory clear-and-convincing unreasonable-result showing. Those are lower findings/rulings, not a new Supreme Court conclusion. The lower court did not reach a claimed categorical Board refusal to consider separate accounting because the threshold unfair-representation showing had not been made.

**Federal policy material and live paths.** Complete Auto's four requirements and Japan Line's additional foreign-commerce concerns—international multiple taxation and impairment of national uniformity—must remain distinct. Container Corp. v. FTB, 463 U.S. 159 (1983), involved a domestic parent and left foreign-parent questions open; the taxpayer difference cannot be erased. Foreign governments objected to worldwide unitary reporting, and potential retaliation is part of the one-voice argument. Taxpayers contend inconsistent national methods impose international double taxation beyond what domestic apportionment tolerates and burden entities lacking normal state political representation. California emphasizes the inevitability of mismatches between imperfect allocation methods, conceded unitary activity, fair attribution to California and political-branch authority over competing international policy.

The original U.S.–U.K. treaty proposal would have restricted subnational worldwide accounting. The Senate did not accept that restriction for subnational governments; the resulting treaty's antidiscrimination terms do not themselves prescribe one accounting method. Several legislative proposals restricting worldwide apportionment did not become law. Nonenactment, an inference of tolerance, and affirmative congressional authorization are different propositions, all contested. Executive communications opposed worldwide reporting. The United States nevertheless urged sustaining these earlier assessments, presenting timing and congressional-policy considerations. No binding executive agreement that itself prohibited these assessments was identified. The legal effect of nonbinding executive statements must not be assumed from their diplomatic importance.

California's water's-edge option took effect in 1988, originally with fees and restrictions; 1993 amendments effective January 1994 relaxed important restrictions. Those amendments do not erase the outstanding refund disputes. This record does not call for judging all qualifications of the revised election.

**Earlier writing map/current law.** Container supplies the domestic-parent formula analysis and its express reservations. O'Connor joined Powell's dissent objecting to international double taxation and the departure from national uniformity; Stevens did not participate in Container, so no Container join can be attributed to him. Wardair Canada, Inc. v. Florida Department of Revenue, 477 U.S. 1 (1986), addresses a different aviation tax within an affirmative federal/treaty framework; comparing that authorization with nonenactment is the doctrinal fork. Stevens and O'Connor joined Brennan's Wardair opinion. Blackmun dissented, arguing that authorization for state interference with foreign commerce must be unmistakably clear rather than inferred from federal activity in a related field. These earlier differences are useful individual evidence, not conclusions about the present cases. Japan Line, Ltd. v. County of Los Angeles, 441 U.S. 434 (1979), concerns ad valorem taxation of foreign-owned instrumentalities, not this income formula. Use the actual Allied-Signal, Kraft, Itel and Associated Industries entries in the prepared slices: unitary attribution, facial foreign-dividend discrimination, and compensatory-tax limits supply distinct inquiries. Actual Associated Industries, May 23, does not immunize every statewide levy merely because a local offset exists. No historical current-term tax opinion overrides that actual law.

## 79. Reed v. Farley — No. 93-5418

**Argument:** March 28, 1994. Federal collateral review under pre-AEDPA 28 U.S.C. §2254 of a state conviction; not direct review of the Indiana Supreme Court's judgment. The question concerns availability of federal habeas relief for the IAD Article IV(c) timing claim. The scope of collateral cognizability, merits of a timing violation, preservation and remedy are separate inquiries.

**Exact posture.** Reed was federally imprisoned at Terre Haute when Indiana charged theft and sought habitual-offender enhancement. Pursuant to an Indiana detainer/request, the State obtained custody on April 27, 1983. Trial occurred October 18, 1983. His sentence was four years for theft, enhanced by thirty years because of habitual-offender status. Indiana's Supreme Court affirmed in **Reed v. State, 491 N.E.2d 182 (April 7, 1986)**. The Northern District of Indiana denied habeas on September 21, 1990, No. S90-226. The Seventh Circuit affirmed in **Reed v. Clark, 984 F.2d 209 (January 19, 1993), No. 90-3264**. Rehearing and rehearing en banc were denied April 27, 1993; Ripple dissented from denial, with Cudahy also favoring rehearing en banc. The federal appellate judgment under review is the denial of habeas relief, not an order affirming the state conviction on direct appeal.

**Timing, preservation and prejudice.** Without a qualifying extension or tolling, 120 days from arrival expired August 25, 1983. At a June 27 conference the judge set September 13 for trial; at an August 1 conference a scheduling conflict moved the date to September 19. Reed did not contemporaneously tell the judge at either scheduling event that the chosen date exceeded Article IV(c)'s 120 days. But **before the deadline he submitted written motions referring to IAD time limits and opposing extensions**, and on August 29 specifically moved to dismiss under the 120-day provision. Thus “made no predeadline objection of any kind” is false. The reported sources differ on a motion's July 25/26 date and on dates associated with the later July/August motion; the precise originals/filing stamps were not obtained. The safe established fact is predeadline written notice, alongside no specific oral scheduling objection. Do not infer intentional concealment, knowing waiver or an unqualified abandonment from silence.

On September 19 Reed sought further preparation time after publicity about his prior convictions and prospective jurors. He selected an October setting among offered alternatives. He posted bond September 28 and trial began October 18. The period after September 19 and the earlier setting beyond August 25 must not be treated as one undifferentiated delay.

The reviewed record identifies no loss of evidence or defense impairment attributable to the August 26–September 19 delay. Reed complained about confinement, loss of the halfway-house setting and preparation difficulties; “no prejudice” here must not become “no adverse consequence whatsoever.” At argument, counsel expressly said the speedy-trial Constitution was **not directly violated** (official transcript, printed p.7). He continued to argue that the IAD implements constitutional purposes and supplies a legislatively prescribed remedy. An independent Sixth Amendment claim should not be invented to bypass the statutory-cognizability issue.

**Lower grounds and boundaries.** The Indiana court relied on failure to object adequately to the dates as set. The district court treated defense pretrial motions/delay as excluding time under the agreement. The Seventh Circuit treated the IAD timing claim as unavailable on collateral review after state consideration, drawing an analogy to Stone v. Powell; it explicitly stated that respondents **had not invoked Wainwright v. Sykes procedural default**. At argument Indiana acknowledged the Seventh Circuit developed its Stone analogy without an argument from the State below (printed pp.32–33). These grounds must not be fused into a finding that the federal claim was procedurally defaulted under an independently invoked adequate-and-independent state rule.

Other claims addressed below included state habitual-offender law and an IAD pretransfer-hearing issue. Those do not expand the granted timing question. The State's merits/tolling argument remains an alternative contention, and at argument it expressly questioned whether the merits of the violation were within the grant (printed p.39). A court can distinguish deciding cognizability on an assumed violation from deciding that Article IV(c) was or was not violated.

**Complete operative IAD terms.** From Pub. L. 91-538, §2, 84 Stat. 1397, 1400–1402:

> Article IV(c): “In respect of any proceeding made possible by this article, trial shall be commenced within one hundred and twenty days of the arrival of the prisoner in the receiving State, but for good cause shown in open court, the prisoner or his counsel being present, the court having jurisdiction of the matter may grant any necessary or reasonable continuance.”

> Article VI(a): “In determining the duration and expiration dates of the time periods provided in articles III and IV of this agreement, the running of said time periods shall be tolled whenever and for as long as the prisoner is unable to stand trial, as determined by the court having jurisdiction of the matter.”

> Article V(c): “If the appropriate authority shall refuse or fail to accept temporary custody of said person, or in the event that an action on the indictment, information, or complaint on the basis of which the detainer has been lodged is not brought to trial within the period provided in article III or article IV hereof, the appropriate court of the jurisdiction where the indictment, information, or complaint has been pending shall enter an order dismissing the same with prejudice, and any detainer based thereon shall cease to be of any force or effect.”

Good cause in open court with prisoner **or** counsel present and inability to stand trial determined by the court are different qualifications. A defense motion is not textually identical to statutory inability; whether the motion caused qualifying delay is disputed. The statute gives the court power to grant a necessary **or** reasonable continuance; it does not command a continuance whenever requested. Article VI(b) separately excludes persons adjudged mentally ill from the agreement and its remedies; no such predicate is established here. Article IV(e)'s return-before-trial rule is a separate anti-shuttling protection, not the timing claim presented. Article III's prisoner-request 180-day period and delivery requirements, addressed by actual Fex, are not substitutes for Article IV's arrival-triggered 120 days.

Section 5 of the federal enactment directs all federal and District of Columbia courts, departments, agencies, officers and employees to enforce the agreement and cooperate in effectuating its purpose. Reed invokes that command. It must be considered together with §2254's collateral-review conditions, not silently read as an enacted grant of habeas for every IAD breach. The later federal receiving-state dismissal discretion is not the governing remedial proviso for Indiana's prosecution.

**Strongest competing paths.** Reed stresses the congressionally sanctioned compact's federal character, the express dismissal-with-prejudice command, his written preservation and the difference between mandatory discharge and a procedural irregularity that could not alter lawful confinement. The State contests habeas jurisdiction over a voluntarily adopted compact, alternatively urges Stone's restriction or Hill's fundamental-defect standard, and maintains the timeline was lawfully extended/tolled. The jurisdictional compact argument was raised after the opposition to certiorari; the State described it as jurisdictional. Reed responds with Cuyler, federal uniformity and the statutory words “laws ... of the United States.” The Court must decide which issues require resolution, without assuming one collateral doctrine answers every distinct question.

**Earlier writing map/current law.** Hill v. United States, 368 U.S. 424 (1962), addresses failure of formal allocution absent an affirmative request and reserves aggravating circumstances. United States v. Timmreck, 441 U.S. 780 (1979), concerns a technical Rule 11 defect without a claimed different plea or relevant ignorance and likewise preserves more aggravated cases. Davis v. United States, 417 U.S. 333 (1974), allows a statutory no-offense claim on collateral review; it is not a rule excluding every statutory claim. Cuyler v. Adams, 449 U.S. 433 (1981), treats the congressionally approved IAD as federal law. United States v. Mauro, 436 U.S. 340 (1978), requires attention to substance of persistent, timely objections rather than only a particular statutory label.

For the participating Justices' positions, use actual Fex (Article III receipt), Withrow (the limits of Stone's Fourth Amendment exclusionary-rule restriction), Keeney (development of facts/default), Brecht (collateral harmless error) and Doggett (constitutional delay/prejudice) from their existing actual authority. These distinct doctrines cannot be treated as one generic finality rule. Withrow in particular must be consulted before importing the Seventh Circuit's extension of Stone. The official argument is used here only for counsel's factual/legal concessions and arguments; Justice questions are not votes or committed positions.

### 5 U.S.C. section 556(d)

[1994 Code source](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-1994-title5-section556&num=0&edition=1994).

> (d) Except as otherwise provided by statute, the proponent of a rule or order has the burden of proof. Any oral or documentary evidence may be received, but the agency as a matter of policy shall provide for the exclusion of irrelevant, immaterial, or unduly repetitious evidence. A sanction may not be imposed or rule or order issued except on consideration of the whole record or those parts thereof cited by a party and supported by and in accordance with the reliable, probative, and substantial evidence. The agency may, to the extent consistent with the interests of justice and the policy of the underlying statutes administered by the agency, consider a violation of section 557(d) of this title sufficient grounds for a decision adverse to a party who has knowingly committed such violation or knowingly caused such violation to occur. A party is entitled to present his case or defense by oral or documentary evidence, to submit rebuttal evidence, and to conduct such cross-examination as may be required for a full and true disclosure of the facts. In rule making or determining claims for money or benefits or applications for initial licenses an agency may, when a party will not be prejudiced thereby, adopt procedures for the submission of all or part of the evidence in written form.

### 28 U.S.C. section 2254(a)–(c)

[1994 Code source](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-1994-title28-section2254&num=0&edition=1994).

> (a) The Supreme Court, a Justice thereof, a circuit judge, or a district court shall entertain an application for a writ of habeas corpus in behalf of a person in custody pursuant to the judgment of a State court only on the ground that he is in custody in violation of the Constitution or laws or treaties of the United States.
> (b) An application for a writ of habeas corpus in behalf of a person in custody pursuant to the judgment of a State court shall not be granted unless it appears that the applicant has exhausted the remedies available in the courts of the State, or that there is either an absence of available State corrective process or the existence of circumstances rendering such process ineffective to protect the rights of the prisoner.
> (c) An applicant shall not be deemed to have exhausted the remedies available in the courts of the State, within the meaning of this section, if he has the right under the law of the State to raise, by any available procedure, the question presented.
