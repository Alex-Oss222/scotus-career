QUESTIONS PRESENTED FOR REVIEW 


1. Whether a state court injunction placing a thirty-six foot 
buffer zone around an abortion clinic which prohibits peaceful pro- 
life speech in a traditional public forum is an unconstitutional 
content-based restriction on free speech and association. 


2. Whether a state court injunction creating a consent 
requirement before speech is permitted within a three-hundred foot 
buffer zone around an abortion clinic and residential areas is a 
reasonable time, place, and manner restriction or an unconstitutional 
prior restraint on free speech. 


3. Whether a state court injunction prohibiting named 
demonstrators and those acting “in concert" from expressing 
peaceful speech within several designated buffer zones violates the 
first amendment’s protection of freedom of speech and association.

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
A. The Amended Permanent Injunction 


Petitioners challenge the constitutionality of an Amended 
Permanent Injunction (hereinafter Injunction") entered on April 8, 
1993, by the Honorable Robert B. McGregor, Circuit Judge in the 
Eighteenth Judicial Circuit of Florida, against Operation Rescue, 
other pro-life groups, individuals, Petitioners, and those acting “in 
concert.” 


In this case Respondents are abortion clinics located in Central 
Florida. Petitioners are opposed to abortion. Certain individuals 
demonstrated outside the Aware Woman Center for Choice 
(hereinafter "Aware" or the "Clinic") expressing their opposition to 
abortion. Petitioners were not associated with those protesters or 
with Operation Rescue. Respondents requested injunctive relief 
enjoining Petitioners and others from trespassing, blocking, or 
obstructing ingress into or egress from Respondents’ abortion 
facilities. 


On July 30, 1992, the parties stipulated that Petitioners had 
never blocked access to the clinics. On September 30, 1992, the 
court entered an order granting a permanent injunction against the 
Petitioners and others. J.A. 5. The 1992 permanent injunction 
prohibits trespassing on, sitting in, blocking, impeding or 
obstructing ingress into or egress from any abortion clinic in 
Brevard and Seminole County, physically abusing persons entering, 
leaving, or using any services of any abortion facility. J.A. 9. 


On March 22, 1993, only Aware moved for a modification of 
the permanent injunction. On April 8, 1993, the court entered the 
Amended Permanent Injunction which is the subject of this action. 
The 1993 Injunction incorporates the language of the 1992 
permanent injunction but adds a thirty-six foot zone around the 


4 


Clinic property in which is contained a public sidewalk, right-of- 
way, and highway. The Injunction also adds a three-hundred foot 
zone around the Clinic and the residences of any employee, staff, 
owner, or agent of the Clinic. The Injunction further forbids 
singing, chanting, whistling, shouting, yelling, sounds, images 
observable, leafletting, congregating, picketing, patrolling, and 
demonstrating. It restricts speech that is intimidating, encouraging, 
or inciting. Non-threatening speech is permitted only with those 
who show a desire to communicate by approaching, inquiring or 
inviting contact. Speech must end when the listener expresses 
words that indicate a desire to end the contact, or words or actions 
of similar import. J.A. 56-64. 


B. The Decision of the Supreme Court of Florida 


On October 28, 1993, in Operation Rescue, et al. v. Women’s 
Health Center, Inc., et al., 626 So. 2d 664 (Fla. 1993), the Florida 
Supreme Court concluded that the Injunction encompassed a 
traditional public forum, was content neutral, and was a permissible 
time, place, and manner restriction. The court noted the Injunction 
could be narrowed, but declined to narrow its reach. The court 
rejected the prior restraint argument concluding that a prior restraint 
challenge was inapplicable in the absence of a content-based 
restriction. 


Rejecting a “hallenge based on vagueness or overbreadth, the 
court stated that sounds or images observable to or within earshot 
of the patients inside the Clinic" was sufficiently specific to give 
proper notice of what would be considered illegal. As to 
overbreadth, the court stated that the phrase "in active concert or 
participation” created no danger that the Injunction would be 
unfairly enforced. The court recognized Cheffer v. McGregor, 6 
F.3d 705 (11th Cir. 1993), but without comment. Pet. App. C. The 
Cheffer case involved a person not a party to the Injunction who 
feared arrest because it had been applied to anyone expressing a 
pro-life view through the phrase “in concert." On October 20, 
1993, the Eleventh Circuit Court of Appeals held the Injunction was 
a viewpoint-based restriction in a traditional public forum. The 


5 


court noted that a viewpoint-specific restriction in a traditional 
public forum is unconstitutional unless it is necessary to serve a 
compelling state interest and it is narrowly drawn to achieve that 
end. The Injunction did not meet either test. Pet. App. C. 


SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


The Injunction violates Petitioners’ First and Fourteenth 
Amendment rights. As applied and on its face, the Injunction is 
content and viewpoint-based, is not necessary to serve a compelling 
State interest, nor narrowly drawn to achieve that end. The 
Injunction fails the O'Brien and the time, place, and manner tests. 
The Injunction is also overbroad and vague. Subjecting numerous 
people not associated with those named in the Injunction to arrest 
proves that the Injunction is substantially overbroad. Since free 
speech is at stake, precision of drafting and clarity is essential. The 
Injunction also acts as a Classic prior restraint and violates 
Petitioners’ right to free association. 


The Injunction is content and viewpoint-based. Judge 
McGregor admitted the Injunction censors only pro-life speech. 
Since the Injunction prohibits “images observable,” enforcement 
requires a content analysis. A “For Sale” sign next to the Clinic is 
permitted, but a “Choose Life” sign violates the Injunction. The 
Injunction allows only invited speech of a “non-threatening” nature. 
The term “non-threatening” is content-based because speech is 
restricted by the reaction of the listener. 


The Injunction is also viewpoint-based. Not only does the 
Injunction violate a broad class of speech (abortion), but it further 
restricts a subclass of that speech (anti-abortion). The Injunction 
has licensed one side of the debate, allowing pro-choice but not pro- 
life speech. If an “agent” of the Clinic or a person “seeking the 
services of the Clinic” is not "interested" in the content of the 
speech, then that person can censor the speech. Censorship occurs 
based on whether the speech is opposed to abortion. Government 
may not regulate in ways that favor some viewpoints at the expense 
of others. 


6 


The Injunction is not necessary and is not narrowly drawn to 
achieve a compelling state interest. One of the stated interests is the 
right to abortion. The right of Petitioners is the right to free 
speech. There is no evidence that any woman was prevented from 
obtaining an abortion. If reasonable state regulations designed to 
persuade a woman to choose childbirth over abortion will be upheld, 
then individual free speech of similar persuasion cannot be 
prohibited. The clash is between an actual prohibition of speech 
and a potential hindrance to the free exercise of abortion. However, 
the state already has numerous laws protecting personal safety, 
public order, free flow of traffic, and property rights. The 1992 
permanent injunction, which is not challenged here, protects the 
interests of the Clinic and patients while not restricting free speech. 
Likewise, Sections (1) and (2) of the Injunction rightfully balance 
these interests, but Sections (3) through (9) squelch free speech and 
are not necessary to achieve any governmental interests. The 
Injunction is not necessary, not narrowly tailored and not the least 
restrictive means. 


The Injunction also fails the O’Brien and the time, place, and 
manner tests. The O'Brien test is inapplicable since the Injunction 
regulates speech. The time, place, and manner test has never been 
applied by this Court to injunctions. However, even if this test 
were appropriate, the Injunction is anything but reasonable. It acts 
as a prior restraint and proscribes more speech than is necessary to 
achieve any stated interest. The Injunction flatly bans speech within 
the thirty-six foot zone, images observable, and all other speech 
outside the thirty-six foot zone at the discretion of the listener. No 
other alternative means of communication is available to Petitioners. 


The Injunction requires the arrest of all pro-life persons 
penetrating the forbidden zone. While Sections (1) and (2) apply to 
otherwise illegal activities and thus do not violate constitutional 
liberties, Sections (3) through (9) provide no tangible criteria for 
enforcement officials to follow. 


The Injunction prohibits sounds, whether willful or not, and 
images which can be seen within the Clinic. The three-hundred foot 


7 


radius, encompassing 6.49 acres, applies to the Clinic and to 
residential areas. It further prohibits "crowding" and language that 
is “intimidating,” “harassing,” or “encouraging.” The Injunction 
further applies outside the three-hundred foot zone, allowing speech 
only if the listener shows a positive interest. 


The Injunction is vague. The phrase “acting in concert" is 
problematic for law enforcement officials. Police officers have 
understood the Injunction to require the arrest of any pro-life 
individual entering the forbidden zone. Clinic "agents" point out 
pro-life individuals for arrest. The Injunction restricts free 
expression around “agents,” but never defines the term. It prohibits 
speech “during surgical procedures and recovery periods” without 
stating when these times begin or end. The Injunction prohibits all 
"images observable." A pro-life person is prohibited from 
approaching any person "seeking the services of the Clinic." A pro- 
life speaker is not able to determine who is "seeking the services of 
the Clinic" one-hundred yards away. If permission to speak is 
granted, the conversation must be of a “non-threatening” nature. 
"Sidewalk counseling” is prohibited but is never defined. While 
allowing “invited contact,” a pro-life speaker is unable to determine 
when consent to speak has ended. 


The Injunction acts as a classic prior restraint. It flatly 
prohibits speech within the thirty-six foot zone and outside the 
thirty-six foot zone if it is “intimidating,” “encouraging,” not 
“invited,” or if the listener has no "interest" in the content. The 
threat of prosecution under the Injunction chills speech. 


The breadth of the Injunction violates Petitioners’ right to free 
association by penalizing all persons having a pro-life viewpoint by 
eradicating group advocacy. Petitioners are lumped together with 
certain named and unnamed individuals simply because they share 
a pro-life belief. Petitioners are not members of Operation Rescue, 
yet the alleged acts of Operation Rescue are imputed to Petitioners. 
Petitioners have not been disruptive, nor have they created violence, 
but because of some other alleged disruptive activity Petitioners 
have lost their right to speak and associate. The Injunction places 


8 


a blanket prohibition on pro-life association. 


The 1992 permanent injunction, which is not challenged here, 
protects the Clinic’s interest without infringing Petitioners’ free 
speech. The 1993 Injunction goes beyond the interest asserted and 
violates the first amendment. This Court should therefore find the 
Injunction unconstitutional, and in so doing, the 1992 permanent 
injunction will remain to protect any government interest. 


ARGUMENT 
I. 


THE INJUNCTION VIOLATES PETITIONERS’ 
RIGHT TO FREE SPEECH. 


The founding fathers “believed that freedom to think as you 
will and to speak as you think are means indispensable to the 
discovery and spread of political truth... . Believing the power

Amendment, it is that government may not prohibit the expression 
of an idea simply because society finds the idea itself offensive or 
disagreeable." Simon & Schuster v. New York Crime Victims 
Board, 112 S.Ct. 501, 509 (1991). 


Clearly the "government may not grant the use of a forum to 
people whose views it finds acceptable, but deny use to those 
wishing to express less favorable or more controversial views." 
Police Department of Chicago v. Mosley, 408 U.S. 92, 95-96

School District, 113 S.Ct. 2141 (1993); Widmar v. Vincent, 454

tolerated. See Forsyth County v. The Nationalist Movement, 112 
S.Ct. 2395, 2404 (1992); Regan v. Time, Inc., 468 U.S. 641, 648- 
49 (1984). Stressing its disdain for content-based prohibitions, this 
Court stated that "[a]ny restriction on expressive activity because of

D. The Injunction Creates An Unlawful Prior Restraint. 


“Temporary restraining orders and permanent injunctions--i.e., 


38 


court orders that actually forbid speech activities--are classic 
examples of prior restraints." Alexander v. United States, 113 S.Ct. 
2766, 2771 (1993). Historically, the term "prior restraint is used 
‘to describe administrative and judicial orders forbidding certain 
communications when issued in advance of the time that such 
communications are to occur.’" /d. Any prior restraint bears a 
"heavy presumption" against its constitutional validity. See Bantam 
Books, Inc. v. Sullivan, 372 U.S. 58, 70 (1963). The state must 
meet a “heavy burden" to impose a prior restraint on speech. See 
Nebraska Press Association v. Stuart, 427 U.S. 539 (1976); New 
York Times Co., 403 U.S. 713; Vance v. Universal Amusement Co., 
Inc., 445 U.S. 308, 315-16 (1980). The presumption against prior 
restraints is heavier, and the degree of protection broader, than that 
against limits on expression imposed by criminal statutes. Society 
"prefers to punish the few who abuse rights of speech after they 
break the law than to throttle them and all others beforehand." 
Conrad, 420 U.S. at 559-60 (emphasis in original). 


"In determining the extent of the constitutional protection, it 
has been generally, if not universally, considered that it is the chief 
purpose of the (first amendment) to prevent previous restraints upon 
publication." Near v. Minnesota, 283 U.S. 697, 713 (1931). In 
Lovell, this Court reaffirmed that "the prevention of [prior 
restraints] was a leading purpose in the adoption of the [first 
amendment]." 303 U.S. at 451. 


"Prior restraint upon speech suppresses the precise freedom 
which the First Amendment sought to protect against abridgement. " 
Carroll, 393 U.S. at 181. “The teaching of our cases is that, 
because only a judicial determination in an adversary proceeding 
insures the necessary sensitivity to freedom of expression, only a 
procedure requiring a judicial determination suffices to impose a 
valid final restraint." Freedman v. Maryland, 380 U.S. 51, 58 
(1965); see also Vance, 445 U.S. at 308.
