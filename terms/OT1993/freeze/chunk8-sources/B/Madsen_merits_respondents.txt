QUESTIONS PRESENTED FOR REVIEW 


1. Whether an injunction designed to protect workers and 
patients at a medical facility from conduct that inhibits access 
to that facility and creates health risks for individuals undergo- 
ing sensitive medical procedures is a valid time, place and man- 
ner regulation under the First Amendment. 


2. Whether an injunction restricting only unprotected ac- 
tivity should be struck down as overbroad because its terms 
might be read literally to reach some protected expression. 


3. Whether an injunction’s detailed provisions, read in light 
of previous unprotected activity that prompted the injunction, 
must be voided for vagueness.

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


This case involves a sustained effort by anti-abortion forces to 
shut down the only women’s health clinic in Brevard County, Florida, 
the Aware Woman Center for Choice in Melbourne (‘‘Clinic’’). The 
campaign against Aware Woman and other abortion clinics in Cen- 
tral Florida began in 1991. Rescue America sent out a leaflet an- 
nouncing the arrival of Operation Rescue-National in Central Florida. 
R. 9-10. Petitioners Madsen, Martin and Hobbs, who are officers 
of Rescue America, were identified as the persons to call for more 
information about Operation Rescue’s activities in Florida. R. 9. 


In 1991, when activities directed toward the Clinic became too 
disruptive, the Clinic brought this action, which resulted in entry 
of a temporary injunction in 1991 followed by a permanent injunc- 
tion on September 30, 1992. The 1992 injunction was based on find- 
ings that ‘‘[Petitioners] are individuals and organizations, acting in

2. The Injunction Seeks to Regulate Only the 
Secondary Effects, Not the Content, of 
Petitioners’ Speech. 


The injunction also should be upheld because it is aimed at 
regulating the ‘‘secondary effects,’’ rather than the content of 
speech. The secondary effects rationale permits the regulation of 
an identifiable category of speech if the regulation is justified 
‘*’ without reference to the content of the regulated speech.’’’ Ci- 
ty of Renton v. Playtime Theaters, Inc., 475 U.S. 41, 48 (1986) (cita- 
tion omitted) (upholding zoning regulation that applied only to adult 
movie theaters). In this respect, the secondary effects rationale com- 
plements the speech/conduct distinction discussed in the previous 
section, since the main point of a secondary effects regulation is 
to address the non-communicative aspects of expression. Likewise, 
many of the secondary effects targeted by an injunction such as the 
one at issue in this case will result from the conduct of Petitioners, 
not their expression. 


However, the secondary effects rationale goes beyond the 
speech/conduct distinction because it permits even the incidental 


29 


regulation of pure speech if the regulation is justified without regard 
to the content of that speech. 


We spoke in [Renton] only of secondary effects of speech, 
referring to regulations that apply to a particular category 
of speech because the regulatory targets happen to be 
associated with that type of speech. So long as the 
justifications for regulation have nothing to do with con- 
tent, . . . we concluded that the regulation was properly 
analyzed as content neutral. 


Boos v. Barry, 485 U.S. 312, 320 (1988) (plurality opinion) (em- 
phasis omitted). See also Clark v. Community for Creative Non- 
Violence, 468 U.S. 288, 298 n.8 (1984) (‘‘Reasonable time, place, 
or rnanner restrictions are valid even though they directly limit oral 
or written expression.’’). 


Although Petitioners cite Boos repeatedly to support their posi- 
tion in this case, see Petitioners’ Brief 12, 14-16, 18, 19, 30, they 
fail to note the crucial difference between the regulation struck down 
in that case and the injunction at issue here. In Boos, the Court 
struck down a prohibition on placing signs within 500 feet of an em- 
bassy because the alleged ‘‘secondary effect’’ offered to justify the 
prohibition depended entirely on the listener’s agreement or 
disagreement with the content of the signs. Boos, 485 U.S. at 321 
(plurality opinion). However, Justice O’Connor specifically noted

interference with ingress or egress, . . . visual clutter, [and] the 
need to protect the security of embassies.’’ Jd.

such things as the threat to patients’ health caused by the surging

stitutes an ‘‘as applied’ challenge, not a claim of overbreadth. The 
overbreadth doctrine voids a law in its entirety if the law ‘‘does 
not aim specifically at evils within the allowable area of [govern- 
mental) control, but . . . sweeps within its ambit other activities 
that . . . constitute an exercise’ of First Amendment rights. Thorn- 
hill v. Alabama, 310 U.S. 88, 97 (1940). Permitting parties whose 
own conduct may not be protected to mount a facial challenge serves 
‘‘the interest in preventing an invalid statute from inhibiting the 
speech of third parties who are not before the Court.’’ City Coun- 
cil of Los Angeles v. Taxpayers for Vincent, 466 U.S. 789, 800 (1984). 


Petitioners is the right of free speech. Thus, if restrictions — such as 
reasonable time, place and manner regulations — may be imposed on the 
underlying right of free speech, the nght of free association is necessarily 
limited to the same extent. The injunction in this case does not deny anyone 
their rights ‘‘merely because an individual belonged to a group,’’ NAACP 
v. Claiborne Hardware Co., 458 U.S. 886, 920 (1982), nor does it make 
‘peaceable assembly for lawful discussion’’ a crime. De Jonge v. Oregon, 
299 U.S. 353, 365 (1937). As shown in this section, the injunction simply 
limits the extent to which Petitioners and those acting in concert with them 
may impede access to medical care, obstruct traffic, harass Clinic person- 
nel, and engage in other unprotected activities. If Petitioners have no con- 
stitutional right to do these things individually, they certainly have no con- 
stitutional right to do these things in a large group.

In this instance, however, allowing Petitioners to invoke the nghts 
of others would not advance that interest. It is Petitioners and those 
acting in concert with them, not hypothetical third parties, who wish 
to penetrate the three-hundred foot buffer zone, Petitioners’ Brief

engage in activities prohibited by sections (3) through (9) of the in- 
junction. Id. at 27-30. See Brockett v. Spokane Arcades, Inc., 472 
U.S. 491, 504 (1985) (action for invalidation of entire statute inap- 
propriate where ‘‘the parties challenging the statute are those who 


desire to engage in protected speech that the overbroad statute 
purports to punish’’). Only to the extent that the injunction forbids

Petitioners correctly recite the principle that an injunction is un-
