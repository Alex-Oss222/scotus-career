INTEREST OF AMICUS CURIAE 


The Lawyers’ Committee for Civil Rights Under Law 
was organized in 1963 at the request of the President of 
the United States to involve private attorneys throughout 
the country in the national effort to assure civil rights to 
all Americans. Protection of the voting rights of racial 
and language minorities is an important part of the Com- 


2 


mittee’s work. The Committee has provided legal repre- 
sentation to litigants in numerous voting rights cases 
throughout the nation over the past thirty years, and has 
frequently represented litigants before this Court, see, 
e.g., Clark v. Roemer, 111 S. Ct. 2096 (1991); Clinton 
v. Smith, 488 U.S. 988 (1988); Connor v. Finch, 431 
U.S. 407 (1977). The Committee has also participated 
as amicus curiae in other significant voting rights cases in 
this Court, see, e.g., Thornburg v. Gingles, 478 U.S. 30 
(1986); Rogers v. Lodge, 458 U.S. 613 (1982); City 
of Mobile v. Bolden, 446 U.S. 55 (1980). 


SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


The Eleventh Circuit properly concluded that the Vot- 
ing Rights Act permits minority voters to challenge as 
dilutive Bleckley County’s governance by a single county 
commissioner, rather than a multi-member board of com- 
missioners. Without any basis in the statute or legislative 
history, petitioners ask this Court to carve out an excep- 
tion from Section 2 for single-member offices. This at- 
tempt conflicts with this Court’s rejection of such an ex- 
ception in Houston Lawyers’ Ass'n v. Attorney General 
of Texas, 111 S. Ct. 2376, 2381 (1991), and conflicts 
with this Ccurt’s analogous Section 5 jurisprudence. The 
Act’s language, legislative history, and purpose demon- 
Strate that Congress intended the Act to cover all elec- 
tions and to eliminate any structure or procedure that 
results in minority vote dilution. 


The concentrated power of Bleckley County’s sole 
commissioner form of government is an extreme form of 
at-large election that is non-existent in other states and 
highly unusual even in Georgia. In determining whether 
minority votes were diluted by this siructure, the Eleventh 
Circuit properiy considered a five-member commission 
as a benchmark for measuring vote dilution because the 
use of a five-member commission is firmly rooted in state 
law and practice in Georgia. Indeed, Georgia law spe- 
cifically authorizes Bleckley County to adopt a five- 


3 


member commission. Because respondents are seeking 
an alteration of the election system that would conform 
ihe practice in Bleckley County to the practice existing 
throughout most of Georgia, it is simply unrealistic to 
argue that altering the sole-commissioner government 
would be unduly disruptive of established state policy. 


Determining an accepted benchmark for a vote dilu- 
tion claim is not a novel exercise under the Voting Rights 
Act. In the context of challenges to at-large, multi- 
member election schemes, the accepted benchmark is a 
voting plan with single-member districts. Similarly, when 
a five-member commission form of government is in gen- 
eral use throughout a state, that standard-sized commis- 
sion provides an available benchmark for a challenge to 
a sole-commissioner form of government. 


Permitting respondents to challenge the discriminatory 
impact of Bleckley County’s sole-commissioner govern- 
ment, therefore, creates no right to proportional rep- 
resentation, contrary to petitioners’ argument. Georgia's 
common practice of using a five-member commission, and 
the availability of this state-law benchmark for respon- 
dents’ vote dilution claim, means that recognizing re- 
spondents’ claim does not create an unlimited right to 
alter the size of the elected body arbitrarily until one or 
more majority black districts can be created. 


Finally, elections for single-member offices may give 
rise to a number of Section 2 claims. The violation al- 
leged in this case is one example of the many ways a 
singie-member office may violate Section 2. Single- 
member offices may be coupled with a wide variety of 
mechanisms that dilute the votes of minority groups— 
such as majority vote requirements, discriminatory slat- 
ing, and the establishment of difficult registration and vot- 
ing procedures. Federal courts must retain the authority 
to eliminate dilutive mechanisms in a manner that gives 
minority voters an equal opportunity to participate in the 
political process and to clect representatives of their 
choice.

A. This Court Has Held That There Is No Single- 
Member Office Exception to Section 2. 


Petitioners urge this Court to adopt a “single-member 
office” exception to the Voting Rights Act. They argue 
that if minority groups are not able to elect their pre- 
ferred candidate to a single-member office, vote dilution 
cannot occur because “there can be no equal opportunity 
for representation within an office filled by only one per- 
son.” Pet. Br. at 19. This view is irreconcilable with 
Section 2.’ 


In Houston Lawyers’ Ass'n v. Attorney General of 
Texas, 111 S. Ct. 2376, 2381 (1991), this Court squarely 
held that there is no “single-member office” exception to 
Section 2. Plaintiffs challenged as dilutive the at-large, 
countywide method of electing Texas trial court judges in 
ten counties. An en banc majority of the Fifth Circuit 
held that the results test of Section 2 did not apply to 
judicial elections since they were not “representatives” 
within the statutory language of Section 2. 914 F.2d 620 
(Sth Cir. 1990). In his concurrence, Judge Higginbotham 
characterized the trial judges “as single-office holders 
instead of members of a multi-member body” because 
each exercises his or her authority independent of the 
other judges serving in the same area or on the same 
court. 914 F.2d at 649. Relying upon the same case 
petitioners rely upon here, Judge Higginbotham advocated

D. The Slippery Slope Arguments Raised by Peti- 
tioners and the Solicitor General Ignore the Careful 
Limits This Court Placed Upon Vote Dilution Chal- 
lenges Under the Voting Rights Act. 


In the final analysis, petitioners and the Solicitor Gen- 
eral clearly view the choice of an acceptable benchmark 
in isolation—as if that decision alone may satisfy re- 
spondents’ burden of proof. Gingles, however, articulated 
three preconditions that respondents must satisfy. Once 
the threshold inquiry is made, federal courts then inquire 
into the “totality of the circumstances.” This inquiry con- 
templates a “searching practical evaluation of the ‘past 
and present reality’ of the community and a “ ‘func- 
tional’ view of the political process.” Gingles, 478 U.S. 
at 45. “ ‘This determination is peculiarly dependent on 
the facts of each case,’ and requires ‘an intensely local 
appraisal of the design and impact’ of the contested elec- 
toral mechanisms.” /d. at 79 (citation omitted). This 
is precisely why this Court concluded that single-member 
office concerns may be relevant to the “totality of the cir- 
cumstances.” Houston Lawyers’, 111 S. Ct. at 2380. 


Consistent with this analytical framework, Bleckley 
County's single-member county commission dramatically 
demonstrates minority vote dilution. As the Eleventh 
Circuit recognized, the black population in Bleckley 
County resides in a contiguous area that could easily 
constitute a majority of the electorate in one of five 
single-member districts. Pet. App. at 74. The court also 
found substantial evidence that blacks in Bleckley County 
are politically cohesive and that the white majority usually 
votes as a bloc. /d. at 75-85. Finally, the violation was 
well-supported by the extremely limited electoral success

of blacks and Bleckley County's long history of racial 
discrimination. /d. The use of a sole-commissioner gov- 
ernment was thus not the only basis for the finding that 
the election structure unlawfully dilutes minority voting 
strength and violates Section 2; rather, that finding de- 
pended upon application of all of the factors relevant 
under Section 2's “totality of the circumstances” test. It 
is precisely a finding on these factors that will constrain 
courts from sliding down the slippery slope the petition- 
ers fear.

As established above, it is clear that the Eleventh Cir- 
cuit properly concluded that respondents satisfied the first 
Gingles precondition since blacks could constitute a ma-

creasing the size of the governing body, however, plain- 
tiffs may still challenge election features having a racially 
discriminatory effect. 


Essential to the concept of minority vote dilution is the 
interaction between white bloc voting against minority- 
supported candidates and electoral mechanisms that have 
a potentially vote dilutive effect. A single-member office 
may be coupled with a wide variety of practices that are 
intended to, or have the effect of, diluting the votes of 
minorities. Federal courts must retain the authority to 
eliminate this vote dilution in a way that gives minority 
voters an equal opportunity to participate in the political 
process and to elect representatives of their choice.”
