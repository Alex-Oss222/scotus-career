## 85 — Gottshall and Carlisle

### Direct lower-court sources and distinct postures

* Gottshall v. Consolidated Rail Corp., 988 F.2d 355 (3d Cir. February 8, 1993), rehearing denied March 11, 1993: [CAP full text](https://static.case.law/f2d/988/html/0355-01.html). The majority reversed summary judgment and remanded for trial. Judge Roth concurred in recognition that some negligent emotional-injury claims can be cognizable under FELA but dissented from allowing this record to proceed. Her objection concerned foreseeability and breach, not an absolute bar on all such injuries.
* Carlisle v. Consolidated Rail Corp., 990 F.2d 90 (3d Cir. March 29, 1993): [CAP full text](https://static.case.law/f2d/990/html/0090-01.html). The panel affirmed the denial of judgment notwithstanding the verdict and a new trial after a $386,500 jury verdict.
* Carlisle v. Consolidated Rail Corp., 790 F. Supp. 521 (E.D. Pa. 1992): [CAP full text](https://static.case.law/f-supp/790/html/0521-01.html). The posttrial opinion supplies the trial court's explanation, references to the negligence charge, quotations from the limiting charge, and evidentiary rulings.

### Gottshall: what the summary-judgment record does and does not establish

The track crew performed heavy work in severe heat. The record included temperatures above 95 degrees, a rail temperature of 118 degrees, a crew with several older or overweight workers, pressure to complete work, and communication failure because the radio base had been taken out of service without notice to the crew. Water was available; the record should not be rewritten as an absolute deprivation of water. The extent of breaks, shade, and the significance of usual railway work conditions are disputed in the lower opinions. See 988 F.2d at 357–61, 374–83.

Gottshall's friend of approximately fifteen years, Richard Johns, collapsed. After the initial episode, the supervisor directed the others back to work. Johns later collapsed again and died despite Gottshall's prolonged resuscitation efforts. Emergency personnel left the covered body pending the coroner. The lower opinions differ over whose instructions required leaving it there; do not convert the majority's attribution of the crew's continued exposure to Conrail into an undisputed fact. Evidence described subsequent severe psychiatric illness with physical manifestations. These are allegations and evidence viewed in the summary-judgment posture, not jury findings against Conrail.

The Third Circuit majority treated genuineness of the injury and ordinary negligence as distinct inquiries. Physical manifestations, the longstanding friendship, and active rescue bore on the former. The alleged excessive working conditions, notice, communication failure, and handling of the emergency bore on duty, breach, foreseeability, and causation. The majority concluded there were triable issues; that conclusion is the lower judgment under review. Roth's separate opinion argued that the usual duties and conditions did not establish foreseeable breach and that the psychiatric response was not reasonably foreseeable from the communication failure. Neither position establishes a fact beyond dispute.

### Carlisle: negligence evidence is separate from proof of genuine injury

Carlisle's case concerned a combination of staffing reductions, obsolete equipment, unusually prolonged shifts, additional responsibilities, and repeated notice of safety problems. Testimony described 12-to-15-hour workdays, staffing reduced from eleven to four, dispatch and supervisory responsibilities and later trainmaster duties, and an abusive supervisor with an alcohol problem. Carlisle complained to management. Evidence included medical attribution of his breakdown to working conditions. The defense medical evidence included an adjustment disorder at least partly caused by the work. See 990 F.2d at 91–93, 96–98; 790 F. Supp. at 523–29.

The appellate opinion separately examined ordinary FELA negligence: a duty to provide reasonably safe working conditions, foreseeability/notice, breach, and causal contribution. It did not infer negligence solely from a diagnostic label or severe distress. The district court states that the jury was charged on duty, breach, causation, and foreseeability, citing N.T. May 20, 1991, 157–62. See 790 F. Supp. at 526–27. The appellate sufficiency discussion asks whether evidence supported a finding that Conrail knew or should have known the extraordinary conditions could cause injury and nevertheless failed to respond. The verdict posture requires review of the supported record under the selected legal standard.

Coworker depositions were admitted **for notice to Conrail**, not as substantive proof that Conrail had caused those coworkers' ailments. The district court quoted a limiting instruction making that distinction. The supplied neutral packet's reference to comparable conditions causing coworker illness must therefore be read as notice evidence and witnesses' accounts, not an adjudicated causal fact. See 790 F. Supp. at 530–31; 990 F.2d at 97–98.

The 1974 Federal Railroad Administration report concerning dispatcher stress and the 1987 report concerning Conrail's Philadelphia operation were also admitted as evidence of notice. These general safety materials were distinguished from the accident-specific reports covered by 45 U.S.C. §41's exclusion. See 990 F.2d at 97–98 & nn.9–10. Their admission did not itself decide negligence.

The district court quotes important limitations actually given to the jury: a few days' temporary reassignment with resulting job stress would not alone suffice; inadequate help, denied vacation, understaffing, or fear for others' safety would not alone suffice; disliking trainmaster work because of long or night hours would not alone suffice. The jury had to find emotional or psychological injury and that it resulted from Conrail's negligence by a preponderance of the evidence. See 790 F. Supp. at 532–33 (N.T. 156–57). The trial court described an objective reasonable railway employee standard accounting for the occupation's expected strength, daring, and character, and required serious debilitating manifestations; these are descriptions of the theory tried below, not a proposed new Supreme Court holding. The appellate court expressly reserved purely emotional injury without physical manifestations. 990 F.2d at 98 n.11.

The appellate opinion rejected additional proposed-instruction, contributory-negligence, and counsel-argument claims without extended discussion. 990 F.2d at 92 n.3. The original entire trial-charge transcript has not been independently recovered. The published district opinion verifies that ordinary negligence elements were charged and quotes significant limitations, but does not reproduce every word of the charge at N.T. 157–62. A claim that some unquoted phrase misstated duty or foreseeability would require the actual transcript; the absence of that transcript does not erase the published sufficiency discussion or the quoted instructions.

### Verified earlier authority, with Justice-specific scope

Atchison, Topeka & Santa Fe Railway v. Buell, 480 U.S. 557 (1987), was a unanimous opinion by Justice Stevens, joined by all sitting Justices, including Blackmun, O'Connor, and Scalia. The full opinion is saved as `Buell.pdf` and `Buell.txt` in the source archive. It holds that availability of Railway Labor Act grievance procedures does not displace an otherwise cognizable FELA injury claim. It does **not** resolve the precise boundaries of recovery for emotional injury. Its discussion stresses distinctions among the nature of the tort, injury, and factual context. References to unconscionable abuse in discussion of intentional-infliction authorities are not a holding that every negligence action has that additional element. 480 U.S. at 562–71. This provides each named joining Justice's actual earlier commitment at that level; it supplies no vote in the present cases.

Monessen Southwestern Railway v. Morgan, 486 U.S. 330 (1988), was written by Justice White. Stevens, Scalia, and Kennedy joined the whole opinion. Blackmun joined Parts I, II-A, and III but dissented from Part II-B, joined by Marshall. O'Connor joined Parts I and II but dissented from Part III, joined by Rehnquist. The full opinion is saved as `Monessen.pdf` and `Monessen.txt`.

Part II-B treats prejudgment interest as a federal FELA question and relies on the established common-law background, longstanding uniform construction, and Congress's failure to alter that construction. It is not a holding that every tort boundary is permanently frozen in 1908. Blackmun's separate writing argues from full compensation, the remedial purpose, and evolving remedies, while distinguishing interest on past economic losses from pain and suffering or future losses. Part III preserves the jury's role in estimating present value and rejects automatic application of a state total-offset rule; O'Connor's separate opinion favors allowing a deliberately chosen reasonable presumption absent a showing that another method is more appropriate. These writings support precise comparisons of statutory method, common-law development, and the jury's role; they do not decide this emotional-injury question. 486 U.S. at 335–55.

## 86 — Shannon

### Direct lower judgment and trial record

United States v. Shannon, 981 F.2d 759 (5th Cir. January 12, 1993), is available in [CAP full text](https://static.case.law/f2d/981/html/0759-01.html). The court affirmed the conviction under 18 U.S.C. §922(g)(1). Its rule denied the trial judge discretion to give the proposed consequences instruction; the lower judgment was broader than a conclusion that no instruction was mandatory. It treated the Act as containing no contrary directive. That is the lower court's construction under review.

Shannon had received the gun from his son the preceding day at an airport so the son would not carry it through security. Shannon knew felons could not possess firearms; he said he intended to deliver it to his mother or parole officer. An officer encountered him about 4 a.m. on August 25, 1990. Shannon shot himself in the chest and survived. A competency hearing found him competent to stand trial. Mental illness and legal insanity at the offense remained different issues.

The trial jury was instructed on the statutory insanity defense: because of severe mental disease or defect, inability to appreciate the nature and quality or wrongfulness of the acts, proved by the defendant by clear and convincing evidence. Two experts, Ellis and Roberts, agreed there was chronic mental illness but testified that the statutory insanity standard was not satisfied. The Fifth Circuit reports that Shannon acknowledged the Government had introduced sufficient evidence if believed. These facts are relevant to any ordinary harmless-error inquiry; they do not disclose why the jury returned its verdict or conclusively establish how it would have acted under another instruction. 981 F.2d at 761–62 & n.2.

The requested instructions were: (1) that an NGI verdict required the court to commit Shannon to a suitable facility; alternatively, (2) that the court was required to hospitalize him until satisfied he would not create the specified substantial risk to others or their property. 981 F.2d at 761 n.3. An instruction using the second formulation must be assessed against the whole statutory procedure, including the initial hearing, state transfer, conditional release, and burdens; it cannot promise a particular period of hospitalization. The actual instruction directed the jury to apply the law regardless of consequences and excluded punishment from deliberation. The reported record identifies no affirmative prosecutor/witness representation that an NGI verdict would produce immediate freedom. The appellate court found no demonstrated actual juror misconception. Id. at 764 n.5. The jury had no sentencing task; the court imposed the statutory fifteen-year sentence under §924(e)(1).

### Text, history, and the distinction between discretion and command

Section 4242(b) specifies the three verdict options when insanity is raised by the prescribed notice. Section 4243(a) mandates immediate commitment to a suitable facility until eligibility for release under subsection (e). An examination/report precedes a hearing within forty days. Section 4243(d) imposes different burdens depending on the offense. The serious-injury/property-damage/substantial-risk category requires clear and convincing evidence; the residual category requires a preponderance. In either category the issue is whether release would create the specified substantial risk due to a **present** mental disease or defect. This supplement does not assume which category governs a particular §922(g) charge.

Failure to establish safe release leads to Attorney General custody, with mandatory efforts to transfer responsibility to the appropriate state. Federal hospitalization continues until the state assumes responsibility or mental condition permits safe release or conditional release, whichever occurs first. Subsection (f) provides the director's certificate and court procedure for unconditional or conditional discharge; conditional release must include an approved prescribed regimen and explicit compliance condition. Subsection (g) requires a hearing for revocation based on noncompliance and resulting risk. Subsection (h) restricts furloughs for the specified clear-and-convincing category but permits court approval after notice/opportunity for hearing, emergency departure, or accompaniment by a federal officer. The full operative statutory texts appear below.

The Senate Judiciary Committee Report, S. Rep. No. 98-225, p.240 (1983), describes endorsement of the District of Columbia procedure under which the jury **may** be instructed concerning hospitalization and treatment and says the instruction need not be given when the defendant requests omission within the court's discretion. This is committee-report language, not language enacted in §§4242 or 4243. A source for the quoted report passage is the official Report at 512 U.S. 583; the report itself has not been independently downloaded. No mandatory instruction or mandatory omission should be attributed to this passage merely by changing “may” to “must.”

The relevant lower-court disagreement includes Lyles v. United States, 254 F.2d 725 (D.C. Cir. 1957), United States v. Frank, 956 F.2d 872 (9th Cir. 1991), and United States v. Blume, 967 F.2d 45 (2d Cir. 1992). These are research leads reported in the lower opinion, not separately recovered full opinions here. Frank's distinction concerning correction of a misleading prosecutorial statement must not be converted into proof that one occurred in Shannon. A post-divergence Supreme Court statement concerning certiorari in Frank is not an eligible independent source of simulated law.

## 89 — Williamson

### Record and lower judgment

The Eleventh Circuit judgment at 981 F.2d 1262 (1992) affirmed **without opinion**. The report therefore does not supply an independently articulated panel rationale about the unit of a “statement.” The district court's stated grounds, as reported in the official source at 512 U.S. 596–98, were Harris's unavailability, the penal-interest character of the statements, and corroborating circumstances under United States v. Harrell, 788 F.2d 1524 (11th Cir. 1986). The record does not establish a district-court assertion-by-assertion analysis merely from that formulation.

An officer stopped Harris's weaving rental car; a consent search uncovered approximately nineteen kilograms of cocaine in two suitcases. Harris first gave a telephone account involving an unnamed Cuban and a dumpster. He later recanted that account and said he was transporting the cocaine for Williamson, who was driving ahead, saw the stop, and therefore could not be reached through a controlled delivery. Other evidence included luggage initials associated with Williamson's sister, Williamson's name as an additional driver on the rental agreement, and an envelope/receipt connecting him or his girlfriend's address to the circumstances. These are independent evidence items; their force as corroboration or harmless-error evidence remains a question to evaluate under the relevant rule.

Agent Walton promised to report Harris's cooperation to the prosecutor; Walton said he did not promise a reward or other benefit. Harris expressed fear of Williamson and refused to sign or record his statements. Despite use immunity and an order to testify, Harris refused at trial and was held in contempt. Rule 804(a)(2) covers a declarant who persists in refusing to testify about the subject matter despite a court order. The report describes charges under 21 U.S.C. §§841(a)(1), 846, and 18 U.S.C. §1952. Both the hearsay objection and the separate confrontation claim were raised.

The prosecutor expressly disclaimed reliance on the coconspirator exception, agreeing that the statements were not made in furtherance of the conspiracy. The source quotes the trial exchange at 512 U.S. 610. Do not use Rule 801(d)(2)(E) as an unexamined alternative admission ground. The same quoted exchange includes a conditional willingness to join a mistrial motion if the court concluded admission was improper; it is not an independently verified blanket concession that every possible evidentiary error was prejudicial. The complete trial transcript has not been recovered.

### Rule 804(b)(3) as then written

The following is the rule's operative text, reproduced in the official Report at 512 U.S. 611:

> A statement which was at the time of its making so far contrary to the declarant's pecuniary or proprietary interest, or so far tended to subject the declarant to civil or criminal liability, or to render invalid a claim by the declarant against another, that a reasonable person in the declarant's position would not have made the statement unless believing it to be true. A statement tending to expose the declarant to criminal liability and offered to exculpate the accused is not admissible unless corroborating circumstances clearly indicate the trustworthiness of the statement.

The express corroboration sentence addresses a statement offered to **exculpate** the accused. Any circuit requirement applied to inculpatory statements must be identified as that circuit's construction rather than silently substituted into the rule's text. The rule also covers pecuniary/proprietary interest, civil liability, and invalidation of claims, not only criminal exposure. Its temporal inquiry is the circumstances at the time of making.

The text leaves the live interpretive dispute over whether a generally self-inculpatory narrative admits collateral assertions, and how context bears on an individual assertion's penal-interest character. This supplement supplies no resolution. The distinct Confrontation Clause objection must remain distinct from a Rule 804 holding. Prior opinions downloaded as research candidates (Lee, Bourjaily, Wright) have not been fully read in this source pass and are not represented as newly verified Justice-specific authority here. The entering-law slice remains the source for any already-established simulated Salerno/White propositions.

## 90 — McFarland

### Direct lower judgment and the two proceedings

McFarland v. Collins, 7 F.3d 47 (5th Cir. October 26, 1993), is available in [CAP full text](https://static.case.law/f3d/7/html/0047-01.html). The court granted leave to proceed in forma pauperis but denied a certificate of probable cause, appointment of counsel, and a stay. Its principal ground was that no habeas proceeding was pending under §2251 because the counsel/stay motion was not a habeas application. It also identified §2283 and stated that no exception supported an injunction. As alternative considerations it referred to the absence of a constitutional right to appointed counsel in state collateral proceedings and the failure to identify a substantial constitutional case. Footnote 1 states that treating the motions as a habeas petition would yield dismissal for failure to exhaust. These are actual lower-court grounds, not a merits determination of an identified exhausted federal claim.

McFarland's Texas capital conviction was entered November 13, 1989 and the state appellate judgment is reported at 845 S.W.2d 824 (1992). On September 19, 1993 he filed a pro se state motion seeking time for collateral review; the state trial judge moved execution from September 23 to October 27. On October 16 the resource center reported it had been unable to obtain volunteer counsel and requested appointment. State relief was denied; the Texas Court of Criminal Appeals denied a stay on October 22.

On October 22 McFarland filed the federal pro se motion at issue, expressly identifying a proposed §2254 challenge and requesting counsel under §848(q)(4)(B) and a stay. The district court denied relief on October 25 because no postconviction proceeding had been initiated; the Fifth Circuit acted October 26. No merits petition had been filed **in the proceeding under review**.

Separately, on October 26 an attorney whom a magistrate had located filed a skeletal habeas petition, expressly seeking counsel to investigate and prepare an adequate one. That separate proceeding was not the counsel-motion proceeding reviewed here. The district court found the petition insufficient and denied a stay, although the state did not oppose that stay request. This distinction prevents the overstatement that McFarland had never filed any petition at all. The supplemental facts are reported at 512 U.S. 851–53 & nn.1–2. No present simulated execution order or Supreme Court interim disposition is supplied here.

### Statutory predicates and qualifications

Section 848(q)(4)(B) applies to a financially unable defendant in a postconviction proceeding under §2254 or §2255 seeking to vacate or set aside a death sentence. It entitles that defendant to one or more appointed attorneys and reasonably necessary services under paragraphs (5)–(9). The source of entitlement is this enacted provision; constitutional limits on a general right to state postconviction counsel do not by themselves construe it.

Paragraph (5) prescribes trial experience for appointment before judgment. Paragraph (6) prescribes appellate admission and felony-appeal experience after judgment. Paragraph (7) permits another attorney for good cause with the described alternative qualifications, considering the penalty and the unique complexity of the litigation. Paragraph (8) requires continuing representation unless replaced by similarly qualified counsel, including available postconviction proceedings, applications for stays, competency proceedings, and available clemency. Paragraph (9), as applicable at the cutoff, uses an **ex parte** finding and **shall** authorization for reasonably necessary investigative/expert/other services, plus possible retroactive authorization when timely procurement could not practicably await prior approval. It must not be replaced by later amended text. Paragraph (10) governs compensation and expenses. Full operative paragraphs are reproduced below.

Section 2251's word is **may**: a judge before whom a habeas proceeding is pending may stay the specified state proceeding at the specified stages. Whether a request for statutory counsel makes a proceeding pending is the question to resolve, not a pre-established statutory quotation. If a stay is granted, the covered state proceeding is void; absent a stay it remains valid notwithstanding pending habeas litigation. Section 2283 has three distinct exceptions: express congressional authorization, necessity in aid of jurisdiction, and protection/effectuation of judgments. Recognizing counsel entitlement and identifying stay authority remain separate steps; neither implies an automatic stay.

### 18 U.S.C. §4242

### 18 U.S.C. §4243

Source: [GPO archived 1994 Code](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1994-title18/html/USCODE-1994-title18-partIII-chap313-sec4243.htm).

(a) 
Determination of Present Mental Condition of Acquitted Person
.—If a person is found not guilty only by reason of insanity at the time of the offense charged, he shall be committed to a suitable facility until such time as he is eligible for release pursuant to subsection (e).

(b) 
Psychiatric or Psychological Examination and Report
.—Prior to the date of the hearing, pursuant to subsection (c), the court shall order that a psychiatric or psychological examination of the defendant be conducted, and that a psychiatric or psychological report be filed with the court, pursuant to the provisions of section 4247(b) and (c).

(c) 
Hearing
.—A hearing shall be conducted pursuant to the provisions of section 4247(d) and shall take place not later than forty days following the special verdict.

(d) 
Burden of Proof
.—In a hearing pursuant to subsection (c) of this section, a person found 
not guilty only by reason of insanity of an offense involving bodily injury to, or serious damage to the property of, another person, or involving a substantial risk of such injury or damage, has the burden of proving by clear and convincing evidence that his release would not create a substantial risk of bodily injury to another person or serious damage of property of another due to a present mental disease or defect. With respect to any other offense, the person has the burden of such proof by a preponderance of the evidence.

(e) 
Determination and Disposition
.—If, after the hearing, the court fails to find by the standard specified in subsection (d) of this section that the person's release would not create a substantial risk of bodily injury to another person or serious damage of property of another due to a present mental disease or defect, the court shall commit the person to the custody of the Attorney General. The Attorney General shall release the person to the appropriate official of the State in which the person is domiciled or was tried if such State will assume responsibility for his custody, care, and treatment. The Attorney General shall make all reasonable efforts to cause such a State to assume such responsibility. If, notwithstanding such efforts, neither such State will assume such responsibility, the Attorney General shall hospitalize the person for treatment in a suitable facility until—

(1) such a State will assume such responsibility; or

(2) the person's mental condition is such that his release, or his conditional release under a prescribed regimen of medical, psychiatric, or psychological care or treatment, would not create a substantial risk of bodily injury to another person or serious damage to property of another;

whichever is earlier. The Attorney General shall continue periodically to exert all reasonable efforts to cause such a State to assume such responsibility for the person's custody, care, and treatment.

(f) 
Discharge
.—When the director of the facility in which an acquitted person is hospitalized pursuant to subsection (e) determines that the person has recovered from his mental disease or defect to such an extent that his release, or his conditional release under a prescribed regimen of medical, psychiatric, or psychological care or treatment, would no longer create a substantial risk of bodily injury to another person or serious damage to property of another, he shall promptly file a certificate to that effect with the clerk of the court that ordered the commitment. The clerk shall send a copy of the certificate to the person's counsel and to the attorney for the Government. The court shall order the discharge of the acquitted person or, on the motion of the attorney for the Government or on its own motion, shall hold a hearing, conducted pursuant to the provisions of section 4247(d), to determine whether he should be released. If, after the hearing, the court finds by the standard specified in subsection (d) that the person has recovered from his mental disease or defect to such an extent that—

(1) his release would no longer create a substantial risk of bodily injury to another person or serious damage to property of another, the court shall order that he be immediately discharged; or

(2) his conditional release under a prescribed regimen of medical, psychiatric, or psychological care or treatment would no longer create a substantial risk of bodily injury to another person or serious damage to property of another, the court shall—

(A) order that he be conditionally discharged under a prescribed regimen of medical, psychiatric, or psychological care or treatment that has been prepared for him, that has been certified to the court as appropriate by the director of the facility in which he is committed, and that has been found by the court to be appropriate; and

(B) order, as an explicit condition of release, that he comply with the prescribed regimen of medical, psychiatric, or psychological care or treatment.

The court at any time may, after a hearing employing the same criteria, modify or eliminate the regimen of medical, psychiatric, or psychological care or treatment.

(g) 
Revocation of Conditional Discharge
.—The director of a medical facility responsible for administering a regimen imposed on an acquitted person conditionally discharged under subsection (f) shall notify the Attorney General and the court having jurisdiction over the person of any failure of the person to comply with the regimen. Upon such notice, or upon other probable cause to believe that the person has failed to comply with the prescribed regimen of medical, psychiatric, or psychological care or treatment, the person may be arrested, and, upon arrest, shall be taken without unnecessary delay before the court having jurisdiction over him. The court shall, after a hearing, determine whether the person should be remanded to a suitable facility on the ground that, in light of his failure to comply with the prescribed regimen of medical, psychiatric, or psychological care or treatment, his continued release would create a substantial risk of bodily injury to another person or serious damage to property of another.

(h) 
Limitations on Furloughs
.—An individual who is hospitalized under subsection (e) of this section after being found not guilty only by reason of insanity of an offense for which subsection (d) of this section creates a burden of proof of clear and convincing evidence, may leave temporarily the premises of the facility in which that individual is hospitalized only—

(1) with the approval of the committing court, upon notice to the attorney for the Government and such individual, and after opportunity for a hearing;

(2) in an emergency; or

(3) when accompanied by a Federal law enforcement officer (as defined in section 115 of this title).

(6) If the appointment is made after judgment, at least one attorney so appointed must have been admitted to practice in the court of appeals for not less than five years, and must have had not less than three years experience in the handling of appeals in that court in felony cases.
(7) With respect to paragraphs (5) and (6), the court, for good cause, may appoint another attorney whose background, knowledge, or experience would otherwise enable him or her to properly represent the defendant, with due consideration to the seriousness of the possible penalty and to the unique and complex nature of the litigation.
(8) Unless replaced by similarly qualified counsel upon the attorney's own motion or upon motion of the defendant, each attorney so appointed shall represent the defendant throughout every subsequent stage of available judicial proceedings, including pretrial proceedings, trial, sentencing, motions for new trial, appeals, applications, for writ of certiorari to the Supreme Court of the United States, and all available post-conviction process, together with applications for stays of execution and other appropriate motions and procedures, and shall also represent the defendant in such competency proceedings and proceedings for executive or other clemency as may be available to the defendant.
(9) Upon a finding in ex parte proceedings that investigative, expert or other services are reasonably necessary for the representation of the defendant, whether in connection with issues relating to guilt or sentence, the court shall authorize the defendant's attorneys to obtain such services on behalf of the defendant and shall order the payment of fees and expenses therefore, under paragraph (10). Upon a finding that timely procurement of such services could not practicably await prior authorization, the court may authorize the provision of and payment for such services nunc pro tunc.
(10) Notwithstanding the rates and maximum limits generally applicable to criminal cases and any other provision of law to the contrary, the court shall fix the compensation to be paid to attorneys appointed under this subsection and the fees and expenses to be paid for investigative, expert, and other reasonably necessary services authorized under paragraph (9), at such rates or amounts as the court determines to be reasonably necessary to carry out the requirements of paragraphs (4) through (9).

### 28 U.S.C. §2251

Source: [House archived 1994 Code](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-1994-title28-section2251&num=0&edition=1994).

A justice or judge of the United States before whom a habeas corpus proceeding is pending, may, before final judgment or after final judgment of discharge, or pending appeal, stay any proceeding against the person detained in any State court or by or under the authority of any State for any matter involved in the habeas corpus proceeding.
After the granting of such a stay, any such proceeding in any State court or by or under the authority of any State shall be void. If no stay is granted, any such proceeding shall be as valid as if no habeas corpus proceedings or appeal were pending.

### 28 U.S.C. §2283

Source: [House archived 1994 Code](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-1994-title28-section2283&num=0&edition=1994).

A court of the United States may not grant an injunction to stay proceedings in a State court except as expressly authorized by Act of Congress, or where necessary in aid of its jurisdiction, or to protect or effectuate its judgments.
