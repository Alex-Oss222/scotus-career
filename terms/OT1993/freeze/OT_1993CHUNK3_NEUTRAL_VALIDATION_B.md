### Granderson — research cutoff immediately before March 22, 1994

[The Eleventh Circuit, 969 F.2d 980](https://static.case.law/f2d/969/cases/0980-01.json), was retrieved and read in full. It affirmed the finding of possession and revocation, vacated twenty months' imprisonment, and ordered release. The sentence below also included three years of supervised release; the neutral record does not authorize an independent revision of that component. Granderson had served more than eleven months. His Guidelines range was zero to six months; that is a Guidelines ceiling, not a statement that §1703(a)'s offense statute itself carried a six-month maximum. The lower court's alternative-to-incarceration argument was not raised in the district court or briefed on appeal and was not decided there. No cross-petition seeking a new disposition is established. The narrower issue whether the Government may restore this twenty-month imprisonment term remains reachable.

Complete §3565 is supplied by the [official 1988 chapter text](chunk3-sources/uscode1988-007018227.txt), with the [1990 correction reflected in Supplement IV](chunk3-sources/uscode1988-043018227.txt). The amendment replaces “of modifying” with “or modifying” in paragraph (a)(1); it does not change the disputed proviso. Section 3565(a) permits, after a Rule 32.1 hearing and consideration of applicable §3553(a) factors, either continuation of probation, with or without extending the term or modifying or enlarging conditions, or revocation and imposition of any other sentence available under subchapter A at initial sentencing. The proviso states in full:

> Notwithstanding any other provision of this section, if a defendant is found by the court to be in possession of a controlled substance, thereby violating the condition imposed by section 3563(a)(3), the court shall revoke the sentence of probation and sentence the defendant to not less than one-third of the original sentence.

The firearm provision separately requires actual possession of a firearm as defined in §921 before probation expires or terminates, a Rule 32.1 hearing, revocation, and another sentence available initially. Section 3565(c) permits delayed adjudication for a reasonably necessary period after expiration only if a warrant or summons alleging the violation issued before expiration. Neither condition is disputed here; neither is transformed into a new requirement for this drug finding.

Section 3583(g), a distinct supervised-release provision, expressly directs termination of supervised release and requires the defendant “to serve in prison not less than one-third of the term of supervised release.” Section 3583(e)(3)'s separate revocation limits and proof provision are not mechanically transplanted into §3565(a). Sections 3561 and 3551 treat probation as a sentence; no conclusion rests on the obsolete proposition that probation is only a suspended prison sentence. Later amendments to the drug-possession proviso are excluded.

Actual Ratzlaf is controlling for the residual-lenity alternative it adopted, not a mandatory result for every criminal statute. Actual R. L. C. makes the Guidelines relevant to an authorized maximum in its own statutory setting and supplies no general majority lenity method. Its public separate writings establish: Souter and Stevens's broader interpretive inquiry; Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas's objection to using legislative history to enlarge ambiguous punishment; Thomas's insistence on established construction principles before lenity; and O'Connor and Blackmun's distinct juvenile-system objection. [Bifulco, 447 U.S. 381](https://static.case.law/us/447/cases/0381-01.json), and [Chapman, 500 U.S. 453](https://static.case.law/us/500/cases/0453-01.json), were consulted for the live punishment and lenity paths. ### Powell — research cutoff immediately before March 30, 1994

[Nevada's opinion, 108 Nev. 700](https://static.case.law/nev/108/cases/0700-01.json), was retrieved and read in full. Its nonretroactivity statement is in a footnote and invokes disruption of justice administration. Its main text separately reasons that voluntary Miranda waiver waived timely arraignment/appearance under state law. It reports voluntary prearrest statements on November 3, November 7 statements after Miranda advice and waiver, a judicial probable-cause determination November 7, and uncertainty about Powell's physical presence that day. Judicial probable-cause review and personally appearing for advice of rights are distinct; the record does not authorize treating them as interchangeable. The opinion characterized the admitted statements as prejudicial in discussing Nevada's delay statute; that characterization is not a completed federal harmless-error analysis.

The state court's discussion of the federal timing question permits review. Its discussion does not determine that the Miranda waiver knowingly waived a judicial probable-cause determination, establish a bona fide emergency, decide federal suppression for this specific delay, or establish harmlessness under the proper federal rule. The Court can leave those issues for lawful remand without promising suppression or acquittal.

[McLaughlin, 500 U.S. 44](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/500/44), allows an individual to challenge unreasonable delay even within 48 hours, including delay to gather evidence justifying arrest, ill will, or delay for its own sake. Beyond 48 hours, government must establish a bona fide emergency or other extraordinary circumstance; intervening weekends or the ordinary convenience of consolidating proceedings do not suffice. These qualifications remain together. [Griffith, 479 U.S. 314](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/479/314), supplies direct-review retroactivity; actual Harper confirms the rule/remedy distinction. [New York v. Harris, 495 U.S. 14](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/495/14), concerns the home-entry interest where police already had probable cause; it supplies a remedial comparison, not a categorical answer to unlawful prolonged detention.

### Oregon Waste — research cutoff immediately before April 4, 1994

[Oregon's opinion, 316 Or. 99](https://static.case.law/or/316/cases/0099-01.json), was retrieved and read in full. The consolidated state proceeding includes Oregon Waste Systems, Gilliam County and Columbia Resource Company. It reviews administrative rules through ORS 183.400, whose scope was limited to the face of the rule, enabling statutes, and rulemaking-procedure materials. It expressly declined factual proportionality review. Accordingly, the statute's commanded nexus to otherwise-unpaid costs is **not** an adjudicated finding that $2.25 equals demonstrated excess costs caused by imported waste. The original packet's cost characterization must be read with that procedural qualification.

ORS 459.297(1) requires the additional surcharge for out-of-state-generated waste beginning January 1, 1991. ORS 459.298 bases the amount on Oregon and local-government disposal costs not otherwise paid under the named waste programs; permissible costs include, but are not limited to, management, new and renewal permits, environmental and groundwater monitoring, and closure and postclosure activities. OAR 340-61-120(6) imposes a $2.25 per-ton out-of-state surcharge. The packet's corresponding domestic charge is $0.85 per ton. These amounts do not establish the facilities' entire bill or support an independently fixed refund amount. The state court also rejected a contention that certification costs could not form part of the surcharge, without adjudicating constitutional cost equivalence.

Oregon severed the legislative-committee approval provisions under its own Constitution. The federal commerce question does not restore those provisions or reopen that state-law severability ruling. No request for review of a government-owned landfill charge is supplied.
