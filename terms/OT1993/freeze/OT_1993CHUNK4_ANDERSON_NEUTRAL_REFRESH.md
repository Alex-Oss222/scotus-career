## What the current petition actually claims

This is an original extraordinary habeas request invoking Rule 20.4(a), 28 U.S.C. §§2241–2254, and asserted inadequacy of D.C. Code §23-110 remedies. It challenges the 1988 D.C. convictions and the subsequent routes for review. It does not merely cite a filing count.

The petition identifies alleged failures to investigate and call Dr. Morant concerning intoxication and Michael Robinson concerning events before the shooting; conflicting identification descriptions; trial and appellate counsel's asserted failures to present particular issues, preserve review, and pursue a new-trial appeal; allegedly inaccurate transcripts; prosecutorial argument; and the initially duplicative burglary convictions. It identifies claimed denial or delay of filing and appellate consideration, a recall-of-mandate route, and a dispute over the certificate-of-probable-cause process. It expressly alleges that lower-court handling has made ordinary remedies ineffective and explains why it invokes original habeas authority. These are allegations, not findings that counsel erred, the transcript was altered, the police lied, or an innocence claim is established.

The strongest adverse evidence is substantial. The attachments include a July 26, 1990 Superior Court opinion applying Strickland and examining the trial record: counsel cross-examined identification and police motives, the proposed additional witness's value was unsupported, and the court found no reasonable probability of a different result given the evidence. The February 28, 1990 direct appeal affirmed the principal convictions and directed removal of one burglary conviction; the petition's double-jeopardy rhetoric does not establish an uncorrected duplicate conviction. The 1993 appellate attachment rejects particular new-evidence and suppression theories: a civil request for admissions does not itself establish trial perjury, and information furnished in Jencks material was not concealed by the prosecution merely because counsel allegedly mishandled it. Its available pages also discuss default, successive motions, counsel appointment, and untimely recusal. The affidavit by itself does not overcome any of these rulings.

The federal district attachments apply D.C. Code §23-110(g) and Swain v. Pressley, 430 U.S. 372 (1977): local remedies' denial or a litigant's inability to use them does not alone show their inadequacy or ineffectiveness. The D.C. Circuit's July 23 and September 17, 1992 orders in 91-5293 treat the notice as a request for a probable-cause certificate, provide an opportunity to show cause, and then dismiss for want of the required showing. Thus the petition's theory that the appellate court could do nothing at all without first granting a certificate is not accepted. No relief follows merely because the claimant wanted an evidentiary hearing.

There is also concrete support for a distinct access contention. The attached D.C. Circuit order in 93-7116 **affirms dismissal of the amended complaint**, but **vacates the June 16, 1993 injunction and remands**, because the record did not reflect whether the district court afforded the required notice and opportunity to be heard before restricting access. It cites In re Powell, 851 F.2d 427, 431 (D.C. Cir. 1988). It does not find that no notice actually occurred, grant habeas relief, vindicate the complaint, or establish continuing obstruction after remand. Another attachment records fee permission and docketing of civil action 94-0022 in January 1994. This cuts against a claim that access was wholly and permanently closed, while confirming that the petitioner identifies particular procedural events rather than only a generalized grievance.

## Applicable law and distinct issues

The governing current public Day and Sassower rulings were read in `../entering-law/OT_1993CHUNK3_COMPLETED_PUBLIC_AUTHORITY.md`. Day left eight current fee motions unadjudicated; Sassower left ten unadjudicated. Their refusals to impose prospective conditions did not grant those motions. Sassower separately declined a noncriminal extraordinary-writ condition on its record. Its rule requires a supported abuse and scope basis; it is not an absolute warning prerequisite, numerical threshold, duty to exhaust every conceivable lesser step, or categorical prohibition of filing controls.

Inherited Martin and Demos v. Storrie require assessment of the particular submission and forbid replacing that assessment with the fact of earlier unsuccessful filings. Demos's nonconclusory official-interference allegation cleared the filing threshold without proving the allegation or entitlement to certiorari. Zatko preserves discretionary denial when the present papers are frivolous and repeated frivolous filings amount to extreme abuse. In re McDonald, In re Sindram, and In re Demos retain their targeted extraordinary-writ scope. Original Rule 20/habeas prerequisites remain independent of Rule 39 financial leave.

The new evidence supports **granting this fee motion** while leaving the original writ for independent consideration. The current paper includes an arguable, particular access-to-review contention supported in part by an actual partial injunction vacatur; the substantial procedural and merits objections do not make its entire request frivolous or malicious for Rule 39.8 purposes. This is a modest threshold conclusion, not acceptance of the petition's broader characterization of its litigation. The same evidence does not demonstrate that individualized treatment is inadequate to handle future extraordinary-writ filings, so the prospective refusal remains appropriate on this record.
