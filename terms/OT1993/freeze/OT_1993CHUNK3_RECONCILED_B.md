## 32. Oregon Waste Systems, Inc. v. Department of Environmental Quality

**Verified event:** Nos. 93-70 and 93-108 (Columbia Resource Co. v. Environmental Quality Commission), argued January 18, 1994; decision date April 4, 1994. Both arise from 316 Or. 99, 849 P.2d 500 (1993), following 114 Or.App. 369, 837 P.2d 965 (1992). The proceedings include Oregon Waste, Columbia Resource and Gilliam County; the federal question concerns the origin surcharge at private disposal facilities. No separate module or different judgment is invented for the companion.

### Historical evidence and preserved neutral qualifications

The official report records reversal and remand, seven to two. Thomas wrote historically for Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg. Rehnquist dissented, joined by Blackmun. The dissent defended the modest cost-based charge and comprehensive environmental program, treated general state taxation as relevant compensation, analogized landfill management to protected resources, and invoked user-fee reasonableness. It did not merely request a factfinding remand. That difference must be confronted for Blackmun rather than recasting his historical position to match the freeze.

ORS 459.298 commands a surcharge based on Oregon and local-government disposal costs not otherwise paid under the designated programs; authorized costs include management, new and renewal permits, environmental and groundwater monitoring, and closure/postclosure activity, without making that list exhaustive. The command is not an adjudicated empirical finding that $2.25 equals distinctive costs of imported waste. ORS 183.400 restricted this review to facial legality and authorized rulemaking materials; the Oregon court expressly declined factual proportionality review. Its statutory nexus therefore cannot silently become a finding satisfying the Commerce Clause.

The challenged $2.25 surcharge and corresponding $0.85 domestic fee are distinct from the whole disposal bill. The report also describes a temporarily extended, unchallenged $0.85 charge on imported waste, subject to a statutory contingency tied to the surcharge's survival. Do not calculate a court-ordered refund, announce that $2.25 was the total imported bill, or alter that separate provision. The surcharge's facial origin line and the defended compensation theory remain the questions for this disposition.

### Blackmun departure audit

**Historical departure:** Blackmun historically joined a defense of Oregon's modest cost-based surcharge and comprehensive regulatory system; here his published Fort Gratiot position is a narrower demand for an evidentiary opportunity, not validation of an origin restriction. Together with his actual Chemical Waste join and the Oregon court's expressly restricted factual review, that changed prior-position premise supports vacating the categorical legal approval and permitting Oregon to attempt the required justification, while rejecting both automatic validity and conclusive invalidation before that opportunity.

The distinction is Justice-specific. Blackmun has already published the very procedural reservation now implicated by the limited review channel. His historical Oregon position is strong contrary evidence and shows that he might favor broader latitude for comprehensive regulation; it does not erase the narrower position actually carried from Fort Gratiot. Preserving that prior position yields a remand, not an assumed empirical defense or a reversal of his nondiscrimination commitments. Armco remains a concrete barrier: any new showing must do more than assert that residents pay general taxes. The seven opposing that additional opportunity regard the defended theory as legally deficient even if its cost arithmetic were established; that is the precise disagreement.

### Proposition-level handoff and remedy

All eight reject the proposition that the statutory cost nexus alone removes facial discrimination. Seven—Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg—support invalidation of this differential on the supplied legal grounds: origin is the trigger; no adequate compensation is identified; income generation and disposal are not substantially equivalent taxable events; and conservation cannot reserve equivalent private landfill access for local waste by imposing this origin burden. Their common rule demands a legitimate local purpose not adequately served by reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives. The ordinary Pike branch for incidental burdens is not the applicable branch.

The compensatory-tax propositions must remain separate: identify the intrastate burden; show the interstate burden roughly approximates and does not exceed it; and show substantially equivalent taxable events that can operate as mutually exclusive proxies. Even assuming general tax receipts could be quantified, the equivalent-events objection remains independently sufficient against the asserted income-tax comparison. The user-fee label supplies no escape: these are private facilities, and nondiscrimination remains distinct from cost approximation even where a genuine government service fee is involved. Do not extend that conditional observation into a resolution of all public-landfill or market-participant questions.

A differential supported by genuinely greater costs attributable to the imported waste would pose a different justification issue; neither the statutory cost command nor Oregon's defended theory establishes that predicate here. Waste's origin alone, small dollars per household, environmental purpose and the presence of a comprehensive program do not resolve the required justification. Neutral charges, capacity restrictions and safety rules remain available without a court-created replacement program.

Eight reject the lower court's cost-nexus/no-discrimination shortcut. Blackmun supplies no join to conclusive invalidation and his proposed evidentiary opportunity is noncontrolling unless later supported at the appropriate proposition level. There is no affirmance vote among the non-Stone Justices. These are commitments, not an assigned opinion or final full-Court lineup.

The seven's remand concerns appropriate relief from the challenged differential. It does not fix refunds, establish McKesson's remedial predicates, set a new charge, require acceptance of particular shipments, reopen every Oregon tax, or restore the legislative-committee approval provisions severed under state law. Blackmun would permit a proper factual forum to consider a legally adequate justification under the same discrimination rule before conclusive relief. Neither path promises that Oregon will succeed. Other fees, neutral regulation and government-owned facilities remain outside the federal decision.
