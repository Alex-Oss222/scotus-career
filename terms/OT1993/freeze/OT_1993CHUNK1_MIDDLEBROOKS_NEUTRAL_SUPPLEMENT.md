## 2. Lower-court judgment and state-law source

*State v. Middlebrooks*, 840 S.W.2d 317 (Tenn. Sept. 8, 1992), affirms the felony-murder and aggravated-kidnapping convictions, reverses the death sentence, and remands for a new sentencing hearing at which the State may seek death again. The jury acquitted on premeditated murder, armed robbery, and aggravated sexual battery. It found the torture aggravator and the felony-murder aggravator. The state court found ample support for the former, but could not find removal of the latter harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. This was not a direction to impose life imprisonment, a categorical prohibition of death for felony murder, or a decision that another defendant's sentencing record supplied a cure.

The materially challenged statutory language is supplied in Part III of the opinion below. Section 39-2-203(i)(7) (1982) included murder committed while the defendant was committing a listed felony, was an accomplice in its commission, was attempting it, or was fleeing after committing or attempting it. The listed offenses were first-degree murder, arson, rape, robbery, burglary, larceny, kidnapping, aircraft piracy, and unlawful throwing, placing, or discharging a destructive device or bomb. The felony-murder offense definition and the aggravator substantially overlapped, but their exact terms should not be silently equated: the aggravator expressly included accomplice and flight alternatives. The adjudicated rule concerns a defendant convicted of first-degree murder **solely on the basis of felony murder**, and use of the duplicative felony aggravator to support death. The state court did not prohibit use of the felony aggravator for a conviction of premeditated murder.

Part III makes a state-law statement before its extended federal discussion: after identifying Tennessee's legislative prohibition of duplication in noncapital sentencing, it concludes that Article I, §16 prohibits such duplication in capital sentencing too. Later it distinguishes Louisiana's narrowing at the offense-definition stage and states that *Lowenfield v. Phelps* supplies no rationale for constitutionality under Tennessee's Constitution. It then states its federal and state grounds and overrules *State v. Smith*, 755 S.W.2d 757 (Tenn. 1988), and its progeny on this issue. Anderson, Reid, and Daughtrey constitute the Part III majority. Drowota and O'Brien dissent from that part.

The separate writings matter to interpretation but do not change the majority's rule. Drowota disputes both the federal and state constitutional conclusions, favors the existing sentencing safeguards and case-specific proportionality review, and would affirm the death sentence; O'Brien joins. Reid, joined by Daughtrey, expressly describes the duplication holding as based on Article I, §16, agrees with resentencing, and would impose additional state constitutional limits on felony-murder death eligibility. Those proposed additional limits did not command the state court's Part III majority and are not incorporated into its judgment.

## 3. Howell, read as a whole

The complete reported majority and Reid concurrence in *Howell* were read. Anderson's majority, joined by Drowota and O'Brien, applies the *Middlebrooks* prohibition in Part II.E and then separately reviews the effect of the invalid factor in Howell's own sentencing record. Daughtrey concurs in the results only; Reid writes separately. Their alignment in Howell must not be substituted for their alignment in Middlebrooks.

Majority footnote 7, accompanying the sentencing discussion, states that the Middlebrooks decision was required by Article I, §16; federal constitutional law was examined to determine whether the duplication **also** violated the Eighth Amendment; and Middlebrooks was decided on separate and independent state constitutional grounds. This is a published statement of the state's highest court about its own constitutional rule. It is not merely the respondent's characterization of the opinion. Its reference to federal authority does not erase its express explanation of why that authority was considered.

The majority's surrounding text is consistent with that clarification: it identifies Article I, §16 as the basis for disallowing the duplicative aggravator in felony-murder cases, distinguishes permissible use for premeditated murder, applies the rule, and only then asks whether the sentencing error can be harmless. Howell thus continues to apply the state prohibition rather than withdrawing it. Its harmless-error discussion considers the strength and number of remaining valid aggravators, sentencing argument, evidence admitted to establish the invalid factor, and the nature and strength of mitigation. It does not announce that every duplicative aggravator is harmless.

Reid's concurrence supplies no contrary construction making the state ground depend on the Eighth Amendment. It again describes Tennessee's independent constitutional authority, characterizes Middlebrooks as a partial state-law narrowing measure, and favors additional restrictions. His concurrence in Howell's harmless-error result uses the particular admissible evidence and prosecutorial argument in that different case. His broader criticisms of capital sentencing and comparative proportionality review are separate positions, not the majority's clarification or a new finding in Middlebrooks.

Howell's offense, prior convictions, mitigation, guilty verdict, sentencing outcome, and sentencing directions are **not** facts or remedies in Middlebrooks. Its quotations from historical post-divergence Supreme Court decisions are not imported as in-world Supreme Court law. ## 5. Law entering the separable questions

**Independent-state-ground jurisdiction.** Under *Michigan v. Long*, 463 U.S. 1032, 1040–1042 (1983), an opinion that fairly appears to rest primarily on federal law or to interweave the two sources, without a clear independent basis, is presumed to rest on federal law. An express bona fide alternative state ground sufficient to sustain the judgment defeats review. The object is review of judgments, not correction of abstract federal reasoning. *Herb v. Pitcairn*, 324 U.S. 117, 125–126 (1945), supplies that no-advisory-opinion rationale as reproduced and applied in Long and *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722, 729 (1991). Coleman's direct-review statement covers substantive as well as procedural state grounds. Its habeas exceptions are not a route around this direct-review limit.

The entering-law slice's **Federal Courts — Adequate and independent state grounds — Sochor v. Florida**, effective June 8, 1992, preserves an expressly independent state disposition despite alternative merits discussion. Souter's Part II-A, joined by Stone, White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas, rejected review of the preserved-ground issue there; Blackmun and Stevens would have reached that instructional claim. Its facts concern a failure-to-object ground. It does not itself decide what Howell means or mechanically decide a substantive state constitutional ground. The shared rule and the precise difference must both be preserved.

**Finality.** Section 1257 and the *Cox Broadcasting Corp. v. Cohn*, 420 U.S. 469 (1975), finality categories remain relevant because resentencing remains below. The original packet identifies the issue; no conclusion that a particular Cox exception applies is supplied by this supplement. If the independent ground ends federal review, a separate Cox ruling is unnecessary. Any later proposal to reach the merits must first resolve finality, exact review scope, and the other review prerequisites from adequate sources.

**Federal narrowing, only if reachable.** *Lowenfield*, 484 U.S. 231 (1988), permits genuine narrowing at the offense-definition or sentencing stage; overlap alone is not a categorical federal prohibition. *Zant v. Stephens*, 462 U.S. 862 (1983), requires genuine narrowing and a principled justification for death eligibility. The original Tennessee majority and dissent supply opposed applications, not a Supreme Court disposition of that disagreement. The entering rule **Facial capital-aggravator formulation and application review**, present operation effective March 30, 1993 under simulated *Arave*, distinguishes a genuinely narrowing authoritative formulation from a constitutional application challenge. Arave does not decide this duplication question.

**Sentence correction, only if reachable.** The slice's **Invalid aggravators in capital weighing and correction of sentencing error**, present operation effective June 29, 1992 under simulated *Espinosa*, with the direct-weighing rule supplied by simulated *Stringer* on March 9, 1992, distinguishes surviving eligibility from adequate correction. A valid remaining aggravator does not automatically cure the error. Genuine reweighing must be permitted by state law; alternatively, the State must establish beyond a reasonable doubt that the invalid factor did not affect the sentence. If no lawful cure exists, resentencing is required. Neither life imprisonment nor a guilt retrial follows automatically. Sochor's documented-reliance and adequate-cure holdings reinforce those distinctions. Howell's own harmlessness finding supplies no such finding for Middlebrooks.

## 6. Competing threshold paths requiring independent judgment

**End review on the independent state ground.** The original opinion supplies actual state-law reasons and a state constitutional holding; Howell expressly confirms the independent role of that holding. The strongest objection is that the original opinion used extensive federal narrowing analysis and the later clarification occurs in another case, rather than an amended Middlebrooks judgment. The narrow answer is to test the original state ground together with the highest state court's explanation, rather than hold that any later assertion may create a new jurisdictional barrier. If the same resentencing judgment survives correction of the federal analysis, federal review cannot provide the State the requested relief.

**Retain review or seek clarification.** Long's presumption would justify federal review if the original state ground were actually dependent on federal law; an appropriate clarification procedure can address genuine unresolved ambiguity. The strongest objection to continuing here is that the state court has already answered the supposed ambiguity in a published opinion, while the original text itself invokes independent Tennessee reasoning. A further remand cannot be justified solely by dissatisfaction with the state rule. Rejecting Howell requires an identified reason why its explicit description is inapplicable, unreliable, or does not independently sustain the judgment. No such fact is established in the authorized materials. This is the precise point for any recovered State objection to address.

**Dismissal form.** A reasoned dismissal for want of jurisdiction and a dismissal of the writ as improvidently granted are distinct available forms. The former may announce a narrow jurisdictional application if adopted and published; an unexplained DIG announces no substantive holding. Neither form affirms or reverses the federal narrowing analysis, vacates the Tennessee judgment, or reinstates the death sentence. The commitments handoff must state which form each Justice supports and any acceptable narrower form; the neutral packet does not equate their precedential effects.
