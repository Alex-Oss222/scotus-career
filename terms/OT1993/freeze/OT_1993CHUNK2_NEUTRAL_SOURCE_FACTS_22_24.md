## 22. Department of Defense v. Federal Labor Relations Authority

Source: [official United States Reports, 510 U.S. 487](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep510/usrep510487/usrep510487.pdf), predecision material at 490–494 and record descriptions at 497–499. The statutory text is reproduced in the report; the underlying administrative records and briefs were not independently recovered.

- The two locals represent employees of the Navy CBC Exchange in Gulfport, Mississippi, and of the Army and Air Force Exchange headquartered in Dallas. The employers supplied names and work stations but withheld home addresses. FLRA ordered disclosure in 37 F.L.R.A. 930 and 652 (1990), relying on its Portsmouth decision, 37 F.L.R.A. 515 (1990).
- The Fifth Circuit enforced, 975 F.2d 1105 (1992). Its majority treated the labor statute's collective-bargaining interest as part of the FOIA privacy balance; Judge Garza dissented, reasoning that Reporters Committee controlled the relevant public-interest inquiry. The court below acknowledged that address disclosure would not meaningfully expose agency action to public scrutiny. This is a lower-court assessment, not a statutory command.
- Section 7114(b)(4)(B) requires an agency to furnish its exclusive representative requested data, to the extent not prohibited by law, that are reasonably available and necessary for full and proper discussion, understanding and negotiation of matters within collective bargaining. The FLRA's determination of necessity is not challenged here; reasonable availability is not disputed. Section 7114(a)(1) requires fair representation of all bargaining-unit employees, members and nonmembers. Section 7101(a) declares labor organizations and collective bargaining in the civil service to be in the public interest.
- The Privacy Act's section 552a(b) generally prohibits disclosure of a record contained in a system of records by any means to a person or another agency absent the subject's written request or prior written consent, unless an enumerated exception applies. Subsection (b)(2) concerns disclosure required under FOIA. Subsection (b)(3), the separate routine-use exception, is not pressed in this case. The written-consent route is likewise not the asserted basis of these orders. These alternatives must not be collapsed into one exception.
- Exemption 6, section 552(b)(6), concerns personnel, medical and similar files whose disclosure would constitute a clearly unwarranted invasion of personal privacy. Exemption 7(C) separately concerns law-enforcement records whose production could reasonably be expected to constitute an unwarranted invasion. Their words and thresholds differ.
- Unions already possess members' addresses and the addresses voluntarily furnished by other employees. They can contact employees at work and request addresses; the unresolved practical request concerns nonmembers who have not supplied them. No adjudicated finding of harassment, unwanted visits, employee hostility to unions, or each employee's reason for withholding an address appears. Do not turn possible consequences into findings.
- The agencies invoke the Privacy Act and ordinary FOIA principles. Respondents urge that the labor statute's declared public interest must be considered because that statute generated the request; they assert impeded communication and unequal treatment compared with private-sector unions. Neither party's position establishes that every employment record has the same privacy balance.
- Eligible earlier Justice writing: then-Judge Ginsburg's concurrence in FLRA v. Department of the Treasury, Financial Management Service, 884 F.2d 1446, 1457–1461 (D.C. Cir. 1989). It addresses the interaction of these same statutes and the scope of Reporters Committee. This is a dated research lead; it is not a report of her position in the present matter.

## 23. Elder v. Holloway

Source: [official United States Reports, 510 U.S. 510](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep510/usrep510510/usrep510510.pdf), predecision material at 512–514 and notes 1–2. Lower opinions and the full summary-judgment record were not independently recovered.

- The arrest occurred in April 1987. Idaho officers learned Florida authorities wanted Elder and planned a public-workplace arrest without obtaining an Idaho warrant. Finding he had left, they surrounded his residence and ordered him out. During the encounter he suffered epileptic seizures; an officer told him to crawl out to avoid falling. He walked out, suffered another seizure and fell, sustaining brain injury and partial paralysis.
- The district court, 751 F.Supp. 858 (D. Idaho 1990), recognized Payton's rule for entry into the home absent exigency but found no clear Idaho or circuit authority for ordering a person out of a surrounded home. It granted qualified-immunity summary judgment. Depositions indicated access to guns, while police had begun planning five days earlier; the court identified a possible triable exigency issue if the home-arrest rule applied. Neither fact settles the issue.
- The Ninth Circuit, 975 F.2d 1388, noticed United States v. Al-Azzawy, 784 F.2d 890 (9th Cir. 1985), decided before Elder's arrest. Elder cited it on appeal, though without elaboration; it was not cited below. The panel treated the clearly-established-law inquiry as a legal question but required the plaintiff to identify its precedents in the district court as legal facts, relying on Davis v. Scherer, 468 U.S. 183 (1984). The later denial of rehearing produced Judge Kozinski's contrary view, 984 F.2d 991 (1993).
- Al-Azzawy treats the arrestee's location as material to whether an arrest occurs inside the home; its actual outcome also depended on exigent circumstances. Its citation cannot by itself decide Elder's ultimate immunity or the presence of exigency. The preserved constitutional theory is the Fourth Amendment seizure claim; this case concerns additional authority supporting that claim, not an unpresented new claim.
- The report's procedural narrative describes the panel decision as 1991, while the supplied packet uses its 1992 reporter citation. No exact appellate-decision date is needed for the judgment modeled here. The event and argument dates remain February 23, 1994, and January 10, 1994.

## 24. Caspari v. Bohlen

Source: [official United States Reports, 510 U.S. 383](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep510/usrep510383/usrep510383.pdf), predecision record at 386–389, finality facts at 390–391, cited pre-finality litigation at 394–395. Underlying briefs and transcripts were not independently recovered.

- The robbery occurred April 17, 1981. Bohlen was convicted of three first-degree robberies, class A felonies. Missouri section 558.011.1(1) allowed ten to thirty years or life for each. The original sentence was three consecutive fifteen-year terms.
- Section 558.016.3 defined a persistent offender as one found guilty of, or who pleaded guilty to, two or more felonies committed at different times. Section 558.021 required proof beyond a reasonable doubt. Ordinarily the jury assessed an advisory sentence and the judge generally could not exceed it. Persistent-offender status transferred the sentencing choice to the judge. **For these class A felonies it did not change the authorized sentencing range.** The packet's reference to enhanced sentences must not be taken as a finding that the statutory maximum increased.
- State v. Bohlen, 670 S.W.2d 119, 123 (Mo. App. 1984), affirmed the convictions but reversed the sentence because no proof had been offered of prior convictions, remanding for proof and resentencing. On remand the State proved four previous felonies; the judge again imposed three consecutive fifteen-year terms. The appellate court affirmed, 698 S.W.2d 577 (1985), rejecting the double-jeopardy objection; it later affirmed denial of state postconviction relief, 743 S.W.2d 425 (1987).
- Federal habeas was denied after a 1989 petition. The Eighth Circuit, 979 F.2d 109 (1992), directed habeas relief, regarding the procedure as sufficiently trial-like under Bullington and its application to this setting as not new under Teague. This is the judgment under review.
- Rehearing on direct review was denied October 3, 1985. No certiorari petition was filed. Finality occurred January 2, 1986: the ordinary ninety-day period extended over the January 1 legal holiday. The State advanced Teague below, in its certiorari petition and on the merits. The petition's principal question concerned successive noncapital sentence-enhancement proceedings; whether its preserved Teague argument is fairly included remains a legal issue for this Court, not a waiver fact supplied by silence.
- Pre-finality authorities addressing repeat noncapital enhancement proceedings diverged: Linam v. Griffin, 685 F.2d 369 (10th Cir. 1982), Durham v. State, 464 N.E.2d 321 (Ind. 1984), People v. Sailor, 65 N.Y.2d 224, 480 N.E.2d 701 (1985), and State v. Lee, 660 S.W.2d 394 (Mo. App. 1983), rejected the bar; Briggs v. Procunier, 764 F.2d 368 (5th Cir. 1985), State v. Hennings, 100 Wash.2d 379, 670 P.2d 256 (1983), and Cooper v. State, 631 S.W.2d 508 (Tex. Crim. App. 1982), supported it. The existence of this division is not a mechanical new-rule test. Linam stated an alternative capital/noncapital rationale as well as trial-error reasoning. No independently recovered lower-opinion corpus is claimed.
- The State asks alternatively to reconsider Bullington. That request does not require reaching it if a properly applicable threshold resolves the case. Only the actual completed Schiro ruling, not its historical comparator, may supply current-term law. Izumi's actual November 30, 1993 question-presentation ruling is also relevant to the fairly-included issue and should be read in the authorized completed public authority copy.
