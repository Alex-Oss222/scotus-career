## Granderson — No. 92-1662; cutoff immediately before March 22, 1994

### Corrected predicates and scope

The original sentence was sixty months' probation **and a $2,000 fine**. The imprisonment range ordinarily applicable at initial sentencing was zero to six months. Section 1703(a)'s five-year statutory maximum is a different limit. No substantial-assistance departure or other downward departure from a higher Guidelines range is established here. The court below upheld the possession finding and revocation, vacated twenty months' imprisonment and ordered release after more than eleven months had been served. Its opinion records three years' supervised release as part of the district court's disposition; this review does not independently revise that component. No cross-petition or preserved request for affirmative renewed probation or a replacement fine is established. The lower court expressly declined the alternatives-to-incarceration argument because it was neither raised in the district court nor briefed on appeal.

These predicates distinguish three questions: what the original sentence comprises; which kinds of replacement sentence the proviso allows; and the minimum and maximum of any authorized imprisonment. The first question is not answered merely by saying that probation is a sentence. A fine actually imposed with probation is also part of the initial penal disposition. Nothing establishes whether the original fine has been paid, remitted or otherwise satisfied; no new monetary remedy follows from its amount alone.

The official 1988 Code and Supplement IV confirm the relevant structure. Section 3551(b) recognizes probation, a fine and imprisonment, and expressly permits a fine in addition to another sentence. Section 3561 makes probation a sentence subject to its own eligibility restrictions, including the bar on sentencing probation simultaneously with imprisonment for the same or another offense. Section 3565(a)(1) permits continuation of probation with the stated alterations; subsection (a)(2) permits revocation and another sentence available initially. The drug proviso, notwithstanding any other provision of section 3565, requires revocation and sentencing to not less than one-third of the original sentence. It does not use the words serve in prison. Section 3583(g) does, and expressly measures its prison requirement by the term of supervised release. The two drug provisions were enacted together by Pub. L. 100-690, section 7303(a)(2) and (b)(2), 102 Stat. 4464. This is a verified drafting relationship, not a legislative-purpose inference. The 1990 correction to section 3565(a)(1) does not change the proviso.

### Independently assessed paths

**Imprisonment measured by the originally available range.** Reading revocation opposite continuation in section 3565(a) supports a mandatory custodial consequence. The initially available imprisonment range supplies a coherent custodial referent: its upper endpoint produces a two-month minimum here, and its upper endpoint remains the six-month maximum. The proviso makes revocation and a fractional minimum mandatory without clearly displacing the existing maximum. The jointly enacted section 3583(g) shows that Congress knew how to convert a noncustodial term expressly into prison time. The newly specified fine makes a literal conversion of the entire actual sentence still less self-explanatory: sixty months and $2,000 cannot simply be divided into prison months. On this construction the proviso sets a custodial floor; it does not require a new fine equal to one-third of the old fine. Any monetary sanction would require its own sentencing authority and proper posture.

The strongest objections are that a Guidelines maximum never pronounced is not ordinarily an original sentence, and that imposing imprisonment imports a noun omitted from this proviso but used in section 3583(g). The range construction answers through the operative revocation context and retained initial-sentencing constraints, with residual lenity where genuine uncertainty survives. It cannot rest on pretending probation was not itself a sentence, treating the $2,000 as nonexistent, or multiplying rival readings and calling that alone ambiguity. Actual Ratzlaf permits residual lenity after construction, not automatic selection of the most lenient outcome case by case. Actual R. L. C. recognizes applicable Guidelines limits in its distinct statute; it does not dictate this denominator.

**Actual probation duration converted to a mandatory prison floor.** The rival construction treats the original liberty-restricting term as the sixty months actually pronounced and reads the mandatory-revocation command in the surrounding scheme as requiring imprisonment. The notwithstanding clause gives the twenty-month floor priority over the ordinary zero-to-six-month range. The two drug amendments' common enactment can support a coordinated mandatory-sanction design despite different wording. On this reading, the original fine remains a distinct monetary sanction; the time-based fraction addresses the probation term, not a conversion of dollars into days. Nothing about the existence of the original fine independently increases twenty months or supplies a new monetary order in this appeal.

Its strongest objection is precisely that the legislature wrote original sentence, not original term of probation, while elsewhere expressly specifying both imprisonment and a noncustodial time denominator. Severing the fine and converting only probation duration requires contextual construction, so this cannot be described as a mechanically literal reading of the complete sentence. Kennedy and Thomas continue to find the revocation setting and priority clause sufficient for the challenged twenty-month term. Chapman permits severe punishment Congress actually specifies; it does not itself resolve either textual omission. This path does not decide every outer maximum or an unpresented constitutional challenge.

**Actual whole sentence, with a new probation sentence permissible.** A distinct reading gives original sentence its actual composite referent and gives the verb sentence the breadth it has in sections 3551 and 3561. Mandatory revocation terminates the old probation sentence; it need not mean that a new eligible sentence of probation is prohibited. The notwithstanding clause matters because the ordinary contrast between continuation in (a)(1) and another sentence in (a)(2) is the principal structural objection. The proviso's own command can control that contrast. Section 3583(g)'s contemporaneous express prison language strengthens this reading. A new probation sentence remains subject to independent eligibility requirements; permission is not a command to impose probation, and it does not authorize probation together with imprisonment contrary to section 3561(a)(3).

On this reading the minimum refers to one-third of the actual sentence as a whole, including the fine. A like-kind replacement can preserve corresponding components without pretending that dollars are time. Where a different kind of punishment is chosen, the command requires a comparison of severity, not a statutory month-for-month exchange or a monetary conversion rate supplied by this Court. The most serious objection is that the statute supplies no common measure for different sanctions and that revocation followed by shorter probation can seem at odds with the drug amendment's mandatory function. The answer is that revocation, a new judgment and a minimum still perform legal work; a penal purpose cannot supply the missing prison language. That answer does not furnish a quantified equivalence between prison, probation and a fine on this record.

Scalia adopts this as the better textual account of the proviso's **permitted kinds of sentence and actual-sentence referent**, while declining an unnecessary cross-sanction valuation. His judgment also rests on the narrower prison-authority ground: nothing in a minimum stated in different sanctions clearly enlarges the imprisonment range otherwise available here beyond six months. The proviso overrides only the conflicting ordinary restriction; it is not an independent authorization for any maximum the sentencing court chooses. His earlier reservation about renewed probation is thus replaced with permission in an otherwise eligible case, not an instruction to impose it here. His reservation about the exact prison minimum remains; he does not join the two-month formula.

**Narrow disposition without a severity conversion.** More than eleven months already served exceeds the six-month prison ceiling accepted by the affirming Justices. That resolves the Government's attempt to restore twenty months for those Justices without deciding that eleven prison months are mathematically equivalent to, or have conclusively discharged, one-third of sixty months' probation plus a $2,000 fine. It requires no new fine, no restored probation and no first-instance finding about payment of the original fine. The alternative reading is available to test the Government's asserted prison authority; absence of a cross-petition prevents treating it as an independently requested alteration of the judgment. The narrower remedy does not decide an unpresented lawful monetary question or hold that every conceivable aspect of the sentence has been satisfied.

**Higher-range departure cases reserved.** Harrison, 815 F. Supp. 494, involved probation after substantial-assistance departures from an otherwise applicable 97-to-121-month Guidelines range and a ten-year statutory minimum. It exposes a genuine problem if an unconditional range-based floor can exceed a fraction of the actual probation sentence. It also rejects the assumption that imposing probation automatically creates a zero-to-six-month range. That lower decision is available argument, not binding law here. None of the eight rows turns Granderson's undisputed ordinary range into a rule resolving what denominator, lawful departure range or maximum governs a materially higher-range departure case. Actual Williams bars invention of departure grounds; Stinson does not let commentary amend the statute. The Court can resolve this appeal while reserving that distinct situation.

## Powell — No. 92-8841; cutoff immediately before March 30, 1994

### Reported record predicates and legal significance

The supplement reports a sworn declaration prepared within one hour of Powell's November 3 arrest, and that the November 7 magistrate's probable-cause determination relied on the facts contained in that declaration. It reports no suggestion that the intervening delay enabled police to gather additional evidence presented to the magistrate. Those propositions materially strengthen Nevada's independent ground. They are accepted here as **reported record predicates**, not as this context's inspection of the declaration or a lower court's completed federal causation finding. Nevada's opinion supplies the two statement dates, the voluntary prearrest interview and November 7 Miranda waiver, and the November 7 probable-cause determination; its omission of the declaration particulars is not a contradiction.

The new predicates support a legal argument that timely review would have confirmed the same lawful basis for custody. They do not, by themselves, establish whether the November 7 statement was obtained through exploitation of the unreviewed interval, its timing relative to the judicial determination, or every remedial fact. A declaration can establish what information supported probable cause without conclusively establishing how a later admission was obtained. The first interview before formal arrest cannot be a product of a later detention violation; its existence does not establish that admission of the second statement was harmless.

### Alternative disposition and remedial paths

**Independent affirmance for absence of a causal product.** Segura, 468 U.S. 796, 813–816, admits evidence obtained under an independent warrant founded wholly on information possessed before the illegal entry. Crews, 445 U.S. 463, 470–474, distinguishes genuinely derived evidence from an independently acquired identification and rejects immunity from prosecution merely because arrest was unlawful. Ceccolini, 435 U.S. 268, 274–280, rejects mechanical but-for exclusion while preserving meaningful causal inquiry. Harris, 495 U.S. 14, 17–21, denies suppression of a station-house statement where probable cause made custody lawful and the illegality concerned entry into the home. Together these authorities permit a supported independent ground even though Nevada's nonretroactivity reasoning was wrong. A reviewing court has power to affirm a judgment on such a ground; a prior express federal causation ruling below is not an invariant jurisdictional prerequisite.

The strongest form of that argument is specific: the magistrate confirmed probable cause from the early declaration alone, so the delay did not enable development of the facts needed for lawful custody; a timely decision would have left the suspect available for a voluntary interview. The answer is also specific. The present constitutional interest is **prompt neutral review of continuing detention**, not merely the location of an otherwise lawful arrest. Harris expressly depended on lawful continued custody and the purpose of Payton's home protection. A postdeadline detention claim cannot be resolved simply by borrowing that conclusion. Segura supplied an independently executed lawful warrant search and Crews an independently grounded identification; neither declares that previously available probable cause immunizes every later statement from the effects of an overdue review. Ceccolini's freely cooperating nondefendant witness and attenuation inquiry cannot be reduced to the fact that Powell received Miranda advice. Thus the declaration strengthens an available no-product argument but does not compel it or make further review futile.

**Vacatur and limited remand.** Griffith governs this still-open direct appeal. Actual Harper confirms the difference between application of a rule and entitlement to relief. The Court can correct Nevada's erroneous direct-review premise and permit the state courts to address the distinct federal remedy, including the now-strengthened causal argument, without deciding that suppression is warranted. The relevant judicial function is to apply McLaughlin to the actual record and assess any properly available independent ground under its own rule. Remand does not order a new trial, release, a declaration of evidentiary taint, or a determination that any defense fails.

The strongest objection is needless remand where an uncontested early probable-cause record may already establish lawful evidentiary acquisition. Each row below considers that argument. They ultimately select remand because the difference between the protected interests leaves a real federal remedial question and Nevada's substituted arraignment-waiver reasoning does not resolve it. This is a conclusion about the necessary legal inquiry on the supplied record, not a factual inference that police actually exploited delay or a rule requiring a remand in every timing case.

**Distinct Krull ground.** The supplement reports that Nevada and its supporting amicus presented objective good-faith reliance on Nevada's initial-appearance statute. Krull, 480 U.S. 340, 349–360, concerns evidence obtained in objectively reasonable reliance on a statute later held unconstitutional. It does not apply if the legislature wholly abandoned its responsibility to enact constitutional laws or if a reasonable officer should have known the law was unconstitutional. It does not excuse conduct outside the statute's authorization. Its substantive/procedural distinction discussion does not dispense with these limits. The standard is objective, not an officer's unsupported statement of subjective good intentions.

Nevada's quoted provision triggers a prosecutorial explanation and possible release after seventy-two hours excluding nonjudicial days; it is not an unqualified command authorizing police to postpone every neutral review until that deadline. Whether the pertinent statutory scheme actually authorized the conduct, whether the officers' implementation and reliance fit that authority, and what Gerstein already required in November 1989 remain relevant. McLaughlin's later articulation of forty-eight hours is a substantial argument for objective reliance; it does not erase Gerstein or automatically establish every predicate. The reported presentation of Krull makes it a live argument, but does not establish where every aspect was preserved or give this context the briefs, suppression record or factual findings necessary to resolve its application. The eight rows leave it for consideration to the extent lawfully available on remand. No new pleading deadline or mandatory evidentiary hearing is invented.

**Other questions remain separate.** Absence of any causal product precedes attenuation; attenuation examines whether an established connection has dissipated. Krull addresses objective reliance even when evidence was obtained through unconstitutional conduct. Waiver requires a legally adequate surrender of the relevant right; Nevada's finding of voluntary Miranda waiver does not itself establish surrender of prompt judicial probable-cause review. Harmlessness concerns the consequence of admitting evidence under the governing federal standard; Nevada's description of the statements as prejudicial is not a completed such analysis. None of these grounds is a synonym for another. Montalvo-Murillo's separate detention-statute timing problem is not adopted as a constitutional no-suppression rule here.

McLaughlin's complete qualifications remain: a review within forty-eight hours can still be unreasonably delayed for gathering evidence to justify arrest, ill will or delay for its own sake; after forty-eight hours the government must establish a bona fide emergency or other extraordinary circumstance, and intervening weekends or routine consolidation do not suffice. A neutral probable-cause determination need not coincide with a personal first appearance. The declaration does not itself constitute the magistrate's review or reset the clock.
