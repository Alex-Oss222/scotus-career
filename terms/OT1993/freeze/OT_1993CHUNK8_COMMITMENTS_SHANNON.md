## Neutral statutory and record premises

Section 17 requires the defendant to establish by clear and convincing evidence that, because of severe mental disease or defect, he could not appreciate the nature and quality or wrongfulness of his acts. Section 4242(b) supplies guilty, not guilty, and not guilty only by reason of insanity as the verdict choices when the defense is properly raised. Section 4243(a) requires immediate commitment to a suitable facility until eligibility for release under subsection (e). An examination and report precede a hearing under §4247(d) within forty days. At that hearing the acquittee bears clear-and-convincing proof for an offense involving bodily injury, serious property damage, or substantial risk of either to another; for other offenses the burden is preponderance. The issue is the specified risk from release due to a present mental disease or defect. The record does not settle which category a particular §922(g) charge occupies, and this decision need not do so.

Failure to establish safe release under the applicable burden leads to Attorney General custody. The Attorney General must make reasonable efforts to secure the appropriate domiciliary or trial State's acceptance of responsibility, and must transfer if it will assume custody, care and treatment. Otherwise federal hospitalization lasts until state acceptance or a mental condition allowing safe release or conditional release under a prescribed regimen, whichever occurs first; reasonable state-transfer efforts continue periodically. Under subsection (f) the director's recovery certificate initiates judicial discharge or a hearing, with the applicable burden and approved treatment regimen and compliance condition for conditional discharge. A court may modify or eliminate that regimen after a hearing employing the same criteria. Subsection (g) requires a hearing before recommitment for noncompliance and resulting risk; arrest may follow notice or other probable cause of noncompliance. Subsection (h)'s furlough limits apply to the specified clear-and-convincing category and permit court approval after notice and opportunity for hearing, emergency departure, or accompaniment by a federal law enforcement officer.

These provisions establish commitment and release procedures. They contain no express nationwide command to instruct the guilt jury on disposition. The cited committee report endorses a procedure under which a jury may be instructed and leaves the effect of a defendant's request for omission to the court's discretion. It is not an enacted instruction command, and permissive omission cannot be converted into compulsory omission.

Shannon sought a short commitment instruction or, alternatively, a longer formulation implying hospitalization until the court found no specified risk. The latter requires correction to avoid erasing the initial hearing, differentiated burdens, state transfer, conditional release and other actual statutory qualifications. No row endorses it verbatim. The first formulation can be made accurate without promising a duration, and its accuracy alone does not decide whether it is mandatory.

The actual charge told jurors not to consider punishment or consequences. The reported record supplies neither an affirmative prosecutor/witness representation that insanity acquittal means immediate freedom nor an express juror question establishing the need to correct such a representation. The Fifth Circuit's lower rule denied judicial discretion altogether; affirmance of this judgment need not adopt that categorical proposition. Two experts agreed that chronic mental illness did not satisfy the statutory offense-time insanity standard, and Shannon acknowledged sufficient Government evidence if believed. This bears on possible harmlessness, not knowledge of the jury's reasoning or a conclusive finding that another instruction could not matter.

## Adversarial comparison of lawful paths

**Ordinary no-consequences rule with a corrective exception.** Affirm because neither the statute, due process on this record, nor sound federal supervisory practice requires the requested instruction. A guilt jury decides the prescribed offense and defense issues from the evidence. An account of possible disposition is not ordinarily relevant to that task and can invite a choice based on confinement rather than the legal insanity test. Rogers v. United States, 422 U.S. 35, supplies the instruction to decide guilt without regard to sentence, while also demonstrating the need to correct a court communication that could distort the jury's role. The general rule therefore is not an absolute prohibition: if an affirmative statement by the prosecutor or a witness misleads the jury that an insanity acquittee will go free, a tailored accurate corrective instruction can be necessary. Its content must address the misleading statement without a guarantee of duration or release outcome. This case supplies no such statement.

The strongest contrary argument is that the unusual third verdict is commonly misunderstood, so silence may drive a conviction based on an unfounded fear of release. The answer for this path is that generalized assumed misunderstanding does not establish inability to follow the express no-consequences charge. A complete description could emphasize possible early release and create the same forbidden disposition-based judgment in the opposite direction. The Court need not deny that a specific misleading trial event warrants correction. Simmons is different: release status there answers the State's actual sentencing argument and bears directly on the jury's authorized task.

**Limited requested explanation as federal supervisory practice.** Vacate the affirmance and remand for preserved, nonconstitutional harmless-error review because a defendant properly presenting insanity and requesting clarification should receive a brief accurate explanation that the special verdict results in immediate commitment subject to statutory judicial review, together with a direction that disposition must not determine guilt. The distinctive verdict combines acquittal and an immediate nonpunitive consequence; a brief explanation can prevent a familiar false choice without turning the jury into the release authority. Lyles supplies persuasive experience with that practice. The new requirement is a supervisory trial rule, not a quotation from §§4242–4243, not a conversion of the Senate report's may into shall, and not an extension of the Eighth Amendment beyond capital sentencing.

The strongest contrary argument is that this information is legally irrelevant to guilt and risks encouraging jurors to compromise on hospitalization. The answer for this path is that a precise explanation plus a firm guilt-only instruction can remove a mistaken implication without inviting a sentencing decision. Rogers's protection against misleading consequences communications does not categorically prohibit accurate explanation of a special verdict. Nonetheless, the error is not automatically structural. Rule 52(a), Kotteakos, Hasting and Bank of Nova Scotia require consideration of its effect on substantial rights; Olano preserves the Government's burden for preserved error rather than imposing the forfeited-error burden on Shannon. This record does not justify inventing juror reasoning or skipping ordinary review. Remand allows the lower court to decide harmlessness; if the Government cannot establish it, a new trial follows. No acquittal or commitment is ordered by this Supreme Court disposition.

A rule requiring every detail of §4243 in every insanity trial is rejected by both paths. The due-process claim can be resolved against an entitlement on this record without settling every possible misleading instruction or argument. A merely discretionary-instruction rule, resting on the committee report alone, neither establishes Shannon's entitlement nor supplies an independent reversal ground here.

## Totals, compatibility and reservations

Six non-Stone Justices—O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg—affirm on no-error grounds. Blackmun and Stevens would vacate and remand for preserved nonconstitutional harmless-error review under their requested-explanation rule. All eight preserve a difference between immediate statutory commitment and guaranteed continuing hospitalization. None adopts the Fifth Circuit's categorical proposition that a trial court lacks power to correct a materially misleading representation about freedom after an insanity acquittal.

The six-vote no-error group supports a corrective exception responsive to an actual affirmative misstatement, with an accurate, limited instruction appropriate to that event. This does not imply that the exception's trigger occurred here, authorize every requested consequences instruction, or make a disposition instruction mandatory merely because insanity is raised. A later proposed broader rule based on generalized anticipated misunderstanding must answer the recorded jury-function objections; accuracy of the proposed script alone is not sufficient for a join.

No opinion assignment, published separate-writing topology or final Court coalition is established. The constitutional issue is resolved only as necessary to deny a general entitlement on this record; no constitutional invalidation of the Act, sentence, initial commitment procedure or release burden is proposed. The possible corrective-instruction scenario remains distinct from a holding about actual events in Shannon.
