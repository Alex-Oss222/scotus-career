## Statutory and regulatory components

1. **Later taxable transfer.** Sections 2501(a)(1) and 2511(a), subject to the chapter's limitations, reach direct or indirect gratuitous transfers of valuable property interests, in trust or otherwise. Irvine's August 23, 1979 partial disclaimer redirected five-sixteenths of her remainder interest to her five children, one-sixteenth each. The transfer taxed is that 1979 disposition, not the 1917 creation of the irrevocable inter vivos trust. State law creates and defines property interests, but federal law determines their federal tax treatment unless Congress makes that treatment dependent on state law. Minnesota's effective relation-back fiction does not establish a federal exclusion. Jewett supplies the treatment of an indirect disclaimer transfer and the state/federal distinction; Dickman supplies the comprehensive statutory reach.
2. **Conditional regulatory alternative.** The parties agree that the 1986 regulation supersedes the 1958 version for this dispute. Its applicability remains unresolved: “taxable transfers” creating an interest before January 1, 1977 and the language concerning a decedent's testamentary transfer each present a genuine textual difficulty for this pre-1932 inter vivos trust. If the regulation applies, however, a disclaimer delayed at least 47 years after knowledge and capacity is not within a reasonable time. Jewett measures from knowledge of the transfer creating the interest, not later possession. The result is the same measuring from knowledge by 1931 or enactment in 1932. The Government would not contest a disclaimer within a reasonable time after enactment. No holding requires a disclaimer before enactment or supplies a new bright-line deadline.
3. **Statutory alternative if the regulation does not apply.** Inapplicability of a regulatory exception does not produce immunity from the statute or make state relation back controlling. The existing federal transfer rule independently reaches this delayed disposition. This route does not delete the decedent language, extend the regulation to inter vivos transfers by fiat, or depend on a new retroactive Treasury rule. The exception's conditions remain cumulative: an unequivocal refusal effective under local law, reasonable time after knowledge, and no prior acceptance. Partial refusal depends on its completeness and effectiveness under the facts and local law. Failure to refuse within reasonable time supports the stated presumption of acceptance absent contrary facts. Nothing here substitutes the later §2518 qualified-disclaimer regime for this 1917 interest.
4. **1932 prospective limitation.** Revenue Act §501(b) protects transfers made on or before June 6, 1932. It does not exempt every later transfer of property acquired before that date. Taxing the 1979 transfer is prospective in the relevant sense. Jacobs supplies the distinction between taxing a later event and referring to preenactment conditions. The due-process argument based on denying any reasonable opportunity to disclaim after the Act is not presented by a taxpayer who waited until 1979; no general approval of retroactive regulations follows.

The estate's strongest alternative combines the old trust, the regulatory limitations, state effectiveness, and the express 1932 exclusion. It prevents an opinion that simply assumes regulatory coverage. It does not defeat either branch of the analysis: unreasonable delay if the exception applies, statutory liability if it does not. The lower en banc majority's “taxable transfers” rationale and Judge Loken's decedent-language rationale therefore can be left unresolved without affirming their further conclusion that state law controls federal taxability.
