## Statute in full and material distinctions

The authorized statute-only supplement reproduces the complete enacted §3001(i), 42 U.S.C. §6921(i):

> A resource recovery facility recovering energy from the mass burning of municipal solid waste shall not be deemed to be treating, storing, disposing of, or otherwise managing hazardous wastes for the purposes of regulation under this subchapter, if—
>
> (1) such facility—
>
> (A) receives and burns only—
>
> (i) household waste (from single and multiple dwellings, hotels, motels, and other residential sources), and
>
> (ii) solid waste from commercial or industrial sources that does not contain hazardous waste identified or listed under this section, and
>
> (B) does not accept hazardous wastes identified or listed under this section, and
>
> (2) the owner or operator of such facility has established contractual requirements or other appropriate notification or inspection procedures to assure that hazardous wastes are not received at or burned in such facility.

The 1991 lower opinion abbreviates the conditions and transcribes “subchapter” as “subsection.” The complete supplement controls. The input limitation, the prohibition on accepting identified or listed hazardous wastes, and the owner's contractual or other appropriate notification or inspection safeguards are distinct conditions. The language does not require all three types of safeguard simultaneously. Nor does satisfying a statutory condition establish any different condition as a matter of fact.

The final prefreeze supplement, `OT_1993CHUNK4_CHICAGO_DEFINITIONS.md`, supplies the exact definitions from the official 1994 Code, with amendment notes verifying the definitions were unchanged. Section 6903 introduces them with “As used in this chapter.” Paragraph (6) states: “The term ‘hazardous waste generation’ means the act or process of producing hazardous waste.” Paragraph (7) states: “The term ‘hazardous waste management’ means the systematic control of the collection, source separation, storage, transportation, processing, treatment, recovery, and disposal of hazardous wastes.” The hazardous-waste-generation requirements are distinct from facility treatment, storage, and disposal requirements. That distinction is important, but does not by itself answer the City's argument that a facility exclusion necessarily protects the ordinary residue of its exempt operation.

The 1980 household-waste regulation and its preamble supplied an exclusion throughout management of household waste, including treatment residues. The later enactment addresses a qualifying facility that also burns nonhazardous commercial or industrial material. Whether Congress extended a waste-stream exclusion or gave a more limited facility exclusion is the live question. An input is not proved hazardous by an ash characteristic, and a nonhazardous input does not ensure nonhazardous ash. No independent ruling on every exclusively household-waste stream or on the validity of the 1980 regulation is necessary.

## Meaningful alternatives and governing agency law

**Narrow statutory exclusion:** the enacted facility language lists treatment, storage, disposal, and management, while omitting the distinct generation category. It does not say the resulting ash ceases to be hazardous waste. Qualifying input controls preserve the facility's protection without eliminating generator obligations when the new residue actually meets hazardous-waste criteria. Under this reading the EPA memorandum exceeds the statutory range; affirmance need not adopt the lower court's assertion that changed agency positions deserve no deference.

**Waste-stream continuity:** the statute's introductory facility protection, its reference to managing waste, the preexisting household-waste rule, and the clarifying enactment context can support protecting the expected residue as well as the incoming material. A mixed stream containing only nonhazardous commercial waste need not logically lose the protection household waste previously enjoyed. The contemporaneous Senate report expressly included generation among covered activities. If the enacted terms leave that question unresolved, EPA can choose a reasonable construction of a technical scheme. This is Chicago's strongest argument; it is stronger than a free-standing policy claim that incineration should be inexpensive.

**Remand for a new agency answer:** the September 1992 memorandum already supplied the agency's position, and the lower court considered it. A further remand is not necessary merely because EPA previously changed its views. A remand for application and remaining relief is different from a remand that avoids the legal interpretation altogether. No preserved notice-and-comment objection is invented or decided.

Chevron, 467 U.S. 837 (1984), requires giving effect to resolved statutory meaning and otherwise respecting permissible agency construction. Its authored opinion by Stevens expressly rejects a categorical refusal to defer whenever an agency has changed position. Pauley, 501 U.S. 680 (1991), Blackmun's opinion joined by Stevens, O'Connor, and Souter among others, emphasizes technical context and reasonable harmonization; Scalia's dissent distinguishes genuine ambiguity from difficult but determinate text. Kennedy did not participate in Pauley. His Bowen opinion supplies an independently verified example of identifying an agency interpretation's actual statutory authorization, not a vote on ash.

Actual simulated John Hancock, December 13, 1993, is the closest current public administrative-law position. Ginsburg's opinion, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, and Souter among others, rejects an agency reading that erases a qualified statutory exclusion; Thomas's dissent, joined by O'Connor and Kennedy, separately rejects the agency's categorical reading while disputing the correct construction. The controlling law expressly leaves general treatment of inconsistent or litigation agency views open. Neither Chicago path may convert the lower court's “no deference” statement into a general rule. Each Justice's opposite-path analysis must explain why this statute either supplies or lacks the dispositive limit.
