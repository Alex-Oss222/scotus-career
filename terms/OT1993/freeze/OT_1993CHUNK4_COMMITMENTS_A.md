## Boca Grande Club, Inc. v. Florida Power & Light Co.

**Event:** April 20, 1994, after the expressly sequenced effective McDermott decision. **Participation:** all eight non-Stone Justices. **Result committed by each participating Justice below:** vacate the Eleventh Circuit judgment and remand for proceedings consistent with McDermott's proportionate-share rule and corresponding bar to contribution for a voluntary settlement shortfall.

**Common ground and scope.** The claim against nonsettlers is reduced by the settling tortfeasors' equitable shares, not their settlement dollars. Because a nonsettler is protected against having the settling tortfeasor's share shifted to it through a deficient bargain, contribution against the settler cannot be used to undo the settlement. No showing that this settlement equaled Boca Grande's share is necessary to establish the governing rule. The fault allocation still must be reliable. The result does not decide an independently sourced indemnity claim, settlement fraud or invalidity, total damages, exoneration, limitation, or a nonsettlement shortfall. The alternative factual ground Boca Grande advanced below remains unadjudicated and is unnecessary to the legal disposition.

**Alternative-path comparison.** The strongest contribution path was Great Lakes' protection of a nonsettler from excessive payment under a different credit rule. Effective McDermott now supplies that protection through proportionate reduction. Applying a contribution remedy premised on dollar credit would combine inconsistent mechanisms. This is therefore a routine application of each Justice's immediately preceding, issue-specific public join, not a fresh choice between abstract settlement policies. The minimum permissible formulation is limited to general maritime voluntary-settlement shortfalls; a categorical abolition of maritime joint and several liability is a join barrier.

## United States v. Irvine

**Event:** April 20, 1994, start-of-day entering baseline. **Participation:** Blackmun takes no part in the decision. The other seven non-Stone Justices' commitments are stated separately below.

**Shared minimum legal proposition.** Sections 2501 and 2511 apply to Irvine's gratuitous disposition in 1979. They do not tax the grandfather's 1917 transfer retroactively. State law establishes the property interest and the legal effectiveness of the refusal, but its relation-back characterization does not decide the federal transfer-tax consequence. The 1932 exclusion of transfers on or before enactment does not immunize every subsequent disposition of property then held. The statutory rule remains relevant even if the disclaimer is outside a regulatory exception. Jewett, 455 U.S. 305, supplies the distinction between state effectiveness and federal consequences and rejects waiting for possession as the federal timeliness measure. Dickman, 465 U.S. 330, supplies the broad transfer rule and rejects an exemption inferred from the absence of earlier administrative enforcement.

**The meaningful fork.** The estate's strongest path is not simply “state law controls.” It combines the 1932 express protection, the later regulation's “taxable transfers” limitation, the older regulation's decedent limitation, Irvine's inability to possess the remainder before 1979, and reliance on the Eighth Circuit's pre-Jewett approach. The majority and concurrence below offer distinct ways to remove the transaction from the regulatory text. Those arguments prevent a reversal that merely asserts the later regulation always covered this inter vivos trust. They do not establish that Congress excluded a 1979 gratuitous disposition from the governing statute. No tax-free substantive entitlement is shown to have matured in 1917 or 1931 to make all later choices immune. The law in force at the later disposition governs that disposition. Jewett's later judicial construction is distinct from a Treasury attempt to promulgate a new substantive rule retroactively.

**Regulatory limits preserved by every reversal commitment.** No Justice is committed to treating the 1958 decedent language as surplusage, inventing an administrative extension to inter vivos trusts, or using the 1986 amendment as a necessary retroactive source of liability. If the estate claims the later disclaimer exclusion, its long interval after knowledge fails Jewett's reasonable-time approach; state timeliness alone is not sufficient. The Government's concession below about which regulatory version governs must be acknowledged. The statutory ground does not contradict that concession. The nine-month qualified-disclaimer provision enacted for interests created after December 31, 1976 is not imposed on this 1917 interest.

## Landgraf v. USI Film Products

**Event:** April 26, 1994. **Participation:** all eight non-Stone Justices. **Components:** compensatory damages; punitive damages; conditional statutory jury election. No later Rivers holding is assumed.

### Authorities, enactment, and competing paths

The actual enacted §102 distinguishes new compensation from preexisting §706(g) relief and separately restricts punitive damages. Section 102(c) permits any party to demand a jury only when §102 compensation or punitive damages are sought under an available statutory claim; it does not independently add juries to all pending Title VII cases. Section 402(a)'s immediate effective date, §109(c)'s limited extraterritorial direction, and §402(b)'s conjunctive exception for certain old disparate-impact cases must be analyzed separately. Neither the Act's remedial purpose nor the inclusion of these limited temporal directions automatically resolves every other amendment's coverage.

**Path A — no new damages for this pre-enactment conduct.** Compensation for emotional and other specified injuries adds a category of legal liability beyond previously available equitable relief. Punitive damages add punishment and have an even stronger new-obligation character. Applying either to completed conduct alters the consequences of that conduct; the fact that harassment was already unlawful does not eliminate the change in liability. No sufficient temporal instruction in §§102 or 402 displaces that objection. With neither new damages category available, this plaintiff cannot activate §102(c) merely by characterizing a completed bench trial as pending procedural business. The minimum disposition affirms the denial of these new forms of relief, without announcing that all procedural, jurisdictional, collateral-fee, or prospective-relief changes are inapplicable to pending cases.

**Path B — compensation for a pending case, punishment withheld.** Bradley applies current law in an open case unless Congress directs otherwise or application produces manifest injustice, considering the nature of the parties and dispute, the character of affected rights, and the impact of the change. The liability prohibition here predates the conduct. Compensation can be viewed as relief for an established wrong, within the Act's immediate effective date and its express exceptions; no protected entitlement to commit harassment exists. Punishment, however, changes the stakes in a distinct way and can be withheld. If §102 compensation is available, either party may elect a jury on remand as the statute provides. The strongest objection is that compensation itself adds substantial exposure even when no backpay was due; Bradley's collateral school-litigation fee award did not create comparable private liability.

**Path C — all §102 relief in pending actions.** Immediate effectiveness and express prospective exceptions might support broad current-law application. But punitive damages for completed conduct add punishment without a clear temporal directive. No Justice below commits to this path. Its rejection does not decide what constitutional limits would apply to an expressly retroactive punitive provision.

Bradley, 416 U.S. 696, is read with Bennett v. New Jersey, 470 U.S. 632, which preserves substantive obligations fixed under earlier law, and Bowen, 488 U.S. 204, which recognizes the presumption against retroactivity in the statutory/delegated-rule setting. Kaiser, 494 U.S. 827, resolved a statutory interest question by congressional intent while leaving the broader Bradley/Bowen tension open; its separate writings show the meaningful division. Harper's actual simulated judicial-retroactivity rule does not itself require retroactive application of a later statute. Robertson involved an express change to the law applicable to identified pending litigation. Franklin permitted appropriate compensation within an existing implied Title IX cause of action absent congressional limitation; it does not silently replace Title VII's earlier express remedial scheme with §102.

### Blackmun

**Frozen result:** reverse in part and remand as to availability of compensatory damages and a consequent §102(c) jury election; affirm denial of punitive damages for the pre-enactment conduct. Availability is not an award or a finding that all predicates for compensation have been established.

**Ground and scope:** adopt Path B for this still-pending intentional-discrimination action. His own Bradley opinion supplies the current-law rule and requires an actual manifest-injustice inquiry. His join in White's Kaiser dissent supports distinguishing pending remedial consequences from vested substantive immunity. The completed unlawful conduct is not made newly unlawful. The Act supplies additional compensation while the case remains open, and §§109(c) and 402(b) demonstrate that Congress considered targeted temporal limits. Those clauses inform the interpretation; they are not recast as a conclusive command that every provision reaches all earlier conduct. Punitive damages present a distinct imposition of punishment and are withheld absent a clearer instruction.

**Strongest adverse comparison:** he joined Bennett, whose protection of substantive grant obligations limits Bradley. The employer is private, this is not Bradley's school-integration fee dispute, and compensation can create a major obligation where old Title VII supplied no money for the proven injury. His chosen distinction is that the remedial change compensates injury from a preexisting federal wrong in an unfinished action, whereas Bennett changed the substantive legal standards governing the underlying grant obligation. This is the vulnerable, Justice-specific fork, not a claim that Bradley compels the answer or that compensation is always procedural.

**Join barriers and narrower position:** no join in punitive retroactivity, wholesale displacement of mature contractual or statutory rights, or mandatory retrial of every settled issue. A narrower writing may permit compensation only in pending, nonfinal Title VII actions for conduct already prohibited and reserve other provisions. Section 102(c) then permits either party to demand a jury for the authorized damages claim; the remand court must determine the issues requiring trial and any effect on prior findings under governing law. This commitment does not itself impose an amount, find malice, reopen the rejected constructive-discharge theory, or erase all findings from the completed bench trial.

### Stevens

**Frozen result:** affirm denial of compensation, punitive damages, and the associated jury request for the pre-Act conduct, on Path A.

**Ground and scope:** the new monetary categories alter legal liability, and immediate effectiveness is not a sufficiently specific instruction to attach them to completed conduct. His join in O'Connor's Kaiser opinion supports first resolving what Congress directed rather than forcing one universal pending-case slogan. His Bennett dissent is the strongest counter: he understood the later grant law there as clarification of the original statutory command. Section 102 here supplies remedies that the old Title VII scheme did not already furnish; treating it as a mere clarification would disregard that change. His Franklin join supports ordinary remedies where Congress has not specified otherwise, not judicial enlargement of an earlier express Title VII remedial scheme.

**Adverse path and join limits:** Bradley supports current-law treatment of remedial provisions, but collateral fees and genuine clarifications differ from new compensation and punishment for completed private conduct. He can join a bounded presumption preserving Bradley's setting. No join is committed to declaring Bradley overruled, classifying all procedure as retrospective, or excluding every Act provision from every pending case. The jury conclusion rests on §102(c)'s unsatisfied condition, with the completed trial as a further remedial distinction; no universal ban on new procedures at future trials is necessary.

### O'Connor

**Frozen result:** affirm on all three components, on Path A.

**Ground and scope:** her own Bennett opinion limits Bradley where a later law would change substantive obligations fixed under earlier law. Her own Kaiser opinion resolves statutory coverage before applying a generalized pending-case presumption. A new damages category changes the employer's legal burden for past conduct even when the prohibition itself is unchanged. Punitive damages are independently outside the permissible temporal reach. Neither the effective-date provision nor two different, limited exceptions supplies a general retroactive command.

**Adverse path and join limits:** the strongest compensation argument is Bradley's remedial setting and Congress's wish to strengthen existing civil rights enforcement. The answer is that §102 creates a new kind of liability here, while Bradley concerned litigation fees and existing substantive obligations. Her narrower permissible position excludes §102 compensation and punishment on these facts without denying that fee rules, genuinely procedural provisions, or prospective relief may follow current law. With no authorized damages claim, §102(c) does not give a separate jury entitlement. No constitutional adjudication of an expressly retroactive Act is needed.

### Scalia

**Frozen result:** affirm on all three components, with Path A's result and a more categorical interpretive ground confined to retrospective legal effect.

**Ground and scope:** his Kaiser concurrence states the presumption of prospective operation absent contrary text or necessary implication and disputes Bradley as a general presumption for ordinary retrospective legislation. His Bowen concurrence requires identifying the event to which the new legal consequence attaches; not every law operating in a pending case is retrospective. Here the new compensation and punishment attach to completed discriminatory conduct, while neither §102 nor §402 directs that operation. The jury provision is expressly contingent on a damages claim under §102 and cannot alone authorize relief from the completed bench proceeding.

**Adverse path and join limits:** Blackmun's Bradley route has stronger support if compensation is merely a remedy for an unchanged wrong. Scalia's ground treats added monetary exposure as a changed legal consequence, not merely a courtroom mode. He can join statutory-text and retrospective-effect portions. He is not committed to a framework that makes free-ranging fairness or the public importance of the suit decide statutory reach, or that adopts Bradley as the general starting presumption for new substantive liability. Minimum compatible opinion: no express or necessarily implied instruction for these new liabilities; leave unrelated future procedures and prospective injunctions alone.

### Kennedy

**Frozen result:** affirm on all three components, on Path A.

**Ground and scope:** his Bowen opinion recognizes that retroactive operation requires affirmative legal authorization rather than an assumption from silence; his Kaiser join permits the statutory inquiry to resolve the case without eliminating Bradley's proper domain. Section 102 compensation and punishment change the consequences of completed private conduct. Section 402(a) sets commencement, not clearly backward coverage. The two specific temporal qualifications do not supply the missing direction for §102.

**Adverse path and join limits:** a law governing the court's future remedial act can be characterized as prospective, especially while review is pending. The response is the identification of the conduct that generates the new liability, not simply the later date a judgment would issue. The minimum join reserves current-law treatment of purely prospective remedies, collateral litigation matters, and genuinely procedural changes. No independent jury claim survives once new damages are unavailable; no holding on a freestanding future-trial jury statute is required.

### Souter

**Frozen result:** affirm on all three components, on the minimum Path A ground.

**Ground and scope:** no directly matching personal predecision §102 temporal writing was established. His actual Franklin join does not permit ignoring an express remedial statutory structure; his actual Robertson join recognizes an express pending-case change, which this Act does not clearly contain for §102. His Harper join concerns judicial decisions and cannot transfer its rule mechanically to later enactments. The statutory distinction between prior equitable relief and new legal damages, read with Bennett, Bowen, and Kaiser's unresolved broader tension, supports withholding these newly created liabilities.

**Adverse path and join limits:** Bradley and the limited temporal exceptions provide a meaningful compensation alternative. The decisive boundary is the new monetary obligation, rather than an assumption that all remedial changes are substantive. Minimum join: these §102 damages for this completed conduct and the consequent failure of the jury condition. Do not generalize to all Act provisions or purport to settle every conflict between Bradley and Bowen. The absence of an exact own writing is a disclosed evidentiary limit, not a fabricated personal precedent.

### Thomas

**Frozen result:** affirm on all three components, on the minimum Path A ground.

**Ground and scope:** his actual Robertson opinion applies Congress's express substitution of governing law in pending timber litigation; his actual Harper opinion applies a judicial rule already given to the deciding parties to other open direct-review cases. Neither supplies the absent instruction to impose new §102 liabilities on earlier conduct. The enacted distinctions between compensation, punishment, and existing equitable relief support treating §102 as a substantive enlargement for temporal purposes. The jury provision follows its textual condition.

**Adverse path and join limits:** the strongest alternative combines Bradley with a negative inference from §§109(c) and 402(b). Those clauses have their own stated fields and do not necessarily imply that every other amendment governs completed acts. Minimum join: statutory presumption and §102's actual added burden, while leaving procedural changes and other Act provisions for their own text. No personal join in pre-appointment Kaiser or Bowen is invented, and Harper is not recast as a statutory retroactivity case.

### Ginsburg

**Frozen result:** affirm on all three components, on the narrow Path A ground.

**Ground and scope:** no exact personal predecision temporal writing was established, and Gersman is not attributed to her. The commitment rests on the enacted text, the material distinction between old Title VII relief and new monetary liability, and the controlling date-eligible authorities. An immediate effective date does not by itself tell a court to attach new damages to completed acts. Harris, which she actually joined, defines the hostile-environment liability inquiry; it does not decide the remedial reach of a later enactment or undo the settled constructive-discharge finding here.

**Adverse path and join limits:** Bradley's pending-case rule and the remedial purpose of the Act make compensation the strongest alternative; punitive damages remain a distinct and more serious obstacle. The chosen minimum ground accepts the compensation category's enlargement of liability and declines to derive a general retrospective command from limited exceptions. She can join only the §102 application and conditional-jury conclusion, reserving broader temporal theory and other provisions. This is an independent bounded legal commitment with a disclosed personal-source limitation, not a claim that an unlocated prior opinion settles her vote.

### Remedial boundaries preserved for reconciliation

The seven Path A commitments affirm denial of §102 compensation, punitive damages, and a jury on the new damages demand. They do not authorize a fresh decision on already rejected constructive discharge, retaliation, nominal relief under the old scheme, or standalone declaratory relief. Blackmun's Path B commitment changes the availability of compensation and permits a consequential jury election, requiring remand to address remaining statutory predicates and proper trial scope. It does not award damages or require punitive relief. Any eventual common opinion must preserve these differences rather than treating agreement on the employer's underlying duty as agreement on every remedy. No Court vote total or opinion authorship is fixed here.
