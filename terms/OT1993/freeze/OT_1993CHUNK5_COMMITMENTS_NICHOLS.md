## Live components, Guidelines and source qualifications

The current federal cocaine-conspiracy prosecution was counseled. Nichols pleaded guilty in 1990 under 21 U.S.C. §846. A 1983 federal drug felony supplied three criminal-history points. A 1983 Georgia DUI conviction on a nolo contendere plea supplied the challenged fourth point: $250 fine and DUI school, no imprisonment. The Georgia provision authorized ten days to one year's imprisonment, or a $100–$1,000 fine, or both. Authorization is not actual custody. At offense level 34, the point moved category II's 168–210 months to category III's 188–235 months. The sentence was 235 months plus eight years of supervised release. The statutory ten-year-to-life range is distinct; twenty-five months is the excess over the lower Guidelines ceiling, not an identified statutory excess or an automatic measure of remedy.

The complete official November 1990 chapter controls over the petition's imperfect reproduction. Section 4A1.1(c) adds one point for each prior sentence “not included” in (a) or (b), capped at four points for that item. The 1990 provision contains (a)–(e), not the petition's later-added (f). Section 4A1.2(a)(1) includes sentences after nolo pleas for conduct outside the instant offense. The ordinary ten-year counting period for other sentences turns on commencement of the instant offense; no new timing dispute is supplied here.

The misdemeanor qualifications remain intact. Under §4A1.2(c)(1), listed or similar offenses count only with probation of at least one year, imprisonment of at least thirty days, or similarity to the instant offense. The list comprises careless/reckless driving, contempt, disorderly conduct/disturbing peace, license offenses, false information to police, fish/game violations, gambling, hindering/failure to obey police, insufficient-funds checks, leaving an accident, local ordinances except violations also constituting state crimes, nonsupport, prostitution, resisting arrest and trespass. Subsection (c)(2) never counts listed or similar hitchhiking, juvenile-status/truancy, loitering, minor traffic, public intoxication and vagrancy offenses. Note 5 separately says DUI and similar offenses are counted and are not minor traffic infractions; no later “always” wording or unpresented exception dispute is imported. The parties do not contest this DUI's ordinary statutory counting apart from the counsel objection.

Amendment 353's background commentary, effective November 1, 1990, counts prior sentences not otherwise excluded, including uncounseled misdemeanor sentences where imprisonment was not imposed. A brief's citation to the accumulated 1993 appendix does not establish application of new 1993 law. Note 6 excludes convictions reversed or vacated for legal error or newly discovered exonerating evidence, and those previously ruled constitutionally invalid; it permits consideration of underlying conduct under §4A1.3. The background expressly leaves collateral-attack availability to courts. Stinson makes permissible interpretive commentary authoritative, subject to the Constitution, statutes, the Guideline and plainly erroneous interpretation; it cannot decide the constitutional objection by its own authority. No separate temporal or ex post facto holding is needed on the supplied version record.

Section 4A1.3 permits, rather than requires, departure upon reliable information that criminal history significantly overrepresents seriousness or recidivism risk. Its reference is the lower category's range. A prior arrest record itself cannot be used; the provision does not authorize going below category I's lower limit on this ground. Nichols is not shown to have requested an overrepresentation departure. The presence of discretion does not establish its exercise or permit inventing a departure to sustain a sentence resting on an unconstitutional score.

Absence of counsel at the DUI is uncontested. Waiver and indigency are not settled. The district court treated waiver as unclear and refused to presume it from silence; the Government disputes that legal treatment and invokes reported attorney consultation and reported contracting income. Neither allegation establishes informed waiver or ability to afford counsel. Scott's actual-imprisonment rule makes the fine-only conviction valid without appointed counsel on its own terms, even though imprisonment was authorized. Whether that valid conviction may support additional incarceration for a later offense is the principal question. Whether an unconstitutional complete denial, waiver or indigency inquiry would instead control under an alternative rule is separate.

## Precedent and adversarial alternatives

**Valid-conviction recidivist path:** Affirm the judgment. A misdemeanor conviction valid under Scott because it imposed no imprisonment may be considered in sentencing a later, counseled offense, including the challenged criminal-history point. The new punishment is for the new offense, and sentencing ordinarily considers prior conduct and record without requiring that every historical fact have been established with the procedures of the current criminal trial. Burgett and Tucker concern constitutionally invalid predicates; their exclusion is not extended to a conviction whose original validity is conceded under the governing rule. The strongest objection is that Scott's no-incarceration premise becomes hollow when that conviction later supplies an obligatory increase in the imprisonment range. This path regards Scott as defining the constitutional requirement in the first prosecution, not as permanently disabling a valid conviction from ordinary later sentencing use. The current sentencing remains subject to its own counsel, factual, statutory and constitutional requirements.

For O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, a purported constitutional bar in Baldasar to this use should be overruled, rather than treated as an unacknowledged exception. Baldasar produced no majority rationale and has not supplied a coherent rule reconciling its judgment with Scott's validity boundary. The argument for adhering is substantial: its judgment is precedent, and constitutional counsel protection cannot be set aside just because the original rationales divided. Their response rests on the tension between that judgment and use of concededly valid prior adjudications, the absence of a workable common rationale, and the absence of a legitimate entitlement to commit a later crime at a sentencing rate fixed by the earlier fine. The absence of such reliance alone does not defeat stare decisis. No claim is made that a Guidelines commentary amendment can overrule a constitutional judgment. Scott remains operative; invalid convictions remain distinct.

**Additional-imprisonment path:** Reverse the stated affirmance ground and remand. Accept Scott's validity for the fine judgment but apply Baldasar's protection to a material increase in the later Guidelines imprisonment range. A classification label—felony conversion versus sentencing category—does not change the liberty consequence. The strongest objection is that the prior is valid and the current defendant receives counsel, while sentencing may consider less formal information. The response is that a formally counted conviction supplies a legal enhancement, and reliability protection cannot depend solely on labeling that consequence punishment for the new crime. Independently proved conduct is a different possible sentencing basis; none is supplied as the actual ground here. This path neither vacates the old fine conviction nor awards a mechanical twenty-five-month reduction.

**Blackmun's distinct invalid-original-conviction path:** His Scott dissent requires counsel where an offense is nonpetty because more than six months may be imposed, or whenever actual imprisonment is imposed. The Georgia one-year exposure meets the first branch if appointed-counsel entitlement and absence of valid waiver are established. His Baldasar concurrence applied that premise, not an express rule disabling use of all convictions conceded valid under Scott. His Parke judgment concurrence permits a rebuttable presumption in sentencing, but expressly distinguishes actually invalid uncounseled convictions; it does not abandon his Scott premise. The strongest objection is Scott's controlling actual-imprisonment rule. His separate ground continues that disagreement rather than disguising it as a majority precedent. Remand must resolve preserved waiver and indigency alternatives before excluding the point on this ground.

**Narrow Guidelines path:** Souter affirms this use in the supplied sentencing scheme without joining a general overruling of Baldasar. The counseled later sentencing applies an ordinary criminal-history score from a Scott-valid conviction, with accurate scoring, exclusion of established invalid predicates and a genuine, though discretionary, mechanism for correcting significant overrepresentation. This is not a new substantive offense or the mandatory felony-conversion mechanism before Baldasar. The strongest objection is that §4A1.3 does not itself undo the point and was not invoked, so a possible departure cannot cure a constitutional loss of liberty. Souter does not find or invent a departure or treat it as a remedy for an otherwise unconstitutional score. He regards the narrower Guidelines use as constitutionally permissible within this framework in the first instance and reserves the broader mandatory-enhancement question. This rationale does not create a general duty to entertain every collateral validity claim, require a downward departure, or decide every possible challenge to an uncounseled prior's accuracy.

**Marks and disposition boundaries:** Stewart's Baldasar concurrence, joined by Brennan and Stevens, accepted Scott and rejected increased imprisonment attributable to the uncounseled conviction. Marshall's concurrence, joined by Brennan and Stevens, emphasized reliability and later liberty despite the original conviction's validity. Blackmun instead considered the original nonpetty conviction invalid. Invalid-from-the-start and valid-but-unavailable rationales are not automatically logical subsets; the latter covers petty priors Blackmun's rule would not necessarily invalidate, and the former does not expressly answer every later use of a Scott-valid conviction. No five-Justice rationale is manufactured. The reversing use-rule proponents independently adopt and apply the liberty principle, informed by Baldasar's judgment, rather than claim four concurring votes plus Blackmun established it at every level. The affirming narrower position does not supply a fifth vote for overruling Baldasar generally.

## Channel, alternatives and operative remedy

Actual Custis is controlling in its own ACCA channel. It neither determines the constitutional use of a Scott-valid fine conviction nor supplies an affirmative finding of waiver or indigency. The Court there retained ordinary counting and classification objections and an unconstitutional complete-denial exception, while reserving other materially different review situations. Blackmun, Stevens and Souter's published dissent is continuity evidence, not current law overriding the channel holding. This case's principal use question remains reachable on either side of Custis.

For the affirming Justices, the sufficient valid-use or narrower Guidelines ground makes the Government's disputed waiver and indigency alternatives unnecessary. They do not affirm on either unproved alternative. For the reversing Justices, the Government must receive consideration of those preserved grounds under the proper legal framework on remand. Actual Parke permits a rebuttable production allocation for its counseled final-plea record and does not decide every other collateral system or establish waiver of counsel from silence. Actual Godinez distinguishes capacity from knowing, intelligent, voluntary waiver but does not allocate this record's disputed proof burden. The alleged consultation and income may be considered with the actual record; this Court does not find them proved or sufficient. The separate argument about warnings of possible future enhancement is not converted into a general requirement to forecast future sentencing; its role, if any, in the actual waiver dispute remains for the appropriate bounded inquiry.

If the point is ultimately found constitutionally unavailable and no lawful independent basis sustains the sentence, a lawful new sentencing proceeding is required. Williams's actual-ground and same-sentence principles foreclose inventing a category-II departure; its nonconstitutional error rule is not declared the universal harmlessness test for a Sixth Amendment violation. No finding is made that 235 months would actually have been imposed without the point. Conversely, the reversing votes do not command a twenty-five-month reduction, vacate the old Georgia fine, or resolve the separate supervised-release term without analysis.
