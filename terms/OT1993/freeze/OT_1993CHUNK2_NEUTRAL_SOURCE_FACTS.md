## 13. Weiss v. United States

Official source: United States Reports, 510 U.S. 163, pp. 165-170, 172, 178, 180-181, and the predecision materials expressly identified below. Reporter PDF stored separately in `chunk2-sources/Weiss-510-163.pdf`; full PDF text has been read by source control.

- Weiss pleaded guilty to UCMJ Article 121 larceny at a special court-martial; sentence: three months confinement, partial pay forfeiture, bad-conduct discharge. Hernandez pleaded guilty to cocaine possession, importation, distribution (Article 112a) and conspiracy (Article 81); sentence: 25 years confinement, all pay forfeiture, rank reduction, dishonorable discharge; the convening authority reduced confinement to 20 years.
- Navy-Marine Corps Court of Military Review affirmed each separately. In 36 M.J. 224 (1992), the Court of Military Appeals rejected the fixed-term challenge on United States v. Graf, 35 M.J. 450 (1992), and divided on appointment: two judges treated duties as germane to an existing commission; Judge Crawford concurred in result because she believed the Clause inapplicable to military officers; Chief Judge Sullivan and Judge Wiss separately dissented because the 1968 Act created sufficiently distinct duties. Hernandez was affirmed by unpublished order reported at 37 M.J. 252 (1993).
- The parties agree that the Appointments Clause applies to military officers and that military judges exercise officer functions. All judges at issue already held presidential commissions with Senate confirmation; civilian appellate judges are outside these facts. The record does not provide complete military appointment/promotion histories for each judge.
- Article 26, 10 U.S.C. section 826: trial military judges must be commissioned officers, admitted to a federal or highest state court bar, and certified qualified by the appropriate Judge Advocate General. A judge is detailed to every general court-martial and may be detailed to a special court-martial. Article 66, section 866: appellate judges are assigned by the relevant Judge Advocate General; they may be commissioned officers or civilians, although only officers are involved here. Neither class has a statutory fixed term.
- Article 26 prevents the convening authority or commanding officer from preparing or reviewing judicial-duty performance reports. Article 37, section 837, forbids censuring, reprimanding, or admonishing a military judge respecting findings, sentence, or judicial functions; Article 98, section 898, permits punishment for knowing, intentional noncompliance. Articles 26(c), 66(h) bar prior participants from judging the same case. Article 41 permits cause challenges. Article 16 permits the accused to learn the judge's identity before choosing a judge-only trial. These are separate safeguards, with separate terms.
- The Court of Military Appeals has five civilian judges appointed by President and Senate to 15-year terms; their appointment and tenure are outside the questions. United States v. Mabe, 33 M.J. 200 (1991), held rating judges on sentence appropriateness inconsistent with Article 26 and contrary to Article 37; Graf held decertification or transfer based on such disagreement likewise unlawful. These date-eligible military cases concern limits on command influence.
- Petitioners allege neither actual nor apparent bias of their judges. Their facial theory concerns lack of fixed terms. They propose Mathews v. Eldridge; the United States proposes Medina. Middendorf v. Henry, 425 U.S. 25, 43-44 (1976), supplies the earlier military due-process deference formulation, independently available to the model. No allegation that judges in these cases suffered actual career retaliation is established.

## 14. Schiro v. Farley

Official source: United States Reports, 510 U.S. 222, pp. 224-228, 233-235, 240-241 n.2, 244 n.5, 245 n.6, 246 n.8 (only reproduced trial/lower-court material extracted here). Reporter PDF stored separately in `chunk2-sources/Schiro-510-222.pdf`; full PDF text has been read by source control.

- Ind. Code section 35-42-1-1 (Supp. 1978): a person who knowingly or intentionally kills another human being, or kills another human being while committing or attempting arson, burglary, child molesting, criminal deviate conduct, kidnapping, rape, or robbery, commits murder, a felony.
- The information contained three counts arising from one death: Count I charged **knowing** killing; Count II killing while committing rape; Count III killing while committing criminal deviate conduct. The State sought death on Counts II and III. Do not rename Count I as a charge confined to intentional killing, although the two mental states become relevant to the arguments.
- Ten verdict choices were supplied: (1) not responsible by reason of insanity; (2) murder but mentally ill; (3) murder as charged in Count I; (4) not guilty; (5) murder in commission/attempt of rape, Count II; (6) murder in commission/attempt of criminal deviate conduct, Count III; (7) voluntary manslaughter; (8) involuntary manslaughter; (9) voluntary manslaughter but mentally ill; (10) involuntary manslaughter but mentally ill. Each was a separate form beginning "We, the jury, find the defendant." Only a single general not-guilty form existed, not separate not-guilty forms by count. After five hours, the jury returned guilty on Count II and left all other forms blank.
- Instruction 4 reproduced the statutory alternative definition of murder. Instruction 8 said that to sustain the charge of murder the State had to prove the defendant engaged in conduct causing the victim's death and, when he did so, knew it would or intended it to cause death; if these propositions were proved beyond reasonable doubt and he was not insane, the jury should find him guilty. Instruction 8 did not expressly identify felony murder or explicitly say that it governed all three counts. Its reach is disputed and must not be converted into a settled trial finding.
- The final instructions told the foreman to sign and date the "verdict(s)" unanimously agreed on. They did not expressly require a verdict on each count or expressly limit the jury to one verdict. Counsel's closing remarks point in different directions: defense counsel spoke of figuring out which of eight or ten verdicts to return; prosecution said jurors would be allowed to return one verdict. Defense counsel also urged consideration of insanity first, after which jurors might stop or go on. These statements are record material; neither counsel's phrasing is the court's legal command.
- Ind. Code section 35-50-2-9(b) required the State to prove at least one of nine statutory aggravators beyond reasonable doubt. The invoked section 35-50-2-9(b)(1) aggravator was intentionally killing during commission or attempt of rape (or another enumerated felony). Section (e) permitted a death recommendation only if the jury found a proven aggravator and that aggravation outweighed mitigation. The trial judge made the final sentence after considering the recommendation and was not bound by it.
- The same jury recommended against death. The judge imposed death, then on Indiana Supreme Court remand made written findings: intentional killing during rape proved beyond reasonable doubt, and no mitigation established. Do not equate the advisory recommendation by itself with a finding that the aggravator failed. Defense counsel at sentencing assumed the guilt verdict probably had resolved the aggravator and confined argument to leniency; this assumption was counsel's, not a special jury finding.
- At trial the defense conceded the killing but sought insanity or guilty-but-mentally-ill treatment. Five experts and lay evidence described mental illness, drug/alcohol dependence, and abnormal behavior. The legal significance of this evidence for knowing or intentional killing remains contested; do not recast the concession of the act as an express concession of every mens rea.
- Indiana decisions: 451 N.E.2d 1047 (1983), 479 N.E.2d 556 (1985), and 533 N.E.2d 1201 (1989). The last rejected an implied acquittal and distinguished Count I's knowing mens rea from the intentional aggravator. Justice DeBruler's dissent argued that the jury's silence had finally rejected the mental state needed for death. Federal habeas denial: 754 F.Supp. 646 (N.D.Ind. 1990); affirmance: 963 F.2d 962 (7th Cir. 1992), which required actual and necessary determination of the issue and rejected an acquittal inference.
- The State did **not** present Teague in either the lower courts or the opposition to certiorari; it invoked Teague in the merits presentation. This is a preservation fact requiring a discrete procedural assessment, not a finding of jurisdictional inability to consider the argument.

## 15. Albright v. Oliver

Official source: 510 U.S. 266, pp. 268-271 and predecision complaint/presentation details reproduced at 276-280, 289, 292-293, 298 n.14, 301 n.17. Full official PDF text read by source control; `chunk2-sources/Albright-510-266.pdf`.

- Review follows Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal, so factual allegations are accepted, not found true after trial. Albright voluntarily surrendered after learning of a warrant, posted bond, and could not leave Illinois without court permission. The later charge was dismissed because it did not allege an offense under Illinois law, not after an acquittal trial.
- The complaint alleged Oliver relied on paid informant Veda Moore's uncorroborated, unreliable account. She first identified an older man, then the possible identity shifted to his sons; the alleged drug was baking powder. The complaint alleged more than 50 false accusations by Moore. Treat these as allegations and distinguish the prior probable-cause ruling that bound Albright over from a finding that Oliver acted lawfully.
- The complaint named Oliver in individual and official capacities, Macomb, and a state common-law malicious-prosecution count. District Court alternative holdings included qualified immunity and insufficient municipal-liability facts; it dismissed the state count without prejudice. Those rulings are outside the limited review question.
- **Preservation precision:** the initial complaint did invoke the Fourth Amendment, but Albright abandoned that claim in District Court and did not reassert it in the Supreme Court. The petition and merits presentation seek a right to be free from prosecution without probable cause under due process; the merits theory pressed is substantive due process, not a challenge to the general adequacy of Illinois procedural safeguards. The petition's question used due process generally, without the word substantive. Therefore, a statement that he never pleaded the Fourth Amendment would be inaccurate; a statement that it is not presented on review is supported.
- The Seventh Circuit, 975 F.2d 343 (1992), required incarceration, employment loss, or another palpable consequence for the asserted constitutional tort. Its suggestion that any Fourth Amendment claim accrued at arrest was dictum; do not treat limitations as a resolved holding against an unpresented federal claim. An interstate-travel argument appeared below but was neither petitioned nor briefed here.
- Under Ill. Rev. Stat., ch.38, sections 111-2(a), 109-3 (1987), felony charges may be filed by information or indictment; information charges cannot be pursued until a preliminary hearing is waived or held and, if held, probable cause found. Albright attacks the reliability and truthfulness of the showing, not the formal availability of this procedure. Illinois supplies a malicious-prosecution tort remedy; its federal significance is contested.

## 16. National Organization for Women, Inc. v. Scheidler

Official source: 510 U.S. 249, pp. 252-255, 256-257 (statutory text and complaint only), 261 (preexisting DOJ guidelines), 262 n.6 (preservation fact). Full official PDF text read; `chunk2-sources/NOW-510-249.pdf`.

- **Only the two clinics**, Delaware Women's Health Organization and Summit Women's Health Organization, brought the RICO claims. NOW did not. Class certification was never granted before dismissal; motions were dismissed as moot. Standing must therefore concern each clinic's allegations, not an assumed certified class or NOW's own RICO claim.
- Complaint paragraphs 97, 104, 106 and 110 alleged threats or force to induce staff and patients to cease work or obtain care elsewhere, injury to the clinics' business/property, and incorporation into the section 1962(c) count. Paragraph 98(g) alleged Scheidler threatened DWHO's administrator with reprisals if she did not quit. Allegations are not findings of extortion or of every RICO element.
- District Court, 765 F.Supp.937 (N.D.Ill.1991), dismissed Sherman Act claims on political/noncommercial grounds; section 1962(a) income claims because voluntary donations were not derived from the alleged racketeering; section 1962(c) for absence of profit-generating purpose; and section 1962(d) derivatively. The Seventh Circuit, 968 F.2d612 (1992), agreed, relying on United States v. Ivic, 700 F.2d51 (2d Cir.1983). The economic-motive question on review concerns sections 1962(c) and (d), not revival of every dismissed count.
- Section 1962(c) applies to a person employed by or associated with an enterprise engaged in, or whose activities affect, interstate or foreign commerce, who conducts or participates directly or indirectly in its affairs through a pattern of racketeering activity or collection of unlawful debt. Section 1961(4) includes legal entities and groups associated in fact although not a legal entity. Section 1961(1) specifies qualifying predicate acts. Section 1951(b)(2) defines extortion as obtaining property from another, with consent induced by wrongful actual/threatened force, violence, fear, or under color of official right. Whether alleged acts satisfy that predicate is distinct from the economic-motive issue.
- The parties seek injunctive relief, treble damages, costs and fees; their availability is not all resolved merely by construing motive. Respondents disputed standing and actual Hobbs Act violation. No constitutional construction argument was made in the Court of Appeals; the First Amendment presentations here principally address the protesters' activity. Those are preservation facts, not a blanket absence of speech protection.
- DOJ's 1981 RICO charging guideline required an economic goal; its March 9, 1984 revision allowed an economic **or other identifiable** goal. Earlier authorities implicated include Turkette, 452 U.S.576 (1981), H.J. Inc., 492 U.S.229 (1989), Sedima, 473 U.S.479 (1985), and Claiborne Hardware, 458 U.S.886 (1982). Postdivergence Supreme Court citations in source reports require checking against the simulation's actual law.

## 17. ABF Freight System, Inc. v. NLRB

Official source: 510 U.S.317, pp.319-322 and statutory text at 324. Full PDF text read; `chunk2-sources/ABF-510-317.pdf`.

- Board decision: 304 N.L.R.B.585 (1991); enforcement: Miera v. NLRB, 982 F.2d441 (10th Cir.1992). Manso was discharged three times. The challenged third discharge followed tardiness on August 11 and 17, 1989. ABF adopted a two-lateness rule after the first occurrence and applied it retroactively only to preferential casual workers. Manso falsely claimed car trouble and repeated the story under oath.
- The ALJ found the lie, accepted much of Manso's other testimony, found the second discharge unlawful, and treated the third as for cause. The Board accepted the lie finding but concluded ABF had not actually discharged him for dishonesty; it found tardiness a pretext for retaliation, relying on previous threats, the earlier discharge, and retroactive rule application. The lie's existence, the actual discharge reason, and the effect of the later false testimony are separate questions.
- The limited petition question is whether an employee forfeits reinstatement with backpay when the ALJ finds purposeful false testimony in the administrative hearing. It does not reopen the finding of antiunion discharge or independently challenge reinstatement merely because the employee lied to the employer. No finding that every discredited employer witness committed criminal perjury is supplied.
- NLRA section 10(c), 29 U.S.C.160(c), authorizes affirmative action including reinstatement with or without backpay to effectuate the Act's policies. It forbids reinstatement or backpay if the worker was suspended or discharged **for cause**. The Board's choice whether hearing dishonesty justifies withholding relief is distinct from that statutory bar. Phelps Dodge, 313 U.S.177,194 (1941), and Virginia Electric, 319 U.S.533,539-540 (1943), concern remedial discretion; Precision Instrument, 324 U.S.806,814 (1945), supplies the competing equitable integrity principle. Permission to withhold relief is not a command to withhold it.

## 18. Department of Revenue of Oregon v. ACF Industries

Official source: 510 U.S.332, pp.335-339 (facts, text, positions) and 348 n.1 (recodification). Full PDF text read; `chunk2-sources/ACF-510-332.pdf`.

- Eight car-leasing companies challenge Oregon's tax under section 11503(b)(4), not (b)(1)-(3). Rail cars are taxable tangible personalty. Oregon exempts agricultural machinery, equipment, nonfarm business inventories, livestock, poultry, bees, fur-bearing animals, and farmers' agricultural products. Standing timber instead bears severance tax; motor vehicles instead bear registration fees. Those substitute burdens should not be erased in comparing treatment.
- District Court considered a stipulated record and found 31.4% exemption of nonrailroad commercial personal property, using a 50% benchmark from other cases. The Ninth Circuit, 961 F.2d813 (1992), concluded that even the calculation most favorable to Oregon exempted 25%, above any de minimis allowance, and awarded total exemption to the Carlines. The 31.4% and 25% are different court calculations, not competing statutory commands. Disputes about the denominator and inclusion of property remain material to a discrimination-on-the-merits path.
- Section 11503(b)(1) addresses assessment ratios; (b)(2) collection on forbidden assessments; (b)(3) ad valorem rates; (b)(4) another tax discriminating against a rail carrier. Section (a)(4) defines commercial and industrial property as property other than transportation property and land used primarily for agriculture or timber, devoted to commercial/industrial use and subject to a property tax levy. Do not merge exclusions from that definition into an express (b)(4) exemption rule: that relationship is the question.
- Original section 306(1)(d), 90 Stat.54, referred to any other tax resulting in discriminatory treatment; 1978 recodification changed wording to another tax that discriminates while expressly disclaiming substantive change (92 Stat.1466).
- Oregon argues (b)(4) reaches only taxes other than property taxes; Carlines argue it is a residual prohibition covering exemption discrimination beyond (b)(1)-(3). Relief sought is declaratory/injunctive protection against assessment and collection. Distinguish threshold cognizability, actual discrimination, and the breadth of remedy. The Solicitor General supports reversal and suggests that real differences, including equivalent alternative tax burdens, can justify disparities.


## 19. Northwest Airlines, Inc. v. County of Kent

Official source: 510 U.S.355, pp.358-363 (record and text), 370 nn.16-17 (lower-court findings), 372 (preservation), and the underlying lower-court facts described below. Full official PDF read; `chunk2-sources/Northwest-510-355.pdf`.

- Seven airlines challenge rates effective after December 31, 1986. Airport ordinance effective April 1, 1988 increased landing and terminal charges, decreased aircraft-parking fees. Buckley cost methodology allocates airfield costs to commercial and general aviation by airfield use, and terminal maintenance by tenant square footage. Concessions bear no airfield-cost allocation and pay market rents (usually 10% gross receipts), rather than allocated-cost rents. Airlines pay 100% of allocated costs; general aviation pays 20% through fuel-flowage/local fees and landing fees for aircraft based elsewhere. General aviation shortfall about $525,000 per year is offset by concession revenues; reserve accumulation exceeds $1 million annually.
- General aviation includes corporate and private aircraft not used for commercial passenger/cargo or military service. No record proof establishes that this diverse group travels chiefly intrastate or seldom outside Michigan. Airlines argue they lacked opportunity because the constitutional claim was disposed of before trial; the AHTA claim did proceed to trial, and their presentation proposed Evansville standards for AHTA reasonableness. Do not infer intrastate travel merely from the label general aviation.
- District Court, 738 F.Supp.1112 (W.D.Mich.1990), found airlines charged break-even costs for their areas, **except aircraft parking**, which it ordered recalculated to break even; Airport did not appeal that order. Sixth Circuit, 955 F.2d1054 (1992), upheld most fees and rejected excessive carrying-charge objection, finding amortization rate should not exceed capital project's initial cost on a net-present-value basis. It reversed the allocation of **100% crash, fire and rescue costs** to airlines and remanded for allocation between airlines and general aviation because both receive that service.
- **Review limits:** Airlines petitioned adverse AHTA and Commerce Clause rulings. They did not seek review of denial of a private AAIA action. Airport did not cross-petition the CFR remand. Airport and United States nonetheless argue no private AHTA action, suggesting initial DOT proceedings instead. Distinguish an alternative reason preserving a judgment from a request that would eliminate the airlines' favorable CFR relief. The source supports no assertion that all lower-court fee rulings were favorable to Airport.
- AHTA, 49 U.S.C.App.1513(a), forbids taxes/fees/head charges directly or indirectly on persons traveling in air commerce, their carriage, air-transportation sales, or gross receipts therefrom. Subsection (b) preserves other taxes (property, net income, franchise, sales/use) and reasonable airport rental, landing, and other service charges on aircraft operators for use of airport facilities. Statutory coverage and the meaning of reasonable are contested; the text does not itself enumerate the Evansville factors.
- Evansville, 405 U.S.707,716-717 (1972), supplies an earlier Commerce Clause user-fee framework: fair approximation of use/privilege for use, no excess relative to governmental benefit, and no discrimination against interstate commerce. Both parties propose it here. AHTA followed Evansville's validation of head taxes. DOT has authority under 49 U.S.C.App.1354(a),1482(a),1486 and 14 C.F.R.pt.13 (1993). No DOT reasoned determination of these fees or generally promulgated reasonableness standard is supplied. Primary jurisdiction was not briefed or argued.
- AAIA section 2210(a)(12) governs airport-revenue use; airlines allege no diversion to nonairport purposes. No general challenge to the amount concessions pay is presented as their own injury under a statute addressed to aircraft-operator fees.
- Participation fact: Blackmun does not participate; no basis is publicly stated. This is a participation fact, not a voting premise for others.


## 20. Hagen v. Utah

Official source: 510 U.S.399, pp.402-408 (procedural and enactment history), 412-419 (statutory text and legislative documents), 420-422 (contemporaneous record), 423-424 (later practice), 425 n.1 (preservation), and 428-442 (historical material reproduced in the separate writing). All 44 PDF pages read; `chunk2-sources/Hagen-510-399.pdf`. These notes extract predecision materials; they state no resolution of the disputed diminishment question.

- The reservation originated in an 1861 Executive Order and the Act of May 5, 1864, ch.77, 13 Stat.63, providing permanent settlement and exclusive Indian occupation. Earlier 1894, 1896 and 1898 legislation pursued allotment/opening through negotiations or Indian consent. Proposals for unilateral opening were not all enacted; proposals and enacted statutes must remain distinct.
- **May 27, 1902, ch.888, 32 Stat.263-264:** with consent of a majority of adult male Uintah and White River Indians, the Secretary was to allot 80 acres of irrigable agricultural land to each head of a family and 40 to each other member before October 1, 1903; the unallotted lands were then to be restored to the public domain. Opening was under homestead laws, with settlers paying $1.25 per acre. Sale proceeds were first to reimburse federal advances for implementing the Act; the remainder benefited the Indians. The separately specified $70,064.48 payment concerned other claims and was conditioned initially on consent; it was not a lump-sum purchase price for all surplus reservation land.
- **June 19, 1902, Joint Resolution31, 32 Stat.744-745:** before opening, the Secretary was to set apart sufficient nonirrigable grazing land as common grazing needed by the Indians. The $70,064.48 could be paid without awaiting consent. This change to the payment condition is distinct from consent to the land-opening program.
- **March 3, 1903, ch.994, 32 Stat.997-998:** the Secretary was to seek consent by June 1, 1903, but could proceed with allotments without consent if it was not obtained. Grazing land was limited to 250,000 acres south of Strawberry River. Opening as provided by the 1902 Act was postponed to October 1, 1904. **April 21, 1904, ch.1402, 33 Stat.207-208:** opening as provided by the 1902 and 1903 Acts was postponed to March 10, 1905; $5,000 was appropriated for surveys carrying out the 1902 purposes.
- **March 3, 1905, ch.1479, 33 Stat.1069-1070:** opening was postponed to September 1, 1905 unless the President set an earlier date. The provision restricting the grazing reservation to the area south of Strawberry River was expressly repealed; this is not an express repeal of every prior land provision. The Act specified disposal under homestead and townsite laws, with presidential proclamation fixing the manner of entry; no other form of entry was allowed during the first 60 days. Land remaining after five years could be disposed of for cash, at not more than 640 acres to one person. Proceeds were applied as provided by the 1902 Act and amendments. The 1905 text did not repeat the public-domain language. Whether this enactment superseded that earlier language or supplied the manner of implementing it is contested, not an agreed record fact.
- The House's 1905 bill H.R.17474 included public-domain language. Representative Howell's unsuccessful amendment would have limited entry for one year to homestead, townsite and mining laws while retaining that language. Senate bills limited entry to homestead and townsite laws but omitted the public-domain wording; the conference adopted the Senate language. Senator Teller discussed restricting speculation. These drafting facts do not themselves settle why the language changed.
- The July 14, 1905 presidential proclamation, 34 Stat.3119-3120, recited the 1902 restoration language and intervening enactments, and opened the unallotted lands on August 28, 1905 in the manner prescribed under the homestead and townsite laws. Other contemporary proclamations concerning forest, reservoir, agricultural and townsite lands referred to the 1905 Act. Distinguish the preamble's statutory recital from the operative entry conditions.
- Indian consent was not achieved: after Inspector McLaughlin's May 18-23, 1903 discussions, 82 of approximately 280 adult male Indians signed. McLaughlin described removing the outside boundary and individual ownership, but also assured continuing agency protection and privileges. Those statements form a contested contemporaneous record, not an enacted congressional declaration. The 1904 postponement request cited incomplete surveys and winter conditions.
- Later congressional terminology alternated between past and present reservation descriptions. State jurisdiction was exercised before the Ute litigation, but was not wholly uncontested. The 1937 federally approved tribal constitution claimed the original reservation territory; the tribal code and a 1972 Roosevelt City agreement also bear on actual jurisdictional practice. Federal services continued in disputed areas. Approximately 85% of residents in the opened area and 93% in Roosevelt were non-Indian in the supplied record. Demography and practice are evidence to evaluate, not subsequent statutory boundary amendments.
- Hagen's 1989 controlled-substance distribution occurred at Myton on land opened in 1905. He pleaded guilty, then sought withdrawal for lack of state jurisdiction. His Indian status was disputed initially but is no longer disputed on review. Utah's appellate court applied Ute Indian Tribe v. Utah, 773 F.2d1087 (10th Cir.1985) (en banc), to find no state jurisdiction; the Utah Supreme Court, 858 P.2d925 (1992), followed State v. Perank, 858 P.2d927 (1992), in reaching the opposite jurisdictional conclusion.
- Hagen did not petition an estoppel question and expressly disavowed seeking it in his December 2, 1992 supplemental certiorari brief. Do not treat it as an ordinary preserved merits component. Indian-country status under 18 U.S.C.1151 and the effect of the opening enactments remain the presented jurisdictional issues. Relevant earlier authorities include Seymour, 368 U.S.351 (1962); Mattz, 412 U.S.481 (1973); DeCoteau, 420 U.S.425 (1975); Rosebud, 430 U.S.584 (1977); Solem, 465 U.S.463 (1984); and Montana v. Blackfeet Tribe, 471 U.S.759 (1985).


## 21. Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation v. Meyer

Official source: 510 U.S.471, pp.473-475 and notes1-3 (record and review limits), 475-476 and note4, 480 (statutory text), and 485 n.10 (earlier contractual claim). All 16 PDF pages read; `chunk2-sources/Meyer-510-471.pdf`. The issues below are kept distinct without stating their resolution on current review.

- On April 13, 1982, the California Savings and Loan Commissioner seized Fidelity and appointed FSLIC receiver under state law; the Federal Home Loan Bank Board appointed FSLIC receiver under federal law that day. Acting through special representative Robert Pattullo, FSLIC followed its general policy of discharging failed thrifts' senior management and terminated Meyer. Former 12 U.S.C.1729(b)(1)(A)(ii) authorized action necessary to put the institution in a sound solvent condition.
- At trial Meyer's sole remaining claim against FSLIC and Pattullo asserted deprivation of a California-law property right to continued employment without Fifth Amendment due process. The jury awarded **$130,000 against FSLIC**, while finding **Pattullo protected by qualified immunity**. The Ninth Circuit, 944 F.2d562 (1991), affirmed both. It treated the constitutional tort as outside FTCA exclusivity, read FSLIC's sue-and-be-sued clause as a waiver permitting the action, and found a due-process violation in discharge without notice or hearing. These are the judgment-below premises to review, not conceded legal conclusions.
- FIRREA abolished FSLIC in 1989; FDIC was substituted as statutory successor. The case concerns the validity of the agency damages award. Meyer's separate challenge to Pattullo's qualified-immunity ruling was denied review, 507 U.S.984 (1993), so the individual judgment is not reopened by the agency's petition.
- Former **12 U.S.C.1725(c)(4)**: FSLIC "shall have power ... To sue and be sued, complain and defend, in any court of competent jurisdiction in the United States." Its 1989 repeal does not erase its relevance to the 1982 conduct. FDIC's own section1819(a)Fourth has a nearly identical clause referring to any state or federal court of law or equity; it should not silently replace the former FSLIC text governing this suit.
- **28 U.S.C.2679(a)**: "The authority of any federal agency to sue and be sued in its own name shall not be construed to authorize suits against such federal agency on claims which are cognizable under section 1346(b) of this title, and the remedies provided by this title in such cases shall be exclusive."
- The pertinent **28 U.S.C.1346(b)** grant is district-court exclusive jurisdiction over civil claims against the United States for money damages for injury or loss of property, personal injury or death caused by a government employee's negligent or wrongful act or omission within the scope of employment, under circumstances in which the United States, if a private person, would be liable under the law of the place where the act or omission occurred. All elements matter; the private-person and law-of-place clauses must not be omitted. Whether the constitutional claim fits this grant determines the section2679(a) issue presented by the parties.
- FDIC argues section2679(a) reaches claims sounding in tort even when they fall outside the private-person/state-law limitation; alternatively it argues the sue-and-be-sued waiver extends only to forms of liability borne by private entities. Meyer contests both and seeks a Bivens-type damages remedy directly against the agency. Waiver of immunity, statutory exclusivity, existence of a cause of action, and the merits of the alleged constitutional violation are distinct questions; resolving one does not mechanically resolve the others.
- **Section2679(b)(1)** concerns exclusivity of actions against an employee and does not use the word cognizable. United States v. Smith, 499 U.S.160 (1991), concerned that subsection and the foreign-country exception; it is not a construction of the section2679(a) language itself. Earlier authorities concerning immunity/cause-of-action distinction include United States v. Mitchell, 463 U.S.206,216-218 (1983). Burr, 309 U.S.242 (1940), Franchise Tax Board, 467 U.S.512 (1984), and Loeffler, 486 U.S.549 (1988), concern sue-and-be-sued clauses in specific settings. Bivens, 403 U.S.388 (1971), Davis v. Passman, 442 U.S.228 (1979), Carlson, 446 U.S.14 (1980), and Schweiker v. Chilicky, 487 U.S.412 (1988), bear on implied constitutional damages remedies. The entering-law slice controls any postdivergence authority.
- Meyer originally asserted and later dropped a contractual claim against FSLIC. Former section1729(d) and 12 C.F.R.569a.6-.7 (1982) provided a receivership-claim process for asserted contractual rights; Coit Independence, 489 U.S.561,580-581 (1989), discusses that process.
