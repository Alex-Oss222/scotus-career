## Lower record and judgment

Bleckley County, created in 1912, covers approximately 219 square miles. The trial used 1980 census figures: population 10,767; approximately 22% Black, 77% white, 1% other; voting-age population approximately 19% Black, 80% white, 1% other. Black and white registration were each approximately 70% of their respective eligible populations. The 1990 census later put total population at 10,430; the appellate opinion notes a slightly increased Black population percentage but supplies no replacement voting-age percentage.

The sole commissioner is the county governing authority under Georgia law, exercising executive and legislative powers. Election is countywide, with a majority requirement since 1964. No Black person had run for commissioner. At trial there was one polling place for the entire county, in the Jaycee Barn in Cochran; it belonged to an all-white civic club. That fact is part of the political setting, not a separately adjudicated challenge to polling-place law. The school board's five single-member districts used different assigned voting machines within the single precinct; one district's electorate was approximately 65% Black, the other districts no more than 12% Black.

The 1985 Georgia local act, 1985 Ga. Laws 4406, authorized five district-elected commissioners **plus a chairman elected at large**. The referendum rejected the proposal, 1,156 to 887. A similar 1982 referendum had approved the school-board district system. Do not describe the local act as simply creating a five-member commission; it authorized six officers, five district seats and a chair. The separate benchmark argument often refers to five district seats. The full local act has not been retrieved; details beyond the reported structure and referendum condition remain unverified.

The plaintiffs also initially challenged school-board and Cochran City Council elections. Those claims were settled before trial. Only the county governing structure was tried. The district court rejected both the statutory dilution and constitutional intentional-discrimination claims. It found a sufficiently numerous and compact Black electorate to constitute a majority in a school-board-like district if the structure changed, but held that cohesion and sufficient white bloc voting had not been proved. It also found no purpose to discriminate in creation or maintenance of the structure, no candidate-slating barrier, no proven official unresponsiveness, and no tenuous policy explanation for the structure. Those findings must be distinguished from its extensive findings of past exclusion and continued socioeconomic disadvantages affecting participation.

The Eleventh Circuit reversed on §2 and remanded **for formulation of a remedy**. It suggested the school-board districts as a possible model, taking account of county-specific concerns and the Act. It did not impose a final number of commissioners or a completed district map. It did not reach the constitutional claim. It applied Rule 52(a)'s clear-error standard to ultimate dilution findings, while correcting legal errors that shaped the fact assessment.

The appellate court's stated reasons were that the district judge wrongly confined the polarization inquiry largely to election statistics and treated its negative Gingles conclusions as a premise for the totality inquiry. The available presidential-primary regression and exit-poll evidence, city elections, community testimony, and socioeconomic history could be considered together insofar as they bore on cohesion and bloc voting. The court did **not** say that the mere existence of other Senate factors substitutes for the predicates: its footnote 19 expressly ties those factors to their bearing on the Gingles inquiries. It found the predicates satisfied, no meaningful rebuttal evidence, and a statutory violation under the totality. It acknowledged an Eleventh Circuit division over whether satisfying Gingles alone establishes liability or instead shifts a burden to defendants; its own decision also considered the totality.

Record qualifications: Willie Basby's electoral success occurred in city elections, not a countywide commissioner contest. The appellate court treated it as exceptional because of crossover support, earlier plurality rules, narrow early wins, and later unopposed campaigns. The Reverend Hall lost a countywide school-board race in 1984 but won in the majority-Black school-board district in 1986. These are evidence bearing on opportunity, not a legal guarantee that a candidate of a particular race must win.

## Operative federal statute

42 U.S.C. §1973, as amended June 29, 1982 and operative for this event:

> (a) No voting qualification or prerequisite to voting or standard, practice, or procedure shall be imposed or applied by any State or political subdivision in a manner which results in a denial or abridgement of the right of any citizen of the United States to vote on account of race or color, or in contravention of the guarantees set forth in section 1973b(f)(2) of this title, as provided in subsection (b) of this section.
>
> (b) A violation of subsection (a) of this section is established if, based on the totality of circumstances, it is shown that the political processes leading to nomination or election in the State or political subdivision are not equally open to participation by members of a class of citizens protected by subsection (a) of this section in that its members have less opportunity than other members of the electorate to participate in the political process and to elect representatives of their choice. The extent to which members of a protected class have been elected to office in the State or political subdivision is one circumstance which may be considered: Provided, That nothing in this section establishes a right to have members of a protected class elected in numbers equal to their proportion in the population.

Official text: [1994 United States Code, title 42, pp. 2604–2605](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1994-title42/pdf/USCODE-1994-title42-chap20-subchapI-A-sec1973.pdf), local `vra_1994.pdf` and `.txt`. The source's amendment history shows no post-1982 amendment to this section; the 1994 compilation is used as a reproduction of the event-effective text, not as later law. Preserve the language-minority cross-reference, the totality condition, the permissive “may,” and the proportional-representation proviso. ## Competing submissions, with attribution and limits

The United States supports the voters' statutory position. It argues that Houston Lawyers rejects an automatic single-member-office exemption; that §§2 and 5 cover changes in electoral structure; and that the single commissioner combines legislative and executive functions. Its proposed comparator is contextual: five district seats are common under Georgia law, the legislature specifically authorized such a structure for this county, and the county already uses five school-board districts. It rejects an entitlement to arbitrarily enlarging a body until any small group can win; it says a tiny group's proposed 25- or 50-member body could be rejected as eccentric or impracticable. It expressly distinguishes ordinary single executive offices such as governor, sheriff, mayor, probate judge, or tax collector, and states that the case does not present enlarging an existing multimember body. These are positions advanced in the brief, not conclusions of this Court.

The United States also reports the officials' opposing arguments, with citations to their merits brief: inability to win is caused by the minority's size rather than race; the Gingles comparator should retain the existing number of offices; otherwise a virtually unlimited expansion could evade the first predicate and create proportional representation; restructuring a county's government intrudes upon state policy; the sole commissioner is economical and efficient; and Gregory v. Ashcroft's clear-statement principle protects the state choice. The government's response invokes the Act's results language, contextually reasonable comparators, the Civil War Amendments enforcement basis, and consideration of genuine state interests under totality and at remedy. The officials' full merits brief is available from IA but was not independently read for this early handoff; this is expressly a source-attributed account of those arguments, not a claim to exhaust them.

The United States' merits brief expressly discloses a change from its petition-stage submission: it had previously accepted Act coverage but opposed dilution cognizability and now supports cognizability. Its prior brief is therefore not interchangeable with the May 1993 merits position. The change is a party filing fact, not Supreme Court decisional history.
