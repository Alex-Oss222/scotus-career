### Two lawful interpretive paths

**Judicial-source ordinary rule with an objective exception.** Adjudication requires forming views from evidence and controlling presentation, so rulings and opinions generated by judicial proceedings ordinarily do not warrant recusal. Their source is an important contextual consideration, not an indispensable element or independently sufficient ground. Judicial conduct can nevertheless require recusal when, objectively understood, it reveals such entrenched favoritism or antagonism that fair adjudication appears impossible. This formulation does not demand confession or proof of subjective bias. Grinnell supports the protection for ordinary judging; §455(a) and Liljeberg prohibit turning that protection into a categorical source exclusion.

The strongest objection is that the exceptional-intensity formulation can replace reasonable appearance with something approaching actual inability to adjudicate. Its supporters retain it only with the express objective qualification, cumulative assessment, and genuine availability of recusal for courtroom-origin conduct. They cannot join the lower courts' unqualified restriction.

**Ordinary appearance inquiry without a mandatory heightened element.** Apply §455(a)'s reasonable, informed observer standard to the full context regardless of where the evidence originated. Legitimate judging explains why ordinary rulings, familiarity, impatience, or correction of counsel will usually be insufficient; it does not add an extreme-hostility prerequisite to the statutory command. Liljeberg's distinction between the general appearance clause and particularized §455(b) duties supports preserving the enacted test independently.

The strongest objection is that unrestricted appearance language may allow dissatisfaction with ordinary trial control to masquerade as a disqualification ground. This path therefore requires an objectively reasonable inference from identifiable facts, with full allowance for the judge's lawful functions. A party's label, offense, or disagreement is not enough. It does not presume bias from an erroneous ruling.
