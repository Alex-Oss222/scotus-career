## 80. Dolan — exact conditions and their relationship to the proposal

### Record and posture

The owner proposed replacing and enlarging an approximately 9,700-square-foot plumbing/electrical supply store on a 1.67-acre parcel in Tigard's central business district with a 17,600-square-foot building, and paving a 39-space parking lot. The plan contemplated another complementary business without specifying it. Fanno Creek runs through the parcel; part lies within the 100-year floodplain. A separate, unchallenged zoning rule limits development coverage to 85 percent and requires 15 percent open space. Do not treat this challenge as one to that general coverage rule.

The permit demanded dedication of the land in the floodplain below elevation 150.0 for a public greenway and a further 15-foot strip adjoining it for a pedestrian/bicycle route. Together the demanded areas were approximately 7,000 square feet, about 10 percent of the parcel. Dedication counted toward the 15 percent open-space requirement. The city's council required the city to maintain the landscaping buffer and survey/mark the floodplain. The property subject to the dedication otherwise remained the owner's property: no completed conveyance is established.

The land-use findings connected added impervious surface to runoff into a constrained drainage system. The traffic estimate was about 435 additional vehicle trips daily, using a rate of 53.21 trips per 1,000 square feet. The city described a connected path as capable of diverting customers/employees to bicycle or pedestrian trips and found it "could offset some" traffic demand. A bicycle rack already existed. A citywide drainage plan also contemplated channel improvements and allocation of costs to benefited properties. These findings supply possible factual connections; their legal sufficiency and the required degree of justification remain contested. They do not establish a measured equivalence between each impact and each demanded property interest.

The Land Use Board of Appeals upheld the conditions and denial of a variance, 22 Or. LUBA 617 (January 7, 1992); the Oregon Court of Appeals affirmed, 113 Or. App. 162, 832 P.2d 853 (1992); the state supreme court affirmed July 1, 1993. The federal challenge concerns constitutional sufficiency, with substantial-evidence support for the findings assumed below. No Oregon constitutional claim was pursued in the state supreme court. The owner did not identify an alternative mitigation proposal in the variance request. The state court did not decide the constitutional consequence of the variance procedure itself.

### Operative code and limits

CDC §18.120.180.A.8 provides:

> Where landfill and/or development is allowed within and adjacent to the 100-year floodplain, the City shall require the dedication of sufficient open land area for greenway adjoining and within the floodplain. This area shall include portions at a suitable elevation for the construction of a pedestrian/bicycle pathway within the floodplain in accordance with the adopted pedestrian/bicycle plan.

CDC §18.86.040.A.1.b requires facilitation of pedestrian/bicycle circulation when a site is on a designated bikepath street or adjacent to designated greenway, open space or park land; the development must provide connecting links through dedication and construction. When direct connections are impossible, construction funds are required. That is a source of the city demand, not proof that every condition imposed under it is constitutional.

The variance standards require peculiar circumstances involving lot or topography; no self-imposed hardship; no material detriment or plan conflict; permitted uses and compliance with city standards as far as possible while allowing economic use; no more adverse effect on affected systems; and the minimum variance necessary. The undisputed 15-percent open-space rule, the floodplain's development limits, public access, and ownership of the additional path strip are distinct interests. A requirement to leave land undeveloped for drainage does not itself explain why the public must be allowed onto it. Conversely, the presence of a public-access interest does not erase possible flood-control or traffic mitigation functions.

### Live arguments and remedies

One path applies Nollan's unconstitutional-conditions principle beyond a bare purpose connection: when this permit requires conveyance of identified property interests, the government must justify their character and extent by the proposed development's effects. The owner can accept flood prevention and traffic reduction as legitimate purposes while disputing public access, the additional strip or the magnitude of the dedication. The strongest counterargument is that the city made site-specific findings under an adopted comprehensive scheme and that exact quantification is difficult, especially for cumulative drainage and traffic impacts.

The competing path regards the demonstrated reasonable relationship, comprehensive planning context, credit against the open-space obligation and remaining substantial use as sufficient, leaving the challenger to establish arbitrariness or disproportion. Its strongest difficulty is preserving Nollan's protection against obtaining a public easement through permit leverage when ordinary acquisition would require compensation. A narrower result could distinguish the floodplain condition from the bicycle/public-access interest, or require more particular findings without deciding that no dedication could be justified.

The challenged action is an individualized permit decision demanding conveyance, although ordinances and plans provide its source. Do not silently extend a rule about this action to generally applicable zoning, development fees, all legislatively imposed obligations or all compelled mitigation. If a condition fails the appropriate legal test, return to the state proceedings for application and lawful permit treatment is an available remedy; this record does not itself establish a completed taking requiring a specified money award. If the lower standard and application are sufficient, affirmance leaves the conditions and variance disposition in place.

The entering-law Lucas rule concerns complete economic deprivation and background property/nuisance law; it is not a finding of complete deprivation here or a ready-made measure of a dedication. Its recorded categorical holding is Scalia's for Stone, White, O'Connor and Thomas. Alaska's unanimous statutory approval of a condition preserving existing submerged-land boundaries excludes compulsory surrender of existing territory and supplies no universal exaction rule. The Nollan/Penn Central authorities discussed in the lower judgment remain necessary context. ## 81. Honda — review of the amount, distinct from punitive entitlement

### Record and posture

Oberg was severely injured when his 1985 Honda ATC350X three-wheeled vehicle overturned backwards while climbing a steep embankment. He sued the manufacturer and seller on negligence and strict-product-liability theories. The jury assessed $919,390.39 compensatory damages and $5 million punitive damages, finding 20 percent comparative fault. Compensatory damages were reduced to $735,512.31; the resulting total was $5,735,512.31. The Oregon appellate courts sustained the judgment. The present federal question concerns the availability of judicial review of punitive amount; it is not a request for this Court to select a constitutionally permissible dollar figure or ratio. Evidentiary and late-witness disputes addressed below are not the question presented here.

The Oregon constitutional rule, Article VII (Amended), §3, as quoted in the judgment below, states:

> In actions at law, where the value in controversy shall exceed $200, the right of trial by jury shall be preserved, and no fact tried by a jury shall be otherwise re-examined in any court of this state, unless the court can affirmatively say there is no evidence to support the verdict.

The $200 amount is the text quoted by the state court for this action; quotations of the historical provision's older $20 amount should not be mistaken for a material issue in the case. Under Van Lom v. Schneiderman, 187 Or. 89, 210 P.2d 461 (1949), the state courts cannot reduce an award merely because it is excessive. Oregon preserves correction for legal error, including erroneous instructions, and review where evidence cannot support punitive liability. Whether those powers amount to adequate review of the amount is the dispute. A hypothesized power to establish a lawful upper bound before submission is not established by this record as a regularly available post-verdict reasonableness review.

### Statute, instructions and safeguards actually used

ORS §30.925(1) requires clear and convincing evidence of wanton disregard for the health, safety and welfare of others for punitive damages in a product-liability civil action. Subsection (2) admits wealth evidence only after a prima facie showing of entitlement. Subsection (3) provides seven criteria: likelihood of serious harm from the misconduct; defendant's awareness of that likelihood; profitability; duration and concealment; attitude and conduct upon discovery; financial condition; and the total deterrent effect of other punishment, including punitive awards to similarly situated persons and criminal penalties imposed or potentially applicable. ORS §41.315 separately addresses the clear-and-convincing burden unless another law specifically provides otherwise, and the prima facie wealth-evidence limitation.

The jury actually received five of the statutory criteria: likelihood, awareness, duration, attitude and financial condition. Honda objected to the profitability instruction as phrased because it assumed misconduct; it waived the other-punishment instruction on the ground that no previous punishment existed. Do not say all seven criteria were instructed. The court told the jury it could award punitive damages in its discretion, not that it was obliged to do so, and imposed the $5 million ceiling pleaded in the complaint. That pleading amount is not a generally applicable statutory punitive maximum.

### Live arguments and remedies

Honda's procedural claim is that instructions, a higher proof burden and review of entitlement do not supply an independent safeguard against an arbitrary punitive amount. Whether evidence supports punitive punishment at all does not establish the reasonableness of every amount the jury might choose. Traditional judicial control of verdicts can support a demand for some meaningful review without constitutionalizing a particular remittitur system, a detailed written opinion or a numerical ratio.

Oberg's opposing position is that due process evaluates the proceeding as a whole: Oregon's proof threshold, constrained wealth evidence, statutory criteria, instructions, pleading ceiling and legal-error/no-evidence review sufficiently confine discretion. Historical procedural forms are not all irrevocably required; a new arrangement can provide equivalent protection. The strongest difficulty for this path is the lack of an established substitute examining the amount after a lawful finding of liability. The strongest difficulty for Honda is explaining why the existing protections are inadequate without transforming every feature of another State's review into a federal mandate.

If amount-review is constitutionally required and absent, an available remedy is further proceedings permitting adequate review of this award. That does not itself set it aside as excessive, calculate a new award, undo compensatory liability or eliminate punitive entitlement. If the full Oregon process suffices, the reviewed judgment stands. The distinct substantive due-process question must not be answered by a rule about procedure.

### Earlier authority and source map

TXO's effective current rule is unusually important. Its five-Justice procedural proposition approves the actual combination of jury process, trial-judge hearing/review and state supreme court review on that record; a missing separate written trial explanation was not independently fatal. The proposition has support from Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. There is no majority ratio, potential-harm formula or substantive-excessiveness framework. Stevens/Blackmun's potential-harm reasoning, Kennedy's rational-punishment approach, and Scalia/Thomas's rejection of substantive numerical review are distinct prior positions. Preserve their differences rather than manufacture a common substantive denominator. Ginsburg did not participate in TXO; no stance is attributed to her from it. The entering-law entry preserves Haslip's existing force and rejects any mandatory reproduction of every Alabama feature. The raw Haslip PDF has been retrieved but is not represented here as a new full independent review or a source of additional unverified joins.

## 82. Heck — damages, invalidity, custody and available review

### Record and posture

Roy Heck is serving a 15-year Indiana sentence for voluntary manslaughter arising from his wife's death. His conviction was affirmed at 552 N.E.2d 446 (Ind. 1990). His §1983 complaint names prosecutors James Humphrey and Robert Ewbank and state investigator Michael Krinoph. It alleges unlawful/arbitrary investigation, intentional destruction of exculpatory evidence and an illegal voice-identification procedure used at trial. These are allegations, not findings that the officials committed them. He demands compensatory and punitive damages, not release or an injunction.

The district court dismissed without prejudice. The Seventh Circuit treated claims whose success would oblige release as habeas claims regardless of the relief requested, required exhaustion, and preferred dismissal to a stay. Its opinion discusses Indiana's two-year limitations period, absence of an imprisonment toll and possible equitable protection for a diligent litigant. Those are the lower court's proposed reconciliation, not already adopted Supreme Court law.

One federal habeas petition had been dismissed for lack of exhaustion; another was denied, with affirmance. Exhausting or losing review does not establish that a conviction was invalidated. Heck remained in custody; an expired sentence, a fine-only conviction and absence of every meaningful review opportunity are not this record. The lower courts characterized the presented action as attacking conviction validity. His principal Supreme Court submissions did not contest that characterization, while his reply sought to distinguish it; the permissible scope of that late contention must be assessed, not silently converted into a finding about each count.

### Operative statutory boundaries

Section 1983 creates a civil remedy against a person acting under color of state law who deprives another of federal rights; it contains no general state-remedy exhaustion requirement. The then-operative §2254(a) concerns an applicant in custody pursuant to a state judgment and a claim of custody in violation of federal law. Section 2254(b)'s exhaustion command allows an application when the State has no available corrective process or circumstances render that process ineffective to protect the prisoner's rights. Preserve those exceptions; do not use later AEDPA text or equate custody, exhaustion, success and accrual.

Preiser's habeas channel for relief determining immediate/speedier release, Wolff's treatment of permissible civil claims, Patsy's no-general-exhaustion rule and ordinary tort remedial principles all bear on the statutory relationship. Section 1738 and applicable state preclusion rules are a distinct question from whether a §1983 cause of action presently exists. Prosecutorial immunity or the merits of any actual constitutional deprivation are not resolved merely by choosing a remedial channel.

### Live arguments and remedies

The damages path starts from the relief actually sought and §1983's text: habeas cannot award the requested damages, and a claim for damages is not literally a request to release a prisoner. A rule excluding damages whenever the underlying wrong bears on a conviction risks importing an exhaustion requirement rejected for ordinary §1983 actions and denying relief to persons who cannot invoke custody-based habeas. Its strongest opposing consideration is that a civil damages judgment necessarily establishing invalid conviction can become a collateral attack bypassing the safeguards attached to review of that conviction.

The competing path harmonizes the statutes or uses the favorable-termination feature of the closest common-law action to prevent a presently valid conviction from being contradicted through damages litigation. It must specify the trigger: success necessarily demonstrating invalidity is different from a constitutional violation whose success can coexist with a valid conviction. It must distinguish a presently unavailable claim from a valid accrued claim subject merely to an exhaustion stay. Analogies to malicious prosecution cannot silently add malice or absence of probable cause to every constitutional damages claim.

A narrower disposition can address only this prisoner and these claims, or separate claims whose success necessarily impugns the conviction from claims that do not. An unreasonable-search claim, for example, may coexist with conviction because of independent source, inevitable discovery or harmless error; that possibility does not establish compensable wrongful-imprisonment injury or prove these particular allegations fall outside invalidity. Injury from a procedural wrong and injury from conviction/imprisonment must remain distinct. If a favorable-termination condition is adopted, specify which authorized reversal, expungement, invalidating tribunal action or habeas relief counts, and whether noncustodial/unavailable-review cases are decided or reserved. Do not confuse failure to obtain such relief with failure to exhaust.

Possible remedies include maintaining a without-prejudice dismissal on a properly identified ground; allowing an independently cognizable damages component to proceed; or remanding to assess the relationship of particular allegations to validity. If accrual awaits a qualifying invalidation, that differs from accrual now plus equitable tolling. If a stay is required to preserve an accrued civil claim, explain its source and conditions. No path supplies an automatic damages award or an order releasing Heck.

### Earlier current law and Justice-specific source positions

The Cavanaugh Public Projection, November 30, 1993, is directly relevant: §1983 permits prospective relief restoring mandatory discretionary-parole consideration when the judgment neither orders release nor establishes an entitlement to release or shorter custody; no general exhaustion attaches after establishing the available channel. O'Connor wrote that rule with Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy and Souter among the joins. Scalia and Thomas opposed that channel; Ginsburg joined their channel objection and supplied a separate explanation. This is concrete earlier position evidence, not a preassigned view about Heck. A damages judgment necessarily invalidating a conviction presents a different issue; preserving Cavanaugh requires identifying that difference.

Albright's earlier Public Projection supplies no majority rationale establishing a freestanding federal malicious-prosecution tort. Custis concerns collateral attacks at an ACCA sentencing, not a general §1983 prohibition: O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Ginsburg joined the channel limitation, while Souter, Blackmun and Stevens supported allowing the constitutional attacks there. Its express reservation for materially different unavailable-review settings must not be converted into a holding that the absence of review is irrelevant. Use the linked Public Projections for the exact writing scopes and doctrinal limits.

## 83. Davis — valid initial waiver and the actual clarifying exchange

### Record and preservation

Davis was convicted of unpremeditated murder under Article 118, 10 U.S.C. §918, by a general court-martial; the sentence included life confinement, dishonorable discharge, forfeiture of all pay/allowances and reduction to E-1. The Navy-Marine Corps Court of Military Review affirmed in an unpublished decision September 16, 1991. The Court of Military Appeals affirmed March 11, 1993. Its earlier-interview/physical-evidence discussion concerns a separate October 20 interrogation issue; it should not be folded into the November 4 invocation question presented here.

On November 4, 1988, NIS agents advised Davis orally and in writing of his Article 31 rights and right to counsel, and obtained a written waiver. The waiver's validity is not the dispute. Questioning began about 4:30 p.m. About 5:53 p.m., he said: "Maybe I should talk to a lawyer."

The credited agent account describes an actual interruption of offense questioning to clarify that remark:

> [We] made it very clear that we're not here to violate his rights, that if he wants a lawyer, then we will stop any kind of questioning with him, that we weren't going to pursue the matter unless we have it clarified is he asking for a lawyer or is he just making a comment about a lawyer, and he said, "No, I'm not asking for a lawyer," ... "No, I don't want a lawyer."

Questioning then resumed. After a break agents briefly reminded Davis of his rights; they did not repeat all warnings or obtain another written waiver. About 6:57 p.m. he said: "I think I want a lawyer before I say anything else." The interview then stopped. Davis supplied a different account at the suppression hearing, but the military judge credited the agents. Do not erase those findings, convert the later request into the earlier one, or write that the agents simply ignored the first remark.

The Court of Military Appeals used a limited-clarification approach: on an ambiguous counsel reference, stop substantive interrogation and clarify whether counsel is desired; clarification cannot be used to persuade the suspect that counsel is unnecessary or to predict counsel's advice. It found the actual questions permissible. Thus affirming the military judgment does not logically require rejecting a clarification duty.

### Live legal paths and disposition limits

Edwards requires cessation after invocation, subject to counsel's presence or the suspect's own initiation followed by a valid waiver; it does not erase the antecedent question whether counsel was invoked. Three distinct approaches are presented: treat the equivocal statement as sufficient to stop interrogation altogether; require limited, neutral clarification before returning to substantive questioning; or require a sufficiently clear request and permit, without obliging, clarification. The initial valid waiver, the accepted ambiguous words and the actual clarification must be applied under whichever rule is chosen.

The demand for a definite request relies on an administrable objective trigger and the distinction between invoking a right and officers' speculation about a suspect's possible wish. Its strongest counterargument is that ordinary speech under custodial pressure can express a request tentatively and that a safeguard meant for laypeople should not require legal precision. A mandatory clarification rule responds to that concern while permitting genuine clarification; its competing difficulty is identifying a workable trigger that does not transform every passing reference into a new automatic bar. Treating any ambiguity as a complete invocation supplies a stronger cessation rule but must confront the valid waiver and the credited clarification rather than presuming facts not found below.

A judgment sustaining admission could rest on the actual compliance with limited clarification, or on the absence of an unequivocal invocation; the grounds are not interchangeable. A rule finding the first remark sufficient would require an exclusion/remedial analysis and review of the military judgment, not an immediate declaration of innocence. Do not decide an unpresented Sixth Amendment attachment issue. The Fifth Amendment's direct operation in the military setting need not be newly decided: the military rules and the courts below applied the Miranda/Edwards framework and the parties did not dispute that framework here. The Government did not invoke 18 U.S.C. §3501 below or rely on it as its merits ground; amici's suggestion is not the Government's preserved submission.

### Independently verified earlier writings and current law

[McNeil v. Wisconsin, 501 U.S. 171 (June 13, 1991)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-501/pdf/USREPORTS-501-171.pdf), read in full, supplies important but bounded evidence. Scalia wrote, with O'Connor, Kennedy and Souter among the full joins, that a request for counsel at an offense-specific judicial proceeding is not itself an invocation of the Miranda interest in counsel during custodial questioning; the latter requires an actual expression reasonably understood to seek that assistance. Kennedy's concurrence separately urged alignment of the Fifth and Sixth Amendment rules on offense specificity. Stevens's dissent, joined by Blackmun, emphasized ordinary understanding, broad construction of counsel requests and the risks of custodial pressure. McNeil did not decide the exact ambiguous-in-interrogation exchange here. Thomas and Ginsburg were not members of that Court; no join is assigned to them.

The current Withrow entry gives Souter's distinction between Miranda and independent involuntariness; Blackmun, Stevens and Kennedy joined his Miranda-review holding, while all then-sitting Justices joined the fair-litigation rule for an unpleaded independent coercion claim. Stansbury's unanimous April 26, 1994 Public Projection makes custody objective and rejects uncommunicated police assumptions; it does not decide the meaning of this suspect's words or establish a clarification rule. Those differences constrain analogies rather than preassign a result.

## 84. Thomas Jefferson University — regulation, allocation and independent grounds

### Record, proceedings and scope of the reimbursement dispute

The university operates a 700-bed teaching hospital and an affiliated medical college. The hospital has been a Medicare provider since 1966. The approved graduate medical education (GME) program trains interns and residents at the hospital through college faculty. No reimbursement was claimed in 1966–1973; the record supplies no explanation for that omission. From 1974–1983 it claimed hospital-paid faculty services, resident/intern salaries and internally transferred payments for faculty time, with the medical school bearing other costs. Some clerical costs were claimed and allowed in 1984; the intermediary later described that allowance as an error but was outside the reopening period.

For the reporting year ending June 30, 1985, a cost study supported $6,614,724 in GME costs and $2,191,481 in physician administrative costs, totaling $8,806,205. The intermediary allowed $5,944,958 and the disputed difference was $2,861,247: $2,431,244 associated with GME and $430,003 administrative costs. The primary dispute now concerns nonsalary costs of the program, including clerical/support and space expenses and general/indirect administration. This must be kept separate from the faculty-compensation issue settled below: the Secretary stipulated to reimburse particular direct faculty salary/fringe and physician-administrative amounts that the Administrator had not actually disallowed, and the district court did not adjudicate the hospital's alternative enforcement claim for those amounts.

The PRRB, decision 90-D05 on November 17, 1989, accepted the study as a refinement of cost finding and allowed its documented costs. The HCFA Administrator modified that decision January 18, 1990. The Administrator relied on redistribution of previously medical-school-borne costs and community support, and also stated that the claimed university general/administrative costs failed separate allowability limits. These are the agency's actual grounds; do not collapse them into one slogan or create a new ground on review.

The Eastern District of Pennsylvania entered summary judgment for the Secretary in a memorandum and order dated May 1, 1992, entered May 4, Civil Action 90-2036. It accepted the Secretary's definitions and found substantial evidence for prior support by the school/community; its four eligibility conditions and factual conclusions are lower-court rulings under review, not quoted statutory commands. The Third Circuit affirmed without opinion by judgment order April 21, 1993, No. 92-1513. The IA certiorari petition reproduces both courts and the final agency decision, resolving the previously missing lower-text source.

### Text and qualifications

The relevant statutory authorization, 42 U.S.C. §1395x(v)(1)(A), defines reasonable cost as actual incurred cost excluding unnecessary cost in efficient delivery and delegates methods and included items to the Secretary. It directs attention to direct and indirect costs, forbids shifting Medicare costs to non-Medicare patients and vice versa, and provides corrective adjustments where the prescribed methods produce inadequate or excessive aggregate reimbursement. These provisions operate within the authorized-method system described in Good Samaritan; they do not by themselves command reimbursement of every asserted actual expense. Judicial review is under §1395oo(f)(1) and the APA, including §706(2)(A).

Section 413.85 was formerly §405.421; the disputed language existed through the applicable cost period. The parties and reviewed decisions cite the recodified provision. Section 413.85(c) reads:

> Educational activities. Many providers engage in educational activities including training programs for nurses, medical students, interns and residents, and various paramedical specialties. These programs contribute to the quality of patient care within an institution and are necessary to meet the community's needs for medical and paramedical personnel. It is recognized that the costs of such educational activities should be borne by the community. However, many communities have not assumed responsibility for financing these programs and it is necessary that support be provided by those purchasing health care. Until communities undertake to bear these costs, the program will participate appropriately in the support of these activities. Although the intent of the program is to share in the support of educational activities customarily or traditionally carried on by providers in conjunction with their operations, it is not intended that this program should participate in increased costs resulting from redistribution of costs from educational institutions or units to patient care institutions or units.

The quoted general payment rule in §413.85(a)(1) says, subject to paragraph (a)(2), that allowable cost **may include** net cost of approved activities under (g), apportioned for Medicare utilization under §413.50. Section (g) deducts tuition revenue and includes trainee stipends, teacher compensation and other direct/indirect activity costs under Medicare cost-finding principles. The exception in the recodified provision must remain explicit: “For cost reporting periods beginning on or after July 1, 1985, payment to hospitals and hospital-based providers for approved residency programs in medicine, osteopathy, dentistry, and podiatry is determined as provided in §413.86.” The September 29, 1989 final rule, 54 Fed. Reg. 40315, supplies that complete (a)(2) text; it replaced the earlier temporary cost limit rather than preserving that limit as the 1993 exception. This reporting year ended June 30, 1985, so the later-period payment-method exception does not itself deny this claim. The later GME reimbursement system makes eligible base-period costs consequential but does not change the reporting year being reviewed here.

Section 413.17(a)'s related-organization rule reads, in pertinent complete paragraph:

> Except as provided in paragraph (d) of this section, costs applicable to services, facilities, and supplies furnished to the provider by organizations related to the provider by common ownership or control are includable in the allowable cost of the provider at the cost to the related organization. However, such cost must not exceed the price of comparable services, facilities, or supplies that could be purchased elsewhere.

The record does not invoke paragraph (d)'s separate-organization exception as the basis for payment. Its complete then-operative text was not independently verified in this preflight; do not decide that exception or describe the above general rule as exceptionless. The dispute is about which affiliated costs are allowable at all, rather than permission to include a profit markup.

Intermediary Letter 78-7, February 1978, says costs for faculty services may be allowable if they would be allowable when incurred directly by the hospital, are allocated to all recipient components on the same basis, remain within comparable-purchase limits and comply with the related-organization rules. It recognizes certain necessary directly related library, physician-office and clerical expenses, but forbids duplicate additional costs where the hospital has its own corresponding facilities/support. Other medical-school costs require documentation, relation to hospital patient care, reasonable allocation and reasonable amount. It limits the identified nonfaculty additions to specified direct and space costs. Thus it is neither a blanket guarantee of all overhead nor a text expressly discussing redistribution. The hospital relies on it and on a 1982 agency response; the Secretary answers that silence about redistribution did not repeal the regulation, as clarified by later guidance. The existence and legal significance of inconsistency remain questions, not a source-established finding that the agency reversed a settled twenty-year policy.

### Live competing readings and possible remedies

The Secretary reads the regulation as maintaining the previous division of costs: activities can qualify as patient-care education yet particular cost categories previously borne by the medical school cannot be transferred to Medicare. Community support includes funding other than Medicare, including university sources, tuition, gifts and public appropriations. Prior failure to claim costs is used as evidence of that support. These are the Secretary's interpretation and factual reasoning; a statute does not conclusively deem every previously unclaimed dollar community-financed.

The hospital reads (c) as general policy about types of educational activity and responsibility, not an immutable institution-by-institution cost-history ceiling. It argues that otherwise reimbursable clinical training expense was newly measured rather than newly shifted, that the medical school and hospital are related, and that (a), (g) and §413.17 expressly accommodate direct/indirect allowable costs. Its strongest textual challenge is to deriving a categorical forfeiture from the policy-oriented wording when the operative net-cost provisions point toward reimbursement. Its strongest difficulty is giving independent effect to the prohibition on increased cost resulting from redistribution and distinguishing legitimate refinement from moving another institution's historic burden.

The Secretary's strongest path is a reasonable reading of the whole regulation within delegated technical cost rules, reconciled with the related-organization provision and agency guidance. Its strongest difficulty is proving that the particular interpretation fits the text and regulatory history rather than obtaining deference from complexity alone. The Sixth Circuit's contrary judgment in Ohio State University v. Secretary, 996 F.2d 122 (June 8, 1993), supplies an actual predecision alternative: it sustained reimbursement of clinical GME costs under its reading of activities and rejected the asserted redistribution bar. That judgment must be addressed, not treated as this Court's law.

A ruling sustaining one legally sufficient actual agency ground can leave the denial intact while reserving other grounds. A ruling rejecting only redistribution must say what happens to the community-support and cost-allowability grounds; it does not automatically entitle the provider to every claimed amount. A remand for lawful interpretation, reasoned application or remaining cost findings is distinct from an order compelling a specific reimbursement. A theory based on failure of the agency's stated grounds cannot be repaired by a court-created rationale. The settled faculty-compensation issue should not be reopened.
