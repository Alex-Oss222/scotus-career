#### C. ENTERING LAW BY ISSUE

##### Issue 1: Contribution following settlement

**Entering Law:** Reliable Transfer, 421 U.S. 397 (1975), establishes comparative fault; Edmonds, 443 U.S. 256 (1979), does not itself settle the effect of settlements. Great Lakes Dredge & Dock Co. v. Miller, 957 F.2d 1575 (11th Cir. 1992), supplies the circuit’s entering contribution approach. A pro tanto rule can leave a nonsettler paying more than its equitable share, generating a contribution question. A proportionate-share rule protects that nonsettler through claim reduction and ordinarily removes the need for contribution against the settler. Which rule is governing at this event depends on the earlier simulated McDermott decision. [P18](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/us-supreme-court/511/222.html) [P18.1](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F2/990/990.F2d.606.92-2391.html)

#### D. STONE’S REVISED REASONING, APPLICATION, AND LIMITS

**Stone’s Position and Scope:** retain vacatur and remand as the companion to Stone's proposed proportionate-share rule, with the dependency explicit.

A nonsettler whose liability is already reduced by a settler's equitable share generally has no excess share to recover through contribution. That consequence makes the settlement-credit framework coherent; it is not freestanding immunity awarded merely because a defendant settled. [Record, 511 U.S. 222](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/222/).

This is **application of the rule Stone recommends in McDermott**, not presently application of an adopted same-day simulated precedent. The review can assess the two recommendations together without waiting for a vote. An actual disposition later entered under a different credit rule would change the analysis: pro tanto credit can leave a nonsettler bearing more than its share, making contribution a genuine problem rather than duplication.

Stone's remedial discipline requires preserving that coupling. Lower courts must reduce the plaintiff's recoverable claim by the settling actor's adjudicated responsibility and allow meaningful litigation of that responsibility. They cannot both withhold that protection and dismiss contribution by invoking settlement finality. The missing percentages in this record are reasons to remand, not opportunities to invent an allocation.

The strongest objection is that contribution protects against cheap, strategic settlements. The proportionate reduction places that settlement risk on the plaintiff, supplying the nonsettler's protection without reopening the bargain. A distinct contractual indemnity promise presents a different obligation and survives for separate source analysis. Preserve limitation-of-liability, exoneration, damages, and settlement-validity questions not resolved here. Stone's position is sound only if its operative instructions implement the same allocation method on both sides of the case.

#### B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

**Review Posture and Requested Relief:** Eighth Circuit, 981 F.2d 991 (1992), affirmed summary judgment for the estate in a refund action. It treated the Treasury regulation as inapplicable, state law as controlling, and taxation as impermissibly retroactive. The United States seeks reversal of the refund judgment. **Stone’s Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Reverse the Eighth Circuit’s judgment.

**Stone’s Remedy and Remand Position:** Reject the asserted basis for the refund and return the case for implementation consistent with taxation of the 1979 transfer. No reassessment of unrelated estate items.

#### C. ENTERING LAW BY ISSUE

##### Issue 1: Federal gift transfer and state disclaimer fiction

**Entering Law:** 26 U.S.C. §§2501(a)(1) and 2511(a) broadly reach gratuitous property transfers. Treasury Regulation §25.2511-1(c)(2), in the applicable version, affords limited disclaimer treatment requiring local-law effectiveness and action within a reasonable time after knowledge of the transfer. Jewett v. Commissioner, 455 U.S. 305 (1982), measures that time from knowledge of creation of the interest, not later possession. State-law relation back does not itself control federal gift taxation. Revenue Act of 1932 §501(b) protects transfers made on or before enactment; it does not automatically exempt every later disposition of an earlier-created interest. [P19](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/224/)

#### D. STONE’S REVISED REASONING, APPLICATION, AND LIMITS

**Stone’s Position and Scope:** retain reversal of the refund judgment.

State law determines the property interest and effective disclaimer; federal law determines whether the 1979 disposition is a taxable gift. The trust's 1917 origin does not make every later gratuitous transfer pre-1932. *Jewett*, 455 U.S. 305, also makes first knowledge, rather than eventual possession, central to the applicable reasonable-time disclaimer inquiry. [Dates and tax provisions, 511 U.S. 224](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/224/).

This fits Stone's source separation and protection of concrete reliance: the correct reliance question concerns the legal consequences of the later act, not whether an old asset can never be subject to later tax law. It is **application of existing federal tax principles**, not an exception to his presumption against retroactive liability.

The proposed alternative reasoning is lawful if each route is independently sufficient. If the regulation applies, decades after actual knowledge is not a reasonable-time disclaimer on these facts. If the regulation does not reach the pre-Act interest, the estate still needs a basis for removing the later transfer from §§2501 and 2511; state relation back alone is not that basis. Explain why the broad statutory transfer language reaches the value directed to the children, rather than treating regulatory inapplicability itself as proof of taxation.

The counterargument is that the state disclaimer means Irvine never owned the transferred interest. Federal taxation need not adopt that relation-back characterization of a real disposition. A prompt refusal before acceptance presents a different case; Stone should not announce an arbitrary deadline or tax every disclaimer categorically. Reverse the asserted refund ground while leaving Minnesota property law intact. Use the applicable historical regulation, not later qualified-disclaimer provisions.

#### E. RECORD AND SOURCE SUPPORT

**Independent Record and Threshold Context:** A grandfather created the trust in 1917. Irvine knew of her contingent interest at least when she reached age twenty-one in 1931. In 1979, following termination of the trust, she disclaimed five-sixteenths of her interest, causing each of her five children to receive one-sixteenth of her distributed share. Minnesota law recognized the disclaimer. Federal tax and interest were paid, and her estate brought this refund action. No factual basis makes the forty-seven-year-plus delay a prompt disclaimer following first knowledge. State law identifies the property interest and effectiveness of the disclaimer; federal law decides the tax consequence. Separate the date the interest was created from the date the challenged gratuitous transfer occurred. The pre-1977 interest governs which regulatory version is relevant; the later statutory qualified-disclaimer provisions are not silently substituted. [P19](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/224/)

#### C. ENTERING LAW BY ISSUE

##### Issue 1: Temporal reach of new liabilities

**Entering Law:** Civil Rights Act of 1991 §§102, 109(c), 402(a)–(b); 42 U.S.C. §1981a. Bradley v. School Board, 416 U.S. 696 (1974), directs use of current law subject to its qualifications; Bowen v. Georgetown University Hospital, 488 U.S. 204 (1988), states the presumption against retroactive rulemaking and statutory retroactivity. Their reconciliation is part of the presented question, not an already announced Landgraf test. The Ex Post Facto Clause is a criminal prohibition, not an automatic bar to all civil retrospective legislation. [R20](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/244/)

#### D. STONE’S REVISED REASONING, APPLICATION, AND LIMITS

**Stone’s Position and Scope:** retain prospective treatment of §102 and candidly adopt the organizing temporal framework.

*Bradley*'s current-law language and *Bowen*'s antiretroactivity language require reconciliation. First ask whether Congress prescribed the relevant temporal reach; absent such direction, ask whether applying the provision would attach new legal consequences to completed conduct. New compensatory and punitive damages do so here. The statute links its jury option to those damages. [Provisions and competing authorities, 511 U.S. 244](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/92-757.ZO.html).

This is **a meaningful clarification of a strained framework**, not an already available “Landgraf test.” Its basis is established fair-notice and settled-expectation presumptions, read alongside Congress's power to alter pending law. *Bradley*'s litigation-fee setting and prospective aspects of pending litigation can remain intact; *Bowen*'s retrospective financial rule is closer to attaching new liability for completed conduct. Simulated *Harper* concerns judicial rules, not automatic reach of statutory amendments.

Stone's Master §16 denies comparable reliance weight to expectations of continued exclusion. That does not justify unforeseen forms or magnitudes of backward-looking liability. He should expressly distinguish reliance on permission to discriminate from legal notice about the consequences of conduct already prohibited. Otherwise the asymmetry would swallow his separate statutory antiretroactivity commitment.

Application must be provision-specific. A procedural rule governing the next hearing can apply without reopening a completed trial; a new damages cause or penalty requires separate temporal analysis. An effective date alone does not answer which past conduct is covered. The difficult boundary is a new remedy for an existing violation: label alone cannot decide whether it adds legal exposure.

The cost is leaving some proved violations without the new compensation Congress considered desirable. Congress could supply sufficiently clear retrospective direction within constitutional bounds; the court should not infer it from remedial purpose. Affirm this judgment without holding the harassment lawful or all civil retroactivity unconstitutional.

**User-directed case emphasis:** Retain the user's nonretroactivity disposition as an administrable limit on remedial ambition. The victims' interest in effective civil-rights enforcement and defendants' notice of new liability are both real; the governing distinction concerns new legal consequences and adequate legislative direction, not a moral entitlement to discriminate or a guarantee against all legal change.

#### E. RECORD AND SOURCE SUPPORT

**Independent Record and Threshold Context:** Landgraf’s workplace harassment occurred in 1984–1986. After a bench trial, the district court found a hostile environment but no constructive discharge, including that the employer had taken corrective action by the time she resigned. The court found no available equitable relief under the then-governing law. The 1991 Act was enacted November 21, 1991, while her appeal was pending. This review concerns the new damages and jury provisions, not a fresh finding about the harassment’s severity. [R20](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/244/)

The case remains live because the requested new remedies would change the judgment. A finding of a Title VII violation is not itself authority for a remedy unavailable under the governing statute. The applicability of the jury provision must be separated from, and then related to, its statutory damages predicate. [R20](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/244/)
