#### C. ENTERING LAW BY ISSUE

##### Issue 1: Changed law versus revision of a completed federal judgment

**Entering Law:** Article III vests judicial power in the federal courts. United States v. Klein, 80 U.S. (13 Wall.) 128 (1872), limits legislative prescription of judicial results, while Robertson v. Seattle Audubon Society, 503 U.S. 429 (1992), permits Congress to change governing law in pending litigation. Pennsylvania v. Wheeling & Belmont Bridge Co., 59 U.S. (18 How.) 421 (1856), distinguishes prospective injunctive operation after a legal change. McCullough v. Virginia, 172 U.S. 102 (1898), addresses completed adjudications. Lampf, 501 U.S. 350 (1991), and James B. Beam, 501 U.S. 529 (1991), supply the limitations and judicial-retroactivity background. Chase Securities Corp. v. Donaldson, 325 U.S. 304 (1945), permits some revival of time-barred claims under due process; it does not by itself decide the Article III status of a final federal judgment. [P28](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/93-609.ZPC.html) [P28.1](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/997/39/382064/)

#### D. STONE'S REASONING, APPLICATION, AND LIMITS

**Stone's Proposed Holding:** Article III does not categorically prohibit Congress from establishing an expressly retrospective, objectively defined statutory procedure requiring federal courts to reinstate qualifying private actions previously dismissed under an intervening limitations rule. Section 27A(b) changes the governing law concerning the availability of adjudication. It does not itself determine the underlying securities-fraud allegations, establish liability, or dictate the findings required to resolve those claims. A federal court must independently determine whether the statutory conditions for reinstatement are satisfied. When those conditions are satisfied, reinstatement is mandatory under the enacted provision. The court retains responsibility for adjudicating the claims, defenses, and remedies that follow. This holding is confined to the statutory reinstatement procedure presented and does not establish an unrestricted congressional power to reopen every completed federal judgment.

**Reasoning:** The finality of an Article III judgment is an indispensable protection of judicial independence. Congress cannot substitute its own findings for those of a court, prescribe the liability of identified parties, or require the judiciary to enter a merits judgment dictated by the legislature. But the constitutional protection of final adjudication is not identical to a prohibition against every subsequently created statutory remedy affecting a completed proceeding.

Congress possesses authority to alter the substantive and procedural law governing federal controversies, subject to the Constitution. That authority may include establishing a new legal entitlement to adjudication where the statute expressly identifies the proceedings to which it applies and leaves the judicial application of that law to the courts. Section 27A(b) establishes such a procedure.

The provision responds to the intervening federal limitations rule announced in *Lampf*. It identifies actions commenced within a defined period, dismissed as time-barred after the intervening decision, and timely under the law applicable before that decision. It requires a motion within the prescribed period. These criteria are matters for judicial determination. Congress has not declared that the defendants committed securities fraud, that the plaintiffs proved reliance or injury, or that particular damages must be awarded.

Petitioners correctly observe that reinstatement alters the consequence of an earlier final dismissal. The Court does not evade that constitutional objection by describing the statute as merely discretionary. Congress has required reinstatement where its conditions are satisfied. The dispositive distinction is that Congress has created a new legal rule requiring access to further adjudication. It has not performed the adjudication itself. This distinction preserves both the legislative power to change governing law and the judicial power to determine the rights and liabilities of parties independently.

**Limits:** The constitutional limitation remains substantial. Congress may not declare that a completed judicial finding was factually erroneous and compel a contrary factual determination. Nor may Congress disguise an individualized legislative award or assessment of liability as a procedural reopening statute. This holding does not decide compelled reopening of merits judgments or individualized legislative commands to adjudicate liability.

#### E. RECORD AND SOURCE SUPPORT

**Independent Record and Threshold Context:** Pacific Mutual filed in March 1991 over 1987 securities transactions. Existing Fifth Circuit law supplied a four-year limitations period. Lampf and James B. Beam, both decided June 20, 1991, led to dismissal with prejudice in August 1991 under the federal one-year/three-year rule. Pacific Mutual did not appeal. Congress enacted §27A on December 19, 1991; subsection (b) required reinstatement, on a timely motion, of specified actions commenced before June 20 and dismissed after June 19 as time-barred if timely under the former law. Pacific Mutual moved on January 31, 1992. The posture is reopening a completed dismissal, not merely changing law during an unresolved appeal. Article III finality is the presented merits limit on congressional authority, not a reason to deny jurisdiction to review the reinstatement judgment. Section 27A’s temporal command is express, so an ordinary presumption against retroactivity does not resolve its constitutional validity. The underlying fraud allegations and the validity of every other provision of §27A are unnecessary to the question. O’Connor does not participate; no reason for that nonparticipation is supplied.
