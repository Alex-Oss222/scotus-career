#### C. ENTERING LAW BY ISSUE

##### Issue 1: Changed law versus revision of a completed federal judgment

**Entering Law:** Article III vests judicial power in the federal courts. United States v. Klein, 80 U.S. (13 Wall.) 128 (1872), limits legislative prescription of judicial results, while Robertson v. Seattle Audubon Society, 503 U.S. 429 (1992), permits Congress to change governing law in pending litigation. Pennsylvania v. Wheeling & Belmont Bridge Co., 59 U.S. (18 How.) 421 (1856), distinguishes prospective injunctive operation after a legal change. McCullough v. Virginia, 172 U.S. 102 (1898), addresses completed adjudications. Lampf, 501 U.S. 350 (1991), and James B. Beam, 501 U.S. 529 (1991), supply the limitations and judicial-retroactivity background. Chase Securities Corp. v. Donaldson, 325 U.S. 304 (1945), permits some revival of time-barred claims under due process; it does not by itself decide the Article III status of a final federal judgment. [P28](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/93-609.ZPC.html) [P28.1](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/997/39/382064/)

#### D. STONE'S REASONING, APPLICATION, AND LIMITS

**Stone's Proposed Holding:** Article III protects the conclusive disposition of a private case by a federal court once its judgment has become final through the ordinary judicial process. Congress may change the law governing cases still pending, but it may not, solely by changing the law previously applicable to a completed private action, require an Article III court to vacate that final judgment and reinstate the same litigation. Finality is itself an Article III protection; it does not disappear because Congress leaves statutory eligibility and the underlying merits for a court to decide. Section 27A(b) cannot constitutionally require reinstatement of Pacific Mutual's final, unappealed dismissal.

**Reasoning:** Section 27A(b) expressly directs reinstatement, so ordinary antiretroactivity construction cannot avoid the constitutional question. The judgment here became final and unappealed before enactment. Congress's power to change governing law in pending cases under *Robertson* does not itself authorize revision of a completed federal judgment, and *Robertson* left that final-judgment question open.

The position rests on Article III's allocation of conclusive judicial adjudication, on *Klein* and *McCullough*, and on the distinction between completed judgments and the prospective equitable operation illustrated by *Wheeling Bridge*.

The strongest counterargument is that Congress changed a generally applicable limitations rule, reviving claims rather than directing findings or revising an adjudicated fraud merits question, and that a court would independently decide eligibility and the merits after reinstatement. *Chase Securities* supports legislative revival against a due-process objection. But a final limitations dismissal is still a judicial resolution, and the independent work a court would do after reinstatement does not restore what the reinstatement command takes away: the completed dismissal itself. Otherwise Congress could reopen any completed case by changing its governing rule and leaving the rest to the courts. The structural limit does not depend on a defendant's moral entitlement to avoid a fraud claim.

The cost is preserving dismissals generated by a sudden intervening limitations decision even when Congress sought to correct unfairness. Concrete finality and the constitutional allocation justify the line.

**Limits:** Application turns on finality before enactment, the availability of direct review, the character of the judicial disposition, and what reopening would alter. Pending appeals differ; continuing prospective injunctions differ; ordinary judicial reopening under valid procedures differs. Congress may not, in any event, declare a completed judicial finding erroneous, compel a contrary finding, or prescribe the liability of identified parties. Stone decides nothing on §27A(a), the underlying fraud allegations, or general severability.

#### E. RECORD AND SOURCE SUPPORT

**Independent Record and Threshold Context:** Pacific Mutual filed in March 1991 over 1987 securities transactions. Existing Fifth Circuit law supplied a four-year limitations period. Lampf and James B. Beam, both decided June 20, 1991, led to dismissal with prejudice in August 1991 under the federal one-year/three-year rule. Pacific Mutual did not appeal. Congress enacted §27A on December 19, 1991; subsection (b) required reinstatement, on a timely motion, of specified actions commenced before June 20 and dismissed after June 19 as time-barred if timely under the former law. Pacific Mutual moved on January 31, 1992. The posture is reopening a completed dismissal, not merely changing law during an unresolved appeal. Article III finality is the presented merits limit on congressional authority, not a reason to deny jurisdiction to review the reinstatement judgment. Section 27A’s temporal command is express, so an ordinary presumption against retroactivity does not resolve its constitutional validity. The underlying fraud allegations and the validity of every other provision of §27A are unnecessary to the question. O’Connor does not participate; no reason for that nonparticipation is supplied.
