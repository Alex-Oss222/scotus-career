#### C. ENTERING LAW BY ISSUE

##### Issue 1: Proof burden and whole-charge interpretation

**Entering Law:** In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358 (1970), requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every element. Holland v. United States, 348 U.S. 121 (1954), examines the charge as a whole without a required verbal formula. Cage v. Louisiana, 498 U.S. 39 (1990), invalidated an instruction lowering that burden. Estelle v. McGuire, 502 U.S. 62 (1991), supplies the reasonable-likelihood inquiry. [R15](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/1/)

##### Issue 2: Remedy for a defective burden instruction

**Entering Law:** Sullivan v. Louisiana, 508 U.S. 275 (1993), treats a constitutionally deficient reasonable-doubt instruction as structural error, not an ordinary trial error cured by a reviewing court’s assessment of overwhelming evidence. Ylst v. Nunnemaker, 501 U.S. 797 (1991), bears on the state-court merits disposition. [R15](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/1/)

#### D. STONE’S REVISED REASONING, APPLICATION, AND LIMITS

**Stone’s Position and Scope:** retain the proposed split result as a defensible, close application; substantially strengthen the comparison of the complete charges.

*Winship*, *Holland*, *Cage*, and *Estelle* ask whether there is a reasonable likelihood of conviction below the constitutional burden, not whether particular phrases are desirable. Simulated *Sullivan* controls the consequence only after a constitutional defect is established. [Both charges and competing readings, 511 U.S. 1](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/1/).

For **Sandoval**, evidence-linked abiding conviction and the surrounding rejection of sentiment support affirmance despite archaic moral-certainty language. Stone can discourage the phrase without requiring his preferred wording or using supervisory authority over a state court.

For **Victor**, the interaction of actual-and-substantial doubt, moral certainty, and strong probabilities provides a serious basis for finding the acquittal threshold overstated. But the supplement understates the strongest answer: the charge contrasts substantial doubt with imaginary doubt, says probabilities must exclude reasonable doubt, uses a hesitation analogy and abiding conviction, and expressly retains the prosecution's burden. Stone must explain why the attempted clarification is circular—probabilities are qualified by a doubt definition that itself arguably demands excessive magnitude—and why the other instructions do not remove that reasonable likelihood. Merely calling the wording confusing would not distinguish Sandoval coherently. [Contemporaneous reasoning supporting this distinction](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/92-8894.ZX.html).

This is **disputed application, not a new categorical word ban**. Future courts should identify the operative definition and contextual cures, not count disfavored expressions. The cost is imperfect predictability in reviewing old charges; a contextual test already carries that cost, while simple modern drafting can avoid it.

Victor arrives through state postconviction review with a state merits ruling, not a federal habeas *Teague* proceeding. If Stone finds the burden constitutionally vitiated, strong evidence cannot make it harmless under *Sullivan*. Remand for state relief and a lawful retrial if pursued; no acquittal follows. Sandoval's distinct judgment remains intact on this issue.

**Egalitarian application:** The presumption of innocence and prosecution's burden are deliberate constitutional asymmetries, not unfairness to the State. The State may use comprehensible ordinary language without a prescribed script, but the whole charge must permit an ordinary juror to apply the correct burden. Compare how the competing phrases actually interact rather than condemning vocabulary in isolation.

#### E. RECORD AND SOURCE SUPPORT

**Independent Record and Threshold Context:** Sandoval’s charge used moral-evidence and moral-certainty language together with an abiding conviction after consideration of all the evidence, and other instructions confined factfinding to received evidence and rejected prejudice or sentiment. It distinguished reasonable doubt from merely possible or imaginary doubt. [R15](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/1/)

Victor did not object at trial or raise the claim on direct appeal, but Nebraska’s last reviewing court actually decided it on the merits. That posture permits Supreme Court review rather than supplying an adequate state procedural ground. Victor is here on state postconviction review, not federal habeas; do not silently apply Teague as a federal jurisdictional bar. Sandoval is on direct review. The questions concern the trial instructions, not reweighing guilt evidence. [R15](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/1/)

#### C. ENTERING LAW BY ISSUE

##### Issue 1: Mandatory revocation sentence and lenity

**Entering Law:** Former §3565(a) contrasts continuing probation with revocation and resentencing and contains a drug-possession proviso requiring revocation and a sentence not less than one-third of the original sentence. The sentencing statutes and then-binding Guidelines define the imprisonment range initially available. Bifulco v. United States, 447 U.S. 381 (1980), and United States v. Bass, 404 U.S. 336 (1971), apply lenity after the established interpretive tools leave serious penal uncertainty; Chapman v. United States, 500 U.S. 453 (1991), cautions against finding ambiguity merely from rival arguments. The differently worded supervised-release provision does not automatically supply this proviso’s terms. [P15](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/39/)

#### D. STONE’S REVISED REASONING, APPLICATION, AND LIMITS

**Stone’s Position and Scope:** retain the two-to-six-month construction, but acknowledge that it is a constructive resolution of defective drafting.

Sixty months of probation cannot naturally be treated as sixty equivalent months of imprisonment. The government's one-third calculation would produce twenty months' imprisonment although the original Guidelines range was zero to six. That disparity is not independently unconstitutional or automatically absurd, but it exposes the ambiguity in “original sentence.” [Operative provision and alternatives, 511 U.S. 39](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/39/).

Stone's fair-notice commitment supports lenity after examining the revocation scheme, available original sentences, and differently drafted supervised-release provisions. *Bass* and *Bifulco* permit resolving residual penal uncertainty against the larger penalty; *Chapman* cautions that rival readings alone are insufficient. Simulated *R.L.C.* does not provide a majority-adopted universal lenity formula, and a proposed *Ratzlaf* decision is not already law.

This is **a meaningful but lawful statutory clarification**, not simple literal application. Stone should explain both choices: the original available imprisonment range is the referent, and its upper end supplies a determinate one-third floor, while the original maximum remains the ceiling. That yields two to six months and gives the mandatory revocation/imprisonment command effect without inventing a probation-to-prison exchange rate. Choosing the zero-month lower end instead would eliminate the mandated custodial minimum; the existing sentencing structure supports rejecting that consequence.

The strongest objection is that Congress said original sentence, not Guidelines maximum. Stone must admit the construction's textual strain and explain why the government's alternative is not sufficiently clear to authorize the additional imprisonment. The boundary is a materially different original range or statutory sentencing structure, which requires its own calculation. Preserve revocation and the release order because eleven months already exceeds the lawful maximum. Do not use a later amendment to repair the earlier statute.

**Egalitarian application:** The defendant's liberty, public protection, legislative penal design, and predictable administration all matter in resolving the defective language. Probation and imprisonment are not interchangeable units. Preserve lawful revocation and the enacted sentencing relationship without converting drafting uncertainty into an unexplained increase in incarceration or a judicial amnesty.

#### E. RECORD AND SOURCE SUPPORT

**Independent Record and Threshold Context:** Granderson pleaded guilty to destruction of mail. The original Guidelines imprisonment range was zero to six months, but the district court imposed sixty months’ probation and a fine. After a positive cocaine test, it applied the proviso requiring not less than one-third of the “original sentence” and imposed twenty months in prison. Granderson had served eleven months when the court of appeals ordered release. The revocation itself is not the point disputed here. Separate the kind of sentence required, the referent for its minimum, and the upper limit. Probation time and imprisonment are not self-evidently interchangeable units. The later amendment of §3565 is not the law governing this event. The Court reviews the legal sentencing range independently and may not replace the enacted words with a preferred numerical penalty. [P15](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/39/)

#### C. ENTERING LAW BY ISSUE

##### Issue 1: Direct-review retroactivity

**Entering Law:** Gerstein v. Pugh, 420 U.S. 103 (1975), requires prompt neutral probable-cause review after warrantless arrest. County of Riverside v. McLaughlin, 500 U.S. 44 (1991), generally treats review within 48 hours as timely, subject to unreasonable-delay challenges; beyond that period the government must demonstrate a bona fide emergency or other extraordinary circumstance. Griffith v. Kentucky, 479 U.S. 314 (1987), applies new criminal-procedure rules to cases not final on direct review. [R16](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/79/)

##### Issue 2: Remedy and alternative affirmance

**Entering Law:** A Gerstein violation does not itself void a later conviction. Suppression requires a lawful exclusionary basis and attention to the connection between delay and evidence. New York v. Harris, 495 U.S. 14 (1990), supplies a remedial analogy, not a holding resolving every delayed-review statement. Courts review judgments and may consider preserved alternative grounds. [R16](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/79/)

#### D. STONE’S REVISED REASONING, APPLICATION, AND LIMITS

**Stone’s Position and Scope:** retain vacatur and remand, with no automatic suppression.

*Griffith*, 479 U.S. 314, fixes direct-review retroactivity by finality, not by the date prosecution began. *McLaughlin*, 500 U.S. 44, therefore applies. Stone's commitment to equal treatment of pending cases aligns with simulated *Harper*, while the criminal rule is independently supplied by *Griffith*. [Record and timing, 511 U.S. 79](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/79/).

This is **straight application of controlling law**. The four-day interval triggers the government's obligation to establish a bona fide emergency or extraordinary circumstance; the ordinary burden is not shifted merely by relabeling the delay administrative. At the same time, the existence of an unlawful delay does not itself show that every later statement was obtained through it or that the conviction must be overturned.

Lower courts should identify the exact arrest and probable-cause-review times, any legally sufficient explanation, preservation of remedial arguments, the circumstances and causation of the statements, and the available exclusionary rule. A freely given statement unrelated to extended detention differs from one obtained through exploitation of the unlawful delay. *New York v. Harris* is an analogy about the interests an exclusionary rule protects, not a preexisting answer for every delayed-review case.

The counterargument is that a right without suppression may be ineffective. Stone should take that seriously but cannot invent an automatic exclusionary consequence from the timing rule alone. Conversely, harmlessness cannot be used before identifying the error and the applicable remedy. The state court's decision to address the federal question allows this review; it does not erase every separate preservation requirement on remand. No acquittal, release, or capital resentencing follows solely from the retroactivity mistake.

**Egalitarian application:** Timely judicial review protects the person arrested and the reliability of enforcement. A delay violation does not itself prove guilt evidence unreliable, and reliable evidence does not excuse the detention violation. Separate justification, causal consequences, and the available remedy so neither side gains an automatic result from the existence or absence of suppression.

#### E. RECORD AND SOURCE SUPPORT

**Independent Record and Threshold Context:** Powell was arrested without a warrant on November 3, 1989; a magistrate made the probable-cause determination on November 7. Statements made on November 7 were used in the prosecution. The child victim’s later death led to murder proceedings. Nevada’s high court raised McLaughlin itself but treated the rule as unavailable because the prosecution began before that decision. [R16](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/79/)

The state court passed on the federal retroactivity issue, supporting §1257 review notwithstanding Powell’s earlier failure to raise it. That does not decide every preservation question governing the remedy. The case was not final when McLaughlin issued. The source and causal prerequisites for suppression remain distinct from the constitutional timing rule. [R16](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/79/)

#### C. ENTERING LAW BY ISSUE

##### Issue 1: Discrimination and asserted compensation

**Entering Law:** Philadelphia v. New Jersey, 437 U.S. 617 (1978), Chemical Waste Management v. Hunt, 504 U.S. 334 (1992), and Fort Gratiot, 504 U.S. 353 (1992), govern waste-origin discrimination. Pike v. Bruce Church, 397 U.S. 137 (1970), governs evenhanded incidental burdens, not an established discriminatory measure. A discriminatory burden requires a legitimate nonprotectionist purpose not adequately served by reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives. Maryland v. Louisiana, 451 U.S. 725 (1981), Armco, 467 U.S. 638 (1984), and Henneford, 300 U.S. 577 (1937), supply compensatory-tax principles. [R17](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/93/)

#### D. STONE’S REVISED REASONING, APPLICATION, AND LIMITS

**Stone’s Position and Scope:** retain rejection of the origin surcharge.

The $2.25 versus $0.85 charge expressly distinguishes otherwise comparable waste by origin. *Philadelphia*, and the inherited simulated *Chemical Waste* and *Fort Gratiot* rules, make that discrimination legally significant. Environmental protection is a permissible objective, but does not change the governing inquiry to ordinary *Pike* balancing. [Charge and asserted justification, 511 U.S. 93](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/93/).

Stone's environmental commitments are coherent with this result because they protect lawful regulation, not geographic favoritism. He can support substantial evenhanded disposal charges and genuine cost differentials while insisting on the compensatory-tax predicates: an identifiable intrastate burden, substantially equivalent taxable events, and a relationship that actually compensates rather than protects. Residents' general income or other taxes do not automatically match a disposal charge.

This is **application of strongly controlling discrimination law**, not a need to redesign dormant Commerce Clause doctrine. Application asks what service or cost the fee recovers, whether origin independently causes an added cost, and whether reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives address the concern. Hazardous waste with materially different handling costs differs from identical waste crossing a border. Out-of-state inspection or enforcement expenses could matter if actually established and lawfully allocated; origin alone proves neither.

The State's strongest concern is that outsiders use scarce disposal capacity financed by residents. Stone should acknowledge financing and conservation costs while explaining why a regulatory surcharge on private facilities is not a proprietary charge at a state-owned facility. Avoid deciding the latter. Strike the discriminatory differential through the available review channel, leaving replacement rates and any refunds to their separate legal sources. No general right to cost-free waste disposal or prohibition on conservation measures follows.

**User-directed case emphasis:** Preserve the user's exact distinction: origin is not itself an additional cost. Oregon may allocate actual additional costs or demonstrable burdens under the governing nondiscrimination rule, and a compensatory levy needs a substantially equivalent local burden serving the same purpose. A cost-recovery label cannot do that evidentiary work.

[R17](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/93/)
