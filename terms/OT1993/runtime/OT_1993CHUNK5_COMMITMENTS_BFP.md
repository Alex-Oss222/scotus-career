## Statute, posture and remedial boundary

The July 12, 1989 sale preceded the October 25, 1989 Chapter 11 petition by less than one year. The challenge concerns §548(a)(2)(A)'s reasonably equivalent value requirement and the separately required insolvency condition under (B)(i). Section 101(54) expressly includes foreclosure of an equity of redemption, and §548 covers voluntary and involuntary transfers. Inclusion as a transfer is not itself a measure of value. Under §548(d)(2)(A), value includes property or satisfaction or securing of the debtor's present or antecedent debt, excluding an unperformed promise of support to the debtor or a relative. No small-capital, inability-to-pay or actual-fraud ground is adjudicated.

The Ninth Circuit affirmed both the district-court route concerning the private buyers and the Bankruptcy Appellate Panel route concerning Imperial. It rejected the alternative premise that BFP lacked a property interest, recognizing the quiet-title judgment's fee co-ownership. That rejected premise cannot support affirmance. Procedural compliance, adequate notice and absence of collusion were upheld. The price was $433,000; the asserted value above $725,000 is an allegation, not a federal valuation finding. Osborne's good faith does not itself establish a complete remedial defense to every possible claim.

Dewsnup protects an allowed real-property lien from the particular §506(d) strip-down in Chapter 7 while preserving separately applicable avoidance powers. Nobelman protects the whole mortgage rights of the holder specified by §1322(b)(2) while preserving separate statutory restrictions and the qualified §1322(b)(5) cure. Neither decides §548. Butner supplies state property rules absent a contrary federal consequence, not immunity from enacted avoidance. A rejected conclusive-value rule therefore produces further proceedings, not automatic avoidance, a title award, a refund or a fixed percentage of ordinary-market value.

## Enhanced lawful-path comparison

**Regular-foreclosure value path:** Affirm both appellate components because the price produced by a noncollusive real-property mortgage foreclosure complying with state law supplies reasonably equivalent value in the particular forced-sale setting. The statutory adjective does not prescribe the ordinary-market price, which assumes conditions inconsistent with compulsory sale. Butner's respect for property arrangements, Gelfert's recognition of forced-sale conditions, and the statute's absence of a nationwide percentage support this construction. Transfer inclusion remains meaningful because collusion or failure to follow the governing foreclosure requirements can defeat the proposed rule; other avoidance grounds are not erased. The strongest objection is that a conclusive equation of price and value can make §548(a)(2)(A) ineffectual even when the price is extremely low. The answer is that the relevant legal value is the price obtainable under the lawful forced-sale conditions, rather than a judicially invented alternative sale regime. This is an interpretation of the value component, not a free-standing federalism exemption.

**Independent federal-value path:** Reverse the conclusive-value ruling and remand both affected routes for a genuine §548 inquiry. The express inclusion of foreclosure and involuntary transfers gives work to the substantive requirement that what the debtor received be reasonably equivalent to the transferred interest. A proceeding's regularity is powerful evidence but does not conclusively resolve the exchanged value. Ron Pair's enacted-Code approach, Owen's recognition that federal avoidance may displace a state-law result, and Butner's countervailing-federal-law qualification support this reading. The strongest objection is impaired title certainty and the absence of a reliable substitute market for forced sales. A bounded response accounts for the actual forced-sale setting and all relevant circumstances, without demanding full ordinary-market value or treating Durrett's percentage discussion as law. Bundles supplies a possible rebuttable/all-circumstances approach; adoption of its every detail is unnecessary to reject conclusiveness.

**Narrower positions and remedy:** No Justice below commits to a nationwide minimum percentage, a mandatory fair-market-value rule or automatic avoidance. Rejecting conclusiveness permits regularity and actual foreclosure constraints to carry substantial evidentiary force while leaving the ultimate value conclusion and other statutory predicates below. Upholding conclusiveness here does not validate tax foreclosures, personal-property sales, collusive transactions or noncompliant procedures. No source establishes the additional findings needed to order recovery or allocate liability among the respondents.
