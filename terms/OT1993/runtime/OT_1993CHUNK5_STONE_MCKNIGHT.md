#### B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

**Review Posture and Requested Relief:** Seventh Circuit order of September 30, 1992, dismissed McKnight’s employment-discrimination appeal and imposed a $500 sanction on his attorney. The underlying appellate issue was whether §101 of the Civil Rights Act of 1991 applied to pre-enactment events. McKnight seeks relief from the adverse appellate disposition and the sanction imposed in connection with his appeal. General Motors defended dismissal and sanctions on the ground that circuit precedent foreclosed the retroactivity argument. [P29](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/92-1113.ZPC.html)

**Stone’s Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Vacate the order insofar as it imposes the $500 sanction on counsel, and remand; leave the underlying dismissal undisturbed on the stated dependency baseline.

**Stone’s Remedy and Remand Position:** Require reconsideration under the correct frivolousness distinction. No automatic new sanction, no invented bad-faith finding, and no automatic reinstatement of the employment claim.

#### C. ENTERING LAW BY ISSUE

##### Issue 1: Sanction for preservation of a disputed legal question

**Entering Law:** Federal Rule of Appellate Procedure 38 authorizes sanctions for a frivolous appeal, not merely an unsuccessful one. The actual source and full grounds of the sanction must be taken from the appellate order; §1927 or an inherent-power bad-faith finding is not invented. Luddington and Mozee bind the circuit as to the merits but do not eliminate the lawful route for seeking Supreme Court correction. The 1991 Act’s §101 and the event-eligible retroactivity authorities govern the distinct underlying claim. [P29](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/92-1113.ZPC.html)

#### D. STONE’S REVISED REASONING, APPLICATION, AND LIMITS

**Stone’s Position and Scope:** retain vacatur of the sanction and keep the merits component separate.

Binding circuit authority required rejection of the retroactivity argument in that court; it did not make preserving a nonfrivolous challenge for Supreme Court review sanctionable. Reasonableness is measured when counsel acted, when the issue remained nationally unsettled, rather than after *Landgraf* or *Rivers*. [Sanction record, 511 U.S. 659](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/659/).

This is **application of the distinction between losing and frivolous litigation**, fully consistent with Stone's fee-access positions. It does not entail a right to relitigate indefinitely or disregard precedent. Counsel should identify the adverse controlling authority, preserve the argument candidly, and use the proper review channel. Fabrication, repetitive delay, or an objectively baseless claim can still support a sanction under the correct source and process.

The strongest objection is that allowing knowingly doomed appeals burdens courts. Some appellate step is required to obtain further review, and a rule penalizing that necessary step would make the opportunity illusory. The material question is whether the request for change is serious and properly presented, not whether the intermediate court can grant it.

The abbreviated order does not establish an independent abusive act. Do not invent a §1927 or inherent-power rationale to save the penalty. Simulated *Willy*'s collateral sanctions authority says nothing about whether this use was justified.

As a coherent recommendation alongside Stone's *Landgraf/Rivers* positions, leave the underlying dismissal intact and vacate the $500 sanction. If actual earlier simulated rulings adopted a different temporal rule, reconsider the merits component under that law without changing the historical reasonableness of counsel's advocacy. No damages award or new employment trial follows from the sanctions ruling itself.
