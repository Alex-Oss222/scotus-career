## 51. Custis v. United States

### SECTION II — STONE POSITION SUPPLEMENT

**Case Name:** Custis v. United States

**Citation or Docket:** 511 U.S. 485 (1994); No. 93-5209

#### A. STATUS AND GOVERNING BASELINE

**Approval Status:** USER-DIRECTED REVISED POSITION (second revision; same disposition as the first, substantially expanded and restructured). The user authorized these preparation changes and supplied case-specific reasoning. This is Stone's current prepared position, subject to its stated legal and factual limits; it is not an Engine vote, an adopted Court holding, or an inferred fallback.

**Stone’s Position:** A defendant must have a meaningful opportunity to challenge the constitutional validity of a prior conviction through the review procedures the law makes available. The defendant must diligently pursue those procedures in the proper sequence, ordinarily presenting the claim through available state remedies before seeking authorized federal habeas review.

That opportunity does not make the later federal sentencing proceeding an additional forum for litigating every constitutional objection to the earlier conviction. Exhaustion alone does not create a new right to relitigate an unsuccessful challenge at sentencing.

**Current-Through Baseline:** Immediately before May 23, 1994. Apply inherited simulated holdings and otherwise applicable law effective before this event. Do not import later restrictions on habeas proceedings, later limitation periods, or later decisions concerning expired convictions and federal sentence correction.

**Inherited-Law Context:** Parke v. Raley preserves the presumption of regularity attached to prior convictions and permits appropriate burdens on a defendant challenging them. It does not establish that every prior conviction is constitutionally valid, decide whether the ACCA authorizes collateral review at sentencing, or eliminate the distinct protection against an unconstitutional complete denial of counsel.

#### B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

**Stone’s Judgment or Disposition:** Affirm the refusal to entertain Custis’s ineffective-assistance and plea-related collateral attacks within the federal sentencing proceeding under 18 U.S.C. §924(e).

Custis did not establish an unconstitutional complete denial of appointed counsel. His challenged convictions had not been invalidated, and he remained in custody for purposes of pursuing review of the state convictions. The appropriate state and federal review channels therefore remained legally relevant rather than merely hypothetical.

This disposition does not determine that the challenged convictions were constitutionally sound. It determines the proper channel for adjudicating the asserted defects.

**Stone’s Remedy Position:** Preserve Custis’s ability to challenge the state convictions through available state procedures and, after satisfying the applicable exhaustion and other requirements, through authorized federal habeas review.

If a predicate conviction is successfully invalidated, he may seek correction of the resulting federal enhancement through an authorized procedural vehicle. The existence, scope, and disposition of that application must be determined under the law governing the application. No automatic resentencing, particular sentence, or unconditional reopening is promised here.

The present federal sentence is not automatically suspended while a collateral challenge proceeds. Any request for a continuance, stay, or other interim relief requires its own legal authority and supporting circumstances.

#### C. ENTERING LAW AND THE QUESTIONS PRESENTED

Sections 924(e) and 921(a)(20) govern the use of prior convictions for the federal enhancement and recognize the legal consequences of qualifying convictions that have been set aside or otherwise brought within the statute’s exclusions.

Congress expressly provided a procedure for certain challenges to prior convictions in 21 U.S.C. §851. That provision governs a different enhancement scheme. It informs the statutory comparison but does not itself supply a procedure under the ACCA.

Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335 (1963), Burgett v. Texas, 389 U.S. 109 (1967), and United States v. Tucker, 404 U.S. 443 (1972), establish the special constitutional significance of convictions obtained through an unlawful denial of counsel and their subsequent use.

Lewis v. United States, 445 U.S. 55 (1980), distinguishes a conviction’s existing legal status from an unresolved collateral claim of constitutional invalidity. Its statutory setting differs from this enhancement proceeding and must not be treated as automatically resolving every sentencing question.

Rose v. Lundy, 455 U.S. 509 (1982), reflects the requirement that state prisoners ordinarily exhaust available state remedies before obtaining federal habeas review. The governing exhaustion provisions recognize circumstances in which state corrective procedures are absent or ineffective to protect the applicant’s rights.

Coleman v. Thompson, 501 U.S. 722 (1991), distinguishes exhaustion from procedural default and preserves the applicable exceptions to a default bar.

Maleng v. Cook, 490 U.S. 488 (1989), supplies relevant limits on habeas custody. Jurisdiction over a challenge cannot be assumed merely because an earlier conviction produces continuing consequences.

The questions are therefore distinct: whether the conviction is constitutionally defective; which court may hear that challenge; what procedural requirements apply; and what consequence successful invalidation has for the federal sentence.

#### D. STONE’S REASONING, APPLICATION, AND LIMITS

**1. Additional punishment requires lawful predicates, but not every sentencing court is a general collateral-review court.**

The federal government seeks additional imprisonment because of the earlier convictions. Their constitutional validity therefore matters. Stone rejects any suggestion that an ineffective lawyer or an invalid guilty plea becomes unimportant merely because the defect arose in an earlier prosecution.

Nevertheless, the importance of a constitutional claim does not establish the forum in which it may be adjudicated. The ACCA’s reference to previous convictions does not itself create a complete procedure for retrying the constitutional validity of each predicate at federal sentencing.

The statute’s treatment of convictions already set aside supports distinguishing an operative judgment from a successfully invalidated one. The express challenge procedure in §851 reinforces the need to identify the authority for collateral review rather than assume that every enhancement statute contains the same mechanism.

These considerations support a limitation on the sentencing forum. They do not establish the constitutional validity of the underlying judgment or excuse denial of review through a channel the law actually provides.

**2. Require the defendant to use available state procedures in the proper manner.**

A defendant seeking to challenge a state conviction must ordinarily give the state courts the opportunity required by law to address the federal constitutional claim.

The claim must be presented through the appropriate state procedure, with sufficient identification of its factual and constitutional basis. Invoking a general objection in one proceeding does not necessarily present a materially different claim in another.

The defendant must pursue the review steps necessary under the applicable exhaustion rules. The defendant may not deliberately withhold an available claim and assume that a later federal sentencing hearing will provide an unrestricted substitute.

“All available avenues” means the procedures legally available and required for the claim. It does not demand every imaginable filing, unauthorized successive applications, or endless repetition after the governing review process has been completed.

Nor must a defendant pursue further proceedings merely to accumulate additional decisions after obtaining effective relief.

**3. Preserve authorized federal review after state exhaustion.**

An adverse state decision does not itself establish that the federal constitutional claim lacks merit or foreclose the federal review Congress has authorized.

After satisfying the applicable exhaustion requirement, the defendant may seek federal habeas review if the jurisdictional and procedural conditions are met. The federal court must apply the law governing that proceeding rather than treat the state’s rejection as an automatic answer to the federal claim.

State exhaustion and entitlement to federal relief are different questions. Exhaustion identifies whether the state courts have received the required opportunity; it does not establish constitutional error, custody jurisdiction, compliance with other procedural requirements, or entitlement to a hearing.

Stone therefore requires diligent use of the available channels without promising that every application must receive a favorable decision or an evidentiary hearing.

**4. Distinguish unavailable procedures from unsuccessful arguments.**

A review procedure is not unavailable merely because success appears unlikely or the relevant courts have previously rejected similar arguments.

Conversely, a nominal procedure does not necessarily provide an effective opportunity when the legal system supplies no usable corrective process or circumstances render the process ineffective within the governing exhaustion exception.

The court must identify the actual obstacle and the legal rule governing its consequence. It should examine the relevant procedure, the defendant’s efforts, the state court’s response, and whether a lawful means of presenting the claim remained available.

Stone would apply recognized exceptions to exhaustion rather than impose an impossible sequence of filings. He would not create a general futility exception based only on pessimism about the merits.

If state procedures are unavailable or ineffective in the legally relevant sense, the first question is whether the governing federal habeas law permits review without further state exhaustion. That circumstance does not automatically create a separate ACCA sentencing challenge.

**5. Keep exhaustion and procedural default separate.**

A defendant may have no remaining state remedy because the proper opportunity was lost through a procedural failure. That does not necessarily mean the claim is ready for unrestricted federal merits review.

The court must determine whether a procedural default exists and apply the governing requirements for overcoming it, including the applicable cause-and-prejudice or recognized miscarriage-of-justice exception.

Stone does not convert every missed deadline or omitted claim into an irrevocable waiver. He also does not erase a legally sufficient default merely because the conviction later increases another sentence.

Diligence has practical significance: what the defendant knew, what procedure was available, what steps were taken, and what prevented proper presentation. Its legal consequences remain those assigned by the governing doctrine.

**6. Exhaustion does not create an additional sentencing appeal.**

A defendant who has properly pursued state and authorized federal review has used the relevant opportunities. An unsuccessful result does not, by itself, require the later federal sentencing court to conduct another merits examination of the same constitutional challenge.

The requirement to exhaust state remedies belongs to the federal habeas framework. It is not an implied ACCA provision under which exhausting both state and federal proceedings automatically unlocks a new collateral attack at sentencing.

Stone’s position therefore protects access to the appropriate review process while preserving its legal limits. It guarantees neither repeated merits determinations nor a favorable outcome.

A genuinely new claim or new evidence must be assessed under the procedures that legally permit its consideration. Its novelty does not independently confer authority on the sentencing court.

**7. Preserve the complete-denial-of-counsel exception.**

An unconstitutional complete denial of appointed counsel receives the distinct treatment established by Gideon, Burgett, and Tucker.

Stone would permit the sentencing court to address that qualifying defect rather than require a defendant to accept enhancement based on a conviction obtained in violation of the governing right to counsel.

The exception requires an actual constitutional denial. The mere absence of an attorney does not establish the defect if counsel was validly waived or the Constitution did not require appointment in the relevant proceeding.

Ineffective representation and an involuntary plea can also invalidate a conviction, but they are not automatically equivalent to complete denial of counsel for purposes of this sentencing exception. Those claims ordinarily proceed through the appropriate collateral-review channels.

Stone acknowledges the cost of that distinction. Deficient representation can be profoundly damaging. The distinction rests on the established treatment of the particular defect and the authorized forum, not a judgment that other constitutional violations are minor.

**8. Successful invalidation must receive the consequence authorized by law.**

If the predicate conviction is lawfully invalidated, the defendant may seek correction of the federal sentence that relied on it.

The reviewing court must determine the proper procedural vehicle, its jurisdiction, the effect of the invalidation, and whether the enhancement continues to have a lawful basis. It must not assume that removal of one predicate necessarily changes the sentence without examining the remaining predicates and the applicable sentencing law.

Stone would preserve the opportunity to seek that correction. He would not treat the federal sentence as immune from examination merely because the predicate was still operative when the sentence was imposed.

At the same time, this case does not establish an automatic reopening mechanism or resolve every procedural issue that a later application might present.

**9. Apply the distinction to Custis without inventing an exhaustion finding.**

Custis presented ineffective-assistance and plea-related objections to the Maryland convictions. He did not establish the qualifying complete denial of appointed counsel, and the predicates remained operative.

His continuing custody in relation to the state convictions made state review and federal habeas pertinent avenues, subject to their requirements.

Stone would therefore affirm the refusal to litigate those collateral claims within this ACCA sentencing proceeding.

He would not characterize the result as a finding that Custis failed every required exhaustion step unless the record established that failure. The immediate ground is that the sentencing proceeding does not supply the requested general collateral-review authority.

Custis’s precise entitlement to state or federal collateral review must be assessed in those proceedings. The present decision neither adjudicates his constitutional claims nor assumes that their eventual success is assured.

**10. Keep challenges to the enhancement itself available.**

This limitation concerns collateral attacks on the constitutional validity of an earlier conviction.

It does not prevent a defendant from contesting whether the government identified the correct person, established the required convictions, counted a conviction already excluded by statute, or correctly classified a predicate under the governing ACCA rules.

Those are challenges to the federal enhancement’s own legal and factual requirements. They cannot be rejected merely by labeling every dispute involving a prior conviction a collateral attack.

**11. Address the competing approach and the practical costs.**

The strongest contrary argument is that the federal court imposes additional imprisonment now and should refuse to rely on a conviction whose constitutional invalidity the defendant can establish now.

Separate proceedings can delay correction, require litigation in multiple forums, and leave uncertainty about reopening the enhanced sentence. Stone treats those burdens as real.

Nevertheless, the remedy for those burdens must have a legal source. The federal sentencing court does not obtain unlimited collateral jurisdiction simply because reviewing the predicate there might be more convenient or faster.

The appropriate balance is to preserve the recognized counsel-denial exception, enforce genuinely available review procedures, apply their established exceptions, and preserve authorized correction following successful invalidation.

A materially different case would arise if a defendant, despite diligence, had never possessed a meaningful lawful opportunity to present the constitutional challenge. Custis does not require resolution of that situation on this record. Stone reserves the question rather than treating hypothetical future review as an adequate answer or announcing an unsupported general exception.

#### E. HOLDING AND RESERVED QUESTIONS

**Stone’s Holding:** The ACCA sentencing proceeding does not authorize general collateral attacks on the constitutional validity of prior state convictions. Preserve the exception for a qualifying unconstitutional complete denial of appointed counsel.

Defendants must diligently pursue the available procedures for challenging their convictions, ordinarily through state review followed by authorized federal habeas review after satisfaction of the applicable exhaustion requirement. Recognized exhaustion and procedural-default exceptions remain operative.

Exhaustion alone does not create an additional right to relitigate the conviction at federal sentencing. Successful invalidation permits an application for correction of the federal enhancement through a legally authorized procedure.

Affirm the refusal to hear Custis’s non-Gideon collateral claims at sentencing, without deciding their merits or foreclosing their consideration in the appropriate review proceedings.

**Reserved Questions:** The precise procedure and relief available after a predicate is invalidated; claims involving expired custody or otherwise unavailable review; circumstances in which diligent defendants lacked any meaningful opportunity to present a constitutional challenge; and any independently preserved objection to the federal enhancement’s own statutory requirements.

## 52. NLRB v. Health Care & Retirement Corp. of America

### SECTION II — STONE POSITION SUPPLEMENT

**Case Name:** NLRB v. Health Care & Retirement Corp. of America

**Citation or Docket:** 511 U.S. 571 (1994); No. 92-1964

#### A. STATUS AND GOVERNING BASELINE

**Approval Status:** USER-DIRECTED REVISED POSITION (second revision, superseding the prior affirmance of the categorical patient-care rejection). The user authorized these preparation changes and supplied case-specific reasoning. This is Stone's current prepared position, subject to its stated legal and factual limits; it is not an Engine vote, an adopted Court holding, or an inferred fallback.

**Stone’s Primary Position — Option 5:** The National Labor Relations Act requires a meaningful distinction between direction attributable to professional or technical expertise and actual supervisory authority delegated by the employer. Skilled employees do not become statutory supervisors merely because their ordinary work involves exercising judgment and giving instructions to less-skilled coworkers. Exclusion requires proof of authority possessing the supervisory character specified by §2(11).

**Express Fallback — Option 4:** If the statute does not compel that distinction with the precision stated in Stone’s primary construction, the Board’s functional approach remains a permissible interpretation of the Act. Stone would sustain its application to these nurses under the governing standards of agency review. The fallback preserves the Board’s interpretive discretion rather than establishing Stone’s entire construction as a mandatory statutory rule.

**Current-Through Baseline:** Immediately before May 23, 1994. Apply inherited simulated holdings and otherwise applicable law effective before this event. The historical opinions in this case supply record context and competing reasoning, not an already controlling simulated disposition. Later interpretations of supervisory status do not govern this decision.

**Inherited-Law Context:** Lechmere preserves binding statutory limits on Board interpretation. Boston & Maine permits recognition of an agency rationale discernible in its own decision, but not judicial invention of a new rationale. Neither resolves the classification of nurses whose direction of coworkers arises within patient-care work. Chevron and the applicable labor-law deference precedents remain governing law.

#### B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

**Stone’s Judgment or Disposition:** Reverse the Sixth Circuit’s judgment insofar as it rejects the Board’s interpretation and sets aside the determination that the four nurses are protected employees. Remand for proceedings giving effect to that determination and addressing any independently preserved issues not resolved by the supervisory-status question.

Stone would reach that disposition primarily because the statutory scheme requires the distinction between skilled direction and genuine supervisory authority. Alternatively, he would reach it because the Board’s interpretation is reasonable, consistent with the Act, and supported by the administrative findings in this case.

**Stone’s Remedy Position:** Preserve the Board’s employee-status determination without creating a categorical exemption for nurses. Give the unfair-labor-practice order the enforcement consequences warranted by that determination and the resolution of any remaining issues.

Protected employee status does not itself establish that every workplace complaint or act is protected, that every disciplinary measure is unlawful, or that every requested remedy is authorized. Those questions retain their own statutory elements and procedural posture.

#### C. ENTERING LAW AND THE STATUTORY TENSION

Section 2(3) excludes supervisors from the statutory definition of employee. Section 2(11) defines supervisory authority through specified functions, including assigning and responsibly directing other employees, when exercised in the employer’s interest with independent judgment rather than in a merely routine or clerical manner.

The listed functions are alternatives. Hiring or firing authority is not indispensable when another listed function is established. The definition concerns possession of authority, not merely how frequently a person happens to exercise it.

Sections 2(12) and 9(b), however, also demonstrate that Congress contemplated meaningful coverage of professional employees. Professional work characteristically involves discretion and judgment. The statutory provisions therefore require an interpretation under which professional employees remain protected when they are not also supervisors.

The categories overlap. Some professionals are supervisors and are excluded. The existence of that overlap does not permit either category to consume the other.

Packard Motor Car Co. v. NLRB, 330 U.S. 485 (1947), preceded Congress’s express supervisory exclusion. Its discussion of employees serving an employer’s interests cannot alone settle the meaning of the later definition within the amended scheme.

NLRB v. Yeshiva University, 444 U.S. 672 (1980), rejects the proposition that professional interests necessarily differ from institutional interests. It also recognizes limits on treating ordinary professional activity as managerial. Yeshiva concerns the managerial exclusion, so its reasoning informs but does not replace analysis of §2(11)’s distinct language.

Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB, 482 U.S. 27 (1987), requires respect for rational Board interpretations consistent with the Act and for applications supported by substantial evidence. SEC v. Chenery Corp., 318 U.S. 80 (1943), confines review to the agency’s actual grounds.

#### D. STONE’S PRIMARY REASONING — OPTION 5

**1. Interpret the definition as a description of a legal role, not an accumulation of occupational labels.**

Stone accepts that providing patient care advances a nursing home’s business. That proposition does not decide whether a particular nurse exercises the employer’s supervisory authority over other employees.

Nearly every employee’s competent performance benefits the employer. If that general benefit answered the disputed question, it would contribute little to distinguishing supervisors from the employees they supervise.

The phrase “in the interest of the employer” must instead be read together with “authority,” the listed supervisory functions, and the independent-judgment requirement. The complete definition describes an employee entrusted to exercise a supervisory function for the employer. It does not encompass every skilled employee whose work requires directing another person.

This is not a claim that patient care and the employer’s interests are mutually exclusive. A nurse can serve both while remaining a protected employee. The relevant distinction concerns the character of the nurse’s authority over coworkers.

**2. Preserve both professional coverage and the supervisory exclusion.**

Congress’s treatment of professional employees would lose substantial practical force if ordinary professional judgment, combined with incidental instruction of assistants, routinely established supervisory status.

The opposite categorical rule would also be impermissible. Professional work does not immunize a person who actually possesses supervisory authority. A nurse who independently exercises disciplinary authority or genuinely directs the workforce on management’s behalf can satisfy §2(11), even when patient welfare informs every decision.

Stone therefore adopts a limiting construction:

**Professional or technical judgment, and direction incidental to performing skilled work, are insufficient by themselves to establish supervisory status. The evidence must establish an actual delegation of authority to perform at least one listed supervisory function, in the employer’s interest, through independent judgment.**

The distinction is required by the statutory scheme. The Board retains responsibility for applying it to particular workplaces and developing permissible subsidiary standards.

This construction does not require authority over hiring, discharge, wages, or institutional policy in every case. Assigning or responsibly directing employees can independently establish supervisory status when the authority has the required character.

Nor does it require supervisors to exercise powers beyond those commonly found in their occupation. An occupation may contain genuine supervisory positions. Occupational custom is relevant context, not an exemption from the statute.

**3. Examine the source, scope, and practical operation of the authority.**

The inquiry should identify the employer’s actual delegation rather than infer it from seniority, licensure, expertise, or a title.

First, identify the claimed statutory function. The employer’s assertion that a nurse is “in charge” does not establish whether the nurse assigns employees, responsibly directs them, disciplines them, or merely coordinates the implementation of existing treatment instructions.

Second, determine what decisions the nurse is authorized to make. Is the nurse communicating a clinical requirement, maintaining an established work arrangement, or exercising authority over how other employees are deployed and directed?

Third, examine the scope and practical consequences of that authority. Are directions expressions of professional knowledge, or decisions the employer has entrusted the nurse to make as a supervisor? Do recommendations effectively influence personnel decisions, or merely supply information for an independent managerial determination?

Fourth, determine whether independent judgment is required in exercising the identified supervisory authority. Professional judgment is not categorically excluded from the meaning of independent judgment. It can satisfy that requirement when used in exercising genuine supervisory authority. What it cannot do is establish that such authority exists merely because the underlying clinical work involves discretion.

The evidence may include operative policies, actual working arrangements, testimony, personnel practices, and the treatment of recommendations. A delegation can be genuine even when occasions to exercise it are infrequent. Conversely, a paper designation unsupported by operative authority does not establish the statutory relationship.

These considerations implement the statutory elements. They are not additional requirements that every supervisor must hire or fire, determine labor policy, spend a fixed percentage of time supervising, or face a particular form of personal sanction.

**4. Explain the difficult boundary rather than conceal it.**

A nurse who instructs an aide how to carry out prescribed care is not necessarily supervising the aide in the statutory sense. The direction may follow from the nurse’s clinical responsibility and superior knowledge.

A nurse who independently determines staff deployment, resolves competing personnel demands, and exercises genuine authority over the execution of employees’ work presents a materially different case. Such responsibility can qualify even without hiring or firing power.

The difficult case is a charge nurse who reallocates aides in response to changing patient needs. Both clinical judgment and supervisory authority may be present. Stone would examine the actual delegation and the nurse’s control over the employees’ work rather than announce that patient-centered decisions always qualify or never qualify.

Likewise, reporting misconduct does not automatically amount to effectively recommending discipline. But a recommendation that genuinely carries disciplinary weight cannot be discounted merely because it arose from a patient-care incident.

The clinical purpose of an act is relevant to understanding it. That purpose does not conclusively determine its statutory classification.

**5. Apply the rule to the Board’s findings in this case.**

The four employees were licensed practical nurses. Their protection does not depend on treating them as registered nurses or assuming that every nurse satisfies the statutory definition of a professional employee.

The same distinction between skilled direction and supervisory authority applies to technical employees and experienced lead workers. Professional coverage supplies structural support for that distinction; it is not the sole source of these nurses’ employee status.

The administrative law judge examined the actual workplace and found that the nurses’ direction resembled the limited direction exercised by skilled lead employees. Assignments commonly followed established patterns, aides sometimes arranged their own patient coverage, and administrators remained available for nonroutine matters.

The employer’s description of staffing, counseling, evaluation, and reporting responsibilities must be considered. Those descriptions cannot be treated as conclusive without examining the authority they actually conveyed.

Stone would sustain the Board’s assessment that these nurses’ responsibilities did not establish the genuine supervisory role required by §2(11). Their seniority during substantial portions of the workweek does not itself overturn the findings concerning the limits of their authority.

This conclusion rests on the administrative findings and the proper statutory distinction. It does not rest on the proposition that caring for patients fails to benefit the employer.

**6. Preserve the agency’s actual rationale.**

Stone would understand the Board’s patient-care formulation in light of its functional distinction and the administrative law judge’s examination of the nurses’ actual role.

That is a clarification of the rationale reflected in the administrative decision, not a judicial finding that the nurses lacked independent judgment under an entirely different theory.

If the decision rested exclusively on the proposition that patient care cannot serve the employer’s interests, without the functional analysis and supporting findings, Stone could not supply a replacement rationale and enforce the order under Chenery. A remand would then be necessary.

On this record, however, Stone finds the relevant distinction sufficiently discernible in the agency’s reasoning. The decision examined the limited character of the nurses’ authority rather than simply attaching an occupational exemption.

**7. Address the strongest statutory objection.**

The employer’s strongest argument is that Congress expressly included assigning and responsibly directing employees. Requiring an additional managerial character could become a means of deleting those terms whenever a professional performs the work.

Stone answers by preserving each listed function and rejecting a universal hiring, firing, or policymaking requirement. Front-line supervisory authority remains sufficient. His construction asks whether that authority actually exists; it does not demand executive authority.

The objection nevertheless identifies a genuine risk. “Professional direction” cannot become a label that defeats otherwise established supervisory authority. Courts and the Board must explain why particular instructions represent skilled work rather than the exercise of a listed supervisory function.

Yeshiva creates a related limit. Professional and institutional interests can coincide, and professional status does not protect employees who actually exercise managerial power. Stone distinguishes that principle from the separate proposition that every exercise of skilled direction constitutes supervision.

He therefore draws support from Yeshiva’s recognition of ordinary professional duties while preserving its refusal to treat professional status as incompatible with management.

**8. State the development and its costs candidly.**

Stone’s primary position goes beyond holding the Board’s interpretation permissible. It establishes a statutory boundary that later Boards must respect.

That conclusion requires more than a judgment that the Board selected a desirable policy. Stone must conclude that the text, the relationship among the statutory provisions, and the distinction Congress drew between protected workers and supervisors require the limiting principle.

The construction leaves substantial room for factual application, but it constrains the agency from equating professional or technical direction alone with supervisory status.

The costs include difficult mixed-role cases and disputes about the character of workplace authority. Those costs are preferable to relying on titles or assuming that all skilled direction is managerial, but they remain real. The Board must articulate its findings sufficiently for consistent application and judicial review.

Stone’s egalitarian commitment supports careful attention to actual authority on every side. An employer retains the statutory exclusion for its genuine supervisors. Skilled employees retain the protections Congress supplied unless the requirements for exclusion are established. Neither institutional power nor employee vulnerability supplies the legal classification.

#### E. EXPRESS FALLBACK — OPTION 4

**Fallback Trigger:** If Stone’s primary construction is not accepted as a mandatory interpretation of the statute, Stone would still conclude that the Board’s functional distinction is a permissible resolution of the statutory tension.

The fallback does not require agreement that the Board’s approach is the only lawful interpretation. It requires the approach to remain within the range the text and structure can reasonably bear.

Under the governing administrative-law framework, the Court should sustain a rational Board interpretation consistent with the Act and an application supported by substantial evidence. The Board’s expertise matters here because the dispute concerns how statutory categories operate within actual workplace arrangements.

The fallback does not authorize deference against clear statutory language. Lechmere’s insistence on statutory limits remains intact. Stone instead concludes that the relationship between skilled direction and supervisory authority is not conclusively resolved against the Board by observing that patient care benefits the employer.

The Board’s distinction gives effect to both the supervisory exclusion and the Act’s protection of skilled employees. Its application is supported by the findings concerning these nurses’ limited authority and the continuing role of higher management.

**Fallback Disposition:** Reverse the Sixth Circuit on the supervisory-status question and sustain the Board’s determination, with further proceedings for any independently unresolved issues.

**Difference Between the Primary Position and the Fallback:** Under the primary position, the statute requires the limiting distinction, while leaving its lawful implementation to the Board. Under the fallback, the Board’s particular distinction is permissible, but the Court does not foreclose every different interpretation another Board might reasonably adopt.

**Common Limits:** Neither position creates a categorical nursing exemption, treats professional judgment as incapable of being independent judgment, requires hiring or firing authority in every case, or guarantees protection to employees who genuinely possess supervisory authority under §2(11). Neither imports later definitions or later holdings into the law available in May 1994.

The strongest counterargument is that recidivism concerns the new offense, the earlier conviction is valid, and sentencing traditionally uses conduct proved with fewer safeguards than a trial. Stone should answer that automatic weight assigned to an uncounseled adjudication differs from independent, reliable proof of past conduct in a proceeding with present counsel. But this is a vulnerable boundary: allowing the conviction itself to be relabeled evidence of the same conduct would erase the protection.

For administration, exclude the automatic conviction point absent counsel or valid waiver; any independent conduct-based sentencing route must be separately authorized and supported, with an actual opportunity to contest underlying conduct. Do not invent a categorical exclusion of all past facts or a new proof standard. This narrower rule is more workable than invalidating every later consideration of the episode, though it may produce different treatment of convictions and conduct.

The government's preserved challenge to the waiver determination under *Zerbst/Parke* is material. Remand to resolve it under the correct burden before ordering removal of the point. *Custis* concerns attacks on predicate validity, not the permitted use of a valid fine conviction. No automatic release, vacatur of the DUI, or promised final sentence follows.

**Egalitarian application:** Examine the added confinement produced by automatic use of the uncounseled conviction while respecting the earlier lawful fine and the State's interest in reliable sentencing history. Counsel or valid waiver, independent proof of conduct, and the legal effect of the increment supply the distinction. Neither a conviction label nor a general appeal to recidivism answers the particular use question.

**Independent Record and Threshold Context:** Nichols’s 1983 DUI conviction resulted in a $250 fine and no incarceration. The federal court found that it was uncounseled and that the record did not establish waiver. After his later federal drug plea, the DUI added a criminal-history point, changing the Guidelines range from 168–210 to 188–235 months. The court imposed 235 months. The statutory ceiling was life, so the disputed increase concerns the Guidelines range, not the statutory maximum. [R31](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/738/)

Nichols challenges the later use of a conviction valid under Scott, not necessarily its original validity. The government preserves an alternative contention that the district court used the wrong burden or legal standard on waiver, invoking Johnson v. Zerbst and Parke v. Raley. That contention becomes material if the Court rejects the broad enhancement rule. The separate asserted duty to warn of future enhancement is not necessary if the Sixth Amendment ground suffices.
