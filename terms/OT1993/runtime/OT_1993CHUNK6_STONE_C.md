#### B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

**Stone’s Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Reverse and remand the Ninth Circuit’s judgment rejecting Livadas’s federal claim.

**Stone’s Remedy and Remand Position:** Permit source-authorized relief against the nonenforcement policy and further proceedings consistent with the federal labor right. Do not automatically award the wage penalty against an employer not adjudicated liable here or personal damages without the separate immunity and remedial inquiry. [R39](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/512/107/)

#### C. ENTERING LAW BY ISSUE

##### Issue 1: Independent state labor standards and federal labor rights

**Entering Law:** NLRA §§7 and 8, 29 U.S.C. §§157–158; LMRA §301, 29 U.S.C. §185; Nash v. Florida Industrial Commission, 389 U.S. 235 (1967); Allis-Chalmers v. Lueck, 471 U.S. 202 (1985); Lingle v. Norge, 486 U.S. 399 (1988); and Fort Halifax, 482 U.S. 1 (1987). Independent nonnegotiable state rights are distinguished from claims requiring interpretation of a collective-bargaining agreement. [R39](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/512/107/)

##### Issue 2: Enforcement channel

**Entering Law:** 42 U.S.C. §1983 and Golden State Transit v. Los Angeles, 493 U.S. 103 (1989), govern enforcement of sufficiently definite federal labor rights against state interference, subject to congressional foreclosure and immunity. Equal-protection rational basis is not the test for statutory preemption. [R39](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/512/107/)

#### D. STONE’S REVISED REASONING, APPLICATION, AND LIMITS

**Stone’s Position and Scope:** retain reversal on the statutory labor-right ground, keeping preemption and §1983 analytically distinct.

Stone's protection of collective action supports the result, but the doctrinal work comes from Lingle, Nash, and Golden State, rather than a new heightened classification for union members. California attached a loss of ordinary enforcement to protected collective bargaining even though its nonwaivable wage rule could be applied without resolving the contract.

The strongest state defense is administrative allocation: arbitration may supply a specialized mechanism and §301 protects uniform interpretation of agreements. It fails on these facts because the wage rate is undisputed, the statutory obligation exists independently, and the agreement itself permits the relevant outside claims. Reading a rate to perform arithmetic is not interpreting a contested contractual entitlement. Lower courts should examine the elements and actual dispute, not the presence of an agreement or a defendant's speculative contractual defense. A genuine disagreement about a bargained-for rate or the meaning of a contractual term may require a different preemption analysis.

For §1983, identify the employee's definite federal right to bargain without the challenged state penalty and the absence of congressional foreclosure of this remedy. The Supremacy Clause's priority rule alone does not create every private damages action. That distinction reconciles this result with the simulated Suter and Stone's remedial source discipline: the right and route are established here, not assumed from the policy's unlawfulness. Relief against the nonenforcement policy does not itself adjudicate the employer's wage liability or defeat an official's individual immunity. No State is required by this holding to create a wage-enforcement service; once it operates one, this federally forbidden condition cannot govern access.

**User-directed case emphasis:** The federal claim concerns a selectively withheld enforcement service burdening protected collective bargaining. Section 1983 can enforce an identifiable statutory federal right as well as a constitutional right, subject to the actual statutory structure and displacement analysis. Do not turn source discipline into a constitutional-rights-only rule or assume every statutory benefit is privately enforceable.

#### E. RECORD AND SOURCE SUPPORT

**Independent Record and Threshold Context:** After discharge on January 2, 1990, Livadas received her wages January 5 and sought the statutory penalty for the delay. California law made immediate payment and the penalty nonwaivable. The Commissioner refused enforcement through a form-letter policy for workers covered by a collective-bargaining agreement with arbitration. The relevant wage rate was undisputed, and calculating the penalty did not require interpreting disputed contract language. The agreement itself permitted direct wage claims not requiring interpretation to proceed elsewhere. [R39](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/512/107/)

The federal claim concerns interference with NLRA-protected bargaining, not merely error in construing state law. Section 301 preemption of the wage claim is an antecedent defense. A source for §1983 relief must be identified separately from the Supremacy Clause’s priority rule. Individual damages immunity remains distinct from invalidity of the policy. [R39](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/512/107/)

#### B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

**Review Posture and Requested Relief:** Ninth Circuit, 972 F.2d 1051 (1992), reversed summary judgment for the United States in Carlton’s refund action, relying on lack of notice and detrimental reliance. The estate concedes that it does not qualify under the amended text. The United States seeks reversal and denial of the refund. **Stone’s Remedy and Remand Position:** Remand for judgment consistent with application of the amended deduction rule. No refund based on this due process theory and no judicial tax-loss compensation.

#### C. ENTERING LAW BY ISSUE

##### Issue 1: Constitutional limits on express retroactive taxation

**Entering Law:** The Fifth Amendment applies to retroactive economic legislation. Usery v. Turner Elkhorn Mining Co., 428 U.S. 1, 15-17 (1976), and Pension Benefit Guaranty Corp. v. R.A. Gray & Co., 467 U.S. 717, 729-734 (1984), require a legitimate legislative purpose rationally served by the retroactive operation itself. Welch v. Henry, 305 U.S. 134 (1938), and United States v. Hemme, 476 U.S. 558 (1986), use the harsh-and-oppressive formulation; R.A. Gray explains its relationship to ordinary irrationality review. United States v. Darusmont, 449 U.S. 292 (1981), and Milliken v. United States, 283 U.S. 15 (1931), sustain tax retroactivity in their settings. These decisions do not create a universal requirement of actual advance notice or a constitutional guarantee against every tax-law change. [P31](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/512/26/)

#### D. STONE’S REVISED REASONING, APPLICATION, AND LIMITS

**Stone’s Position and Scope:** retain application of the retroactive amendment and acknowledge the estate's real reliance loss.

There is genuine tension with Stone's protection of legal reliance, but no inconsistency once the legal sources are identified. The amendment expressly governs the earlier period. Landgraf-type interpretive presumptions cannot negate that command, and R.A. Gray, Turner Elkhorn, and the tax cases leave Congress substantial power to enact rational retroactive economic legislation. The simulated Romein supplies a closer comparison than a case protecting final Article III judgments.

Congress could rationally repair the unexpectedly broad estate deduction and reach back to its enactment so that the correction did not reward the very transactions producing the unintended cost. Carlton's reliance was nonetheless understandable: the original text contained no decedent-ownership condition, and the executor completed the transaction before administrative notice. Stone should not rewrite that history as notice or misconduct. A costly tax-motivated transaction can be lawful reliance without creating a contractual guarantee that the provision will remain unchanged.

The constitutional inquiry should consider the retroactive operation's own purpose, the short corrective interval, the nature of the changed obligation, and any arbitrary targeting. Prompt notice and legislative activity help explain the interval but do not retroactively provide advance notice. Reject a fixed one-year rule, automatic reliance exemption, or unlimited revenue-raising safe harbor. A decades-old closed transaction newly burdened for unrelated punitive reasons would present a different case. The cost here is that the estate bears a real loss caused by a rational legislative correction; Stone should accept that cost under the governing standard instead of inventing compensation. He can reverse the refund judgment without endorsing an agency's power to insert the same condition into the unamended statute.

#### E. RECORD AND SOURCE SUPPORT

**Independent Record and Threshold Context:** The decedent died in 1985 without owning the securities. In December 1986 the executor bought MCI stock for about $11.2 million and promptly sold it to the ESOP for about $10.575 million, sustaining a $631,000 loss while claiming a deduction that reduced estate tax by about $2.5 million. The parties stipulated that the transaction was undertaken to obtain the deduction. The original October 1986 enactment did not require ownership by the decedent. Congress added that requirement on December 22, 1987, effective as though included in the original law. The IRS had announced its position on January 5, 1987, and corrective bills were introduced on February 26, 1987. This is an actual payment-and-refund dispute with an express retroactivity clause. A presumption of prospective construction cannot override that clause. The question is the constitutionality of the retroactive application, not whether an agency can add an ownership requirement to an unamended statute. Loss and reliance are established circumstances, not an independent constitutional exemption. [P31](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/512/26/)

#### C. ENTERING LAW BY ISSUE

##### Issue 1: Trader regulation and lawful tax collection

**Entering Law:** The Indian Trader Statutes, 25 U.S.C. §§261-264, confer federal regulatory authority over trade with Indians. Warren Trading Post Co. v. Arizona Tax Commission, 380 U.S. 685 (1965), invalidates the particular state tax interfering with federally regulated reservation trade. Moe v. Confederated Salish and Kootenai Tribes, 425 U.S. 463 (1976), Washington v. Confederated Tribes of Colville, 447 U.S. 134 (1980), and Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Citizen Band Potawatomi Tribe, 498 U.S. 505 (1991), preserve valid nonmember taxation and reasonable collection burdens while protecting exempt member purchases. White Mountain Apache Tribe v. Bracker, 448 U.S. 136 (1980), requires attention to the actual federal, tribal, and state interests and regulatory setting rather than a universal territorial rule. [P32](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/512/61/)

#### D. STONE’S REVISED REASONING, APPLICATION, AND LIMITS

**Stone’s Position and Scope:** retain rejection of categorical facial preemption, with enforceable limits on the collection mechanism.

Stone's tribal-sovereignty commitments do not make federal trader licensing an exemption for sales ultimately subject to valid nonmember taxes. Moe, Colville, and Potawatomi permit reasonable collection burdens, while Warren Trading Post preserves protection against the distinct tax and interference it addressed. This is a legitimate clarification of their coexistence, not a general state regulatory jurisdiction over reservation commerce.

The difficult point is that a quantity limit can burden exempt members without nominally taxing them. The State's evidence of extraordinary untaxed deliveries supports addressing diversion; it does not prove the accuracy of every future quota. Tribal population, actual consumption, qualifying purchasers, seasonal demand, and access to exemption documentation matter. A system that meaningfully considers tribal evidence and adjusts to genuine demand differs from a rigid statewide average that repeatedly prevents exempt purchases. Recordkeeping burdens also must remain reasonably connected to the valid nonmember collection objective.

The federal-regulation counterargument is strongest if preapproval or reporting effectively gives state officials control over federally regulated tribal commerce beyond collecting the tax. Stone should test that concern under the actual federal, tribal, and state interests, rather than treating licensing or the State's collection interest as conclusive. Facial rejection leaves those operational questions open because no implemented quota or denied exempt supply is established. The simulated Sac and Fox and Yakima concern particular residence, source, and statutory land-tax rules; neither mechanically settles this program. Reverse the categorical injunction and preserve concrete challenges to shortages, arbitrary approvals, and excessive compliance burdens. A promise to entertain such challenges matters only if the legal rule actually permits relief when the exempt channel fails.

#### E. RECORD AND SOURCE SUPPORT

**Independent Record and Threshold Context:** New York taxes cigarette purchases by nonexempt consumers but recognizes exemption for qualified tribal members purchasing for their own consumption on a qualified reservation. The rules limit unstamped deliveries to probable exempt demand; use tribal evidence where the tribe regulates distribution, otherwise use enrollment and state consumption data; require purchase approval, exemption documents, records, and monthly reporting; and allow a tribe to enter a regulatory agreement. The State submitted evidence that reported untaxed deliveries far exceeded ordinary per-capita demand. That evidence supports the asserted collection problem but is not a finding that every reservation purchaser evaded tax. No implemented quota or actual denial of an exempt member’s supply is adjudicated. This is a facial preemption challenge, not a demonstrated as-applied deprivation or a direct tax on exempt consumption. BIA wholesaler licensing, tribal tax immunity, and burdens on collection of valid nonmember taxes are separate. The State’s power must be tied to taxable transactions; a program label does not authorize taxing members or extinguishing tribal self-government. [P32](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/512/61/)

#### C. ENTERING LAW BY ISSUE

##### Issue 1: Scope of the turnover warning duty

**Entering Law:** LHWCA §5(b), 33 U.S.C. §905(b), permits an injured longshoreman’s negligence claim against a vessel while retaining the employer’s statutory compensation structure. Scindia Steam Navigation Co. v. De Los Santos, 451 U.S. 156, 167-178 (1981), distinguishes turnover, active-control, and intervention duties. The turnover duty requires ordinary care to provide conditions in which a competent stevedore can work safely, together with warning of a hazard known or discoverable through the vessel’s reasonable care, likely to be encountered, and neither known nor obvious or reasonably anticipated by a competent stevedore. Federal Marine Terminals v. Burnside Shipping, 394 U.S. 404 (1969), supplies the underlying ordinary-care formulation. Absent a contract, positive law, or custom to the contrary, Scindia does not impose general supervision or inspection of cargo operations assigned to the stevedore. [P33](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/512/92/)

#### D. STONE’S REVISED REASONING, APPLICATION, AND LIMITS

**Stone’s Position and Scope:** retain vacatur and clarify ordinary care within the vessel's assigned responsibilities.

Stone's concern with preventable workplace injury supports a meaningful warning duty; Congress's replacement of unseaworthiness with §905(b) negligence prevents restoration of vessel responsibility for all cargo hazards. Scindia provides room to clarify the turnover duty while preserving the stevedore's responsibility and expertise.

The proposed rejection of an unexplained actual-knowledge-only rule is sound only if “discoverable through reasonable care” remains tied to what the vessel must reasonably do. It cannot smuggle in a general obligation to inspect the completed stow or supervise independent loading operations. Crew observations and knowledge gained in performing the vessel's own work may establish the duty; absence of a separate universal inspection obligation does not erase that information. Supplying plastic and being present during loading are relevant, but neither automatically proves knowledge of a concealed dangerous arrangement.

Lower courts must ask whether the hazard was known or discoverable through the vessel's required care, likely to be encountered, and neither known nor obvious or reasonably anticipated by a competent unloading stevedore. Visibility while the cargo was being loaded is not the same as visibility when bags cover clear plastic during unloading. Equally, a competent unloading operator may be expected to anticipate ordinary cargo conditions and manage them safely. This boundary demands evidence of the operation rather than hindsight from the fall.

Remand disputed knowledge and concealment questions without finding negligence or causation. Additional duties grounded in an actual contract, law, or custom remain possible if established; they cannot be invented to obtain Stone's preferred compensation result. Keep turnover, active-control, and intervention duties separate. That division makes the clarification administrable and reconciles safety protection with the statutory allocation of responsibility.

#### E. RECORD AND SOURCE SUPPORT

**Independent Record and Threshold Context:** Howlett, a longshoreman unloading cargo, slipped on clear plastic beneath cargo bags. The plastic had been used when the ship was loaded at a foreign port. The vessel supplied plastic and members of its crew were present during aspects of the loading, but what they knew and what a competent unloading stevedore could perceive are disputed. The claim is confined to the turnover duty to warn of the cargo-stow condition, not active control during unloading or a separate duty to intervene. Apply summary-judgment standards without resolving disputed crew knowledge or confusing the perspective of the loading and unloading stevedores. Section 905(b) negligence replaced strict unseaworthiness liability for this claim; neither injury nor vessel ownership alone proves liability. [P33](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/512/92/)
