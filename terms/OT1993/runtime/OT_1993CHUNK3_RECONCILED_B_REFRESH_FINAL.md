## 30. United States v. Granderson

**Event and posture:** No. 92-1662; argued January 10, 1994; merits date March 22, 1994. Review of 969 F.2d 980, which upheld revocation, vacated twenty months' imprisonment and ordered release after more than eleven months had been served. The United States seeks restoration of that imprisonment term; Granderson seeks affirmance. The initial sentence comprised sixty months' probation and a $2,000 fine. The ordinary Guidelines imprisonment range was zero to six months; the offense's statutory maximum was five years. No lawful departure supporting imprisonment beyond six months is established in this record. No new disposition of the original fine or supervised-release component is requested or inferred. Research cutoff: immediately before March 22, 1994; uncoordinated same-day events supply no entering law.

### Historical evidence and what the refresh changes

The complete official report, 511 U.S. 39–78, records affirmance, seven to two. Ginsburg wrote for Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter. Scalia and Kennedy separately concurred in the judgment. Rehnquist dissented, joined by Thomas. These are historical facts only. The supplied comparator's citation span ending at page 75 is incomplete; the complete dissent ends at page 78.

The five historical majority Justices accepted mandatory imprisonment and, for this record, a two-month floor and six-month ceiling. Their rejection of renewed probation was a contextual construction, not a quotation of an express prison command. The report's footnote 12 distinguishes replacing revoked probation from changing an unrevoked fine. Footnote 15 expressly recognizes the shorter-sentence problem when probation followed a downward departure from a much higher range; it identifies alternative lenity explanations without choosing between them. The refreshed ordinary-range limitation therefore preserves a real qualification. It may not become a categorical declaration that the high, predeparture range always determines the floor, that probation necessarily creates a zero-to-six-month range, or that lenity is unavailable in the departure situation. A precise rule choosing between the Harrison alternatives remains outside this case.

Kennedy's historical premise was that probation is an enacted kind of sentence; the unqualified verb sentence can include it; revocation terminates the old sentence without inherently specifying the new kind; and the expressly different prison language in section 3583(g), enacted alongside the proviso, is consequential. The contrast between section 3565(a)(1) and (a)(2) supplies an ordinary structural inference, but the proviso's notwithstanding clause prevents that inference from silently becoming an invariable prison command. Kennedy takes the minimum's referent from the probation component because that is the component expressly revoked. He rejects the majority's never-imposed Guidelines sentence as the meaning of original sentence, but that objection does not entail the Government's twenty-month prison floor.

Scalia shares permission for a new probation sentence and rejects the two-month mandatory prison formula. He differs from Kennedy on the denominator: original sentence includes the actual fine as well as probation. His historical concurrence acknowledges that this construction requires comparing unlike sanctions. The refreshed six-month prison ceiling alone did not answer whether his own whole-sentence minimum remained unsatisfied. A final reconciliation cannot silently drop that additional question while attributing the whole-sentence reading to him.

### Shared law, divergent remedy and exact reservations

All eight reject Nevada's commencement-date rule. Griffith applies the governing criminal-procedure rule to a case still on direct review; actual Harper confirms equal application while keeping genuine remedy questions distinct. This is direct review, not a new collateral-review Teague inquiry. Even assuming McLaughlin announced a new rule, Powell's nonfinality brings him within Griffith.

McLaughlin's complete rule remains operative: review within forty-eight hours can still be unreasonably delayed to gather evidence justifying arrest, from ill will, or for delay's own sake. Beyond forty-eight hours the government must show a bona fide emergency or other extraordinary circumstance; an intervening weekend or routine consolidation is insufficient. The four-day interval is presumptively unreasonable under that rule. A neutral probable-cause determination need not coincide with a personal first appearance; the police declaration is not such a determination and does not restart the clock. No Justice creates a different timing deadline here.

The seven's vacatur leaves open the legally available remedy for the delay, including whether the second statement is a causal product, any consequential attenuation question, appropriate preservation consequences, the separately asserted good-faith defense and harmlessness. They do not announce a general rule that every such question must always be remanded. They decide that this judgment should be reconsidered without Nevada's erroneous nonretroactivity premise. Nevada's state-law arraignment waiver does not itself settle waiver of federal prompt-review protection. Admission of a shorter prearrest statement does not alone establish harmlessness under the governing federal standard.

Krull is distinct from causation, attenuation, waiver and harmlessness. Objective reliance on a statute later held unconstitutional is subject to the limits already stated in the neutral refresh: the legislature must not have wholly abandoned its constitutional responsibility; a reasonable officer must not have been bound to recognize the defect; and the conduct must fall within the statutory authorization on which reliance is asserted. If an arrested person is not brought before a magistrate within seventy-two hours, excluding nonjudicial days, Nevada's quoted provision requires the magistrate to give the prosecutor an opportunity to explain the delay and permits the magistrate to release the person upon determining that the person was not brought before a magistrate without unnecessary delay. It is not an unqualified command authorizing every postponement of judicial probable-cause review. The reported defense remains available for lawful consideration, not established on this record by the occurrence of a later McLaughlin decision. No final reconciled row needs a merits ruling on that defense.
