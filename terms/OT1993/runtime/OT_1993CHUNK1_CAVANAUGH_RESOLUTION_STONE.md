#### C. ENTERING LAW BY ISSUE

##### Issue 1: Prospective channel and retroactive punishment

**Entering Law:** U.S. Const. art. I, §10; Weaver v. Graham, 450 U.S. 24 (1981), evaluates retrospective laws disadvantaging an offender by increasing punishment; Miller v. Florida, 482 U.S. 423 (1987), applies that protection to changed sentencing rules. Collins v. Youngblood, 497 U.S. 37 (1990), confines the Clause to its punishment-related categories rather than all procedural disadvantage. Dobbert v. Florida, 432 U.S. 282 (1977), recognizes that genuinely procedural changes are not necessarily forbidden. The substance of the effect, not a statutory label or a vested-right theory, matters. Morales (1995), Garner (2000), and later formulations are not entering law. #### D. STONE’S REVISED REASONING, APPLICATION, AND LIMITS

**Stone’s Position and Scope:** affirm the Fourth Circuit's reversal of summary judgment on the prospective §1983 claim; strengthen and confine the ex post facto rationale before directing annual review.

Success restores a discretionary hearing, not necessary release. *Preiser* therefore leaves a substantial basis for §1983, while *Patsy* rejects a general exhaustion requirement. This is a defensible development of the existing channel distinction, not application of later *Wilkinson* doctrine. The damages dispositions are outside the appealed relief.

The merits fit Stone's opposition to retrospective increases in punishment, but the statement that losing any chance of earlier release necessarily violates the Clause is too broad after *Collins*, 497 U.S. 37. *Weaver* and *Miller* concern changes increasing punishment; *Dobbert* permits genuinely procedural alterations. Stone should identify the sentencing significance of the withdrawn opportunity: annual mandatory reconsideration, a legally available release decision at that point, replacement by a categorical two-year interval, and absence of an effective interim mechanism. [Predecision Fourth Circuit judgment, 984 F.2d 120](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/984/120/356289/).

On the supplied facts, finding an impermissible retrospective reduction of release opportunities is **a plausible, meaningful extension**, not clearly compelled law. Lower courts would examine the old and new eligibility rules, actual decision authority, interim review, and whether the change can lengthen confinement rather than merely rearrange paperwork. The difficult case is an offender for whom the board has lawfully determined that no material change could justify release during the omitted year. Stone must distinguish that individualized scheduling from a categorical withdrawal based solely on offense class.

The cost is litigation over speculative parole prospects. Avoid requiring proof that parole would actually have been granted, which would defeat the protection; equally avoid constitutionalizing every procedural inconvenience. If the record cannot establish the absence or adequacy of interim review, remand on that point rather than invent it. Neither *Morales* nor *Garner* was available at this date.

**Egalitarian application:** The practical question is whether the new calendar removes a meaningful chance to obtain an earlier discretionary release, not whether the State still labels the prisoner eligible. The State retains lawful scheduling and safety judgments; the prisoner need not prove that a hearing would have produced parole. Protect both interests through the actual opportunity and available interim mechanisms.
