#### C. ENTERING LAW BY ISSUE

##### Issue 1: Counsel, valid fine convictions, and later imprisonment

**Entering Law:** Scott v. Illinois, 440 U.S. 367 (1979), permits an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction when no imprisonment is imposed. Baldasar v. Illinois, 446 U.S. 222 (1980), barred the particular use of such a conviction to convert a later misdemeanor into an imprisonable felony, but produced no single majority rationale. Marks v. United States, 430 U.S. 188 (1977), governs treatment of fractured reasoning. Argersinger, 407 U.S. 25 (1972); Burgett, 389 U.S. 109 (1967); and Tucker, 404 U.S. 443 (1972), frame counsel and sentencing safeguards. McMillan, 477 U.S. 79 (1986), recognizes different sentencing proof requirements. [R31](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/738/)

#### D. STONE’S REVISED REASONING, APPLICATION, AND LIMITS

**Stone’s Position and Scope:** retain Stone's proposed protection against the automatic Guidelines increment, but identify it as an extension and resolve waiver before relief.

*Scott* permits the original uncounseled fine-only conviction. *Baldasar* barred a particular later use but had no single controlling rationale. Extending its protection from misdemeanor-to-felony conversion to a mandatory Guidelines increment is **a meaningful doctrinal choice**, not an obvious *Marks* extraction. [Conviction, ranges, and competing reasoning, 511 U.S. 738](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/738/).

Stone's counsel and actual-operation commitments support looking at the extra point's legal effect: the range rose from 168–210 to 188–235 months, and the imposed 235 months exceeded the old ceiling. The statutory maximum remained life; do not misstate this as an increased statutory maximum or rely on later *Apprendi/Booker*. His proposed line preserves *Scott*'s conviction while limiting its automatic use to authorize more confinement.

The strongest counterargument is that recidivism concerns the new offense, the earlier conviction is valid, and sentencing traditionally uses conduct proved with fewer safeguards than a trial. Stone should answer that automatic weight assigned to an uncounseled adjudication differs from independent, reliable proof of past conduct in a proceeding with present counsel. But this is a vulnerable boundary: allowing the conviction itself to be relabeled evidence of the same conduct would erase the protection.

For administration, exclude the automatic conviction point absent counsel or valid waiver; any independent conduct-based sentencing route must be separately authorized and supported, with an actual opportunity to contest underlying conduct. Do not invent a categorical exclusion of all past facts or a new proof standard. This narrower rule is more workable than invalidating every later consideration of the episode, though it may produce different treatment of convictions and conduct.

The government's preserved challenge to the waiver determination under *Zerbst/Parke* is material. Remand to resolve it under the correct burden before ordering removal of the point. *Custis* concerns attacks on predicate validity, not the permitted use of a valid fine conviction. No automatic release, vacatur of the DUI, or promised final sentence follows.

**Egalitarian application:** Examine the added confinement produced by automatic use of the uncounseled conviction while respecting the earlier lawful fine and the State's interest in reliable sentencing history. Counsel or valid waiver, independent proof of conduct, and the legal effect of the increment supply the distinction. Neither a conviction label nor a general appeal to recidivism answers the particular use question.

#### E. RECORD AND SOURCE SUPPORT

**Independent Record and Threshold Context:** Nichols’s 1983 DUI conviction resulted in a $250 fine and no incarceration. The federal court found that it was uncounseled and that the record did not establish waiver. After his later federal drug plea, the DUI added a criminal-history point, changing the Guidelines range from 168–210 to 188–235 months. The court imposed 235 months. The statutory ceiling was life, so the disputed increase concerns the Guidelines range, not the statutory maximum. [R31](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/738/)

Nichols challenges the later use of a conviction valid under Scott, not necessarily its original validity. The government preserves an alternative contention that the district court used the wrong burden or legal standard on waiver, invoking Johnson v. Zerbst and Parke v. Raley. That contention becomes material if the Court rejects the broad enhancement rule. The separate asserted duty to warn of future enhancement is not necessary if the Sixth Amendment ground suffices. [R31](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/738/)
