#### B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

**Stone’s Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Reverse the Fifth Circuit’s enforcement judgment insofar as disclosure was ordered through the Privacy Act’s FOIA exception.

**Stone’s Remedy and Remand Position:** Set aside that statutory basis for compelled disclosure. Reserve the routine-use exception and any other independently authorized route, including consent; do not order a categorical prohibition against union communication or disclosure under a different valid exception. [R11](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/510/487/)

#### C. ENTERING LAW BY ISSUE

##### Issue 1: Interlocking disclosure statutes

**Entering Law:** Section 7114(b)(4) requires necessary bargaining information only to the extent not prohibited by law. The Privacy Act, §552a(b), generally bars disclosure without consent, with a FOIA-required-disclosure exception in subsection (b)(2). FOIA Exemption 6, §552(b)(6), protects personnel and similar files against clearly unwarranted privacy invasions. Department of Justice v. Reporters Committee, 489 U.S. 749 (1989), identifies the relevant FOIA public interest as illumination of government operations and does not vary access by a requester’s identity. [R11](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/510/487/)

#### D. STONE’S REVISED REASONING, APPLICATION, AND LIMITS

**Stone’s Position and Scope:** retain rejection of disclosure through the FOIA exception, without foreclosing other statutory routes.

Section 7114(b)(4)'s “not prohibited by law” condition makes the Privacy Act's coverage material. The FOIA exception incorporates FOIA's public-interest inquiry, including *Reporters Committee*'s focus on government operations rather than the requester's special purpose. Union bargaining utility cannot silently substitute for that inquiry. This is **application of a strong statutory precedent**, even though the linked statutes leave genuine competing arguments. [Record and provisions, 510 U.S. 487](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/510/487/).

There is no inconsistency between Stone's labor protections and employees' interest in controlling home-address disclosure. Simulated *Ray* requires a concrete privacy/transparency balance; it does not make confidentiality promises conclusive. Names and work locations already supplied reduce the incremental government-transparency value of home addresses, while unsolicited contact is a cognizable privacy consequence without proof of actual harassment.

The strongest opposing argument is that the labor statute independently makes bargaining information necessary and a universal FOIA balance frustrates exclusive representation. Stone should answer through Congress's proviso and distinct exceptions, not by downgrading collective bargaining as an interest. If he wished to alter *Reporters Committee*'s requester-neutral rule for interlocking statutes, that would require candid statutory-precedent analysis; it is unnecessary to decide this request.

Application should identify what the particular information reveals about government and what disclosure exposes. A record linking addresses to governmental wrongdoing may present a different transparency interest; availability in scattered public sources need not erase privacy in compiled disclosure. Consent and the Privacy Act's routine-use exception remain distinct possible channels, not guaranteed alternatives. Reverse the stated basis for disclosure and leave those channels to properly presented proceedings.

#### E. RECORD AND SOURCE SUPPORT

**Independent Record and Threshold Context:** The unions requested names and home addresses of employees in their bargaining units. The agencies supplied names and work stations, but withheld home addresses. The Authority ordered release. The Fifth Circuit balanced collective-bargaining interests against employee privacy, reasoning that a labor-statute request need not use FOIA’s ordinary public-interest boundary. [R11](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/510/487/)

The reviewed agency orders present a concrete statutory dispute, not a freestanding constitutional privacy claim. Only the FOIA-based route is presented for disposition; the separate Privacy Act routine-use exception, §552a(b)(3), is not resolved by this packet. No factual finding of actual unwanted visits or harassment is required to identify the asserted privacy interest. [R11](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/510/487/)
