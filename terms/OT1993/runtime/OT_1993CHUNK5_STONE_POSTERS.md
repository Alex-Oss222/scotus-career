#### C. ENTERING LAW BY ISSUE

##### Issue 1: Item definition, culpability, and notice

**Entering Law:** Former §857(a)(1) prohibits use of postal or other interstate conveyance in a scheme to sell drug paraphernalia. Section 857(d) defines items by primary intended use or design for illicit controlled-substance use. Section 857(e) supplies nonexclusive considerations: instructions, descriptive material, advertising, display, legitimate related business, sales ratios, lawful community uses, and expert testimony. These are considerations, not eight mandatory elements. Section 857(f) preserves authorized manufacture, possession, or distribution and normal lawful trade in items traditionally intended for tobacco use. Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246 (1952), and United States v. United States Gypsum Co., 438 U.S. 422 (1978), support a culpable-knowledge reading absent a convincing strict-liability command. Hoffman Estates v. Flipside, 455 U.S. 489 (1982), distinguishes manufacturer design from retail marketing and assesses clarity in the actual application. [P26](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/513/)

#### D. STONE’S REVISED REASONING, APPLICATION, AND LIMITS

**Stone’s Position and Scope:** retain affirmance on the presented knowledge and as-applied vagueness questions.

Former §857's object definition and the seller's culpability do different work. Objective design and primary likely use can identify paraphernalia, while conviction requires the defendant's knowledge that the items are likely to be used with illegal drugs. Neither knowledge of the legal classification nor proof of a particular customer's planned use is necessary. [Offense-date statute and record, 511 U.S. 513](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/513/).

This is **a source-grounded statutory clarification** consistent with *Morissette*, *Gypsum*, and *Hoffman Estates*. It need not conflict with Stone's *Staples* proposal: both require knowledge of materially incriminating facts; neither automatically requires knowledge of legal prohibition. The marketing and inventory here place the business within the statute's core as applied.

The critical administrability limit is dual use. Consider design, advertising, instructions, display, sales patterns, lawful uses, and other statutory evidence without converting the nonexclusive list into eight elements. An ordinary scale does not become paraphernalia because one buyer uses it unlawfully; a contrived innocent explanation does not erase a bong's design or a seller's targeted drug marketing. The prosecution must establish the accused's knowledge rather than substitute what a reasonable retailer should have known.

The strongest objection is that the “likely use” account creates uncertainty for general retailers and risks guilt by store identity. Stone should preserve the statutory exemptions, demand object- and context-specific proof, and reserve genuinely marginal applications. A head-shop label is evidence of context, not an offense element or a substitute for knowledge.

Affirm only the preserved questions under the charged historical statute. Do not silently substitute §863, revisit separate laundering or drug counts, or use hypothetical retailers to decide an unpresented facial overbreadth claim.

**Egalitarian application:** Protect lawful retailers against guilt by association while permitting proof against knowing sellers of drug paraphernalia. Design, marketing, likely use, and the seller's actual knowledge must do separate work. The same standard must apply whether the defendant runs an unpopular shop or an ordinary commercial business.

#### E. RECORD AND SOURCE SUPPORT

**Independent Record and Threshold Context:** The business sold bongs, roach clips, pipes, scales, diluents, and other merchandise through a head-shop operation and interstate ordering. Acty also faced separate drug, laundering, and proceeds counts; those convictions are not independently reopened by the narrowed questions. The classification of an item by its design or likely use is distinct from the seller’s knowledge. Dual-use merchandise cannot be classified solely by a hypothetical illicit use. Use the offense-date version, former §857, rather than treating its 1990 replacement, §863, as the charge. The vagueness challenge is as applied to this business and record. No First Amendment overbreadth challenge permits hypothetical customers or ordinary retailers to substitute for these petitioners. [P26](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/513/)
