## Necessary premises and separate components

Pacific Mutual's March 1991 action was dismissed with prejudice in August 1991 after Lampf and Beam; it did not appeal. The judgment was final and unappealable before December 19, 1991 enactment. The January 31, 1992 reinstatement motion was within sixty days. The Fifth Circuit reversed denial of reinstatement and remanded under §27A(b). Simmons/TGX's without-prejudice, relation-back and waiver facts are not this case's facts.

Section 27A(b) requires a private implied §10(b) action commenced on or before June 19, 1991, dismissed as time-barred after that date, and timely under the jurisdiction's law including retroactivity principles as that law existed on June 19, together with a plaintiff's motion within sixty days of enactment. Subsection (a)'s treatment of pending actions is distinct. The text supplies explicit temporal direction. No model participant uses a default anti-retroactivity presumption to defeat that direction. The dispute instead concerns constitutional power over this final private judgment.

Three legal components remain distinct: Congress's power to change substantive or limitation law rather than prescribe a decision under unchanged law; Article III's protection of completed adjudication; and due process respecting judgment rights and a repose defense. A legislature can change applicable law without necessarily possessing power to reopen final judgments. Conversely, describing a judgment as final identifies the constitutional question rather than mechanically answering it under a later precedent.

Robertson authorizes the identified replacement of substantive timber rules in pending litigation and expressly reserves final-judgment reopening. Harper reaches still-open direct review and preserves final judgments, but does not itself decide Congress's power. Landgraf and Rivers respect explicit temporal commands within constitutional limits and reserve this constitutional issue. The present case differs from Kokkonen's unincorporated private settlement: §27A is an express statutory reinstatement mechanism, so the missing-ancillary-jurisdiction ground cannot be copied into this dispute. Central Bank's preserved culpable-assistance claim supplies neither a reopening power nor a finding that Pacific Mutual can prove liability.

## Enhanced legal-path comparison

**Final-private-judgment path:** A dismissal with prejudice that has become final without appeal is a completed exercise of judicial power even when it rests on limitations rather than a finding about fraud. Congress may change the law governing pending litigation, but requiring a court to reopen this concluded case withdraws the conclusiveness of its judgment. Leaving the underlying securities issues for judicial determination does not answer that objection: the judicial act already completed is the dismissal itself.

This path reads McCullough's protection of completed adjudication and Klein's protection of the judicial function as limits on the mandatory reopening command. Robertson's changed-law rule addresses pending litigation; Harper's direct-review rule does not reach ended cases. Wheeling Bridge concerns continuing prospective relief, and Sioux Nation concerns the Government's own defense. Neither establishes power to withdraw the effect of this private final judgment. A general class and an express temporal command do not remove the constitutional objection. These Justices would reverse the Fifth Circuit and require denial of Pacific Mutual's §27A(b) reinstatement motion, without deciding §27A(a), due process or the securities merits.

**Permissible-restoration path:** This path holds that finality protects judicial independence without barring every statutory remedy affecting a completed proceeding. Robertson v. Seattle Audubon Society permits Congress to change governing law while courts determine its application; it leaves this final-judgment question open. United States v. Klein bars legislative prescription of judicial results, not every enacted change in the law governing access to adjudication.

Section 27A(b) supplies a new rule for a defined class of limitations dismissals following Lampf. Congress identifies the filing period, the intervening time-bar dismissal, the former limitations law and a short period for seeking relief. Courts determine those predicates. Congress has found no fraud, reliance, injury or damages and has prescribed no defendant's liability. Once the predicates are established, reinstatement is mandatory. Its validity does not depend on judicial discretion to withhold relief.

The defendants' strongest objection is that Pacific Mutual's unappealed dismissal was already final. That fact is accepted. The distinction is between creating a bounded legal entitlement to further adjudication and performing the adjudication itself. McCullough v. Virginia protects completed judicial determinations; the present statute supplies no contrary factual finding or legislatively fixed liability. The holding concerns a limitations dismissal, not compelled reopening of a judgment adjudicating the underlying securities merits. Wheeling Bridge's treatment of prospective relief and Sioux Nation's treatment of the Government's own defense are distinct settings, not controlling answers to this case.

Landgraf and Rivers distinguish an express temporal command from an inference about statutory reach; express reach does not itself settle constitutionality. Harper's rule for still-open direct review and Beam's protection of finality against later judicial doctrine do not establish that Congress can never enact this confined remedy. Chase Securities concerns due-process revival and does not independently resolve Article III. The courts retain the essential work of adjudication under the new law.
