#### C. ENTERING LAW BY ISSUE

##### Issue 1: Contribution following settlement

**Entering Law:** Reliable Transfer, 421 U.S. 397 (1975), establishes comparative fault; Edmonds, 443 U.S. 256 (1979), does not itself settle the effect of settlements. #### D. STONE’S REVISED REASONING, APPLICATION, AND LIMITS

**Stone’s Position and Scope:** retain vacatur and remand as the companion to Stone's proposed proportionate-share rule, with the dependency explicit.

A nonsettler whose liability is already reduced by a settler's equitable share generally has no excess share to recover through contribution. That consequence makes the settlement-credit framework coherent; it is not freestanding immunity awarded merely because a defendant settled. [Record, 511 U.S. 222](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/222/).

This is **application of the rule Stone recommends in McDermott**, not presently application of an adopted same-day simulated precedent. The review can assess the two recommendations together without waiting for a vote. An actual disposition later entered under a different credit rule would change the analysis: pro tanto credit can leave a nonsettler bearing more than its share, making contribution a genuine problem rather than duplication.

Stone's remedial discipline requires preserving that coupling. Lower courts must reduce the plaintiff's recoverable claim by the settling actor's adjudicated responsibility and allow meaningful litigation of that responsibility. They cannot both withhold that protection and dismiss contribution by invoking settlement finality. The missing percentages in this record are reasons to remand, not opportunities to invent an allocation.

The strongest objection is that contribution protects against cheap, strategic settlements. The proportionate reduction places that settlement risk on the plaintiff, supplying the nonsettler's protection without reopening the bargain. A distinct contractual indemnity promise presents a different obligation and survives for separate source analysis. Preserve limitation-of-liability, exoneration, damages, and settlement-validity questions not resolved here. Stone's position is sound only if its operative instructions implement the same allocation method on both sides of the case.

#### C. ENTERING LAW BY ISSUE

##### Issue 1: Federal gift transfer and state disclaimer fiction

**Entering Law:** 26 U.S.C. §§2501(a)(1) and 2511(a) broadly reach gratuitous property transfers. State-law relation back does not itself control federal gift taxation. Revenue Act of 1932 §501(b) protects transfers made on or before enactment; it does not automatically exempt every later disposition of an earlier-created interest. [P19](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/224/)

#### D. STONE’S REVISED REASONING, APPLICATION, AND LIMITS

**Stone’s Position and Scope:** retain reversal of the refund judgment.

State law determines the property interest and effective disclaimer; federal law determines whether the 1979 disposition is a taxable gift. The trust's 1917 origin does not make every later gratuitous transfer pre-1932. *Jewett*, 455 U.S. 305, also makes first knowledge, rather than eventual possession, central to the applicable reasonable-time disclaimer inquiry. [Dates and tax provisions, 511 U.S. 224](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/224/).

This fits Stone's source separation and protection of concrete reliance: the correct reliance question concerns the legal consequences of the later act, not whether an old asset can never be subject to later tax law. It is **application of existing federal tax principles**, not an exception to his presumption against retroactive liability.

The proposed alternative reasoning is lawful if each route is independently sufficient. If the regulation applies, decades after actual knowledge is not a reasonable-time disclaimer on these facts. If the regulation does not reach the pre-Act interest, the estate still needs a basis for removing the later transfer from §§2501 and 2511; state relation back alone is not that basis. Explain why the broad statutory transfer language reaches the value directed to the children, rather than treating regulatory inapplicability itself as proof of taxation.

The counterargument is that the state disclaimer means Irvine never owned the transferred interest. Federal taxation need not adopt that relation-back characterization of a real disposition. A prompt refusal before acceptance presents a different case; Stone should not announce an arbitrary deadline or tax every disclaimer categorically. Reverse the asserted refund ground while leaving Minnesota property law intact. Use the applicable historical regulation, not later qualified-disclaimer provisions.

No factual basis makes the forty-seven-year-plus delay a prompt disclaimer following first knowledge. State law identifies the property interest and effectiveness of the disclaimer; federal law decides the tax consequence. Separate the date the interest was created from the date the challenged gratuitous transfer occurred. The pre-1977 interest governs which regulatory version is relevant; the later statutory qualified-disclaimer provisions are not silently substituted. [P19](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/224/)

#### D. STONE’S REVISED REASONING, APPLICATION, AND LIMITS

**Stone’s Position and Scope:** retain prospective treatment of §102 and candidly adopt the organizing temporal framework.

Simulated *Harper* concerns judicial rules, not automatic reach of statutory amendments.

Stone's Master §16 denies comparable reliance weight to expectations of continued exclusion. That does not justify unforeseen forms or magnitudes of backward-looking liability. He should expressly distinguish reliance on permission to discriminate from legal notice about the consequences of conduct already prohibited. Application must be provision-specific. A procedural rule governing the next hearing can apply without reopening a completed trial; a new damages cause or penalty requires separate temporal analysis. An effective date alone does not answer which past conduct is covered. The difficult boundary is a new remedy for an existing violation: label alone cannot decide whether it adds legal exposure.

The cost is leaving some proved violations without the new compensation Congress considered desirable. Congress could supply sufficiently clear retrospective direction within constitutional bounds; the court should not infer it from remedial purpose. Affirm this judgment without holding the harassment lawful or all civil retroactivity unconstitutional.

**User-directed case emphasis:** Retain the user's nonretroactivity disposition as an administrable limit on remedial ambition.
