### Rule, alternative paths, and historical check

All eight frozen commitments to proportionate-share credit and limited reversal/remand survive reconciliation. General maritime law supplies authority to choose the settlement rule; no governing maritime enactment in this record supplies a contrary credit method. Reliable Transfer establishes responsibility-based allocation where it can reasonably be made. The new settlement rule is a choice in that field, not a claim that Reliable Transfer had already settled this question.

Under the supported rule, settlement reduces the injured party's claim against nonsettlers by the settling tortfeasors' equitable share of the obligation, rather than by the settlement dollars. Plaintiff and settler bear their bargain's risk. A nonsettler cannot seek a favorable dollar credit from an unusually high settlement and cannot be made to bear the plaintiff's loss from a meager voluntary settlement. No contribution against the settling tortfeasor is needed or permitted for the settlement shortfall under this proportionate-share arrangement; this does not dispose of the separate Boca Grande proceeding without its own posture and assembly.

The frozen alternative analysis remains sound. Dollar credit with contribution subjects the settler to later contribution and potentially indemnity litigation, weakening settlement peace. Dollar credit with a contribution bar can make the nonsettler pay more or less than its share; good-faith review cannot reliably cure that mismatch without duplicating much of the trial and still accounting imperfectly for trial risk. Neither settlement incentives nor judicial economy clearly defeats the proportionate method. These policy comparisons explain the rule; they do not create a mandatory three-factor test for lower courts or authorize choosing different credit methods case by case.

The one-satisfaction objection does not require reduction solely because settlement plus judgment could exceed damages allocated after trial. A settlement buys certainty and finality; its price is not an adjudication. Here $1 million plus $798,000 is $1.798 million, less than $2.1 million; the alleged overcompensation depends on treating the combined 30% as McDermott's own fault, which the jury did not separately find. The rule does not depend on proving undercompensation in this case. Conversely, the plaintiff can recover less than full loss after a low settlement. This is neither a guarantee of full compensation nor approval of double recovery under every statute or tort regime.

Edmonds remains binding in its statutory, nonsettlement setting. Joint-and-several liability may place an externally caused collection shortfall, such as insolvency, on other defendants. A voluntary settlement is different. Neither Reliable Transfer nor this settlement rule generally abolishes joint-and-several liability. AmClyde's contract ruling is left intact without converting every contractual limitation into a universal legal settlement category; the final opinion need not adopt the comparator's “quasi settlement” characterization to honor the unreviewed ruling and relief requested.
