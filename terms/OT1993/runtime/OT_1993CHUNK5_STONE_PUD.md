#### B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

**Stone’s Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Affirm the Washington Supreme Court’s judgment sustaining the challenged minimum-flow certification condition.

**Stone’s Remedy and Remand Position:** Leave the condition operative in the certification process. Do not issue a federal license, adjudicate water ownership, prescribe an independent judicial flow level, or resolve a speculative conflict with later federal licensing action. #### C. ENTERING LAW BY ISSUE

##### Issue 1: Certification scope and designated uses

**Entering Law:** 33 U.S.C. §§1341(a), 1341(d), 1313, and 1370 distinguish the discharge trigger from limitations assuring the applicant’s compliance. Section 303 water-quality standards include designated uses and criteria. Event-date 40 C.F.R. §§121.2(a)(3), 131.12 support activity compliance and antidegradation. Chevron, 467 U.S. 837 (1984), remains inherited interpretive law. [R30](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/700/)

##### Issue 2: Water allocation and federal licensing

**Entering Law:** CWA §§101(g), 510(2), 33 U.S.C. §§1251(g), 1370(2), preserve state water allocation. First Iowa Hydro-Electric Cooperative v. FPC, 328 U.S. 152 (1946), and California v. FERC, 495 U.S. 490 (1990), govern federal licensing conflicts; their operation must be reconciled with Congress’s express §401 certification requirement. [R30](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/700/)

#### D. STONE’S REVISED REASONING, APPLICATION, AND LIMITS

**Stone’s Position and Scope:** retain the minimum-flow condition within a carefully stated statutory nexus.

Section 401(a)'s discharge trigger is conceded. Section 401(d)'s applicant-compliance language and incorporated §303 standards can reach a condition protecting an applicable designated use from the project's effects. Fish habitat can be lost through insufficient water quantity, so a quality/quantity distinction cannot alone defeat the condition. [Project and statutory structure, 511 U.S. 700](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/700/).

This is **a substantial but text-supported clarification of certification scope**, consistent with Stone's environmental federalism. Simulated *Arkansas v. Oklahoma* supports statutory use of water-quality standards, not unlimited state project conditions. The State's highest court controls state-law authority; the federal court still must decide what §401 permits.

The strongest counterargument is that project-wide conditions convert a limited discharge certification into a state veto over federal hydropower licensing. Stone should answer with §401(d)'s language and a real limitation: the condition must assure compliance with an applicable water-quality requirement or another requirement within the provision's lawful scope. Generic fishery improvement, unrelated land policy, or a preferred energy mix would not suffice merely because a discharge occurs somewhere in the project.

Application asks which designated use is legally protected, how diversion threatens it, what technical evidence supports the required flow, and whether the condition serves protection rather than an unsupported enhancement objective. The supplied controlling judgment treats this condition as preservation; the Supreme Court should not substitute its own optimal flow calculation.

Water-allocation savings provisions and *First Iowa/California v. FERC* require reconciliation, not erasure. No issued federal license conflicts here, but absence of conflict alone cannot create state authority; §401 supplies it. Affirm without granting a federal license, deciding water ownership, or asserting that every later state condition prevails over federal licensing.

#### E. RECORD AND SOURCE SUPPORT

**Independent Record and Threshold Context:** The proposed Elkhorn project would divert water around a 1.2-mile reach of the Dosewallips River. Washington classified the river as Class AA and protected salmonid uses through approved water-quality standards and an antidegradation policy. Applicants proposed residual flows of 65–155 cubic feet per second, seasonally; the certification required 100–200. The administrative appeals board characterized the condition as fishery enhancement, but the superior court found protection and preservation, and the state supreme court sustained the condition. FERC had not acted on the license application. [R30](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/700/)

Applicants concede that construction and tailrace discharges trigger §401 certification. The dispute is the permitted scope of conditions, not whether any discharge exists. State-law authority is determined by the State’s highest court; the federal issue is whether §401 recognizes this condition. No actual conflict with an issued FERC license is present. [R30](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/700/)
