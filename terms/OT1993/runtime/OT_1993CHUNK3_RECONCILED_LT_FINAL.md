### Reconciled propositions and exact source distinction

All eight reject an absolute outside-source prerequisite. All eight also reject the converse proposition that any outside origin necessarily establishes disqualification. Proper judicial experience and ordinary adverse rulings, criticisms, impatience, and courtroom management do not alone establish partiality. The setting and the complete supplied conduct must be considered; neither its judicial origin nor a party's description disposes of the inquiry.

O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas, and Ginsburg retain the judicial-source ordinary rule with an objective exception: opinions formed from current or prior proceedings do not warrant recusal unless they reveal deep favoritism or antagonism making fair judgment appear impossible. This is an objective appearance inquiry, not a requirement to prove the judge's actual mental state. It permits judicial-source disqualification and does not immunize courtroom misconduct. The heightened description addresses judicially generated attitudes; it must not be extended into a universal requirement for outside-source appearance claims. An outside opinion remains insufficient by origin alone, and the general reasonable-appearance command still governs whether it reveals improper partiality.

Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy, and Souter retain the same reasonable, informed-observer appearance standard regardless of source. Judicial origin remains useful context explaining why ordinary judging usually supplies no reasonable doubt; it is not an additional exceptional-intensity element. Their position is demanding in the sense that suspicion must be objectively reasonable and arise from identifiable circumstances. It is not a rule granting recusal whenever a litigant is offended. They reject the further requirement that fair adjudication must appear impossible, and would require disqualification where a detached observer reasonably concludes that a fair and impartial hearing is unlikely. Their independent §455(a) analysis also rejects mechanically importing every limitation of §455(b) into the general appearance clause.

The complete historical report confirms this real statutory disagreement, including the outside-source qualification. It does not justify collapsing the two formulations into one test. The majority's acknowledgement that subjective bias is unnecessary, 510 U.S. 553–554 n.2, answers a possible misdescription of its position; it does not remove the concurrence's objection that the impossibility formulation narrows the statutory appearance protection. The concurrence's acceptance of ordinary judicial experience, 510 U.S. 561–562, likewise does not make it a join in the majority's heightened rule. No general kinship holding or new rule under every §455 subsection is imported from the historical discussion.
