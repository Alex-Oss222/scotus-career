#### C. ENTERING LAW BY ISSUE

##### Issue 1: FELA injury and the common-law boundary

**Entering Law:** FELA, 45 U.S.C. §§51-60, imposes liability for employee injury resulting in whole or in part from rail-employer negligence. Sections 53-55 alter contributory negligence, assumption of risk, and contractual avoidance. Rogers v. Missouri Pacific Railroad, 352 U.S. 500 (1957), defines the statute’s causal contribution standard; it does not remove the need for duty and breach. Urie v. Thompson, 337 U.S. 163 (1949), applies FELA to occupational disease; Ellis v. Union Pacific Railroad, 329 U.S. 649 (1947), rejects insurer liability. Atchison v. Buell, 480 U.S. 557 (1987), leaves the precise emotional-injury question open. Common-law approaches include contemporaneous physical impact, exposure to immediate physical danger, and qualified close-relative bystander recovery. They are alternative boundaries, not cumulative elements. Monessen Southwestern Railway v. Morgan, 486 U.S. 330 (1988), assigns weight to common-law background without making every modern tort development automatically FELA law. [P38](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/512/532/)

##### Issue 2: Separate application to trial access and the verdict

**Entering Law:** Summary judgment requires sufficient evidence for a lawful factfinder without judicial resolution of material credibility conflicts. Review of the verdict respects supported findings while independently testing the legal duty. The employee carries the burden of establishing the claim; neither medical labels nor a severe outcome proves negligence by itself. [P38](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/512/532/)

#### D. STONE’S REVISED REASONING, APPLICATION, AND LIMITS

**Stone’s Position and Scope:** retain the broader FELA injury rule and the differentiated affirmances, but strengthen the duty boundary beyond “genuine and serious.”

FELA's unqualified injury language, its alteration of restrictive common-law defenses, and Urie's treatment of occupational disease support recognizing serious psychiatric injury without contemporaneous impact. Buell left the precise issue open. Stone may therefore adopt a different boundary from the zone-of-danger alternative without overruling a governing Supreme Court holding. It is nevertheless a meaningful expansion beyond common-law restrictions, not an inevitable consequence of remedial purpose.

The statutory employment relationship limits the class of plaintiffs, but does not alone limit the range of stress-related claims or establish a duty to eliminate them. Genuine illness proves neither unreasonable working conditions nor foreseeability of that kind of injury. A workable rule requires serious injury attributable to directly experienced conditions that the employer unreasonably failed to address despite an objectively foreseeable risk of serious harm. Ordinary demands, disappointments, and personnel disputes do not become negligent because an unusually vulnerable employee suffers. Knowledge of an employee's particular vulnerability can change foreseeable precautions; it does not make the employer an insurer. Clinical evidence, contemporaneous complaints, duration, similar incidents, and available precautions are relevant rather than mandatory forms of proof.

The strongest competing rule—immediate risk of physical harm—offers a clearer boundary and has substantial common-law support. Stone should admit his standard sacrifices some predictability and increases litigation over foreseeability. His justification is that the physical-danger proxy arbitrarily excludes demonstrable occupational psychiatric disease from an injury statute already extending beyond sudden accidents. Rogers's relaxed causal contribution cannot relax duty or breach. The [historical opinions](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/512/532/) also show that Carlisle's case involved a separate foreseeability analysis, not simply the genuineness screen used in Gottshall.

Gottshall's direct emergency involvement, heat, communication failure, and alleged work demands permit a trial, not a liability finding. Carlisle's verdict rests on exceptional sustained conditions, warnings, comparable health problems, and medical causation; Stone may preserve it under ordinary negligence rather than characterize it as compensation for a stressful occupation. He should verify that the instructions and supported findings actually embody that standard, and not affirm merely because the injury was genuine. The new rule should replace any free-floating judicial authenticity screen with the ordinary negligence inquiry and meaningful legal sufficiency review.

Carlisle’s supervisory and dispatch work involved prolonged shifts, staffing and equipment problems, repeated safety complaints, and evidence that comparable conditions had caused health problems among coworkers. Medical witnesses attributed his breakdown at least in part to the work. His case reached a verdict; the existence of a stressful occupation alone was not the only evidence. Gottshall’s entitlement to trial is not a damages award, and Carlisle’s verdict cannot be discarded by treating his evidence as merely unproved allegations. #### C. ENTERING LAW BY ISSUE

##### Issue 1: Special-verdict explanation and the general no-consequences rule

**Entering Law:** The Insanity Defense Reform Act, 18 U.S.C. §§17 and 4242-4243, assigns the defendant clear-and-convincing proof of the statutory insanity defense, provides a special NGI verdict, and requires custody pending a judicial hearing within 40 days. Later hospitalization or release follows §4243’s separate standards and burdens, which differ with the offense’s character. Rogers v. United States, 422 U.S. 35 (1975), instructs non-sentencing juries to decide guilt without regard to punishment. Lyles v. United States, 254 F.2d 725 (D.C. Cir. 1957) (en banc), supports a special-verdict explanation in the D.C. commitment setting; it is persuasive circuit authority, not a nationwide Supreme Court rule. The IDRA has no express nationwide instruction command. [P39](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/512/573/)

##### Issue 2: Preserved error and remand

**Entering Law:** Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(a), Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750 (1946), United States v. Hasting, 461 U.S. 499 (1983), and Bank of Nova Scotia v. United States, 487 U.S. 250 (1988), limit reversal for nonconstitutional error affecting substantial rights. United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725, 734 (1993), distinguishes the Government’s burden concerning a preserved error from the defendant’s burden under plain-error review. Supervisory authority does not dispense with harmless-error analysis. [P39](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/512/573/)

#### D. STONE’S REVISED REASONING, APPLICATION, AND LIMITS

**Stone’s Position and Scope:** retain the limited federal-practice instruction and harmless-error remand, explicitly identifying the departure from ordinary practice.

Stone's concern with accurate adjudication supports explaining that the special insanity verdict does not produce immediate release. Sections 4242–4243 establish that consequence, but contain no express instruction requirement. Rogers ordinarily keeps punishment consequences from a nonsentencing jury. Stone should ground his qualification in supervisory authority over federal trials, informed by the statutory verdict structure and Lyles's experience, rather than claim the statute or due process already commands it.

The strongest objection is that accurate consequences still distract from whether insanity has been proved and can encourage disposition-driven verdicts. Unlike Simmons, this jury decides guilt and the defense, not which punishment to select; no reported State-created misstatement or jury question supplies an individualized rebuttal need. Thus the proposed rule is prophylactic, with no finding that Shannon's jury actually misunderstood. It can nevertheless be defended as explaining the legal nature of an unusual verdict while expressly prohibiting consequence-based decisionmaking. The distinction from ordinary punishment information must remain narrow to avoid instructions on every mandatory sentence or collateral consequence.

Upon a proper request where insanity is submitted, explain custody pending a separate judicial process, without promising indefinite hospitalization, release only after complete recovery, or a particular outcome at the hearing. The shorter request raised the issue; the longer request's overstatement need not justify refusing all accurate explanation. Section 17's clear-and-convincing burden remains intact. Stone's simulated Foucha position concerns permissible continued confinement, not what this jury must hear, and warns against an inaccurate promise of permanent custody.

Use Rule 52(a) and Kotteakos for preserved nonconstitutional error, with the Government's burden. Supervisory authority cannot evade Hasting or Bank of Nova Scotia by demanding automatic reversal. Remand because the supplied record does not establish the error's effect; a new trial follows only if harmlessness fails. No insanity acquittal or release is authorized. This is a defensible procedural innovation with acknowledged policy costs and a properly limited remedial branch.

**Egalitarian application:** The special verdict must be intelligible without turning a guilt jury into a sentencing body. Explain immediate custody and the separate judicial process accurately; protect the defendant against an unfounded fear of immediate release without promising the public permanent hospitalization. Apply the proper harmless-error rule rather than assume what these jurors believed.

#### E. RECORD AND SOURCE SUPPORT

**Independent Record and Threshold Context:** After a 1990 firearm incident in which he shot himself, Shannon survived and was prosecuted under §922(g)(1). He raised the statutory insanity defense. He requested either an instruction that an NGI verdict requires commitment or a longer instruction suggesting hospitalization until he posed no specified risk. The court gave neither, telling jurors not to consider consequences or punishment. No prosecutor or witness is reported to have told this jury that an NGI verdict would produce immediate freedom. The record supplied does not reveal the jury’s actual reasoning or establish that the insanity evidence necessarily compelled acquittal. The jury had no sentencing role. Distinguish statutory construction, federal supervisory practice, and constitutional due process. An instruction must not promise prolonged hospitalization or a particular later release decision. Shannon preserved the request, but a nonconstitutional instructional error remains subject to the ordinary direct-review harmless-error rule. [P39](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/512/573/)

#### D. STONE’S REVISED REASONING, APPLICATION, AND LIMITS

**Stone’s Position and Scope:** retain vacatur for factual development under intermediate scrutiny, but give fuller reasons for distinguishing compelled editorial publication.

The Master's protection of the press function applies to corporate cable operators. Stone's rejection of superior civic status for artificial wealth cannot diminish a genuine editorial right, and private control of an important communications route is not state action. Tornillo and the relevant Pacific Gas & Electric reasoning create serious tension with compulsory carriage, not an analogy to dismiss merely by saying “competition.”

The legitimate distinction is a carriage rule directed at a physical distribution bottleneck and preservation of independently available broadcast service, triggered without judging the stations' individual messages. Carriage of separately identified signals also need not attribute their positions to the operator. These features support O'Brien/Ward treatment without extending Red Lion's spectrum rationale to cable. But source neutrality is not automatic content neutrality: the statutory distinction between commercial and noncommercial educational stations, Congress's references to valuable programming, and actual administration deserve examination for message preference. Stone should conclude that the operative scheme addresses distribution and access rather than selecting favored ideas, not declare those concerns legally irrelevant.

That is a substantial application across media, rather than a general right to compel private publication. Lower courts should test Congress's prediction of threatened broadcast service, cable incentives and capacity, the effects of denied carriage, and the programming actually displaced. Evidence of some dropped stations does not alone prove systemic jeopardy; spare capacity does not prove absence of burden on every affected operator. Reasonable forecasts can justify preventive action without waiting for failures. Judicial review must assess whether those forecasts have support and whether burdens are materially excessive; it need not demand scientific certainty or the least restrictive imaginable alternative.

The unresolved record prevents summary judgment for the defenders. Remand with those questions, leaving final validity and proper interim relief open. Stone's Ladue reasoning about realistic alternatives reinforces attention to actual speech effects, while MCI's limit on an agency's delegation says little about Congress's enacted choice. The cost of the intermediate approach is allowing some compelled carriage despite editorial preference; its bounded transmission rationale must prevent expansion into an editorial-balance mandate for newspapers or other media.

**User-directed case emphasis:** Congress may make supported predictions about market structure and access before the feared damage is complete. Test the connection between the particular speech burden and the identified danger without demanding certainty or treating findings as conclusive. This institutional lesson does not transplant the First Amendment's scrutiny framework into equal protection.

#### C. ENTERING LAW BY ISSUE

##### Issue 1: Denominational neutrality, accommodation, and public authority

**Entering Law:** First and Fourteenth Amendments; Lemon v. Kurtzman, 403 U.S. 602 (1971); Larson v. Valente, 456 U.S. 228 (1982); Larkin v. ##### Issue 2: Educational context and available alternatives

#### D. STONE’S REVISED REASONING, APPLICATION, AND LIMITS

**Stone’s Position and Scope:** retain invalidation of this special allocation, but avoid a categorical rule against geographically homogeneous religious communities.

Stone's commitment against assigning civic authority by denomination directly supports scrutiny of Chapter 748. The statute, however, creates a public district with an elected lay board, not a religious official's veto of public action. Larkin is therefore an analogy about allocation of governmental authority, not an exact factual match. The program's secular curriculum and the children's real educational needs count in the analysis.

The key factual and legal question is whether the State selected religious community membership as the organizing basis for a special governmental unit, rather than responding under neutral criteria to a geographically concentrated educational need. The special act's design and context support the former conclusion; the mere coincidence of a village boundary and a faith community would not. One-off legislation is not inherently an establishment, and a general statute is not automatically neutral if engineered to benefit only a denomination. Larson's neutrality concern, Lemon's governing framework, and Larkin's structural rationale supply a legitimate basis to invalidate this design while retaining Amos's space for accommodation.

There is an apparent comparison with Stone's simulated Shaw: both involve demographic attention in territorial boundaries. It is not enough to say religion is different. Shaw held racial design alone did not establish that particular equal-protection deprivation within a common electorate; it preserved other sources and injuries. Here the Establishment Clause independently constrains the State's use of denomination to organize public authority. Explain that source-specific institutional injury rather than importing the rejected Shaw design theory or requiring excluded voters to prove an election loss.

A neutral local public program, including the separate-service option recognized in Wieder, can protect these children without conveying a general district on the forbidden basis. Lower courts should inspect actual eligibility, reasons, authority conveyed, and access for similarly situated communities. Do not presume religious residents incapable of public office or invalidate their village incorporation. Preserve IDEA entitlements and arrange lawful continuity of secular services. Simulated Zobrest decided no establishment merits, and Carter supplies no exception to denominational neutrality. No revision of Aguilar, Ball, or Lemon is necessary to this disposition.

**User-directed case emphasis:** The children retain equal access to secular disability services and religious residents retain equal civic standing. Neutral accommodation can serve those needs; an exceptional allocation of governmental authority keyed to one denomination requires its own constitutional justification. A legitimate access aim cannot cure a defective delegation, and religious homogeneity alone does not prove one.

#### E. RECORD AND SOURCE SUPPORT

**Independent Record and Threshold Context:** The village formed under generally available incorporation law and was inhabited by Satmar Hasidim. Most children attended private religious schools. #### D. STONE’S REVISED REASONING, APPLICATION, AND LIMITS

The legal basis is the First Amendment together with Carroll's precision requirement, Claiborne's protection of lawful advocacy and association, Yamasaki's remedial limits, Ward's distinction between content-neutral and content-based burdens, and Frisby's specific residential protection. The proposed standard asks whether each restraint burdens no more speech than necessary to remedy the established conduct and serve the corresponding significant interest. This is an express development of injunction doctrine, not an inherited holding or a general rule that all injunctions receive strict scrutiny. Content- or viewpoint-based discrimination remains subject to its own more demanding rules.

The review-scope objection must be answered rather than ignored. The supplied questions support a narrower approach, as the [contemporaneous scope objection](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/93-880.ZX.html) explains. **Post-refusal rule.** Outside the fixed access zone, an enjoined person may make a peaceful initial approach, offer literature, or request conversation. Words or clear conduct communicating that further individualized contact is unwanted—such as “no,” “stop,” or “leave me alone”—trigger protection once a reasonable person would understand the refusal and the enjoined speaker has notice. Silence, disagreement with the viewpoint, or mere entry into the vicinity is not an automatic refusal. After a clear refusal, that speaker must cease following, shadowing, accompanying, repeated reapproaches, surrounding, or directing individualized speech at close range, including coordinated substitution of another participant to continue the same pursuit, and maintain at least eight feet of separation while the person proceeds toward or away from the facility within the defined clinic vicinity.

General expression remains protected: lawful stationary signs, speech to the public, offers to willing listeners, and communication from outside the separation remain available. Refusal terminates the particular unwanted encounter, not all exposure to the viewpoint. The order must not penalize an unavoidable passing encounter, a patient voluntarily approaching a stationary protester, or momentary inability to separate safely. It should require the enjoined person to stop targeted pursuit and take reasonable safe steps to comply once able, rather than permit the listener to force the speaker from an otherwise lawful stationary location. Renewed voluntary engagement can reopen communication, but the speaker may not solicit that permission through repeated unwanted approaches.

Eight feet is Stone's proposed practical separation rule, not a constitutional distance derived from text or already established by 1994 precedent. Its justification is to make termination of a close encounter observable and enforceable after conduct that defeated earlier protection. The counterargument is serious: moving personal zones can burden leafleting, be hard to measure on narrow sidewalks, and turn aversion to speech into suppression. The refusal trigger, limited parties, finite route-related geography, stationary-speech protection, and treatment of unavoidable proximity address those concerns, but do not prove the number's necessity by assertion. Remand for the state court to specify an ascertainable clinic-vicinity boundary, implement the eight-foot rule with record-supported findings on space and necessity, and consider concrete less burdensome means. If eight feet cannot be justified on that record, require a supported narrower implementation instead of imposing an unsupported numerical floor. Stone adopts the post-refusal protection and proposes this operational measure; the Supreme Court should not pretend it has found unprovided site facts. Later floating-buffer or consent-zone cases supply no entering authority.

Touching, grabbing, intentional obstruction, threats, vehicle interference, surrounding, and coordinated physical intimidation may be prohibited independently of refusal when the record and governing law support them. The rule binds identified defendants and persons legally shown to act in concert with notice, not every person sharing their views. No direct private-liability right follows simply from simulated Casey, and Bray's preservation of independent state relief does not dilute the First Amendment. Contempt requires notice and the process appropriate to the actual sanction; Bagwell remains a separate question.

This is egalitarian protection in application: patients and companions retain usable access and a way to end persistent pursuit; protesters retain an initial peaceful offer and public advocacy; clinicians retain an environment fit for treatment; and enforcement rests on observable conduct rather than ideological identity. It does not assign a general right to avoid offensive ideas in public or an unlimited right to pursue an unwilling listener. The cost and uncertainty of the new personal-separation remedy remain explicit, with geographic precision and lawful implementation essential to its defensibility.
