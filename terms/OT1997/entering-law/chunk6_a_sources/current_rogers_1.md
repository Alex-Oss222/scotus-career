### Rogers — January 14, 1998

[Public Projection](../records/rogers_v_united_states_dig_1998-01-14.md#public-projection). Thomas's Part II, joined by Stevens, Ginsburg, and Breyer, reads defined-firearm knowledge as submitting silencer-characteristic knowledge and the later disclaimer as addressing legal knowledge. On that vehicle reading, the requested formulation's possible legal-classification demand does not establish an omitted factual premise. The writing does not find that only legal knowledge was requested, approve every instruction, or make admission cure error, and it is not an opinion of the Court.

O'Connor, joined by Stone-Zsela and Scalia, concurs in Part I and dismissal while declining the affirmative construction. Competing readings make the intended omission question too uncertain; the registration disclaimer can concern ignorance of law without conclusively resolving the complete charge. The concurrence preserves the requested-instruction objection and the distinction between evidence and intentional waiver.

Kennedy, joined by Souter, dissents from dismissal. The later charge language, colloquy, then-existing circuit law, and the appellate omission-and-harmlessness ruling adequately present factual knowledge in their view. They would reach the granted question but state no present disposition on whether an assumed omission was harmless. These competing presentation analyses supply no controlling merits rule, and their published joins do not supply a separate announced numerical tally.
