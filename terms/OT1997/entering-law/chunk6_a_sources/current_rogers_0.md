### January 14, 1998 — Rogers v. United States

[Public Projection](../records/rogers_v_united_states_dig_1998-01-14.md#public-projection). The writ is dismissed as improvidently granted because the intended omitted-element/admission question is inadequately presented by this charge. Thomas's Part I is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Ginsburg, and Breyer. The Court announces no separate numerical tally. Thomas's Part II affirmative charge construction is joined only by Stevens, Ginsburg, and Breyer and is not an opinion of the Court; neither competing charge reading becomes a controlling merits ruling.

The charge defined firearm to include a silencer and required knowing possession of the defined firearm, while later wording addressed knowledge that the item was a firearm requiring registration and separately described knowing possession and firearm status. Factual characteristic knowledge and knowledge of registration law are distinct. The requested instruction and later objection survive counsel's earlier acceptance of a knowledge term. Identification of the object is evidence, not automatic intentional waiver of jury determination or an admission of knowing possession of the bag. The supported mismatch supplies the procedural disposition, not a general policy of avoiding difficult constitutional questions.

No omitted-element harmless-error question is decided. The dismissal establishes neither universal instructional correctness, harmlessness of an actual omission, structural treatment of every omission, forfeiture, nor an extension of Staples to every silencer or §5861(i) issue. Staples retains its actual machinegun-scope distinction between weapon-characteristic knowledge and registration-law knowledge; Gaudin preserves jury allocation; Sullivan retains its vitiated-reasonable-doubt rule; Roy retains its qualified actual-verdict analysis. Olano and Johnson keep forfeiture, intentional waiver, and their distinct review predicates separate; Izumi retains question-presentation discipline. None of these merits rules is applied to sustain the convictions, and no admission exception or other substantive rule is adopted.

The Eleventh Circuit's two silencer convictions stand without Supreme Court merits approval; its separate machinegun reversal remains intact. No remand, retrial, acquittal, release, resentencing, or further review follows. Published lower-opinion and reproduced trial passages support the vehicle ruling. The full petition, grant wording, charge, appendix, and briefs are not independently reproduced; the granted question is reported in substance, and count three's charge is comparable rather than verified identical. Conflicting bag-location or color descriptions play no role. Missing originals establish no missing Court filing, waiver, or litigant default.
