## Kawaauhau v. Geiger, No. 97-115. — 1998-03-03

Source Record: kawaauhau_v_geiger_merits_1998-03-03.md

## Event

Kawaauhau v. Geiger, No. 97-115. Plenary merits decision, March 3, 1998; October Term 1997, chunk 5. Argued January 21, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit, sitting en banc. The question is whether a malpractice debt arising from negligent or reckless medical treatment is for willful and malicious injury under 11 U.S.C. §523(a)(6). The Bankruptcy Court held the debt nondischargeable, the District Court affirmed, and the Eighth Circuit reversed. The creditors seek reversal of that appellate judgment.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate. No Justice is recused.

## Public Action

The judgment of the Eighth Circuit is affirmed.

## Judgment & Remedy

Affirmed, 9–0. Supporting: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. The malpractice debt remains dischargeable under §523(a)(6). The Court does not revisit the jury’s malpractice finding or damages calculation; it decides dischargeability under §523(a)(6). No new intent finding, remand for relitigation of malpractice or decision under another exception is ordered.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer | Affirms | The complete injury-intent holding, application, precedent treatment and limits |

No separate writing is filed.

## Holdings

### Deliberate injury is required; a deliberate act causing negligent or reckless harm is insufficient

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 523(a)(6) requires a deliberate or intentional injury, in addition to the separately stated malicious character; intentionally doing an act that negligently or recklessly causes injury does not satisfy willfulness. This malpractice debt, established on negligence or recklessness rather than injury-directed intent, is therefore dischargeable under that paragraph.

**Authority:** Ginsburg’s opinion of the Court, joined in full by all eight other Justices identified in Opinion Topology, directly controls the rule, application and stated limits.

**Controlling explanation:** The statutory modifier describes injury. Moving it to the treatment decision would make many voluntary acts producing negligent harm sufficient and deprive the injury requirement of independent work. The medical choices here were deliberate, but that does not establish deliberate injury. The exceptionally deficient standard of care found below establishes malpractice; calling it willful and malicious does not answer the federal question.

Tinker involved an intentional invasion of a legally protected right, and McIntyre involved deliberate unauthorized appropriation of property. Neither requires personal hostility, but neither makes a voluntary act alone enough. Tinker itself distinguished negligent driving from intentionally running over a victim. To the extent its broader language is read to include merely reckless injury under the present statute, that reading is rejected. Davis confirms that even conversion liability does not automatically establish willful and malicious injury. Field likewise keeps a federal discharge predicate distinct from a state tort label.

The creditors’ cumulative account of inferior treatment, canceled transfer and discontinued antibiotics establishes the gravity of the alleged wrong, but supplies no additional finding of intended injury. Nor does uninsured practice authorize a judicially created exception. Congress fixes the exceptions. The Court reserves whether subjective knowledge of substantial certainty suffices, the independent meaning of malicious, any other independently pleaded discharge exception and the treatment of proved injury-directed harm. No categorical exemption for medical debts or new criminal-law proof requirement follows.

## Precedent Treatment

- Tinker v. Colwell, 193 U.S. 473, retains its intentional-invasion holding and rejection of a personal-hostility requirement; a reading that makes merely reckless injury sufficient under §523(a)(6) is rejected.
- McIntyre v. Kavanaugh, 242 U.S. 138, is distinguished as deliberate appropriation; it does not make all conversions or negligent professional acts nondischargeable, and its old-Act imputation question is not decided.
- Davis v. Aetna Acceptance Co., 293 U.S. 328, is applied to preserve the distinction between tort liability and willful and malicious injury.
- Field v. Mans is applied for the independent federal discharge predicates; its actual-fraud reliance rule is unchanged. In re Cohen’s debt-extent rule remains distinct because a qualifying injury predicate must first exist here.

## Law After Decision

From March 3, 1998, §523(a)(6) excludes debts for negligently or recklessly inflicted injury from its willfulness category even when the underlying act was intentional. Actual deliberate injury presents a different question. The independent malicious requirement, substantial certainty, other discharge exceptions and state-law liability retain their separate roles.

## Separate Writings

No separate writing is filed.

## Procedure After Action

The Eighth Circuit’s discharge determination under §523(a)(6) stands. This review is complete; no additional hearing, intent finding or reconsideration of malpractice is directed.

## United States v. Ramirez, No. 96-1469. — 1998-03-04

Source Record: united_states_v_ramirez_merits_1998-03-04.md

## Event

United States v. Ramirez, No. 96-1469. Plenary merits decision, March 4, 1998; October Term 1997, chunk 5. Argued January 13, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit. The United States challenges the affirmance of an order suppressing firearms evidence under the Fourth Amendment and 18 U.S.C. §3109 because officers broke a garage window while executing a no-knock warrant. The questions concern the announcement standard when property is damaged, the reasonableness of that damage and the separate statutory ground.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate. No Justice is recused.

## Public Action

The judgment of the Ninth Circuit is reversed, and the case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with the Court’s opinion.

## Judgment & Remedy

Reversed and remanded, 9–0. Supporting: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. The suppression judgment cannot stand on a heightened announcement standard triggered by damage, or on the challenged entry and single-window damage under the standards applied here. This decision orders no acquittal, conviction or sentence and establishes no automatic exclusionary exception for later statements, a later warrant or recovered evidence.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Breyer | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg | Reverses and remands | All constitutional and statutory holdings, the bounded application and remedy |

No separate writing is filed.

## Holdings

**Common authority:** Breyer’s opinion of the Court is joined in full by all eight other Justices identified in Opinion Topology. That direct unanimous coalition controls each holding and its limits; no combination of separate rationales is necessary.

### I. Property damage does not raise the announcement threshold

**Holding and operative rule:** Damage during warrant execution does not categorically require a greater exigency than particularized reasonable suspicion that ordinary announcement would be dangerous or futile, or would inhibit effective investigation, including through destruction of evidence. The facts supporting that exception must be known when the challenged force begins, and the means and extent of any destruction remain independently subject to Fourth Amendment reasonableness.

**Controlling explanation:** Wilson makes the common-law announcement protection part of constitutional reasonableness while preserving supported exceptions. Richards requires particular facts and rational inferences rather than an offense category, unsupported hunch or a no-knock label on a warrant. Breaking property does not change that announcement inquiry into a separate higher tier. The contrary rule correctly recognizes that destruction aggravates an intrusion, but addresses that concern in the wrong place. Whether the particular destructive act is reasonable remains a separate question; justified surprise does not authorize needless or disproportionate damage. The Court adopts no universal waiting period and revisits no disputed standard-of-review rule.

### II. The contemporaneous danger and limited damage justified this execution

**Holding and operative rule:** The reliable report of a dangerous escapee, corroborating observation and location-specific information about garage weapons supplied reasonable suspicion that ordinary announcement would create danger. Breaking one garage window to control access to those weapons, with a firearm directed through the opening for that purpose, was reasonable on this record; the justification permits neither gratuitous destruction nor any amount of damage merely because announcement may be excused.

**Controlling explanation:** Richards requires circumstances known before force. The informant’s report was partly corroborated by the agent’s observation, and the reported weapons gave the officers a concrete reason to prevent a rush into the garage. The limited window break addressed that identified danger. The number of officers outside did not eliminate a possible armed person’s access to weapons inside. Their security purpose is supported by those facts; purpose alone would not suffice. Ramirez’s later shot supplies no retrospective justification, and the later failure to find Shelby does not erase the information reasonably available beforehand. The Court decides the challenged entry and window damage, not every subsequent act. Gratuitous, punitive, disproportionate or objective-unrelated destruction receives no approval, and the remedy for materially different damage remains open.

### III. Section 3109 preserves a supported common-law danger exception

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 3109 ordinarily permits breaking to execute a search warrant after notice of authority and purpose and refused admission, and separately permits breaking when necessary to liberate the officer or someone assisting execution. Its codification of the common-law announcement rule also preserves a danger exception satisfied by particularized reasonable suspicion that following the ordinary process would expose officers or occupants to danger; the pre-entry escapee and garage-weapons information satisfies that exception here.

**Controlling explanation:** Simultaneous announcement and breaking do not establish prior refused admission. Nor does this record establish an officer or assistant needing liberation. The entry instead rests on the incorporated common-law danger qualification. Miller identifies the common-law source of the statutory protection but reserved a general exigency question. Sabbath treats opening an unlocked door as breaking and, in its discussion of common-law exceptions, supplies direct interpretive support without having held that every asserted danger suffices. Indeed, its unsupported danger claim failed. The Court now adopts the danger construction for the concrete supported circumstances here; it does not attribute that holding to those earlier reservations.

The statutory protection carries its traditional safety qualification, rather than automatically acquiring every constitutional exception. Wilson’s common-law account and Richards’s particularized standard guide this bounded construction. The express liberation alternative concerns freeing an impeded officer or helper and remains distinct. Because breaking already includes entry without physical damage, the statute creates no greater announcement threshold merely because property is damaged. No statutory rule for every possible futility, escape or evidence-destruction situation is necessary. Both asserted grounds for suppression fail, so the Court need not decide attenuation, independent source, inevitable discovery or the exclusionary consequence of an established violation.

## Precedent Treatment

- Wilson v. Arkansas is applied for the common-law announcement protection and supported exceptions; it does not constitutionalize every federal statutory detail.
- Richards v. Wisconsin is applied for particularized reasonable suspicion and contemporaneous justification; its announcement quantum is not increased because damage occurs, and its separate review-method holding is unchanged.
- Miller v. United States, 357 U.S. 301, supplies the statutory common-law background. Its reservation of a general exigency exception is respected rather than described as an earlier holding.
- Sabbath v. United States, 391 U.S. 585, retains its rule that opening a closed unlocked door is breaking and its rejection of unsupported danger. Its common-law discussion supports the danger qualification adopted here; physical damage creates no new announcement tier.
- Aponte Matos v. Toledo Dávila retains its separate conduct-date qualified-immunity scope and supplies no direct suppression immunity here.

## Law After Decision

The constitutional announcement inquiry retains Richards’s particularized reasonable-suspicion standard when execution damages property, while the destructive means receive independent reasonableness review. Section 3109 separately incorporates the supported common-law danger exception applied here. Neither the validity of a warrant nor the availability of an announcement exception authorizes unreasonable damage. No suppression-causation doctrine or rule for materially different destruction is resolved.

## Procedure After Action

Those asserted grounds cannot sustain the suppression judgment. Any genuinely separate and properly preserved question retains its own requirements; no such claim is invented and no particular prosecution result is ordered.

## Holdings

### I. The present fee motion

**Holding and operative rule:** Rule 39.8 permits, but does not require, denial of financial leave when the particular submission is frivolous or malicious, even if the applicant cannot prepay. The Court exercises that discretion here because the present petition supplies no arguable route through the independent state-action, conspiracy and judicial-immunity obstacles to the federal action; it allows payment of the Rule 38 fee and submission in Rule 33.1 form by March 30, 1998, without deciding certiorari.

**Authority:** Part I of the per curiam opinion, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; seven Justices adopt this rule, application and limited filing opportunity. Stevens and Stone-Zsela would grant the motion as a permissible discretionary choice.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court examines the grounds Glendora actually presents. She alleges false service, lack of personal jurisdiction and denial of a hearing to challenge service, and contests the appellate financial warning and clerk-signed order. Those allegations are not rejected as false. They nevertheless supply no developed arguable challenge connecting the service dispute to the independent immunity, state-action and conspiracy grounds defeating this federal action. A clerk's signature and the challenged panel composition do not themselves establish a federal defect. The Court does not confuse its own appellate jurisdiction with the District Court's inability to review a state judgment.

Martin requires particular-paper assessment and preserves a discretionary denial where an independent obstacle receives no arguable answer. Demos and Anderson permit an arguable access allegation to clear the fee threshold without proving entitlement to relief, but do not make the allegation's label conclusive. Arteaga applies the same distinction between considering an actual procedural explanation and finding an arguable route through the operative obstacle. The lower court's characterization, financial warning and unsuccessful filing history do not substitute for this examination. Financial denial is not a finding of wealth, dishonesty or default, and it decides neither the alleged misconduct nor any claim's ultimate merits. Payment and proper form permit ordinary processing, not a promised grant of review.

### II. The proposed restriction on future noncriminal petitions

**Holding and operative rule:** A prospective fee-and-form restriction requires demonstrated applicant-specific abuse, a supported fit to the future category restricted, and support for the inadequacy of individual-paper treatment. The present record does not establish those predicates for Glendora's unidentified future noncriminal certiorari petitions, so the Court imposes no new restriction.

**Authority:** Part II of the per curiam opinion, joined by all nine Justices: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Controlling explanation:** Martin, Day, Sassower, Anderson, Brown and Arteaga require an independent justification for future restraints. The present petition's deficiencies and the lower-court warning do not demonstrate repeated applicant-specific abuse fitted to all future noncriminal petitions, or why individual treatment is inadequate. A count of unsuccessful filings cannot establish the content of unidentified future papers. Keeping paid and criminal routes available would narrow the proposed condition but would not supply the missing justification. This refusal creates no numerical threshold, universal prior-warning requirement, duty to exhaust every conceivable lesser measure, or categorical prohibition on adequately supported restrictions. Targeted controls remain available on their own demonstrated predicates.

## Gray v. Maryland, No. 96-8653. — 1998-03-09

Source Record: gray_v_maryland_merits_1998-03-09.md

## Event

Gray v. Maryland, No. 96-8653. Merits decision, March 9, 1998; October Term 1997, chunk 5. Argued December 8, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the Maryland Court of Appeals, which reinstated Gray's conviction after the intermediate court set it aside. The question is whether a nontestifying codefendant's confession remains within Bruton when Gray's name is replaced by conspicuous blanks or spoken deletions. Gray seeks relief from the conviction; harmless error remains a distinct question.

## Participation

All nine Justices participate at argument and decision: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. No recusal is stated.

## Public Action

The Maryland Court of Appeals' judgment is vacated and the case remanded. The Court holds the challenged redaction unconstitutional without deciding harmlessness.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Vacated and remanded, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

The state court receives the case for proceedings consistent with the confrontation holding, with any properly available Chapman harmless-error issue preserved. The Court makes no harmlessness finding and orders neither automatic retrial nor acquittal. Stone-Zsela agrees with vacatur and remand but would expressly direct the state court to determine harmless error.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | O'Connor | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer | Supports vacatur and remand | Direct-accusation redaction rule and application |

## Holdings

### I. Obvious deletions that retain a direct accusation

**Holding and operative rule:** When a nontestifying codefendant's confession replaces a defendant's name with a conspicuous blank, spoken deletion, symbol or equivalent substitution that directly identifies the defendant from the statement and its immediate trial context, admitting that confession at their joint trial with only an instruction to consider it against the declarant violates the other defendant's right of confrontation under Bruton. Richardson continues to govern a confession neutral on its face that becomes incriminating only through later evidentiary linkage; this decision does not condemn every neutral pronoun, redaction or joint trial.

**Authority:** Part I of O'Connor's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; six Justices adopt the rule and this application. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas dissent. This is direct majority authority, not a combination of unlike rationales under Marks.

**Controlling explanation:** Bruton recognizes that a powerful direct accusation by an unavailable codefendant cannot reliably be confined to the declarant by an instruction. Richardson upheld a different kind of statement: the redaction removed every reference to the other defendant's existence, and only other evidence later supplied the incriminating link. Its reservation concerning symbols and substitute words leaves this question open.

Here the spoken deletions and written blanks leave the accusation's grammar and obvious missing identities in place. At this joint trial they invite the jury immediately to supply the other accused's name. Two omissions and testimony describing additional participants do not convert that transparent accusation into Richardson's otherwise neutral narrative. The distinction concerns the kind of inference, not whether any inference is involved. The limiting instruction therefore cannot cure the preserved confrontation violation.

Zafiro preserves a specific trial right even when instructions ordinarily provide adequate joint-trial protection; the benefits of joinder do not answer whether this particular accusation can be admitted. The holding rests on the challenged redaction itself. The prosecutor's follow-up question connecting Bell's information with Gray's arrest is not adjudicated as a separate constitutional wrong, and no separate preservation requirement is bypassed. The Court does not prescribe substantive rewriting of confessions or decide the validity of every neutral substitute, an independently admissible statement, or testimony subjected to cross-examination.

### II. Constitutional error and the remand remain separate

**Holding and operative rule:** The Maryland judgment reinstating Gray's conviction is vacated and the case remanded for proceedings consistent with the confrontation holding. Any properly available harmless-error contention requires the State to establish beyond a reasonable doubt under Chapman that the constitutional error did not contribute to the verdict; this Court makes no harmlessness finding and orders neither an unconditional new trial nor acquittal.

**Authority:** Part II of O'Connor's opinion, joined by Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; five Justices adopt the preservation-qualified remand. Stone-Zsela supplies the sixth vote to vacate and remand, but would affirmatively direct the state court to determine harmless error and does not join Part II's narrower instruction.

**Controlling explanation:** Establishing an admission error does not itself establish the remedy required on the whole trial record. Chapman supplies the State's demanding harmlessness burden for constitutional trial error. Williamson preserves the distinction between determining an evidentiary or constitutional wrong and deciding its consequences, without substituting the standard for nonconstitutional error. The present materials do not permit this Court to find the confrontation error harmless. Further proceedings must respect the holding and any properly available error review; a materially different redaction or lawful trial-management alternative presents its own question. No conviction is reinstated by the possibility of harmlessness, and the State receives no favorable finding on an unexamined record.

## Precedent Treatment

- Bruton v. United States, 391 U.S. 123, is applied to obvious name deletions retaining a direct accusation; its limiting-instruction exception remains bounded to that confrontation risk.
- Richardson v. Marsh, 481 U.S. 200, is distinguished because its redaction removed reference to the defendant's existence; its rule for neutral statements requiring later evidentiary linkage remains effective.
- Zafiro v. United States, 506 U.S. 534, preserves specific confrontation protection alongside discretionary joint-trial management; it supplies no universal instruction cure.
- Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18, supplies the State's beyond-a-reasonable-doubt harmlessness burden for constitutional trial error, without an application finding here.
- Williamson v. United States, 512 U.S. 594, preserves separate analysis of error and remedy; its assertion-specific evidentiary holding and different nonconstitutional harmlessness standard are not displaced.
- White v. Illinois, 502 U.S. 346, retains its exception-specific confrontation rules; no new categorical testimonial definition or exception for an accusatory police confession is adopted.

## Law After Decision

An obvious name deletion that retains a direct accusation falls within Bruton. Richardson's neutral-statement rule remains available, and no general prohibition on pronouns, lawful redaction, joint trials or cross-examined testimony follows. Preservation, constitutional harmlessness and lawful trial management remain separate inquiries.

## Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and in the judgment.** Stone-Zsela joins Part I. The blanks, grammar, limited named participants and prosecutor's immediate connection between Bell's information and Gray's arrest directly identify whose name was removed. The jury need not wait for later independent evidence to identify the accused, so an instruction cannot cure the uncross-examined accusation. His reliance on immediate context explains the preserved admission objection; it creates no separate ruling that the follow-up question itself was a preserved independent constitutional wrong. He would direct the state court to determine harmless error, without deciding it here, and permit consideration of materially different redactions or lawful trial-management alternatives. He does not join Part II's limitation of the remand instruction to a properly available harmless-error contention. Joint trials, genuinely neutral wording, independently admissible confessions and cross-examined testimony remain outside his prohibition.

**Scalia, joined by Kennedy and Thomas, dissenting.** The dissent would apply Richardson's distinction between a confession that identifies the accused on its face and one that becomes incriminating through inference from other evidence. Bell's statement contains two deletions, while other testimony describes six participants and only one other defendant is at this trial. A blank does not uniquely identify Gray without a further inference. Conspicuous editing alone is not the powerfully incriminating express accusation for which Bruton displaces the ordinary presumption that jurors follow limiting instructions. The dissent objects to a rule encouraging concealed edits or substantive rewriting, and would affirm on the preserved confession-admission issue. It does not treat instructions as a cure for every confrontation problem or eliminate Rule 14 severance and evidentiary protection beyond the constitutional minimum. The prosecutor's follow-up question presents a separate issue: the State says an arrest warrant preceded the confession and characterizes the question as a transition, and no contemporaneous objection to that question is identified. The dissent would not use it to circumvent preservation or to transform the preserved redaction question into a different adjudication.

## Procedure After Action

The Maryland judgment reinstating Gray's conviction is vacated. Further state proceedings must apply the redaction holding and, for any properly available harmless-error contention, Chapman. Materially different wording and lawful trial-management alternatives may be addressed in those proceedings on their own records. No present new-trial, acquittal or harmlessness result is directed.

## Event

Quality King Distributors, Inc. v. L'anza Research International, Inc., No. 96-1470. Merits decision, March 9, 1998; October Term 1997, chunk 5. Argued December 8, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, which affirmed summary judgment for L'anza after holding the first-sale defense unavailable against §602(a). The question concerns copyrighted labels on copies made in the United States, sold abroad and reimported without renewed copyright permission. Quality King seeks application of the first-sale rule and reversal.

## Participation

All nine Justices participate at argument and decision: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. No recusal is stated.

## Public Action

The Ninth Circuit's judgment is reversed and the case returned for proceedings consistent with the Court's statutory holding. Section 602(a) does not exempt the distribution right from §109(a) for the domestic-manufacture round trip presented here.

## Holdings

### I. The first-sale limitation governs these domestically made copies

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 602(a) defines unauthorized importation as infringement of the distribution right in §106(3), and that right remains subject to §109(a). For a particular copy lawfully made in the United States and sold abroad with the copyright owner's authorization, its lawful owner, or a person authorized by that owner, may sell or otherwise dispose of that copy without renewed copyright permission; reimportation does not revive the exhausted distribution right.

**Authority:** Part I of Scalia's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; eight Justices adopt the complete owner-or-authorized-person rule and its domestic-manufacture boundary. Stone-Zsela joins the rule only as it concerns reimportation by the lawful owner, giving that narrower proposition nine votes; he does not join the extension to a person authorized by that owner.

**Controlling explanation:** The enacted provisions operate together. Section 602(a) does not declare a freestanding right immune from the limits on the distribution right it expressly invokes. Section 106 subjects its rights to §§107–120, including §109(a), which permits the owner of a qualifying copy to dispose of it without the copyright owner's authority. A sale abroad does not, by itself, remove that limit for these domestically made copies. L'anza's interest in maintaining different domestic and foreign prices cannot supply an exception Congress did not enact.

The contrary argument that this reading makes §602(a) meaningless overlooks its remaining operation when the copy is not lawfully made, or the importer possesses it without ownership or the owner's authorization. The Court need not determine every remaining application to reject an independent importation right exempt from §109.

Bobbs-Merrill supplies the first-sale background: copyright's distribution authority does not itself enforce a continuing resale restriction after the owner's authorized sale. That principle does not decide contract liability. Campbell and Fogerty likewise respect Congress's actual rights and limitations; general market-policy preferences cannot enlarge the enacted entitlement. Here the claim concerns distribution and importation of copyrighted labels on domestically manufactured goods, not unauthorized reproduction. The Court leaves foreign-manufactured copies unresolved and does not decide counterfeiting, reproduction rights, independent contractual duties, trademark confusion, labeling or consumer-protection law.

### II. The categorical importation judgment cannot stand

**Holding and operative rule:** The Ninth Circuit's judgment, which treated §602(a) as barring the first-sale defense to this domestic-manufacture round trip, is reversed and the case returned for proceedings consistent with Part I. This disposition supplies no new finding about the first purchasing intermediary, exact place of title transfer or actual importer, and directs no damages, contract judgment or final judgment on an independently preserved theory.

**Authority:** Part II of Scalia's opinion, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; all eight Associates adopt that limited remedial direction. Stone-Zsela joins reversal of the legal rule but would direct judgment for Quality King on the copyright-importation theory.

**Controlling explanation:** The District Court rejected the first-sale defense and entered summary judgment for L'anza; the Court of Appeals affirmed because it regarded the importation right as independent of §109. That categorical legal premise is incorrect. Reversal restores the statutory limitation without making new findings about the transaction's precise title sequence or resolving claims governed by different law. The published account supports the domestic-origin export-and-return setting but does not settle every intermediary detail. The Court therefore states the governing rule and returns the case for its consistent application, without turning the rejection of L'anza's legal theory into an unexpressed adjudication of every remaining issue.

## Precedent Treatment

- Bobbs-Merrill Co. v. Straus, 210 U.S. 339, supplies the first-sale principle limiting copyright control over a particular copy after an authorized sale; its resale-notice rule is not enlarged into a general contract holding.
- Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569, preserves enforcement of enacted copyright limits rather than dispositive market-policy presumptions; its four-factor fair-use inquiry is unchanged and is not substituted for §109.
- Fogerty v. Fantasy, Inc., 510 U.S. 517, preserves copyright's actual statutory boundaries; its discretionary fee rules do not determine first sale or any fee award here.

## Law After Decision

The first-sale limit applies to the §106(3) distribution right invoked by §602(a) for the domestically made copies at issue. Foreign sale alone does not recreate that right on reimportation. Ownership or authorization by the copy's owner and lawful manufacture remain necessary; foreign manufacture and the distinct legal regimes identified above remain unresolved. No infringement, damages, fee or independent contract rule is added beyond the stated holding.

## Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** Stone-Zsela joins Part I only insofar as it addresses copies made in the United States, sold abroad with the copyright owner's authorization and later reimported by their lawful owner. He does not join its broader rule for a person authorized by that owner. Section 602(a) invokes the §106(3) right, which §109(a) limits; crossing the border cannot recreate an exhausted distribution right, and territorial-pricing policy cannot do so either. He would direct entry of judgment for Quality King on the claim that this reimportation infringed that distribution right through §602(a), rather than leave that theory for further proceedings. Any preserved independent contract, trademark, labeling or consumer-protection claim remains governed by its own law. His disposition would not decide foreign manufacture, unauthorized reproductions, counterfeits, reproduction rights, trademark confusion or contractual duties enforceable against a contracting party.

## Procedure After Action

The appellate judgment upholding L'anza's copyright-importation claim under the categorical no-first-sale rule is reversed. Further proceedings must apply the Court's statutory construction. No particular damages amount, contract result or final judgment on an independent claim is directed. Stone-Zsela's preferred direction of judgment on the importation theory is not the Court's mandate.
