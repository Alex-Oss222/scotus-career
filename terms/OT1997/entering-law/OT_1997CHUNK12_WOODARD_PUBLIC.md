## Event

Ohio Adult Parole Authority v. Woodard, No. 96-1769. Argued December 10, 1997; decided March 25, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the Sixth Circuit, reviewing 107 F.3d 1178. The questions concern due process in Ohio's capital-clemency procedure and whether the offered interview compels self-incrimination.

Woodard brought this §1983 action after receiving notice of a voluntary interview and a clemency hearing. He did not request the interview. The District Court granted judgment on the pleadings. The Sixth Circuit rejected an independent entitlement to clemency, but ordered further proceedings on an established-process theory of due process and on a possible unconstitutional condition on silence.

## Participation

All nine Justices participate at argument and decision: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the Sixth Circuit's directions for further proceedings on the presented due-process and self-incrimination claims.

## Holdings

### I. Required clemency procedures do not create an entitlement to mercy or triallike review

**Holding and operative rule:** Ohio's required investigation, hearing and advisory recommendation do not create a substantive entitlement to clemency because they do not constrain the Governor to grant it on specified facts. The analogy to a first appeal as of right does not itself require appellate representation, triallike evidence procedure or a new determination of guilt in this discretionary executive process.

**Authority:** Part I-A has all eight Associates, with Stone-Zsela also joining the no-right-to-mercy conclusion. Part I-B has O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; Stone-Zsela joins only the mercy-versus-criminal-trial distinction. Stevens does not join the rejection of the established-process analogy.

**Controlling explanation:** Dumschat distinguishes seeking clemency from entitlement to its grant. Sandin requires a genuine substantive constraint on a meaningful deprivation; Doe and Johnson likewise distinguish required administration from an entitlement to a favorable discretionary decision. Ohio must investigate, hold the required hearing and recommend action even after an execution stay, but its recommendation is advisory and the Governor retains complete discretion. A later hearing or revised recommendation on new information is permitted, not required. These provisions do not compel clemency.

Evitts protects the first appeal as of right within adjudication of guilt. Clemency performs a different executive function. Its availability after conviction does not itself import appellate or trial procedure. The Court rejects the Sixth Circuit's general integral-stage theory as a basis for the requested remand, while preserving independently retained constitutional rights. Herrera's record-specific innocence ruling establishes no rule that executive mercy is the exclusive constitutionally sufficient substitute for every claim of innocence.

### II. Capital-clemency procedures retain a constitutional minimum, satisfied on these pleadings

**Holding and operative rule:** An established capital-clemency process must afford minimal safeguards against fundamentally arbitrary procedure, including arbitrary denial of any access or substitution of an entirely arbitrary device such as a coin flip for consideration. The notice and offered interview here, considered with the pleaded restrictions, satisfy that minimum; they establish no entitlement to clemency, trial protections, a favorable recommendation or judicial review of mercy's merits.

**Authority:** Part II-A's minimum safeguard has six direct joins: O'Connor, Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Part II-B's sufficient-process application has five: O'Connor, Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Stevens disputes the application; Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas agree with rejection of the claim on their distinct entitlement/adjudication ground. Both controlling propositions rest on direct majority joins, without combining separate rationales under Marks.

**Controlling explanation:** A valid death sentence reduces the process owed before discretionary mercy is denied, but the prisoner remains alive. That retained interest supplies a minimum protection independent of a state-created entitlement to release. Ford illustrates the distinction between the interest protected and the flexible procedure appropriate to it; this case does not import Ford's separate prohibition on executing an incompetent prisoner or its particular hearing requirements.

Woodard alleges three days' interview notice, ten days' hearing notice, difficulty preparing while postconviction proceedings continued, exclusion of counsel from the interview, chair-controlled counsel participation at the hearing, and inability to testify or submit documents at the hearing. Accepting that pleaded account, the notice and offered personal interview provided access, and those restrictions do not establish fundamentally arbitrary procedure requiring further adjudication. The Court does not find an unmentioned alternative document channel or reject the allegations as untrue. It establishes neither a fixed list of required safeguards nor an accuracy code for every disputed item in a clemency file. Johnson's rejection of a parole-file process entitlement does not extinguish independent constitutional protection.

### III. The optional interview does not establish compelled self-incrimination

**Holding and operative rule:** An optional clemency interview does not compel self-incrimination merely because the prisoner must choose between providing potentially harmful information and remaining silent; absence of counsel, confidentiality or immunity does not itself convert this invitation into compulsion. The conclusion also holds if an ordinary civil adverse inference from silence is assumed, but it does not authorize an automatic privilege penalty, compelled waiver or criminal use of compelled answers.

**Authority:** Part III-A's present voluntary-interview holding has all nine Justices. Part III-B's additional ordinary-inference ground and its limits have all eight Associates; Stone-Zsela does not join that additional ground.

**Controlling explanation:** Murphy distinguishes a difficult evidentiary choice from a command backed by a genuine threatened penalty for asserting the privilege. Woodard was invited, not required, to attend or answer. The State's refusal to promise confidentiality or immunity does not establish that it compelled answers. No automatic loss of clemency eligibility for silence, compelled waiver or criminal use of compelled testimony is established.

The eight Associates additionally apply Baxter's distinction between ordinary adverse inferences in civil proceedings and automatic sanctions attached to silence. Even assuming such an inference in this civil clemency evaluation, the voluntary choice does not become compelled testimony. The record does not establish Ohio's actual use of silence or answers; the assumption supplies an alternative legal ground, not a factual finding. LaChance preserves separately supported compulsion and waiver claims, and Johnson confirms that absence of entitlement to a benefit does not excuse an independent unconstitutional penalty. A materially supported coercive condition would therefore require its own analysis.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat:** Applied to discretionary mercy and the distinction between seeking relief and entitlement to its grant; not extended to extinguish independent constitutional interests.
- **Sandin v. Conner:** Applied at its substantive-constraint rule. Mandatory procedure alone is insufficient, and directly retained constitutional interests remain separate.
- **Doe v. District of Columbia:** Applied to distinguish investigative duties from a substantive entitlement.
- **Johnson v. Rodriguez:** Applied to the same procedure/entitlement boundary and preservation of independent constitutional wrongs; its ordinary initial-parole rule does not displace the retained-life floor.
- **Evitts v. Lucey:** Distinguished at the adjudicatory first appeal as of right; it does not itself require the requested capital-clemency procedures.
- **Ford v. Wainwright:** Its distinction between a protected interest and appropriate flexible procedure informs the minimum-process rule; its separate incompetency protection remains unchanged.
- **Herrera v. Collins:** Preserved as a record-specific innocence disposition; no exclusive-clemency rule is attributed to it.
- **Minnesota v. Murphy:** Applied to distinguish voluntary choices from supported threats of a privilege penalty.
- **Baxter v. Palmigiano:** Applied by the eight Associates to ordinary civil adverse inferences, distinct from automatic punishment or compelled criminal testimony.
- **LaChance v. Erickson / McManus:** Preserved for separately established compulsion and waiver; it supplies no general prohibition on lawful civil inferences.

## Law After Decision

Capital clemency remains discretionary executive mercy, separate from judicial collateral review. Required administrative steps create no right to a favorable outcome. A directly controlling minimum protects remaining life against fundamentally arbitrary clemency procedure, but the pleaded restrictions here do not breach it. The optional interview establishes no compelled self-incrimination, including on the eight-Justice alternative assumption of an ordinary civil adverse inference. No general absence of constitutional protection is declared.

## Separate Writings

**Kennedy, joined by Scalia and Thomas, concurring in part and in the judgment.** Ohio's procedure does not constrain the ultimate mercy decision, and the appellate analogy supplies no right to the process requested. Dumschat and the substantive distinction applied in Sandin and Johnson resolve these pleaded process claims. These Justices do not join the affirmative retained-life framework or its application; their judgment agreement adds no join to Part II. They preserve independently grounded constitutional prohibitions and fully join the self-incrimination analysis, including its actual-compulsion and automatic-penalty limits.

**Stevens, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** A prisoner remains alive, and an established procedure integral to the State's final decision to take life must afford basic fairness and meaningful participation. His Dumschat position and Evitts's established-process principle support bounded inquiry into the process as a whole, even though clemency need not replicate a trial and no right to mercy exists. He joins the minimum safeguard but would preserve the due-process remand to consider the relevant materials rather than hold these pleadings insufficient. He finds no violation already established and prescribes none of the requested safeguards automatically. He joins both self-incrimination grounds, distinguishing this voluntary invitation from a command backed by a serious threatened sanction.

## Procedure After Action

The Sixth Circuit must conform its disposition to rejection of the presented due-process and self-incrimination claims. Its no-entitlement ruling stands. The decision neither retries guilt nor prevents lawful executive clemency review under Ohio's existing duties; it orders no particular recommendation, hearing, release, stay or execution.
