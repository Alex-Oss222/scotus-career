## Standards and Tests selection: Relevant-conduct sentencing and a later distinct prosecution

### Relevant-conduct sentencing and a later distinct prosecution

**Governing question:** Whether use of uncharged conduct to select a sentence constitutes prior prosecution or punishment for that conduct.

**Current rule:** Considering relevant uncharged conduct to choose a sentence within the authorized statutory range for the offense of conviction is not itself prosecution or independent punishment for the uncharged offense. That sentencing use therefore does not alone bar a later distinct prosecution under the retained Grady rule. A disguised separate penalty, punishment beyond statutory authority and independent sentencing safeguards remain outside this permission. A general acquittal also does not categorically prohibit reliable relevant conduct proved by a preponderance in ordinary Guidelines sentencing within the punishment legally authorized by the conviction. That use supplies no new conviction, disguised separate acquitted-offense punishment, excess punishment or removal of an actual offense element from the jury. Independent due-process, evidentiary, Guidelines and Rule 32 requirements remain.

O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, constitutional-characterization discussion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; six support this rationale. Scalia and Thomas affirm only in the judgment on the narrower distinct-prosecution ground. 
- General acquittal and ordinary conviction-based sentencing: United States v. Watts; United States v. Putra — United States v. Watts / United States v. Putra — I. A general acquittal does not categorically bar these ordinary sentencing findings, January 6, 1997; per curiam opinion joined by Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. This resolves Witte’s acquitted-conduct reservation only at that scope.

**Present operation effective:** Watts and Putra, January 6, 1997, for the ordinary acquitted-conduct rule; Witte’s uncharged-conduct and successive-prosecution boundaries survive.

**Related current rules:** Successive prosecutions, evidentiary overlap and criminal contempt retains the governing successive-prosecution law. Conditional coordination of undischarged sentences addresses a later sentence separately; permission to prosecute orders no conviction or punishment.

## Standards and Tests selection: Forfeited error and discretionary correction under Rule 52(b)

### Forfeited error and discretionary correction under Rule 52(b)

**Governing question:** What instructions govern forfeited error and discretionary correction under Rule 52(b)?

**Current rule:** Rule 52(b) permits correction of forfeited error only if there is error, it is plain, and it affects substantial rights; valid intentional relinquishment of a known right extinguishes error, unlike failure timely to assert it. Plain means clear or obvious under current law. The defendant ordinarily must show outcome-affecting prejudice. Even then correction is discretionary and should occur only if the error seriously affects fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings; actual innocence is unnecessary, but substantial-rights impact alone is insufficient. Presence of alternates instructed not to participate supplies neither prejudice nor a presumption without a showing of participation or chilling effect. Initially unsettled trial law, special outcome-independent categories, justified prejudice presumptions and hearing authority remain unresolved. Johnson assumes substantial-rights injury without deciding whether withholding materiality is structural or harmless; its fourth-condition application supplies no general guilt-based exception or innocence prerequisite. When settled law permitted the trial action but intervening controlling law makes it erroneous during direct review, plainness is measured at appellate consideration. A forfeited omission of a genuine jury element proceeds under the distinct Rule 52(b) conditions rather than automatic reversal.

#### Griffin v. United States, 502 U.S. 46 (1991)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 90-6352.  
**Decided:** December 3, 1991.

##### Whether factual insufficiency on one lawful conspiracy object requires reversal of a general verdict supported on another object

**Holding:** A general guilty verdict on a federal multiple-object conspiracy need not be set aside merely because the evidence is inadequate for one legally valid object when another object is supported sufficiently under the unchanged beyond-a-reasonable-doubt standard. This rule does not govern a legally invalid object, failure of proof on every route, an element-diluting instruction, or a properly presented independent unanimity defect.

**Proposition-level authority:** Scalia's opinion for the Court, joined on the proposition by Stone, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, and Souter; seven of eight participants. Blackmun's judgment-only concurrence is not counted as a join in this reasoning.

**Current force:** Remains controlling as stated for the multiple-object conspiracy verdict. *Sochor v. Florida*, decided June 8, 1992, extends the factual-versus-legal distinction only to rejecting an inference of reliance on a legally available but factually unsupported aggravator from a general Florida advisory death recommendation; it does not settle broader capital-jury error rules.

**Material application:** Both alleged §371 conspiracy objects were legally permissible. The Government conceded insufficient evidence connecting Griffin to the DEA object, but the IRS object had sufficient evidentiary support.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The excluded legal-invalidity, all-routes-insufficiency, elemental-instruction, and independent unanimity questions remain unresolved. Preferred withdrawal of an unsupported route on timely request creates no independent constitutional reversal rule.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The Seventh Circuit is affirmed; the supported IRS object sustains the conviction against this challenge. No retrial or acquittal is ordered.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Turner v. United States is applied to factual insufficiency. Stromberg v. California and Yates v. United States are distinguished and preserved for legally forbidden verdict grounds. In re Winship preserves proof beyond reasonable doubt, and Jackson v. Virginia preserves the rational-factfinder sufficiency inquiry.

**Later-authority backlinks:** *Sochor v. Florida*, 504 U.S. 527 (1992), No. 91-5843, decided June 8, 1992, question concerning inferred jury reliance on the coldness aggravator; see Criminal Procedure, the Sochor entry.

#### United States v. LaBonte

**Docket or dockets:** No. 95-1726.
**Decided:** 1997-05-27.

**Companion or consolidated matters:** Respondents George LaBonte, Alfred Lawrence Hunnewell and Stephen Dyer have distinct sentencing postures. David Piper is not a respondent and receives no mandate.

##### An applicable enhanced maximum supplies the statutory benchmark

**Holding:** For an offender covered by §994(h) and subject to a lawfully invoked statutory recidivist enhancement, the maximum term authorized is the enhanced statutory maximum. Amendment 506 is invalid insofar as its career-offender benchmark excludes that applicable increase because it rests on the defendant's prior criminal record; the Commission may not replace the legally operative maximum with the unenhanced maximum.

**Proposition-level authority:** Kennedy's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Thomas; six direct joins.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Section 994(h) concerns a defendant at least eighteen years old with a current qualifying felony and two or more prior qualifying felonies. The current and each prior felony must be a crime of violence or an offense described in 21 U.S.C. §841, §§952(a), 955 or 959, or the Maritime Drug Law Enforcement Act. Eligibility is not disputed here. Potential enhancement eligibility alone does not make an uninvoked maximum applicable. The Court requires no prosecutor to seek an enhancement, approves no improper charging basis, and decides no unpresented constitutional challenge or defect in notice or predicates. The holding concerns Amendment 506's prior-record exclusion, not every conceivable statutory or guideline enhancement.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Stinson limits commentary by statute. Neal preserves distinct lawful statutory/guideline measures, not replacement of Congress's benchmark; MCI examines actual delegated power rather than forbidding every consequential policy change. The text resolves this issue without deciding general Commission entitlement to Chevron deference; Auer supplies no contrary rule.

##### The maximum benchmark and proximity remain distinct

**Holding:** Identifying the enhanced maximum does not require imposing that maximum in every covered case. Section 994(h)'s at-or-near language leaves lawful implementation, adjustments and departures within the governing statutes and guidelines; the Court sets no numerical proximity threshold and rejects Amendment 506 on the benchmark defect without a separate quantitative near holding.

**Proposition-level authority:** Kennedy's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Thomas; six direct joins.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No percentage, every-case maximum sentence, aggregated maximum for multiple counts, or probation rule is adopted; §3584 term arrangement remains distinct.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Gonzales preserves separately authorized choices at its statutory scope and neither determines this benchmark nor compels every sentence to reach it.

##### Invalid commentary cannot authorize these sentence reductions

**Holding:** Amendment 506's retroactive designation cannot supply lawful authority under §3582(c)(2) for a reduction based on its invalid benchmark. The reviewed sentencing components must be reversed and remanded for proceedings consistent with that invalidity, preserving the separate statutory gates, discretionary choices, unreviewed claims and respondent-specific posture.

**Proposition-level authority:** Kennedy's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Thomas; six direct joins.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Section 3582(c)(2) requires a sentence based on a range lawfully lowered under §994(o), consideration of applicable §3553(a) factors and consistency with policy statements; the defendant, Bureau of Prisons Director or court may initiate consideration, and an authorized reduction remains discretionary.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Reverse the affirmed LaBonte reduction from 188 to 151 months and the Amendment 506 premises for Hunnewell's and Dyer's possible-reduction remands, 6–3 each. Dyer's non-sentencing §2255 dismissal and Piper's separate appeal are untouched. No original term is automatically reinstated, replacement sentence fixed or every final career-offender judgment reopened.

### Almendarez-Torres v. United States, No. 96-6839 — 1998-03-24

[Record](../records/almendarez_torres_v_united_states_merits_1998-03-24.md#public-projection)

#### Event

Almendarez-Torres v. United States, No. 96-6839. Argued October 14, 1997; decided March 24, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit, reviewing its affirmance of an 85-month sentence. The question is whether the prior aggravated-felony conviction in 8 U.S.C. §1326(b)(2) is an element of aggravated unlawful reentry or a sentencing factor that need not appear in the indictment.

The September 1995 indictment charged unlawful reentry under §1326 without alleging the prior aggravated-felony convictions. At his December 1995 plea, Almendarez-Torres admitted the prior convictions and deportation; he preserved his objection that the indictment authorized no more than §1326(a)'s two-year maximum. The District Court imposed the enhanced sentence in March 1996.

#### Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate at argument and decision.

#### Public Action

The Court reverses the Fifth Circuit's judgment and remands for resentencing under the charged offense.

#### Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Souter and Ginsburg.
**Opposing:** O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer.

The enhanced sentence is vacated. On the existing indictment and plea, the sentence may not exceed §1326(a)'s two-year maximum. The guilty plea is not withdrawn and no acquittal is entered. Whether another source of law permits a further proceeding on a properly charged aggravated offense is not decided.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Scope and relationship to judgment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | Scalia | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg | Statutory element classification, charging consequence and resentencing remedy |
| Opinion announcing the judgment, Part II | Scalia | Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg | Constitutional-avoidance explanation; no definitive constitutional classification rule |
| Concurrence in Part I and in the judgment | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Definitive constitutional ground for the element classification; does not join Part II's reservation of that question |

#### Holdings

##### Section 1326(b)(2) requires an aggravated-offense allegation before its higher maximum may apply

**Holding and operative rule:** The prior aggravated-felony conviction specified in §1326(b)(2) is an element of the aggravated unlawful-reentry offense. It must be alleged in the federal indictment and, unless validly admitted as an element of the charged offense, proved to the jury beyond a reasonable doubt; an admission to the fact of a prior conviction does not cure its omission from this indictment, so the existing conviction permits no sentence above §1326(a)'s two-year maximum.

**Authority:** Part I of Scalia's opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg. These five expressly adopt the statutory rule, its ordinary element consequences and the remedy. Four explain the construction through constitutional avoidance; Stone-Zsela gives a definitive constitutional ground separately. No majority adopts a general constitutional rule governing all facts that increase a statutory maximum.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that subsection (a)'s complete unlawful-reentry offense and two-year maximum must be distinguished from subsection (b)(2)'s aggravated offense carrying a twenty-year maximum. The prior conviction authorizes a different punishment ceiling; it does more than inform selection of punishment within the ordinary range. The provisions' cross-references permit that element construction. Hamling requires the indictment to state the offense's elements, and Gaudin requires proof of an actual element to the jury beyond a reasonable doubt when it has not validly been admitted. Almendarez-Torres admitted the prior convictions, but the indictment did not charge that aggravating element. His factual admission therefore does not answer the preserved objection to the punishment legally authorized by this charge.

The traditional use of recidivism at sentencing does not decide this statute's classification. McMillan addressed a minimum within an unchanged maximum, and Watts permits ordinary sentencing findings within conviction-based authority while reserving element evasion and other constitutional limits. Custis addresses collateral attacks on existing convictions, not the indictment's content. Old Chief confirms that prior-conviction status can be proved without unnecessary prejudicial detail; that evidentiary concern does not require treating status as a sentencing factor. The decision does not determine a subsidiary standard-of-proof claim, invalidate the admitted prior convictions, select a replacement sentence, or decide facts that do not increase the statutory maximum.

**Precedent treatment:** Hamling and Gaudin govern the consequences of the statutory classification; McMillan, Watts, Custis and Old Chief retain the distinct boundaries stated below.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Hamling v. United States:** Applied to require an indictment alleging each element of the aggravated offense.
- **United States v. Gaudin:** Applied after statutory classification; its jury-and-proof rule for actual elements remains controlling without deciding all sentencing classifications.
- **McMillan v. Pennsylvania:** Distinguished because its mandatory minimum left the statutory maximum unchanged; its limited holding remains intact.
- **United States v. Watts / United States v. Putra:** Preserved for ordinary sentencing within punishment authorized by the conviction; it does not resolve this higher-maximum classification.
- **Custis v. United States:** Distinguished as a collateral-review decision; the existing limits on attacking prior convictions remain intact.
- **Old Chief v. United States:** Applied to the distinction between element status and the form of adequate prior-conviction proof; no broader compulsory-stipulation rule is created.

#### Law After Decision

Section 1326(b)(2)'s higher range requires the aggravated-offense element and the corresponding charging and proof protections. This statutory rule binds lower courts. The Court establishes no general constitutional prior-conviction exception and no general constitutional rule that every maximum-increasing fact is an element. Ordinary consideration of prior convictions within an independently authorized range remains available, subject to governing law.

#### Separate Writings

**Scalia, joined by Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg, Part II.** These Justices regard the tenfold increase in maximum punishment as presenting serious doubt about Congress's ability to assign the aggravating fact solely to a sentencing judge. The element reading is fairly available, so they apply constitutional avoidance and decline to decide that ultimate question. McMillan's unchanged ceiling and Watts's ordinary sentencing setting do not eliminate the doubt. The risk of prejudice from prior-conviction evidence can be addressed through adequate admissions or other proper proof rather than removal of an element from the charge.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in Part I and in the judgment.** The constitutional allocation turns on the fact's legal effect. A fact necessary to increase the authorized maximum from two years to twenty years performs the work of an element and must be charged and either validly admitted as an element of that offense or proved to a jury beyond a reasonable doubt. Legislative placement cannot transfer that work entirely to the sentencing judge. Gaudin protects element determination; Watts permits ordinary judicial sentencing findings only within conviction-based authority. Custis concerns collateral attack, and Old Chief demonstrates how adequate status proof can avoid unnecessary prejudice. The admitted facts here do not cure an indictment that never charged the aggravated offense. This position permits consideration of prior convictions within a separately authorized range, preserves Custis's collateral-review limits, requires no prejudicial details when adequate admission or stipulation establishes the element, and decides no sentencing fact that leaves the statutory maximum unchanged.

**O'Connor, joined by Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer, dissenting.** Subsection (b)(2)'s recidivist subject, reciprocal cross-references and punishment structure make it a penalty provision for the alien already described in subsection (a). Existing conviction status differs from facts about commission of the present crime, and Congress may treat that prior adjudication as a sentencing factor in this setting. The admission supports the application but is not a substitute for their statutory classification. These Justices acknowledge that McMillan and Watts do not alone resolve an increased ceiling; the distinct recidivism ground does that work. They would affirm without adopting unlimited legislative power to remove offense elements or resolving an unpresented subsidiary proof-burden question.

#### Procedure After Action

The case returns through the Fifth Circuit for resentencing. The enhanced sentence cannot remain in effect under the existing indictment and plea; the sentencing court must act within §1326(a)'s maximum. No new aggravated-offense proceeding or particular replacement term is ordered.
