## Standards and Tests selection: Congressional reopening of completed private federal judgments

### Congressional reopening of completed private federal judgments

**Governing question:** Whether Congress may, by changing the law previously applicable to a completed private action, require an Article III court to vacate its final judgment and reinstate the same litigation.

**Current rule:** Article III protects the conclusive disposition of a private case by a federal court once its judgment has become final through the ordinary judicial process. Congress may amend substantive or procedural law, change the law while a case remains pending, expressly make a new limitations rule applicable to pending cases subject to other constitutional limits, change the law governing the future operation of an injunction within the applicable remedial framework, and authorize judicial relief through constitutionally permissible procedures, including reopening on valid Rule 60(b) grounds. It may not, solely by changing the law previously applicable to a completed private action, require an Article III court to vacate that final judgment and reinstate the same litigation. Finality does not disappear because Congress leaves eligibility and the underlying merits to later independent judicial determination: the judicial act set aside is the completed judgment itself, and an objectively defined class with retained adjudication answers only the separate objection that Congress selected parties for liability. Section 27A(b) is unconstitutional to that extent and cannot require a court to reinstate an action whose private judgment had become final through the ordinary judicial process. Congress may not in any case require judges to adopt particular factual findings or impose predetermined liability.

**Authority by component:**

- Article III finality rule, its qualifications, its application to a final unappealed limitations dismissal and the extent of §27A(b)'s invalidity: Plaut v. Spendthrift Farm, Inc. — Congress may not, solely by changing the applicable law, require an Article III court to reopen a final private judgment, April 18, 1995. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas; those five direct rationale joins control every proposition, qualification, and limit. Breyer concurs only in the judgment, on his closed-class ground, and joins no proposition; Stevens, Souter, and Ginsburg dissent.

**Present operation effective:** Plaut v. Spendthrift Farm, Inc., April 18, 1995.

**Exceptions and limits:** The rule is confined to legislative commands of this kind and leaves existing law in place. It does not hold that every statute affecting a final judgment is invalid or decide every form of later judicial reopening. Section 27A(a), the application of §27A(b) to litigation still pending on direct review, any due-process question, severability beyond withholding this application, and the Government's waiver of its own defenses are not decided. Fraud, reliance, causation, liability, damages, and ordinary merits defenses are not adjudicated. Morgan Stanley & Co. v. Pacific Mutual Life Insurance Co., May 23, 1994, affirmed by an equally divided Court without opinion, established no precedent on this question, which Plaut decides as one of first impression. Breyer's closed-class ground and the dissent's statutory-entitlement position are noncontrolling.

**Related current rules:** Federal rules applied to parties govern still-open direct review concerns judicial retroactivity alone, which supplies no reopening entitlement; Beam's finality limit on judicial retroactivity is consistent with, but does not itself decide, this limit on legislative reopening. Statutory temporal reach and new consequences for completed conduct separates express temporal direction from constitutional authority; because §27A(b) expressly directs reinstatement, the ordinary presumption against retroactivity cannot avoid the constitutional question. Robertson's rule that Congress may change the law governing pending cases remains intact; Plaut answers, for completed private judgments, the final-judgment question Robertson left open.

**Holdings navigation:** Plaut v. Spendthrift Farm, Inc. — Congress may not, solely by changing the applicable law, require an Article III court to reopen a final private judgment.

## Standards and Tests selection: Presented questions, subsidiary arguments and exceptional Supreme Court review

### Presented questions, subsidiary arguments and exceptional Supreme Court review

**Governing question:** When an issue outside the stated certiorari question may be considered.

**Current rule:** Rule 14.1(a) creates a strong prudential presumption against deciding questions neither stated in the petition nor fairly included, subject to exceptional circumstances. A distinct intervention denial is not fairly included merely because reversing it would permit reaching a settlement-vacatur question; discussion in the petition’s body and merits briefing does not alone suffice. A consistently asserted Teague defense may be subsidiary where it limits application of the very constitutional rule and habeas relief challenged by the petition. Geographic tailoring is fairly included in a challenge to exclusion from designated speech zones. An exceptional extension requires its own supported basis; adversarial briefing, preservation, logical priority or usefulness alone does not enlarge the questions. The duty to notice jurisdictional defects remains separate. A respondent may defend the existing favorable judgment on a record-supported alternative rejected below when doing so neither enlarges its own rights nor reduces the adversary’s rights under that judgment. The Court may also elect to consider a deliberately disavowed legal argument supporting the same preserved claim when it falls within the question presented and can be resolved on the established record without a new factual theory or additional findings. That discretion imposes no duty to consider abandoned grounds and does not erase limits on distinct claims, relief, factual stipulations or missing facts.

#### Plaut v. Spendthrift Farm, Inc.

**Docket or dockets:** No. 93-1121.
**Decided:** April 18, 1995.

##### Congress may not, solely by changing the applicable law, require an Article III court to reopen a final private judgment

**Holding:** Under Morgan Stanley, Article III permits Congress to require reinstatement under §27A(b) of a qualifying private §10(b) action even though its limitations dismissal became final without appeal before the provision's December 19, 1991 enactment: the action must have commenced on or before June 19, 1991, been dismissed as time barred after that date, and been timely under the jurisdiction's law, including retroactivity principles, as it existed June 19, 1991, with a reinstatement motion filed within sixty days after enactment. The court independently determines those cumulative conditions and, when they are satisfied, must reinstate without discretionary balancing, retaining independent responsibility for all claims, defenses, and remedies; this expressly retrospective entitlement does not authorize legislative findings, dictated liability, or unrestricted reopening of completed merits judgments.

**Proposition-level authority:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas. Those five direct rationale joins control every proposition, qualification, and limit stated here. Breyer concurs only in the judgment, on a narrower ground, and joins no proposition; Stevens, Souter, and Ginsburg dissent. No combination of separate rationales is necessary.

**Material application:** Finality is itself an Article III protection; it does not disappear merely because Congress leaves the underlying merits undecided. Petitioners' action was not pending. It was dismissed as time barred on August 13, 1991, and the dismissal became final without appeal before §27A(b)'s December 19, 1991 enactment. Section 27A(b) does not change the law for litigation still pending; it commands a court to vacate that completed judgment and reinstate the same litigation because the law that produced the dismissal has since changed, and the February 11, 1992 motion sought exactly that vacatur and reinstatement. The statute's objective class and retained adjudication answer the contention that Congress selected parties for liability, but not the finality objection: the judicial act Congress sets aside is the completed dismissal itself, and later independent adjudication cannot restore its conclusiveness. Because §27A(b) expressly directs reinstatement, Landgraf's and Rivers's rules of statutory construction cannot avoid the constitutional question. Morgan Stanley's affirmance by an equally divided Court established no precedent, so the question is decided as one of first impression.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Morgan Stanley & Co. v. Pacific Mutual Life Insurance Co., May 23, 1994: its affirmance by an equally divided Court, 4–4, issued no opinion and established no precedent; it is neither applied nor distinguished, and the Article III question is decided as one of first impression.
- Robertson v. Seattle Audubon Society, March 25, 1992: answers, for completed private judgments, the final-judgment question Robertson left open; its rule that Congress may change the law governing pending cases remains intact.
- United States v. Klein, 80 U.S. (13 Wall.) 128: applies its bar on legislative prescription of judicial results; it continues to forbid legislatively dictated findings or outcomes in any case.
- McCullough v. Virginia, 172 U.S. 102: applies its protection of completed judicial determinations against legislative displacement; it supports the finality rule without being extended to every statute that affects a judgment.
- James B. Beam Distilling Co. v. Georgia, 501 U.S. 529: its finality limit on judicial retroactivity is consistent with, but does not itself decide, the limit on legislative reopening; its force is unchanged.
- Pennsylvania v. Wheeling & Belmont Bridge Co., 59 U.S. (18 How.) 421: distinguishes new law governing the future operation of continuing equitable relief within the applicable remedial framework; that prospective setting does not authorize vacating a completed private judgment.
- United States v. Sioux Nation of Indians, 448 U.S. 371: distinguishes the Government's waiver of its own defense; it does not concern a command to reopen a private defendant's final judgment.
- Chase Securities Corp. v. Donaldson, 325 U.S. 304: remains a due-process revival authority only; it supplies no answer to the Article III question.
- Lampf, Pleva, Lipkind, Prupis & Petigrow v. Gilbertson, 501 U.S. 350: supplies the limitations rule under which the August 13, 1991 dismissal was entered and to which §27A responds; its force is unchanged.
- Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244, and Rivers v. Roadway Express, Inc., 511 U.S. 298: retained; their presumption against statutory retroactivity does not avoid the question because §27A(b) expressly directs reinstatement, and Congress's power to prescribe expressly retrospective rules for pending litigation remains subject to other constitutional limits.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that finality is itself an Article III protection. It does not disappear merely because Congress leaves the underlying merits undecided. Morgan Stanley's affirmance by an equally divided Court established no precedent, so the question is one of first impression. Robertson permits Congress to change the law governing pending cases and left the final-judgment question open. Klein and McCullough supply the governing principle: Congress may not prescribe the results of cases or legislatively revise completed adjudications. Petitioners' action was not pending. It was dismissed as time barred on August 13, 1991, and the dismissal became final without appeal before §27A(b)'s December 19, 1991 enactment. Section 27A(b) does not change the law for litigation still pending; it commands a court to vacate that completed judgment and reinstate the same litigation because the law that produced the dismissal has since changed. The February 11, 1992 motion sought exactly that vacatur and reinstatement. The principal contrary argument is that §27A(b) dictates no finding or liability, because courts would independently determine eligibility and then decide the securities claims, defenses, and remedies. The statute's objective class and retained adjudication answer the contention that Congress selected parties for liability, but not the finality objection: the judicial act Congress sets aside is the completed dismissal itself, and later independent adjudication cannot restore its conclusiveness. Because §27A(b) expressly directs reinstatement, Landgraf's and Rivers's rules of statutory construction cannot avoid the constitutional question. The rule is confined to legislative commands of this kind. Congress may amend substantive or procedural law, prescribe expressly retrospective rules for pending litigation, and authorize judicial relief through constitutionally permissible procedures. A court's reopening on valid Rule 60(b) grounds is judicial action under general rules, not legislative displacement of the judgment. Section 27A(b) is therefore unconstitutional to the extent it requires reopening this dismissal; the reinstatement motion was properly denied, and the Sixth Circuit's judgment is affirmed. The Court makes no determination of the underlying securities claims.

**Qualifications:** The rule leaves existing law in place. Congress may change the law while a case remains pending. It may expressly make a new limitations rule applicable to pending cases, subject to other constitutional limits. It may change the law governing the future operation of an injunction within the applicable remedial framework, as Wheeling Bridge illustrates. It may not require judges to adopt particular factual findings or impose predetermined liability in any case; Klein and McCullough forbid that legislative adjudication. Relief from a final judgment through constitutionally permissible judicial procedures, including reopening on valid Rule 60(b) grounds, remains available.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court does not decide §27A(a), the application of §27A(b) to litigation still pending on direct review, any due-process question, or severability beyond withholding this application. It does not hold that every statute affecting a final judgment is invalid, decide every form of later judicial reopening, or address the Government's waiver of its own defenses. Fraud, reliance, causation, liability, damages, and ordinary merits defenses are not adjudicated; the affirmance establishes none of them.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The judgment of the Sixth Circuit is affirmed. The August 13, 1991 dismissal remains final, and the February 11, 1992 reinstatement motion was properly denied. Section 27A(b) is unconstitutional to the extent it requires the district court to reopen that dismissal. Supreme Court review is complete; no remand is ordered, and no determination of the underlying securities claims is made.

**Material judgment:** Affirmed, 6–3. Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas support affirmance; Breyer concurs in the judgment only, on the ground that §27A(b) reopens final judgments in a closed, identifiable class of cases without general prospective application. Stevens dissents, joined by Souter and Ginsburg; they would reverse and remand for reinstatement. Breyer's closed-class ground and the dissent's statutory-entitlement position are noncontrolling.

## Event

Lexecon Inc. v. Milberg Weiss Bershad Hynes & Lerach, No. 96-1482. Plenary merits decision, March 3, 1998; argued November 10, 1997. On writ of certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) to the Ninth Circuit. The question is whether an MDL transferee court may use 28 U.S.C. §1404(a) to assign a transferred action to itself for trial despite §1407(a)’s remand command. Lexecon’s Illinois diversity tort action was transferred to Arizona for coordinated pretrial proceedings. The Arizona court entered pretrial dispositions, denied requests to suggest remand and retained the defamation claim for a defense trial judgment; the Ninth Circuit sustained the self-transfer. Lexecon preserved the objection through remand requests, mandamus and direct appeal.

## Public Action

## Holdings

### I. The MDL transferee cannot defeat mandatory return through self-transfer

**Holding and operative rule:** Unless the transferred action has previously terminated, §1407(a) requires the Panel to remand it to the original district at or before completion of coordinated pretrial proceedings. Section 1404(a) does not authorize the transferee court to retain that continuing action for trial by transferring it from itself to itself.

**Authority:** Part I is joined by all nine Justices and supplies direct statutory authority.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 1407 authorizes coordination for pretrial proceedings and commands return of each continuing action. The Panel may separate and remand particular claims earlier; an action that has actually terminated needs no return. Changing the asserted venue basis does not terminate the action. Addressing the command to the Panel does not authorize the transferee court to prevent its performance. Section 1404(a)’s general permission to transfer to another eligible district or division coexists with, rather than defeats, this specific temporary allocation. Section 1407(f) permits only rules consistent with Acts of Congress, so the cited self-assignment practice cannot alter the return obligation. Subsection (h)’s specific Clayton Act trial-transfer authorization does not confer that power in this tort action. The investment in coordinated pretrial work is substantial, but efficiency cannot delete the enacted sequence. A later lawful transfer by the proper court and consent or waiver questions not presented remain open.

### II. The preserved trial-forum error requires correction of this judgment

**Holding and operative rule:** A party that preserved its §1407 return objection need not prove that a different jury would have reached a different verdict to obtain correction of the resulting unauthorized trial forum. This uncured violation requires setting aside the defamation trial judgment and enforcing statutory remand.

**Authority:** Part II is joined by all nine Justices; its remedy follows the mandatory return right asserted here.

**Controlling explanation:** Lexecon repeatedly requested remand, sought mandamus and then pursued the promised direct appeal. The possibility that Arizona could originally have been a proper venue does not supply the procedure Congress required after an MDL transfer. Caterpillar preserves a completed judgment when the jurisdictional defect actually disappeared before trial; the prohibited retention for this trial never received an equivalent cure. Good Real Property rejects inventing dismissal of a substantive action as a sanction for administrative delay. This case instead directly enforces a particular allocation of the trial forum without extinguishing the cause of action. No constitutional or subject-matter-jurisdiction label is necessary, and the Court adopts no universal rule that every procedural error defeats a completed judgment. The statutory return must occur before any independently lawful later transfer; no permanent right against such a transfer is adjudicated.

### III. Authorized pretrial work and unreviewed tort issues remain separate

**Holding and operative rule:** The improper self-transfer does not automatically invalidate decisions made within authorized pretrial proceedings or decide the merits of the underlying tort claims. Properly remaining appellate challenges must be addressed in their own posture; the unpreserved request for prospective application does not alter this disposition.

**Authority:** Part III is joined by all eight Associate Justices. Stone-Zsela agrees with these dispositions without joining an additional rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** The malicious-prosecution and abuse-of-process rulings and the Cotchett Rule 54(b) judgment are pretrial dispositions, not the defamation trial verdict. Lexecon disputed the Rule 54(b) exercise of discretion without denying power to enter an authorized pretrial judgment. Those distinctions prevent wholesale reinstatement of every claim merely because trial retention was unlawful. The argument that the defamation claim should independently have been dismissed before trial remains for proper consideration, not a new factual or tort-law ruling here. The prospectivity request was not presented below or in opposition to review and supplies no basis for a different result in this case. No comprehensive retroactivity doctrine, new waiver finding or adjudication of a later transfer is adopted.

## Precedent Treatment

- Caterpillar’s completed-judgment protection requires an actual pretrial cure; the preserved trial-forum defect here is distinguished as uncured. Its statutory remand duty remains mandatory.
- Good Real Property preserves mandatory administrative duties while rejecting an unstated forfeiture-dismissal sanction; direct enforcement of the specified trial allocation is different.
- City of Chicago retains the distinction between statutory power and convenience or discretionary retention; it supplies no authority to enlarge §1407’s temporary pretrial grant.

## Law After Decision

Section 1407’s return command cannot be displaced by a transferee’s §1404 self-transfer. Authorized pretrial adjudication remains distinct from unauthorized trial retention, and a preserved objection secures the statutory forum remedy without independent proof that the verdict would differ. No general subject-matter-jurisdiction invalidity, bar on consensual arrangements or decision about a later lawful transfer follows.
