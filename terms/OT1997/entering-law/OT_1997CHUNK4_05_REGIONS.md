#### Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244

**Docket or dockets:** No. 92-757.

**Decided:** 1994-04-26.

For the other questions in Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 1994-04-26, No. 92-757, see Federal Courts — Determine statutory temporal reach before applying the presumption against new retrospective consequences.

##### Section 102 does not add its new damages to this preenactment conduct

**Holding:** Section 102’s new compensatory and punitive liability does not attach to discriminatory conduct completed before November 21, 1991, because it adds legal consequences without sufficient congressional direction for that reach. Its Title VII compensation supplements, rather than replaces, §706(g) relief and excludes backpay, interest on backpay and other relief authorized by that provision; punitive damages have the distinct statutory predicates of malice or reckless indifference to federally protected rights and exclude government, government-agency and political-subdivision respondents. No such damages award or predicate finding is made here.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stevens’s §102 application, joined by Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Souter and Ginsburg, controls by five votes. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas independently reach the same unavailability of new damages through their concurrence. Blackmun disagrees about compensation and reserves punitive damages.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Bennett and Bowen: applied to the new monetary burden attached to completed conduct.
- Bradley: distinguished; the collateral fee award did not create this new compensation or punishment for the underlying private conduct.
- Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools, 503 U.S. 60 (1992): distinguished; appropriate relief in an implied Title IX action does not enlarge Title VII’s earlier express remedial scheme.
- Robertson v. Seattle Audubon Society, 503 U.S. 429 (1992): distinguished; its express substitution of law governing identified pending disputes does not supply the temporal direction missing here.

##### The jury election depends on an available statutory damages claim

**Holding:** Under §102(c), if the complaining party seeks compensatory or punitive damages available under that section, any party may demand a jury trial, and the court must not inform the jury of the statutory monetary limits. Because §102 damages are unavailable for this preenactment conduct, its conditional jury provision supplies no independent right to a new trial; this conclusion does not prohibit applying a genuinely procedural change to an appropriate future procedural act in another case.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stevens’s jury portion, joined by Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Souter and Ginsburg, supplies the controlling five-Justice explanation. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas reach the same conditional conclusion in their concurrence; Blackmun would permit a jury election because he would make compensation available.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Kaiser: applied to the enacted condition linking the jury option to §102 damages.
- Bradley: distinguished; current-law treatment of collateral matters does not erase an express condition on a new remedy or automatically reopen a completed trial.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The rule is provision-specific, not a declaration that the whole 1991 Act is inapplicable in pending cases. Section 402(a)'s enactment-date clause, §109(c)'s later-conduct limitation for extraterritorial amendments, and §402(b)'s exception for disparate-impact complaints filed before March 1, 1975 with initial decisions after October 30, 1983 remain distinct. Judicial retroactivity under Harper does not control legislative reach. An express congressional direction concerning the temporal operation of a statute resolves the threshold statutory question, subject to applicable constitutional limits. Such a direction does not alone determine whether Congress may constitutionally require reconsideration of an already completed Article III judgment. The present case concerns statutory consequences for earlier conduct in litigation properly before the courts and does not decide that separate final-judgment issue.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The Fifth Circuit judgment is affirmed, 8–1. No new damages trial, award or remand is ordered, and the existing equitable-remedy judgment and unreviewed constructive-discharge findings remain intact.

#### Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244

##### Determine statutory temporal reach before applying the presumption against new retrospective consequences

**Holding:** When Congress has prescribed a civil statute’s temporal reach, courts apply that direction subject to constitutional limits; absent sufficient direction, they ask whether applying the provision would impair rights possessed when a party acted, increase liability for past conduct, or impose new duties concerning completed transactions. If it would, the provision does not govern those events without clear congressional intent; a pending case or reference to earlier facts alone does not establish such an effect, and prospective relief, jurisdiction, procedure and collateral matters require examination of what the particular provision actually changes rather than an automatic reopening of completed acts.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stevens’s general-framework portion, joined by Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Souter and Ginsburg; five Justices adopt this organizing rule. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas concur on a distinct relevant-activity approach, not as additional joins at this level of generality.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Kaiser Aluminum & Chemical Corp. v. Bonjorno, 494 U.S. 827 (1990): applied to make statutory temporal direction the starting inquiry.
- Bowen v. Georgetown University Hospital, 488 U.S. 204 (1988): applied to the presumption against retrospective legal burdens; no automatic civil constitutional prohibition is inferred.
- Bennett v. New Jersey, 470 U.S. 632 (1985): applied to protect substantive obligations fixed under earlier law.
- Bradley v. School Board of Richmond, 416 U.S. 696 (1974): retained in its collateral litigation-fee setting and reconciled with the new-consequence inquiry, not overruled.
- Harper v. Virginia Department of Taxation, 509 U.S. 86 (1993): distinguished as judicial retroactivity; its equal-application rule remains unchanged.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The rule is provision-specific, not a declaration that the whole 1991 Act is inapplicable in pending cases. #### Thomas Jefferson University v. Shalala, 512 U.S. 504

**Docket or dockets:** No. 93-120.

**Decided:** 1994-06-24.

##### The redistribution provision reasonably limits newly shifted educational costs

**Holding:** Under the governing rule giving an administering agency's interpretation of its own regulation controlling weight unless plainly erroneous or inconsistent with that regulation, the Secretary reasonably construes 42 C.F.R. §413.85(c) to bar increased reimbursement resulting from transferring previously medical-college-borne educational costs to the hospital's patient-care account, even when the underlying clinical education is generally eligible. That construction sustains the challenged denial for the reporting year ending June 30, 1985; it does not establish a universal historic-dollar ceiling, make every newly measured expense ineligible, or decide the separate community-support ground.

**Proposition-level authority:** Justice Kennedy's Opinion of the Court, Part I, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Scalia and Souter. Five participating Justices directly adopt this regulatory construction, its application and its limits.

**Material application:** Section 413.85(a)(1) provides that allowable cost may include the net cost of approved educational activities under paragraph (g), apportioned for Medicare utilization under §413.50. Permission to include those costs is not an unconditional payment command. Paragraph (g) deducts tuition revenue and includes trainee stipends, teacher compensation and other direct and indirect educational-activity costs under Medicare cost-finding principles. The recodified paragraph (a)(2) directs payment for approved residency programs in medicine, osteopathy, dentistry and podiatry to §413.86 for cost reporting periods beginning on or after July 1, 1985. That later-period exception does not itself disallow the reporting year ending June 30, 1985, and the Court fixes no later-period payment or base-year amount.

Section 413.17(a), subject to the separate exception in paragraph (d), includes the cost of services, facilities and supplies furnished by organizations related through common ownership or control at the related organization's cost, capped at the price of comparable items that could be purchased elsewhere. It does not independently make an otherwise excluded educational cost allowable. No party's entitlement under paragraph (d) is decided, and its separate exception is not a premise of this holding. Section 1395x(v)(1)(A)'s reasonable-cost system takes direct and indirect costs into account within authorized methods and included-item rules, excludes unnecessary costs in efficient delivery, and bars shifting Medicare costs to non-Medicare patients or the reverse. It does not guarantee payment of every asserted actual expense.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Bowles v. Seminole Rock & Sand Co., 325 U.S. 410 (1945): applies controlling weight to a permissible agency interpretation of its own regulation; the plainly-erroneous-or-inconsistent limit remains operative.
- Udall v. Tallman, 380 U.S. 1 (1965): applies the same own-regulation standard; an alternative reasonable reading does not by itself displace the Secretary's permissible construction.
- Gardebring v. Jenkins, 485 U.S. 415 (1988): preserves the limit where an alternative reading is compelled by the regulation's plain language or other indications of the Secretary's intent at promulgation; no such compulsion is established here.
- Martin v. Occupational Safety and Health Review Commission, 499 U.S. 144 (1991): applies its account of reasonable own-regulation interpretation and administrative expertise; its allocation of interpretive authority under the Occupational Safety and Health Act remains specific to that statutory structure.
- Good Samaritan Hospital v. Shalala, June 7, 1993: preserves reimbursement within lawful cost methods and limitations, including authorized exceptions and independent challenges to unlawful methods; actual expenditure alone does not establish entitlement here.
- MCI Telecommunications Corp. v. American Telephone & Telegraph Co., June 17, 1994: preserves the requirement to respect operative statutory limits; its mandatory-tariff holding does not invalidate this textually supported regulatory restriction or replace the own-regulation standard.
- Director, Office of Workers' Compensation Programs v. Greenwich Collieries, June 20, 1994: preserves the distinction between delegated authority and its actual exercise; its treatment of a general reasonable-doubt policy does not render every statement of regulatory intent nonoperative or create a Medicare proof burden.
- Ohio State University v. Secretary, Department of Health and Human Services, 996 F.2d 122 (6th Cir. 1993): rejects its contrary construction insofar as it excludes this transfer of otherwise eligible clinical-education costs from the redistribution provision; its separate community-support analysis is not resolved.

##### The actual agency ground survives the asserted inconsistency

**Holding:** The Administrator's expressed redistribution ground and the established history of college-borne costs support this denial; a memorandum's silence about redistribution and an intermediary's prior allowance do not, on this record, establish an authoritative contrary interpretation that defeats it. A considered, otherwise permissible interpretive change is not automatically invalid, but contrary text, genuine inconsistency and the required agency explanation must be examined, and a reviewing court may not substitute a new ground or missing finding.

**Proposition-level authority:** Justice Kennedy's Opinion of the Court, Part II, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Scalia and Souter. The same five Justices adopt this answer to the consistency and actual-ground objections; it supports application of the redistribution holding rather than furnishing an alternative reimbursement bar.

**Material application:** Letter 78-7 requires that faculty-service costs would be allowable if incurred directly, be allocated consistently among recipient components, comply with related-organization and comparable-price limits, and satisfy the applicable patient-care relation, documentation, reasonable-allocation and reasonable-amount requirements. Its identified additional nonfaculty costs are limited to direct and space costs. It does not permit duplicative additions where the hospital maintains its own corresponding library, administrative structure, office or clerical support. Those conditions survive; the Court does not read silence about redistribution as an unconditional promise of all overhead. The record does not explain the failure to claim reimbursement before 1974, and the Court does not turn that silence into a finding of community support or an estoppel rule.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court affirms on redistribution alone. It neither endorses nor rejects the separate construction treating all non-Medicare funding as community support; decides no merits of the Administrator's additional university general-and-administrative-cost ruling under §§413.9, 413.85 and Letter 78-7; and orders no reimbursement, penalty, recoupment, reopening of the settled faculty-compensation amounts, or relief on the unadjudicated alternative enforcement claim. The original $2,861,247 disputed difference is not adjudicated as an amount remaining after settlement. Materially different retrospective applications, later §413.86 calculations and entitlement under §413.17(d) remain unresolved. No independently sufficient alternative holding is adopted, and no fractured-rationale synthesis is needed.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Good Samaritan Hospital v. Shalala, June 7, 1993: applies its rule that considered interpretive change is not automatically disqualifying while preserving scrutiny of text, delegation and reasons; unsupported litigation positions receive no automatic controlling weight.
- National Railroad Passenger Corp. v. Boston & Maine Corp., March 25, 1992: applies the requirement of a discernible actual agency rationale; the express redistribution ground satisfies it without a new judicial explanation.
- SEC v. Chenery Corp., 318 U.S. 80 (1943): preserves review on the agency's own ground rather than a substitute invented on review.
- Motor Vehicle Manufacturers Assn. v. State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Co., 463 U.S. 29 (1983): preserves required reasoned agency decisionmaking and the prohibition on appellate rationalizations supplying missing findings.
- Bowen v. Georgetown University Hospital, 488 U.S. 204 (1988): preserves the rejection of unsupported litigation rationalizations; this decision rests on the Administrator's stated rationale and decides no new retroactive-rulemaking question.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The challenged reimbursement denial is affirmed, 5–4, on redistribution alone. No additional reimbursement or remand is ordered. The separate community-support construction and general-and-administrative-cost ruling are neither independently sustained nor rejected; settled faculty amounts and the unadjudicated alternative enforcement claim remain untouched.

#### Shalala v. Guernsey Memorial Hospital

**Docket or dockets:** No. 93-1251.
**Decided:** March 6, 1995.

##### The accounting regulations do not compel immediate recognition of this loss

**Holding:** Section 1395x(v)(1)(A) and 42 C.F.R. §§413.20 and 413.24 do not make every generally accepted accounting recognition rule conclusive for Medicare reimbursement or require immediate recognition of this advance-refunding accounting loss. Provider records remain the accounting base, while lawful reasonable-cost allocation may distribute the relevant expense elements across the periods benefited, subject to the distinct existing allowance and calculation conditions.

**Proposition-level authority:** Kennedy's Parts I–III, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer: five Justices.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Good Samaritan Hospital v. Shalala, 508 U.S. 402 — lawful cost methods and allocation remain distinct from a demand for individualized correction of every claimed shortfall.
- Thomas Jefferson University v. Shalala (June 24, 1994) — its different education-cost regulation does not resolve these accounting terms; no general change to its existing review rule is adopted.
- MCI Telecommunications Corp. v. AT&T Co. (June 17, 1994) — agency action must remain within actual legal authority; no power to contradict a binding regulation is recognized.

##### The manual explains a lawful allocation rather than changing binding law

**Holding:** PRM §233’s application to the advance-refunding timing at issue is interpretive because it explains a permissible application of the existing reasonable-cost regulations rather than imposing a conflicting new legal obligation; APA §553(b)(A) therefore does not require notice and comment for that explanation. The Secretary’s actual adjudicative allocation ground is sustained within existing law, not as authority to evade a binding regulatory command.

**Proposition-level authority:** Kennedy's Parts I–III, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer: five Justices.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Lincoln v. Vigil, 508 U.S. 182 — interpretive or policy material is distinguished from a new binding substantive rule; form does not authorize evasion of statutory procedure.
- SEC v. Chenery Corp., 332 U.S. 194 — lawful adjudication and the agency’s actual stated ground matter; a court cannot supply a different administrative decision.
- Good Samaritan Hospital v. Shalala — reasoned implementation remains subject to governing methods and legal limits.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Section 1395x(v)(1)(A) defines reasonable cost by actual incurred cost, excluding unnecessary cost in efficient delivery of needed services, under regulations specifying methods and included items. The Secretary must consider accepted accounting principles, not necessarily adopt them. Methods may differ with circumstances and employ estimates, limits, per-unit measures, or charges where reasonable. Direct and indirect costs must be allocated so Medicare does not bear non-Medicare costs and the converse; suitable corrective adjustments operate within lawful methods. Section 413.20(a) requires sufficient standardized financial records and statistical data, says accepted accounting and reporting practices are followed, says changes to those systems will not be required to determine payable costs, and uses basic accounts as the starting point for equitable and proper payment. Section 413.24 requires adequate verifiable data, approved cost finding, and accrual accounting: revenue when earned and expense when incurred, regardless of collection or payment. Accrual accounting alone does not compel GAAP reimbursement timing. Sections 413.9 and 413.153 retain reasonable patient-care cost and necessary-and-proper-interest limits.

PRM §233.1 covers advance refunding through new debt initiated on or after July 1, 1983; recall without new debt is excluded and referred to §215. Initiation is provider or official issuer action clearly intending refunding; board authorization, hiring an underwriter, or applying to the issuer are examples, not cumulative requirements. Under §233.2, legal defeasance need not mean the old holders have received principal. For necessary patient-care debt defeased or repurchased through advance refunding, §233.3 treats expense elements separately: new debt issue costs amortize from incurrence to scheduled maturity of the new debt; old unamortized discounts, premiums, and issue costs amortize from new issuance until old holders receive principal, with proportionate treatment for serial bonds; call premiums or penalties are allowed when old holders receive principal, prorated by principal payments for serial bonds. Redemption and miscellaneous expenses and annual authority/trustee fees are allowed as paid or accrued subject to the separate §2305 qualification. Interest on both debts is allowed annually as paid or accrued; new discount/premium amortization adjusts interest, and income from investment of new proceeds offsets interest whether earned directly or through a trust. Individual expense elements, rather than the accounting loss as an undifferentiated sum, determine reimbursement.

Section 233.4 retains necessary-and-proper and reasonable-cost requirements. Available sinking funds count in necessary borrowing, and existing funded depreciation counts when excess borrowing purchases depreciable assets. Ordinarily net allowable refinancing costs cannot exceed costs allowable without refinancing. Compelling factors, such as necessary cash flow or removal of a restrictive borrowing covenant, may justify excess costs upon a showing satisfactory to the intermediary; absent that showing, the annual ceiling is the cost without refinancing. Section 233.5 permits qualifying debt and proceeds in equity-capital calculations but excludes debt and related assets to the extent interest is disallowed under §233.4. No equity-capital award is decided. Exact §2305 allowance conditions are not established, and no expense-specific entitlement dependent on them is adjudicated. Medicare allocation, ordinary calculation limits, and these distinct periods remain in place; no universal amortization period or new amount is fixed.

The Manual lacks regulatory force. APA §553(b)(A)’s interpretive-rule exemption is distinct from the benefits exemption, on which the decision does not rely. The later §1395hh rulemaking provision does not govern this preexisting manual dispute. No agency may use interpretive form or adjudication to contradict binding statutes or regulations.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The Sixth Circuit's advance-refunding timing judgment is reversed and remanded consistently with sustaining the Secretary's determination, subject to existing allowance and calculation rules. The separate interest-offset affirmance is undisturbed. No dollar award or expense-specific entitlement is fixed.

#### Brown v. Gardner

**Docket or dockets:** No. 93-1128.
**Decided:** December 12, 1994.

##### Section 1151 requires causation, not provider fault or accident

**Holding:** Section 1151 requires an injury or aggravation resulting from covered VA hospitalization, medical or surgical treatment, chapter 31 vocational rehabilitation, or examination, producing additional disability or death and not resulting from the veteran's own willful misconduct; it does not additionally require negligence, other provider fault, or an unforeseen untoward accident. Section 3.358(c)(3)'s added fault-or-accident prerequisite is invalid, and Gardner's claim must be redetermined under the surviving statutory conditions without an automatic benefits award.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Souter), unanimous.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc., 467 U.S. 837 (1984): applies the statutory-meaning inquiry before deference; the clear causal standard leaves no authority to add provider fault, without abolishing deference in genuine interpretive gaps.
- King v. St. Vincent's Hospital, 502 U.S. 215 (1991): applies contextual reading of protective veterans legislation and respect for express rather than invented limits; the veterans-protective canon confirms the text instead of replacing it.
- MCI Telecommunications Corp. v. American Telephone & Telegraph Co., 512 U.S. 218 (1994): applies the limit that administrative expertise cannot supply statutory power Congress did not grant; the distinct tariff-modification rule is not extended into a general rejection of agency discretion.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The natural-progress provision is left undisturbed. No complete rule governing consent or necessary consequences is adopted. The separate FTCA coordination sentence remains: for a qualifying judgment under 28 U.S.C. §1346(b), or settlement or compromise under §§2672 or 2677, on or after December 1, 1962, for a disability, aggravation, or death treated as service connected under §1151, benefits for months after finality are withheld until the aggregate otherwise payable equals the total recovery. That is a coordination rule, not a provider-fault requirement; no qualifying recovery is found here.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The Federal Circuit is affirmed. Administrative redetermination must proceed without the invalid fault-or-accident prerequisite, with causation, additional disability and the remaining statutory conditions preserved; benefits are not automatically awarded.

### January 26, 1998 — Allentown Mack Sales & Service, Inc. v. NLRB

[Public Projection](../records/allentown_mack_sales_service_inc_v_nlrb_merits_1998-01-26.md#public-projection). The following complete public blocks preserve this event's action, proposition-specific authority, published positions, procedure and source limits.

#### Event

Allentown Mack Sales & Service, Inc. v. NLRB, No. 96-795. Plenary merits decision, January 26, 1998; argued October 15, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit, 83 F.3d 1483 (1996), which enforced the Board's unfair-labor-practice and bargaining order. The question concerns the evidence an employer must possess before polling employees about an incumbent union's majority support, including the asserted substitution of proof of lost support for the announced reasonable-doubt standard.

Allentown rehired 32 of 45 former unit employees after purchasing the branch. The successor finding and appropriate unit are unchallenged. The ALJ found the procedural polling safeguards satisfied but found insufficient objective doubt. The later secret ballot was 19–13 against representation; that result does not establish what management knew when it announced the poll.

The judgment is reversed, and the case is remanded to the D.C. Circuit with instructions to deny enforcement of the Board order under review. A different majority sustains the facial validity of the Board's good-faith reasonable-doubt threshold for incumbent-union polling.

#### Holdings

##### The announced reasonable-doubt polling threshold is permissible

**Holding and operative rule:** The Board may require an employer to possess a good-faith reasonable doubt, supported by objective considerations, of an incumbent union's majority support before conducting a safeguarded poll. Using the same prerequisite for polling, withdrawing recognition and seeking a Board election is not, by itself, irrational or inconsistent with the Act; objective doubt is uncertainty about majority support, not proof that the union has lost it.

**Authority:** Scalia's Part II, joined by Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies five direct votes.

**Controlling explanation:** The Act protects employee choice and established bargaining relationships. Auciello permits explained stability policies within statutory boundaries, while Fall River separates their rationality from factual application. Neither decides this polling rule in advance. Even a safeguarded employer poll may unsettle employees and the bargaining relationship. The Board may account for that risk before permitting a poll.

The employer argues that evidence sufficient to withdraw recognition makes polling pointless. But an employer may reasonably wish to determine actual preferences before taking the more consequential step of withdrawing recognition; a lawful option to withdraw is not an obligation to do so. A poll therefore retains a confirmatory use. Differences between a poll and a Board election—including the statutory one-year bar after a valid election—do not compel the same consequences or a judicially selected lower polling threshold. An unsuccessful union in an employer poll may seek a Board election.

Sections 7 and 8(c) protect employee choice and noncoercive expression, but do not themselves establish an unconditional right to poll an incumbent unit. Gissel left undecided whether its recognized allowances for reasonable inquiry are mandatory. Compliance with Struksnes safeguards does not alone answer the separate incumbent-polling prerequisite. The rule is permissible, not compelled for every future Board, and does not authorize a concealed requirement of actual minority proof. Certification and contract protections during their lawful duration, statutory representation routes and protection against coercion remain distinct. No freestanding First Amendment question is decided.

##### The presented challenge reaches the standard actually applied

**Holding and operative rule:** A challenge expressly directed to the amount of evidence demanded before polling fairly includes whether the Board applied its announced reasonable-doubt standard to the order under review. That inquiry reaches the associated whole-record application here; it creates no general permission to add an independent factual question merely because merits briefs discuss it.

**Authority:** Scalia's Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas, supplies five direct votes.

**Controlling explanation:** The petition challenges a requirement of so much evidence of lost support that a poll becomes meaningless. Allentown's contention that the agency substituted individual repudiations for reasonable uncertainty concerns that asserted evidentiary demand, not a separate judgment. Hubbard preserves the difference between supporting arguments and enlarged questions. Examining the actual standard and the material reports is necessary to resolve this challenge. The Court does not reopen the successor finding, the unit or independently unpreserved objections.

##### An announced doubt standard cannot silently become minority proof

**Holding and operative rule:** Under NLRA §§10(e)–(f) and APA §706, the Board's finding must be supported by substantial evidence on the record as a whole under the substantive standard actually governing the case. When the announced predicate is objective reasonable doubt, the agency cannot sustain its order by effectively demanding proof approaching actual loss of majority support, or silently convert recurring evidentiary outcomes into a different substantive or proof rule.

**Authority:** Scalia's Parts III–IV have Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas, providing five direct votes for this framework and its application.

**Controlling explanation:** Universal Camera requires relevant evidence a reasonable mind could accept as adequate, considered together with what fairly detracts from the finding. It preserves the Board's choice between fairly conflicting views; it does not permit the governing standard to change during application. Auciello likewise distinguishes circumstantial objective doubt from a numerical showing of actual minority. The agency's §10(c) preponderance burden and the reviewing court's substantial-evidence inquiry remain separate.

An employer's uncertainty may be reasonable without direct repudiations from a counted majority. A course of adverse findings cannot, without a lawful change in rule, redefine doubt as established opposition. Expertise and recurring evidentiary judgments matter, but do not replace the announced legal predicate. Lawful, expressly adopted presumptions or evidentiary rules remain possible, subject to their statutory basis and ordinary review; the Court abolishes no such authority and makes no finding of systematic manipulation. Neither subjective suspicion nor every unverified report necessarily establishes objective doubt. The question remains whether this whole record supports the particular finding under the rule the Board announced.

##### This record requires denial of enforcement

**Holding and operative rule:** The finding that Allentown lacked objective reasonable doubt when it announced the poll is unsupported by substantial evidence on the whole record. The enforcement judgment must therefore be reversed with instructions to deny enforcement of the challenged Board order; the record does not call for another agency weighing under the same or a newly demanding threshold.

**Authority:** Scalia's Part IV has Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas, supplying five direct votes for the application and complete mandate.

**Controlling explanation:** The ALJ accepted six individual expressions and assumed a seventh supported doubt. Those seven are neither a numerical threshold nor the entire evidentiary picture. Marsh's dissatisfaction with representation, Bloch's report that the night shift did not want the union, and Mohr's assessment as shop steward and bargaining-committee member add material circumstantial force. Group reports are not mechanically counted as additional, nonoverlapping repudiations.

The ALJ identified weaknesses in the reports' sources, scope, interview setting and relation to the rehired workforce, and expressly found Mohr's statement insufficient even in combination with the other evidence. The Court does not erase that cumulative assessment. But the Board separately said the employer could not legitimately rely on Mohr because he described the old workforce. His union responsibilities and the substantial continuity of employees make that dismissal untenable. His failure to specify the size of a majority or personally renounce the union does not answer whether management reasonably doubted continued support.

Taken together, the material reports establish objective uncertainty even though they do not establish actual minority. The source and coverage limitations do not adequately sustain this contrary finding under the announced doubt standard. This is not substitution of a preferred inference between fairly conflicting views: the required quantum was effectively raised toward proof of lost support. The later 19–13 poll supplies no retroactive justification. The holding determines the legality of this enforcement request, not present preferences, an independent right to cease bargaining in every setting, or a permanent representation result.

##### The separate remedy objection remains outside review

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 10(e) bars an objection not urged before the Board unless extraordinary circumstances excuse the omission. The separate objection to the bargaining order's explanation and decertification bar was not preserved, and no such excuse is established; that objection is not reviewed.

**Authority:** Part V has all eight Associate Justices. Stone-Zsela agrees only in leaving the objection unreviewed.

**Controlling explanation:** Auciello preserves §10(e)'s distinction between the particular objection presented to the Board and another objection later asserted in court. The preserved threshold and application challenges do not themselves preserve this separate remedial attack. Correcting the predicate of the existing order resolves enforcement without deciding the barred objection or approving every possible bargaining remedy.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Auciello's accepted-offer cutoff remains intact. Its statutory limits, distinction between circumstantial doubt and actual minority, and separate §10(e) requirement are applied; it had not resolved incumbent polling or the weight of these reports.
- Fall River supplies separate review of rational Board policy and supported factual application. Its continuity and successor principles are not reopened, and its rebuttable presumption does not become proof of employee preference.
- Universal Camera supplies whole-record substantial-evidence review, including contrary evidence and the examiner's actual findings, while retaining deference between fairly conflicting views. The Court applies that standard to this unsupported finding rather than replacing it with de novo factfinding.
- Gissel retains its protection of noncoercive expression and its attention to the employment setting. Its reservation concerning mandatory permission for reasonable inquiry is preserved; the Court derives no unconditional polling entitlement from it.
- Hubbard's distinction between supporting arguments and new questions is applied to the wording and challenged order here. No exception based merely on merits briefing is adopted.
- Struksnes's procedural safeguards and the additional advance-notice requirement remain operative. Their satisfaction does not eliminate the separately permissible incumbent-polling prerequisite.

#### Law After Decision

The reasonable-doubt prerequisite remains a permissible policy for polling an incumbent unit with a rebuttable presumption of majority support. The decision adds a controlling application of that rule: objective uncertainty may rest on cumulative circumstantial evidence, and an agency must apply its announced predicate without silently raising it toward proof of actual minority. No headcount or replacement polling test is adopted. The Court's direct majorities govern their respective questions; the statutory invalidity proposal and the application dissent are not controlling law.

The existing safeguards remain: a purpose of determining the truth of the union's majority claim, communication of that purpose, assurances against reprisal, secret ballot, absence of unfair labor practices or a coercive atmosphere, and advance notice of time and place. Threats, reprisals, promises of benefit and coercive questioning remain regulable. Lawful certification and contract protections, representation procedures and the distinct withdrawal standard retain their own predicates. A future lawful policy may alter the polling rule within statutory bounds, but may not supply a retroactively higher prerequisite to revive this order. No independent First Amendment holding or determination of current employee support is made.

#### Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, joined by O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas, concurring in Parts I and III–IV and the judgment and dissenting from Part II.** The Act's protection of employee choice does not adequately justify demanding essentially the same stringent showing before a safeguarded inquiry as before recognition withdrawal or a binding Board election. Those acts have different consequences: inquiry can resolve uncertainty, while withdrawal immediately changes bargaining and an election invokes distinct statutory rules. Stability is legitimate, but cannot make the rebuttable presumption practically untestable. Compliance with safeguards, independent secret-ballot supervision and the absence of a finding that this poll itself coerced employees support the statutory objection. The writing would invalidate the existing unitary polling threshold while preserving neutral purpose, notice, anti-reprisal assurances, secrecy, advance notice and protection against coercive unfair labor practices. It leaves lawful conclusive periods, the withdrawal standard and a prospectively adopted, source-grounded polling policy intact, and decides no independent First Amendment question. The authors also fully join the Court's independent substantial-evidence ground and denial-of-enforcement mandate even though the facial standard survives.

**Breyer, joined by Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg, concurring in Parts I–II and V and dissenting from Parts III–IV and the judgment.** The granted rule challenge does not require an independent factual-sufficiency decision. If that issue is reached, Universal Camera requires respecting a supported agency choice between competing inferences. Objective doubt operates in the labor setting, where interviews, ambiguity about dissatisfaction versus opposition, incomplete sources for group reports, departmental coverage and the retained workforce affect evidentiary force. The ALJ gave actual reasons and expressly assessed Mohr together with the other reports; the adopted assessment need not be recast as a hidden majority-count requirement. Mohr's union role and continuity give his account weight, but do not require the conclusion management urges. These Justices accept that the agency must obey its governing legal and proof standards and reject a categorical bar to circumstantial evidence; they disagree that the actual application crossed those limits. They would affirm the enforcement judgment and order no further agency proceeding.

#### Procedure After Action

The case returns to the D.C. Circuit with instructions to deny enforcement of the Board order under review. This Supreme Court review is complete. No renewed agency application, evidentiary hearing, new poll, election, permanent decertification, present-preference finding or retained Supreme Court jurisdiction is ordered. Ordinary future representation proceedings remain subject to their own lawful predicates and safeguards.

### Statutory temporal reach and new consequences for completed conduct

**Governing question:** How courts decide whether new legislation governs events preceding enactment.

**Current rule:** First determine whether Congress prescribed the statute’s temporal reach. If it did, that direction governs subject to independent constitutional limits. Otherwise ask whether applying the provision would attach new legal consequences to completed events by impairing existing rights, increasing liability for past conduct or imposing new duties concerning completed transactions. Such operation requires clear congressional intent. Pending litigation alone does not answer the question. Procedural, jurisdictional and prospective-relief labels are not substitutes for examining the provision’s actual operation. Legislative correction of a judicial construction, or a purpose to restore an earlier understanding, does not by itself establish retrospective reach; this legislative inquiry differs from the temporal operation of judicial decisions.

**Authority by component:**

- Sequential temporal inquiry: Landgraf v. USI Film Products — Determine statutory temporal reach before applying the presumption against new retrospective consequences, 1994-04-26. Stevens’s general-framework portion, joined by Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Souter and Ginsburg; five Justices adopt this organizing rule. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas concur on a distinct relevant-activity approach, not as additional joins at this level of generality.
- Corrective purpose and distinction from judicial interpretation: Rivers v. Roadway Express, Inc — Corrective legislative purpose does not itself give an amendment retrospective reach, 1994-04-26. Stevens’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Souter and Ginsburg, controls by five votes at this explanatory level. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas accept the judicial/legislative distinction through their separate textual method, not through a presumed full join.

**Present operation effective:** Rivers v. Roadway Express, Inc, 1994-04-26.

**Holdings navigation:** Determine statutory temporal reach before applying the presumption against new retrospective consequences; Corrective legislative purpose does not itself give an amendment retrospective reach.

**Exceptions and limits:** An express congressional direction concerning the temporal operation of a statute resolves the threshold statutory question, subject to applicable constitutional limits. Such a direction does not alone determine whether Congress may constitutionally require reconsideration of an already completed Article III judgment. Landgraf concerns statutory consequences for earlier conduct in litigation properly before the courts and does not decide that separate final-judgment issue.

**Related current rules:** Congressional reopening of completed private federal judgments supplies Morgan Stanley's and Plaut's separate Article III rule for §27A(b).

### Medicare accounting records and lawful allocation explanations

**Governing question:** Whether GAAP recognition and an interpretive manual compel or defeat a reimbursement timing rule.

**Current rule:** Section 1395x(v)(1)(A) and §§413.20 and 413.24 make adequate, verifiable accrual records the accounting base but do not make every GAAP recognition rule conclusive for Medicare reimbursement. Lawful reasonable-cost methods may distribute relevant advance-refunding expense elements across benefited periods. A manual explaining a permissible application of existing regulations is interpretive under APA §553(b)(A), rather than a new conflicting obligation requiring notice and comment. Neither interpretive form nor adjudication permits contradiction of binding law.

**Authority by component:**

- The accounting regulations do not compel immediate recognition of this loss: The accounting regulations do not compel immediate recognition of this loss, 1995-03-06. Kennedy’s opinion of the Court, Parts I–III, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Ginsburg, and Breyer. - The manual explains a lawful allocation rather than changing binding law: The manual explains a lawful allocation rather than changing binding law, 1995-03-06. **Current rule:** The responsible agency's fair and considered interpretation controls unless plainly erroneous or inconsistent with the regulation.
