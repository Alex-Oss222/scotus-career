## Standards and Tests selection: State taxation of qualifying General Allotment Act fee land

### State taxation of qualifying General Allotment Act fee land

**Governing question:** What state taxes Congress authorized on reservation land validly patented in fee under the General Allotment Act.

**Current rule:** The General Allotment Act's fee-land rule and Burke Act proviso authorize ad valorem taxation of land validly patented in fee under that Act, including qualifying land later held by the Nation. The tax follows the land, but patent source and independently applicable federal protection must be determined parcel by parcel. The Indian Reorganization Act does not automatically return every existing fee parcel to tax-protected trust status. This permission does not unmistakably authorize a tax on an Indian owner's sale of the land. The tax's legal incidence controls; securing a transactional sales tax with a lien does not convert it into an authorized land tax.

**Authority by component:**

- Ad valorem permission and parcel-specific limits: County of Yakima v. Confederated Tribes — Ad valorem holding and operative rule, January 14, 1992; Scalia's opinion joined by Stone, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas.
- No implied sales-excise permission and unchanged legal incidence: County of Yakima — Sales-excise holding and operative rule, January 14, 1992; all nine Justices join.

**Present operation effective:** *County of Yakima*, January 14, 1992.

**Holdings navigation:** County of Yakima v. Confederated Tribes and Bands of the Yakima Indian Nation, 502 U.S. 251 (1992) — State taxation of reservation fee land.

## Standards and Tests selection: Indian-country residence, source and state member taxation

### Indian-country residence, source and state member taxation

**Governing question:** What instructions govern indian-country residence, source and state member taxation?

**Current rule:** Absent express congressional authorization, a State may not tax a tribal member's Indian-country income when the member resides there; tribal employment alone is insufficient. Residence encompasses §1151 reservations, dependent Indian communities and allotments with unextinguished Indian titles, including rights-of-way. For resident members, vehicle excise and registration levies constituting prohibited property taxation cannot be saved by collecting the same charges from later purchasers. Genuine apportioned off-country road-use charges and every vehicle-sale tax are not decided. Membership, residence, source, tax character and authorization remain separate; broader outside-residence self-government questions remain open. A State may apply its generally applicable income tax to members living outside Indian country who earn wages from tribal work within it; the Treaty of Dancing Rabbit Creek does not exempt that specified employee income. Protection remains for members both residing and earning the covered tribal income in Indian country.

**Authority by component:**

- Member income protection depends on Indian-country residence and source: Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Sac and Fox Nation — Member income protection depends on Indian-country residence and source, 1993-05-17. O'Connor’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct majority support for this exact proposition; no Marks synthesis.
- The challenged vehicle levies cannot be sustained merely as off-country use charges: Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Sac and Fox Nation — The challenged vehicle levies cannot be sustained merely as off-country use charges, 1993-05-17. O'Connor’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct majority support for this exact proposition; no Marks synthesis.

- Resident taxation and the surviving treaty promise: Resident taxation and the surviving treaty promise, 1995-06-14. Ginsburg’s income portion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; five Justices adopt the off-country-resident treaty construction.

**Present operation effective:** Chickasaw Nation, June 14, 1995, for the off-country-resident treaty construction; Sac and Fox’s residence, source and vehicle-tax components remain.

**Holdings navigation:** Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Sac and Fox Nation, 508 U.S. 114 (1993); Resident taxation and the surviving treaty promise.

#### County of Yakima v. Confederated Tribes and Bands of the Yakima Indian Nation, 502 U.S. 251 (1992)

**Docket or dockets:** Nos. 90-408 and 90-577.
**Decided:** January 14, 1992.

##### Whether state ad valorem taxation may reach reservation land patented in fee under the General Allotment Act

**Holding:** The General Allotment Act's established fee-land rule and the Burke Act proviso authorize state ad valorem taxation of reservation land validly patented in fee under that Act, including qualifying land later held by the Nation. The tax follows the land, but patent source and independently applicable federal protection must be determined parcel by parcel.

**Proposition-level authority:** Scalia's opinion, joined by Stone, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas; these eight Justices support the ad valorem holding. Blackmun dissents on this component.

**Material application:** The stipulated record did not establish every parcel's patent source. The Indian Reorganization Act did not automatically restore every existing fee parcel to tax-protected status, and the specific land-tax authority did not create general personal taxing jurisdiction over reservation Indians.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The holding concerns qualifying General Allotment Act fee land and preserves independently applicable federal protections. It does not rest on alienability alone or authorize a governmental-impact inquiry under Brendale.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The judgment is affirmed and the case remanded with modified instructions for parcel-specific patent-source analysis and relief consistent with the two distinct tax holdings.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Goudy is reaffirmed for taxation of qualifying General Allotment Act fee land. Blackfeet supplies the requirement of unmistakably clear congressional authorization. Mancari requires compatible enactments to operate together. Moe's rejection of general personal taxing jurisdiction is preserved. Brendale's fractured analysis of tribal regulation of nonmember land supplies no governmental-impact test for this statutory tax question.

##### Whether permission to tax the land also authorizes a sales excise imposed on an Indian owner

**Holding:** Congressional permission to tax qualifying fee land ad valorem does not unmistakably authorize a tax imposed on an Indian owner's sale of that land. Securing the transactional tax with a lien does not change its legal incidence.

**Proposition-level authority:** Scalia's opinion states this proposition for all nine Justices; all nine Justices support the sales-excise holding.

**Material application:** The annual property levy is triggered by ownership and burdens the realty; the sales excise is triggered by a transaction. A collection lien cannot enlarge Congress's permission by transforming an unauthorized transactional charge into a land tax.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The sales excise remains prohibited, and remand relief must respect that prohibition.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Blackfeet requires authorization clear at the level of the tax actually imposed. Capoeman's allotment-context discussion of taxation after fee patent does not supply blanket permission for every tax related to land.

#### Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Sac and Fox Nation, 508 U.S. 114 (1993)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 92-259.
**Decided:** May 17, 1993.

##### Member income protection depends on Indian-country residence and source

**Holding:** Absent express congressional authorization, a State may not tax a tribal member’s income earned in Indian country when the member resides there; tribal employment alone does not establish that protection. The residence inquiry includes §1151 reservations, dependent Indian communities and allotments whose Indian titles have not been extinguished, including rights-of-way, rather than only an undiminished formal reservation.

**Proposition-level authority:** O'Connor’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct majority support for this exact proposition; no Marks synthesis.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- McClanahan v. Arizona State Tax Commission, 411 U.S. 164: applied with both residence and income-source predicates.
- Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Citizen Band Potawatomi Indian Tribe, 498 U.S. 505: applied against a reservation-label-only geography.
- County of Yakima v. Confederated Tribes, January 14, 1992: distinguished; limited land-tax authorization supplies no income-tax authority.

##### The challenged vehicle levies cannot be sustained merely as off-country use charges

**Holding:** For tribal members residing in Indian country, the challenged vehicle excise and registration levies fall within the prohibition on state property taxation absent congressional authorization; collecting the same forbidden charges from later purchasers does not save them. Their application to the established residents must be reconsidered on remand; the Court does not decide a genuinely apportioned off-country road-use charge or every tax connected to a vehicle sale.

**Proposition-level authority:** O'Connor’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct majority support for this exact proposition; no Marks synthesis.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Tribal employment or registration alone does not establish protection. Outside-Indian-country self-government claims, genuinely apportioned off-country road-use charges and nonmember taxation are not decided.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Vacate in the reviewed respects and remand for actual member residence, income source and the vehicle levies. Qualifying allotments or other Indian country may resolve residence without deciding every reservation boundary; no blanket member exemption or refund amount.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Moe v. Confederated Salish and Kootenai Tribes, 425 U.S. 463: applied to member vehicle property taxation.
- Washington v. Confederated Tribes of the Colville Indian Reservation, 447 U.S. 134: applied to the substance of the vehicle levies; genuinely tailored off-country use charges remain distinct.

#### Hagen v. Utah, 510 U.S. 399

**Docket or dockets:** No. 92-6281.

**Decided:** 1994-02-23.

##### Congressional diminishment and the jurisdiction asserted at Myton

**Holding:** Congress diminished the Uintah Reservation as to the unallotted lands covered by the opening program: the 1902 operative direction restoring those lands to the public domain remained effective through the 1903–1905 amendments and implementation. Myton's location in that opened area is therefore outside the reservation on this record, and Utah may exercise the territorial criminal jurisdiction asserted over Hagen's offense; opening or fee ownership alone does not establish that result elsewhere.

**Proposition-level authority:** O'Connor's opinion of the Court, joined by Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Ginsburg. These six Justices adopt the statutory construction, its clear-intent basis and its application to this prosecution. There is a direct majority rationale; no fractured-opinion inference is necessary.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The collateral-estoppel issue arising from Ute Indian Tribe v. Utah was omitted from the petition and expressly disavowed by Hagen; the Court does not decide its merits. This case does not adjudicate private titles, all remaining trust or allotted lands, every tract of the larger Uintah and Ouray territory, unrelated tribal powers, or every sovereign's jurisdiction over every offense. The $70,064.48 payment is not relied upon as a purchase of the surplus land. Later population and state administration do not independently change boundaries.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Solem v. Bartlett, 465 U.S. 463: applies its clear-intent inquiry and unequal weighting of text, contemporaneous context and later treatment; distinguishes its nonoperative public-domain reference from the surviving operative command here.
- Rosebud Sioux Tribe v. Kneip, 430 U.S. 584: applies statutory continuity across a sequence of enactments and preserves the rule that no particular formula or fixed payment is indispensable.
- Seymour v. Superintendent, 368 U.S. 351: preserves the distinction between opening and boundary change and the rule that fee ownership within retained boundaries does not itself remove Indian-country status.
- Mattz v. Arnett, 412 U.S. 481: preserves the need for congressional diminishment beyond mere sale and settlement; this sequence supplies the additional direction.
- DeCoteau v. District County Court, 420 U.S. 425: preserves clear congressional cession as sufficient evidence without making its precise words or payment structure mandatory.
- South Dakota v. Bourland, June 14, 1993: preserves its particular displacement of exclusion-derived regulation and its separate residual-authority inquiry; it does not itself establish reservation boundaries.
- Negonsott v. Samuels, February 24, 1993: preserves its express Kansas-specific criminal authorization; it supplies no independent Utah authorization within Indian country.
- Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Sac and Fox Nation, May 17, 1993: preserves the distinct §1151 categories and the need to determine actual Indian-country status.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The Utah Supreme Court is affirmed, 6–3, leaving its reinstatement of Hagen's conviction intact. No adjudication of private titles or general reallocation of regional jurisdiction is ordered.

## Event

South Dakota v. Yankton Sioux Tribe, No. 96-1581. Plenary merits decision, January 26, 1998; argued December 8, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the Eighth Circuit, 99 F.3d 1439, which affirmed a declaration that the 1894 Act left the reservation's exterior boundary unchanged and that the waste-site tract lies within it. Review concerns the ceded, unallotted lands and the declaration's continuing consequences after EPA permitted a compacted-clay liner.

The Tribe did not appeal the separate denial of its injunction and rejection of its asserted regulation of this non-Indian activity. The Waste District supported the State without appealing after construction could proceed. The existing state permit and operative reservation-status declaration remain the subject of the controversy.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate.

## Public Action

The Eighth Circuit's judgment is reversed and the case remanded. The 1894 Act removed the ceded, unallotted settler lands, including the established waste-site tract, from the reservation. The Court does not decide whether the entire reservation was disestablished.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reverse and remand, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The Court of Appeals shall enter judgment recognizing diminishment as to the ceded lands and address properly remaining parcel-specific or statutory jurisdiction questions consistently with that boundary. South Dakota has primary territorial jurisdiction at this site; no universal allocation of every sovereign's authority follows. The unappealed regulatory/injunction ruling and the EPA waiver remain undisturbed.

## Opinion Topology

Stevens delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine adopt the three holdings and their qualifications. No separate writing issues.

## Holdings

### The existing declaration presents a continuing concrete controversy

**Holding and operative rule:** The EPA's waiver of a particular liner requirement does not moot the State's challenge where the operative declaration continues to determine the territorial legal regime governing its issued permit for this identified site. That continuing legal relationship permits review; anticipated similar projects or a wish to erase unfavorable precedent alone would not.

**Authority:** The unanimous Opinion of the Court supplies direct authority.

**Controlling explanation:** Lee requires concrete personal application throughout review and distinguishes limited jurisdictional correction from power to decide the merits. This case concerns an actual permitted facility and an extant declaration classifying its land, rather than a chain of future requests or possible enforcement. Reversing that declaration changes the parties' current legal relationship. The EPA waiver permits clay-lined construction but does not vacate the territorial determination. The Court adopts no rule that an adverse declaration always preserves jurisdiction after every underlying consequence disappears. Nor does jurisdiction to review this declaration reopen the Tribe's separate, unappealed regulatory and injunction ruling.

### The cession and purchase diminished the reservation as to the ceded lands

**Holding and operative rule:** The Act of August 15, 1894 diminished the Yankton Sioux Reservation by removing the ordinary ceded, unallotted lands disposed of under its settler-opening program. The comprehensive surrender and unconditional federal purchase, read with the entire agreement, establish clear congressional intent. Article XVIII's treaty-saving language protects its annuity and associated entitlement assurances without preserving the 1858 boundary over those ceded lands.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join this entire statutory construction, its evidentiary limits and its application to the identified site.

**Controlling explanation:** Solem makes statutory text most probative and gives exceptional, but not conclusive, weight to comprehensive cession coupled with guaranteed compensation. Article I surrenders all tribal claim, right, title and interest in the specified unallotted lands; Article II obligates the United States to pay $600,000. The appropriation and distribution provisions confirm a purchase, not an undertaking merely to sell for the Tribe and remit proceeds. Hagen preserves that clear-cession route even though this Act lacks the operative public-domain restoration command decisive there. No particular formula is indispensable.

Article XVIII is substantial contrary text: it disclaims treaty abrogation, preserves all 1858 provisions as though the agreement had not been made, and separately guarantees continued annuities. Ownership and reservation status can differ, so a sale alone would not answer the Tribe's boundary-preservation argument. But the whole agreement's comprehensive surrender and federal purchase carry territorial significance beyond opening for settlement. Literal preservation of the treaty's possession and exclusion provisions would also contradict the settlement commanded by the transaction. The documented concern over federal annuities, past claims and future entitlements explains the clause's reassurance; its broad words do not undo the specific land surrender. The Court neither deletes those words nor decides every other treaty entitlement.

The state school-section disposition and Article XVII's distinction between ceded and other lands reinforce this reading. A specific school exception and a permanent liquor restriction could also serve purposes within retained boundaries, so neither independently controls. Article VIII's retained agency and school uses caution against total termination. Contemporary common-property and state-law statements corroborate the enacted choice without establishing every signatory's understanding of future boundaries. Conflicting later labels carry little force. State administration, settlement and demographics remain subordinate clues, not events changing boundaries.

Dion's clear-and-plain requirement and the tribal ambiguity canon remain intact. The complete enactment supplies the congressional choice here; genuine unresolved ambiguity would favor retention. No quantified proof burden or demographic threshold is created.

### The supported parcel category determines the mandate's limits

**Holding and operative rule:** The identified waste-site tract falls outside the reservation because it is established as ceded, unallotted land disposed of under this program, not merely because a non-Indian now owns it in fee. The declaration must be corrected on that ground. Total disestablishment and the status or authority associated with other land categories remain undecided.

**Authority:** The unanimous Opinion of the Court adopts this application and bounded remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The reported 1904 homestead deed matches the complaint's parcel and supplies affirmative evidence of the land category. Seymour's distinction between title and reservation boundaries remains controlling; fee ownership alone cannot remove land from a retained reservation. The Court therefore decides the ceded-land question without classifying every allotment, trust parcel, Article VIII agency or school tract, or later acquisition. Separate dependent-community and unextinguished-allotment categories under §1151 retain their own predicates. Hagen likewise requires the territorial holding to remain distinct from every sovereign's powers. Neither the present holding nor remand restores the unappealed tribal regulatory claim, cancels the EPA waiver, orders a composite liner or adjudicates private title. Properly remaining parcel-specific and statutory jurisdiction questions may be addressed below consistently with this boundary ruling.

## Precedent Treatment

- Hagen is applied for an operative congressional-act inquiry, unequal evidentiary weights and geographic limits. Its surviving public-domain restoration command is distinguished from this Act's cession-and-purchase ground; neither mere opening nor fee ownership suffices.
- Solem is applied to the complete Act and its strongly weighted cession/guaranteed-compensation inference. Its sales-agency setting is distinguished; its tribal ambiguity canon and subordinate treatment of later evidence remain intact.
- DeCoteau's controlling clear-cession principle supports the reading without making its precise words, purchase structure or negotiations mandatory for every case.
- Dion retains the clear-and-plain requirement concerning the particular treaty right affected; no general cancellation of treaty obligations is adopted.
- Rosebud preserves the absence of a mandatory verbal formula. Seymour and Mattz continue to distinguish sale, settlement and fee ownership from congressional diminishment.
- Bourland's separate inquiry into exclusion-derived regulation and residual authority is not converted into a boundary rule. Sac and Fox's distinct Indian-country categories remain intact; Negonsott's Kansas-specific authorization supplies no general territorial rule here.
- Lee is applied to the actual continuing permit/declaration controversy, without treating corrective jurisdiction alone as power to decide merits.

## Law After Decision

The original 1858 line does not preserve reservation status over the ordinary ceded, unallotted settler lands governed by the 1894 transaction. The State has primary territorial jurisdiction at the established waste site. This whole-act construction adds that specific diminishment holding while retaining the existing congressional-intent framework, tribal ambiguity canon and distinction between ownership and territory.

The decision supplies no complete regional boundary map, total-disestablishment holding, universal state jurisdiction or cancellation of every treaty promise. Allotments, trust property, retained agency and school parcels, later acquisitions, other §1151 categories and surviving federal or tribal powers require their own legal and factual predicates. Demographic change and long state administration cannot themselves create or reverse diminishment.

## Event

Alaska v. Native Village of Venetie Tribal Government, No. 96-1577. Plenary merits decision, February 25, 1998; argued December 10, 1997. On writ of certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) to the Ninth Circuit, 101 F.3d 1286. Review concerns whether the former Venetie Reservation lands conveyed under the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act are a dependent Indian community under 18 U.S.C. §1151(b), supplying the territorial authority asserted for the nonmember school-construction tax. The District Court rejected that classification in Alaska’s action to enjoin collection; the Court of Appeals reversed.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate. No Justice is recused.

## Public Action

The judgment of the Ninth Circuit is reversed.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reverse, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The lands at issue lack dependent-community status. The asserted territorial basis for the approximately $161,000 nonmember construction tax therefore fails. Further proceedings below must conform to that ruling; no new hearing, title adjudication or region-wide jurisdictional decree is ordered.

## Opinion Topology

Stevens delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine adopt both holdings and the bounded disposition. No separate writing issues.

## Holdings

### Dependent-community status requires a federal land relationship

**Holding and operative rule:** A dependent Indian community under 18 U.S.C. §1151(b) requires land set aside by the Federal Government for Indian use and federal superintendence over that community’s land relationship. Tribal recognition, Indian ownership and federal assistance to residents do not independently supply those territorial predicates.

**Authority:** The unanimous Opinion of the Court directly adopts this rule; no fractured-opinion synthesis is involved.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court reads the statutory category through Sandoval, which recognizes federal protection and supervision of Pueblo lands despite fee ownership, and McGowan, which treats a federal Indian-use set-aside under continuing supervision as Indian country. The rule does not require federal fee title, a reservation label or a prescribed administrative form. It does require the federal relationship those cases describe. Political dependence and territorial jurisdiction are distinct: Cherokee Nation and Worcester do not make every parcel owned by a federally recognized tribe Indian country. The Ninth Circuit’s six-factor approach cannot excuse the absence of either statutory predicate by balancing cohesiveness, government services and general dependence. Hagen and Yankton Sioux preserve the difference between title and territory; neither opening land nor fee ownership alone determines status.

### ANCSA removed both predicates from these lands

**Holding and operative rule:** These former-reservation lands, conveyed in unrestricted fee after ANCSA expressly revoked the reservation and without continuing federal supervision of their use or disposition, are not a dependent Indian community. The Tribe’s asserted territorial authority to tax this nonmember construction consequently fails.

**Authority:** All nine join the Court’s complete statutory construction, application and geographic limit.

**Controlling explanation:** ANCSA §1618(a) expressly revoked this reservation, §1603 extinguished aboriginal land claims, and the settlement authorized fee conveyances without continuing federal restrictions on transfer. The corporations’ §1618(b) election selected former-reservation land instead of monetary and other land benefits; it did not preserve the revoked reservation or impose a new Indian-use restriction. Their later conveyance to the Tribe likewise did not recreate a federal set-aside. Section 1636(d) protects land against adverse possession, real-property taxes and certain judgments while it remains unsold, unleased and undeveloped. Those conditional asset protections do not require federal approval of ordinary transfers or federal supervision of land use. Health, welfare, social and development programs benefit people without supplying the missing territorial supervision. The Tribe’s self-determination argument cannot substitute political recognition for the land relationship Congress ended. No allotment is at issue. The Court leaves other Native parcels, trust land, tribal identity, benefits, member-based powers and independently established consensual authority untouched.

## Precedent Treatment

- Sandoval and McGowan supply the federal set-aside and supervision predicates; their allowance for nonfederal fee ownership remains intact.
- Hagen and Yankton Sioux retain the operative-congressional-act inquiry and the distinction between title and territorial status; ANCSA’s express revocation and unrestricted settlement disposition supply the relevant change here.
- Cherokee Nation and Worcester do not convert recognized political dependence into territorial jurisdiction over every tribal-owned parcel.

## Law After Decision

Section 1151(b) supplies no territorial tax authority on these former Venetie Reservation lands. The two-predicate rule governs dependent-community classification; it leaves the reservation and allotment categories distinct. No universal rule against tribal fee land, federal benefits or tribal government follows.

## Event

Montana v. Crow Tribe of Indians, No. 96-1829. Argued January 14, 1998; decided May 18, 1998. On certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 92 F.3d 826, which required transfer to the Tribe of the entire disputed state tax collection from Westmoreland.

The state taxes on coal from the Tribe's ceded-strip minerals were held invalid. The present dispute concerns approximately $46.8 million in severance taxes collected during 1975–1982 and $11.4 million in gross-proceeds taxes during 1975–1987, before the respective registry deposits. The question is whether the established invalidity requires payment of all those receipts to the Tribe, rather than an inquiry into its entitlement and the proper equitable remedy.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the mandatory whole-collection award and remands for proceedings consistent with the distinction between tax invalidity and the Tribe's entitlement to restitution.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

No amount is awarded. The invalidity judgment and completed registry distributions remain undisturbed. Proper consideration of an independent partial restitution claim remains open; the decision orders neither a new compensatory award nor an automatic reopening of evidence or allowance of amendment.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stone-Zsela | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Common rejection of automatic total recovery and express preservation of properly supported independent partial relief |

## Holdings

### 1. Invalidity of these taxes does not establish entitlement to every receipt

**Holding and operative rule:** An invalid state tax does not automatically entitle a tribe to all sums a nontribal taxpayer paid. The claimant must establish entitlement to the particular fund or remedy; lawful tribal taxing authority and a protected interest in the taxed resource do not alone transfer ownership of the whole state collection.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's opinion is joined in full by all eight Associate Justices. That coalition controls both holdings; the additional partial-restitution framework in Souter's concurrence is not adopted by the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** The tax-invalidity judgment remains binding. Cotton Petroleum nevertheless distinguishes concurrent state and tribal taxing authority from taxation that unlawfully burdens the tribal interest. Rejection of these excessive taxes therefore does not establish that every possible state levy would have been unlawful or that the Tribe owns every dollar collected.

California separates the taxpayer's claim, economic burden and a different claimant's direct entitlement. Fulton and Reich require lawful correction in their proper claimant and procedural settings; neither commands payment of every invalid tax to any economically affected entity. Chickasaw Nation identifies statutory tax incidence but supplies no universal award of receipts to a different claimant.

The Tribe's resource interest and the United States' trustee role matter, but they do not prove the particular total award. Who paid, who bore the relevant loss, the actual agreements and preserved claims, and prior distributions must be examined. The taxpayer's failed or relinquished refund opportunity is not automatically the Tribe's claim or a release of it. Conversely, labeling the State's gain unlawful does not establish the amount belonging to this claimant. No compensatory measure is pleaded and proved for an appellate award here.

**Precedent treatment:** The earlier tax-invalidity judgment remains intact; Cotton Petroleum preserves concurrent taxation; California, Fulton, Reich and Chickasaw retain their claimant-specific and remedial limits.

### 2. Equitable analysis must use the actual legal and procedural interests

**Holding and operative rule:** The remand must distinguish lawful tribal tax coverage, actual payment arrangements, independent claims against the State and funds already distributed. None of the approval dispute, taxpayer refund history or Tribe-taxpayer agreement categorically extinguishes a properly supported independent tribal claim on this record; further relief must follow its lawful basis and proven scope.

**Controlling explanation:** The 1988 ruling treated Interior's original reservation-tax approval as covering the ceded-strip minerals. Its contrary exclusion was legally mistaken. That ruling distributed registry funds under the 1982 agreement; it did not determine Westmoreland's liability before that agreement. Actual refusal to approve, the company's pass-through difficulty and its unwillingness to pay remain relevant practical facts, but they cannot be recast as a conclusive legal absence of tribal authority.

The United States intervened in June 1983. The severance and gross-proceeds taxes entered the registry at different times, and the completed distributions must be considered without awarding the same benefit twice. Neither the agreement's treatment of Westmoreland nor its refund difficulties establish a tribal release of the separate claim against Montana.

The contrary full-recovery theory neglects these distinctions; a categorical no-recovery theory would do the same. Proper consideration of independent partial restitution remains open in this action. The district court may take a lawful course under the Federal Rules, but this decision does not compel reopening proof, allowing an amendment, measuring compensatory injury or paying a fixed lesser sum. Equity must explain entitlement; it may not become an unexamined excuse to retain an unlawful gain.

**Precedent treatment:** California's distinction between derivative and independent entitlements governs without barring a properly established direct tribal claim; the earlier distribution ruling retains its actual, limited scope.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Prior Crow Tribe tax-invalidity judgment:** Preserved; no relitigation of these taxes' invalidity.
- **Cotton Petroleum:** Preserves possible concurrent state and tribal taxation and distinguishes all receipts from an excessive share.
- **United States v. California:** Separates taxpayer refunds, economic burdens and another claimant's independent entitlement.
- **Fulton and Reich:** Preserve adequate, procedurally lawful correction for the appropriate claimant without predetermining this award.
- **Chickasaw Nation:** Its incidence analysis does not create a general right to another taxpayer's entire payments.
- **1988 registry-distribution ruling:** Retains its approval-coverage determination and distribution effect, without becoming a decision of pre-agreement taxpayer liability.

## Law After Decision

Established tax invalidity does not itself establish a tribe's right to the entire collection from another taxpayer. The court must examine the claimant, fund, agreements, prior distributions and lawful theory of relief. A properly supported independent partial claim remains open; neither a total award nor a complete bar follows automatically.

## Separate Writings

Souter, joined by O'Connor, agrees that the whole-collection award cannot stand because Montana could impose a tax below the excessive level. He emphasizes that the existing request for illegally collected money encompasses a lesser claim to excessive receipts, grounded in the Tribe's own protected property interest. Interior's legally erroneous refusal and the agreement with Westmoreland do not dispose of that independent claim against the State. A dollar-for-dollar compensatory-loss measure is not the only possible basis for restitution of an improperly obtained benefit.

He would require genuine consideration of that existing claim: determine the excessive share, consider registry distributions and the remaining equities, and decide whether all, some or none of that share should be restored. This does not permit treating every state tax dollar as excessive or establish entitlement to an additional penny. The concurrence joins the Court's bounded opinion because it leaves that inquiry substantively open; its affirmative restitution framework goes beyond the Court's common holding.

## Procedure After Action

The Ninth Circuit's compulsory transfer of the whole preregistry collection is reversed. Further district-court proceedings must respect the preserved independent claim and the actual scope of existing rulings and agreements. No refund claim is revived for Westmoreland, no registry distribution is disturbed, and no additional amount, amendment or evidentiary proceeding is directed.

## Event

Kiowa Tribe of Oklahoma v. Manufacturing Technologies, Inc., No. 96-1037. Plenary merits decision, May 26, 1998. Argued January 12, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the Oklahoma Court of Civil Appeals, First Division, after the Oklahoma Supreme Court declined review. The intermediate court affirmed a contract money judgment against the Tribe under an off-reservation commercial-activity exception to immunity. The question is whether federal tribal immunity permits this direct contract suit without a clear waiver or congressional authorization.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate.

## Public Action

The judgment is reversed, and the case is remanded with instructions to dismiss the action against the Tribe on immunity grounds.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded with instructions to dismiss, 6–3.** **Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer. **Opposing:** Stevens, Thomas and Ginsburg. The direct contract money claim against the Tribe must be dismissed. The three opposing Justices would remand under a limited off-territory commercial exception, with its factual predicate determined from the actual record; they do not unconditionally sustain a money judgment on an assumed location finding.

## Opinion Topology

Breyer delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter. Stevens dissents, joined in full by Thomas and Ginsburg. All six members of the Court's opinion adopt the complete contract-immunity rule, its application, qualifications and dismissal mandate.

## Holdings

**Holding and operative rule:** A federally recognized Indian tribe retains sovereign immunity from a direct suit on its contract, whether the activity is governmental or commercial and whether the contract concerns activity on or off reservation land, unless the tribe clearly waives immunity or Congress unequivocally authorizes the suit. No such waiver or authorization supports this action, so the direct contract claim against the Kiowa Tribe must be dismissed without deciding the debt's merits or resolving the disputed place of execution.

**Authority:** Breyer's opinion for the six Justices identified in Opinion Topology controls the entire rule and its application.

**Controlling explanation:** Tribal immunity and the substantive obligations a tribe may owe are different questions. Potawatomi preserves that distinction even when the underlying activity is commercial and the State has a valid substantive interest. Santa Clara Pueblo requires an unequivocal waiver; Puyallup distinguishes relief against the tribal sovereign from claims against members. Those cases do not already decide every off-reservation contract, but their federal immunity framework provides no adequate basis for the geographical and commercial exception adopted below.

The proposed exception has substantial practical appeal. Modern tribal commerce can affect nonmembers far from tribal land, and immunity can leave a creditor without the ordinary means of enforcing a debt. The doctrine's early foundations and present policy costs merit attention. But determining which commercial activities, locations, counterparties and reliance interests warrant an exception requires choices intertwined with Congress's treatment of tribal sovereign status. Congress can revise the rule and specify its boundaries. The Court declines to create this new exception through a direct contract action.

Mescalero and Chickasaw Nation address the substantive operation of state law; they do not establish that a court may enter a money judgment against an immune tribe. Here the note preserves sovereign rights, and neither signing it nor defaulting clearly consents to suit. Whether execution occurred in Carnegie or Oklahoma City therefore does not affect the immunity result. The decision does not extinguish contractual obligations, automatically protect individual agents, decide a tort claim, determine an asset's immunity from execution or establish that some adequate alternative remedy exists. A clear waiver or valid congressional authorization would present a different case.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Citizen Band Potawatomi Indian Tribe, 498 U.S. 505:** Applied for immunity distinct from substantive obligation and its rejection of a commercial-business limitation in the trust-land setting; the present holding extends the direct contract-immunity rule to the off-reservation commercial setting.
- **Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez, 436 U.S. 49:** Applied to the requirement of an unequivocal waiver and Congress's authority over the immunity rule.
- **Puyallup Tribe, Inc. v. Department of Game of Washington, 433 U.S. 165:** Applied to the distinction between the tribal sovereign and individual members; jurisdiction over others supplies no automatic authority to bind the Tribe.
- **Mescalero Apache Tribe v. Jones, 411 U.S. 145:** Distinguished as a rule concerning substantive state-law application outside reservation borders, not a direct contract-suit immunity exception.
- **Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Chickasaw Nation, June 14, 1995:** Its territorial and tax-incidence holdings remain intact and create no automatic contract remedy against the Tribe.
- **Alaska v. Native Village of Venetie Tribal Government, February 25, 1998:** Its separation of tribal recognition from Indian-country land status is preserved; loss of a territorial classification does not itself eliminate sovereign status or decide suit immunity.
- **Montana v. Crow Tribe of Indians, May 18, 1998:** Its requirement of an independently grounded restitutionary remedy is preserved; it establishes neither automatic tribal recovery nor an automatic private contract action.

## Law After Decision

Direct contract immunity now expressly covers the off-reservation commercial transaction presented. It remains subject to clear tribal waiver and unequivocal congressional authorization. Substantive contract obligations and ordinary state-law applicability are separate from the power to adjudicate this direct money claim. Claims against nonimmune parties, collateral security and property enforcement depend on their own actual legal bases; the Court recognizes no particular such remedy here and decides no general tort-immunity question.

## Separate Writings

Stevens, joined by Thomas and Ginsburg, would decline to extend tribal immunity to genuinely off-reservation commercial conduct unrelated to tribal land or sovereign functions. Potawatomi resolved a trust-land operation, and Puyallup protected the tribal defendant in its own setting; neither requires the broader extension. Mescalero's ordinary-state-law baseline informs, but does not itself decide, the distinct amenability question. The dissent regards the extension as new federal common-law displacement of state adjudicatory authority without an identified federal interest sufficient to justify it. Congress's power to legislate does not compel courts to enlarge the received doctrine meanwhile, and unusually broad immunity for outside commerce burdens parties dealing beyond tribal territory.

The dissent preserves established immunity within its received scope and infers no waiver from commerce or a promise to pay. It would remand for application of its limited rule to the actual transaction and valid existing findings. The note's Carnegie recital and the creditor's Oklahoma City account remain distinct; if existing findings settle the legally material location and commercial predicates, no new factfinding is needed. Only a genuinely unresolved material predicate would require further ordinary proceedings. The dissent does not convert the location dispute into a finding against the Tribe or direct an unconditional money judgment.

## Procedure After Action

The state courts must dismiss the presented direct contract action against the Tribe on immunity grounds. They need not resolve the competing execution-location accounts to implement that direction. Breach, damages, another defendant's liability, particular security or execution measures, and any actually authorized waiver-based or statutory action are not adjudicated by this mandate.
