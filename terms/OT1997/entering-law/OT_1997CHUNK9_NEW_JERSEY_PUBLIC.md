# Effective public law

Mechanically copied Public Projection; effective only for later events.

### Event

New Jersey v. New York, No. 120, Original. Original-jurisdiction merits decision on exceptions, May 26, 1998. Argued January 12, 1998. The Court considers both States' exceptions to the Special Master's March 31, 1997 Final Report and May 30, 1997 Supplement after trial and full briefing. The questions concern the 1834 Compact's treatment of later artificial fill at Ellis Island, prescription and acquiescence, laches, the original island's tide line, historical filled-pier land and a proposed practical adjustment of the boundary. New Jersey claims the later fill; New York claims the enlarged island. There is no lower appellate judgment.

### Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate.

### Public Action

The Court recognizes New Jersey's sovereignty over the later artificial fill and preserves New York's entitlement to the original island. It overrules New York's three exceptions, overrules New Jersey's high-water and pier exceptions, sustains New Jersey's objection to exchanging territory for administrative convenience and recommits the matter for a proposed conforming decree. No final geographic decree is entered today.

### Holdings

#### 1. The Compact leaves the later artificial fill in New Jersey

**Holding and operative rule:** The 1834 Compact preserves New York's entitlement to the original Ellis Island but does not transfer New Jersey's surrounding sovereign territory whenever artificial fill enlarges that island. The later artificial additions therefore remain in New Jersey unless a legally sufficient subsequent change of entitlement is established.

**Authority:** Part I of Souter's opinion, joined by all five full joiners identified in Opinion Topology, Stone-Zsela and Stevens: seven direct votes.

**Controlling explanation:** Article First sets the midwater sovereignty line, west of which Ellis Island lies; Article Second reserves the original island; Article Third gives New York distinct authority serving commerce and navigation. Central Railroad distinguishes that authority from sovereignty. Reading the agreement as a whole against ordinary artificial-change principles preserves the original island without allowing federal construction to transfer another State's territory.

The geographic conclusion does not rest on the word present alone as though it were a metes-and-bounds description. Foreseeable reclamation, earlier filling practices and omission of an acreage limit do not establish an agreement that every later addition would become New York territory. Nor does continuing to call the enlarged physical feature Ellis Island decide sovereign entitlement. Georgia distinguishes artificial or avulsive change from natural accretion, but its different reservation and naturally formed-island questions do not settle this Compact. Here the substantial additions are artificial.

The Compact supplies no relevant displacement of the ordinary boundary consequence. The Court does not establish a rule for every federal dredging project or every subsequently connected feature. The United States' express jurisdictional reservation remains, and sovereignty does not itself decide federal or private ownership.

#### 2. New York has not established prescription and acquiescence

**Holding and operative rule:** A State claiming territory by prescription must prove by a preponderance of the evidence long, continuous possession and assertion of sovereignty over the actual disputed land, together with the other State's acquiescence. That acquiescence requires knowledge of the adverse claim or adverse acts sufficiently open, notorious, visible and uninterrupted to support the inference; New York's cumulative proof does not establish acquisition of this fill.

**Authority:** Part II, joined by the five full joiners and Stone-Zsela: six direct votes. Stevens disagrees with the evidentiary conclusion; Scalia and Thomas do not reach prescription as a necessary ground.

**Controlling explanation:** Illinois requires proof directed to the disputed territory, and Georgia makes notice and acquiescence depend on the actual sovereign conduct. The record includes specifically identified electoral registrations of fill residents and hospital records on the fill, as well as census treatment, labor standards, compensation practice and limited civil services. Those records are not dismissed as merely generic island references. But their intermittent character and the mixed governmental understandings do not establish the necessary continuous adverse assertion and notice.

New York's conceded authority over the original island makes generic references equivocal. Federal administration limited both ordinary state activity and the notice or protest reasonably expected of New Jersey. The 1904 deed is qualified, and the 1933 permit must be assessed by its actual object. Federal or local action is not automatically state action. The 1935 federal return to New York's position and the 1949 wage reversal are considered; they prevent treating earlier contrary federal acts as permanent New Jersey control, but do not eliminate the underlying uncertainty.

New York argues that modest surviving records reflect the only sovereign acts possible on a federal installation and that contrary episodes concerned property or were abandoned. Their cumulative force remains insufficient under the ordinary burden, without demanding every municipal service or a formal state protest. A late protest could not undo title already acquired; the necessary earlier acquisition has not been proved. The Court adopts no fixed prescriptive period and leaves open whether federal occupation could categorically prevent prescription in another case. Missing papers or witnesses supply no invented evidence for either side.

#### 3. Laches does not excuse proof of affirmative territorial acquisition

**Holding and operative rule:** New York cannot use laches based on evidentiary losses affecting its own prescriptive claim to replace proof of that affirmative acquisition. Although prescription is pleaded as a defense, New York bears the claimant's burden on that entitlement; the asserted losses do not establish a laches defense to New Jersey's Compact-based boundary.

**Authority:** Part III, joined by the five full joiners: five direct rationale votes. Stone-Zsela agrees only in overruling the exception.

**Controlling explanation:** New York points to deteriorated and lost records, expired retention periods and deceased witnesses, contending that New Jersey delayed suit until acquisition became harder to prove. Even assuming those circumstances impaired its prescriptive proof, the asserted defense cannot relieve New York of the affirmative burden to establish that it acquired the territory. It identifies no lost proof defeating New Jersey's underlying entitlement under the Compact and the artificial-change rule.

Illinois's boundary-repose analysis supplies context, while Kansas leaves general laches availability in compact litigation open. Neither requires a finding here that New York suffered no evidentiary prejudice. The Court rejects the proposed legal use of that loss rather than deciding whether every asserted loss occurred or whether another interstate compact controversy could support laches. The ruling does not infer the contents of missing documents, decide an independent lack-of-diligence issue or turn dismissal on laches into a new sovereignty award.

#### 4. The original island extends to its mean low-water line in 1834

**Holding and operative rule:** Article Second's retained original island is bounded by its mean low-water line as it stood in 1834. New Jersey's proposed mean-high-water boundary is rejected; ordinary property conveyances and approximate deed acreage do not independently fix the interstate sovereign line.

**Authority:** Part IV, joined by the five full joiners: five direct rationale votes. Stone-Zsela agrees only in overruling the high-water exception; conditional views of the whole-island dissenters are not counted.

**Controlling explanation:** Handly's Lessee and Vermont supply the general sovereign-boundary rule against which the island reservation is read. Their river settings differ from this tidal island, but the territorial boundary principle explains the reservation's reach without treating ordinary private ownership as sovereignty. The Court does not enact language omitted from the unsuccessful 1827 proposal.

Article Third's express low-water limit along the New Jersey shore answers a distinct question: how far New York's authority over waters and submerged lands could move inland with the rising tide. That problem differs from identifying the edge of an island already reserved to New York. Express wording for the former does not require high water for the latter. The 1808 conveyance's high-water limit and approximate acreage concern the property conveyed, not an agreed interstate decree.

Louisiana established continuity even under a proposed high-water test and expressly declined a universal island definition; it therefore does not select high water here. The interpretive rule does not require proof that modern access has been obstructed. The resulting historical line must still be located accurately for the decree.

#### 5. The historical filled-pier land belongs within the retained original island

**Holding and operative rule:** On independent evaluation of the circumstantial evidence, the historical pier rested on filled land before 1834 and that land belongs within New York's retained original island. New Jersey's exception is overruled, but the implementing description must include the accepted historical land only once and reconcile its acreage; no exact additional 0.2-acre award or final numerical total is entered.

**Authority:** Part V, joined by the five full joiners: five direct rationale votes. Stone-Zsela agrees only in the exception's disposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The identified 1819 map appears to show filled material around the pier location. The credited evidence about piling practice and the relative ease of shallow-water filling supports the likely inference that the pier stood on fill. The alternative possibility of a pile-supported structure, its changing shape, objections to the witness's engineering qualifications and uncertain ammunition-load assumptions do not defeat that narrower map-and-construction inference. A structure projecting over water is not automatically land.

Louisiana requires independent original-action review and appropriate evidentiary weight, not appellate deference under Rule 52. The Court applies that distinction and does not presume that every detail of the Master's description is correct. His reference to an 1839 chart is not treated as a new exhibit; the identified map supporting the inference is from 1819.

The Master explains his 0.2-acre allowance as land omitted from the 4.69-acre base; New Jersey disputes duplication. Acceptance of the filled-pier finding does not independently measure or resolve that numerical disagreement. The proposed decree must locate the historical land once, explain the pier's treatment and reconcile the description with its acreage. Existing supported work may supply that technical implementation without reopening the pier finding or requiring an unnecessary new trial.

#### 6. Implementation must locate the Compact boundary, not exchange territory

**Holding and operative rule:** The Court may not replace the established Compact boundary with different parcels merely to simplify administration or preserve an approximate acreage. New Jersey's exception to the practical reconfiguration is sustained, and the matter is recommitted for preparation of an authenticated, internally consistent proposed decree implementing the 1834 mean-low-water boundary and accepted historical filled-pier land; this Court must separately consider and enter the decree.

**Authority:** Part VI, joined by the five full joiners and Stone-Zsela: six direct votes for this implementation and recommittal. The dissenters' rejection of judicial exchange does not make them supporters of the divided-island mandate.

**Controlling explanation:** Cuyler establishes the Compact's federal-law force. Texas v. New Mexico and Kansas require enforcement of its bargain rather than a judicially preferred substitute. Georgia's treatment of an undefined maritime line does not authorize transferring territory already allocated by this Compact. Louisiana permits the technical work necessary to locate an adjudicated line and requires reliable geographical implementation.

The Master's modified 5.1-acre Survey A moves the line to accommodate buildings, margins, corridor turns, ferry access and staircase treatment. Those are territorial reallocations, not simply identification of the historical tide line. Survey B's 4.89 acres is not lawful merely because it matches an approximate area. The parties' survey assistance, lack of objection to execution of a survey or preferences among proposed options do not establish consent to exchange sovereignty.

Administrative convenience and uncertain old measurements justify careful location, not substitute parcels. Existing supported survey work may be used, and neither a new full survey nor a new merits trial is required merely by recommittal. No exact coordinates, final acreage, presently operative injunction or decree date is supplied. Federal jurisdiction expressly reserved by Congress, ownership and unpresented municipal, regulatory and tax consequences remain distinct.

### Precedent Treatment

- **Central Railroad Co. of New Jersey v. Jersey City, 209 U.S. 473:** Applied to distinguish Article First sovereignty from Article Third commerce/navigation jurisdiction; it is not a prior judgment binding these States on the precise island boundary.
- **Georgia v. South Carolina, 497 U.S. 376:** Applied at its prescription, notice and artificial-change principles; its different island reservation, natural formations and undefined maritime line do not decide this Compact or authorize a territorial exchange.
- **Illinois v. Kentucky, 500 U.S. 380:** Applied for the preponderance burden, adverse control over the actual disputed land and acquiescence. Its boundary-repose discussion is not enlarged into an absolute laches rule for every compact case.
- **Handly's Lessee v. Anthony, 18 U.S. 374:** Applied for the sovereign low-water boundary principle, without confusing it with ordinary title to tidelands.
- **Vermont v. New Hampshire, 289 U.S. 593:** Applied to the low-water construction of the territorial reservation; the different river setting does not establish a high-water rule here.
- **Cuyler v. Adams, 449 U.S. 433:** Applied to the congressionally approved Compact's status as federal law.
- **Texas v. New Mexico, 462 U.S. 554:** Applied to forbid relief inconsistent with the actual compact bargain.
- **Kansas v. Colorado, May 15, 1995:** Applied to faithful compact enforcement, independent original review and the distinction between entitlement and remedial flexibility; its reservation of general laches availability remains.
- **Louisiana v. Mississippi, October 31 and December 4, 1995:** Applied to independent evidentiary review and an authenticated, internally consistent implementing decree. Its continuity holding supplies no universal island definition or automatic high-water boundary.
- **Mississippi v. Louisiana, December 14, 1992:** Preserved at the distinction between this Court's exclusive interstate adjudication and otherwise lawful private-title litigation.
- **Hess v. Port Authority Trans-Hudson Corp., November 14, 1994:** Its actual-compact, entity-specific immunity inquiry remains distinct from territorial sovereignty and supplies no power to rewrite this boundary.

### Law After Decision

New Jersey retains sovereignty over the later artificial additions; New York retains the original island to its 1834 mean low-water boundary, including the accepted historical filled-pier land. New York has not proved a later prescriptive acquisition, and its asserted laches theory supplies no substitute for that proof. The Court adds the Compact-specific artificial-fill allocation and shore/pier determinations while preserving ordinary prescriptive burdens, independent original review and the prohibition on judicially replacing a compact bargain. The exact geographic decree remains to be prepared and entered. Sovereignty does not adjudicate federal or private ownership, the precise extent of federal enclave jurisdiction, every aspect of New York's retained jurisdiction, or unpresented municipal, regulatory and tax issues.

### Separate Writings

Breyer, joined by Ginsburg, fully joins the Court. They find no relevant ambiguity in Articles First and Second that displaces the background artificial-change rule, without declaring every Compact clause unambiguous or denying all contextual interpretation of silence. Federal control also matters to acquiescence. Modest civil acts may be all that a State can perform on a federally administered installation, but that does not make them adequate notice of an adverse claim or require the neighboring State to mount a major protest against each one. The contrary inference would make loss of sovereignty too easy. Conflicting federal understandings, including the Wyzanski statement and later reversals, are evidence to evaluate, not correct legal holdings that the island lies outside every State.

Stevens agrees with Part I's original Compact allocation but would find New York acquired the enlarged island by prescription. The identified fill-resident electoral and hospital records, civil administration, census treatment and public affiliation cumulatively show a continuing sovereign understanding appropriate to a federal installation. Sparse surviving examples need not negate consistent practice. He infers notice from the public character and significance of those acts and treats contrary episodes as isolated, property-related, attributable to different actors or abandoned. He does not equate every registration with an actual vote, locate every marriage on the fill or treat every federal wage choice as compulsory state law. A late protest cannot undo an acquisition already complete. The difficulties of dividing buildings reinforce his evidentiary criticism but supply no independent power to exchange territory. His sufficient prescription ground would eliminate the need for a divided-island decree.

Scalia, joined by Thomas, would recognize New York's whole-island entitlement through practical construction of the Compact, without deciding that prescription has been proved. Articles First through Third form an integrated settlement; the retained island's scope is sufficiently ambiguous for the parties' administration to inform its meaning. Article Third helps interpret the agreement even though New York advances no preserved independent claim of title under that article. Air France v. Saks, Choctaw Nation and Vermont support consulting practical construction where the agreement is ambiguous. The long treatment of the enlarged island as one New York jurisdiction, without comparable New Jersey island administration, supports their construction. Interpreting an ambiguous reservation differs from dispossessing an otherwise undoubted owner by prescription. They do not read Article Second as a transfer clause for every conceivable future fill and do not authorize exchanging a boundary once its meaning is settled. Their ground makes prescription, laches and divided-island implementation unnecessary to their preferred judgment.

### Procedure After Action

The matter is recommitted to the Special Master for a proposed decree implementing the adjudicated historical boundary, including the accepted filled-pier land without double counting. The proposal must provide an authenticated, internally consistent geographic description and reconcile its acreage. Existing supported work may be used; no unnecessary new trial or full survey is ordered. The Court retains the original action for separate consideration and entry of the decree. The Master's March 31 proposal and May 30 Designated Survey remain proposals. No final geographic decree, presently operative undefined injunction or docket closure follows from today's merits decision.
