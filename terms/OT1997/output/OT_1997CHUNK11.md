### Forney v. Apfel, No. 97-5737

Decision, June 15, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 22, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit. The question is whether a benefits claimant may appeal a sentence-four remand when she sought an immediate award but received only further agency proceedings.

The District Court found the denial of disability benefits inadequately supported and remanded under sentence four of 42 U.S.C. §405(g). The Ninth Circuit refused to entertain Forney’s appeal seeking an outright award. Forney and the Government support appealability; an appointed advocate defends the judgment below.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The court of appeals must consider the challenge to the District Court’s choice of remedy under the applicable law. The Court awards no benefits and makes no disability finding.

#### Opinions and Joins

Justice Scalia delivered the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine join the complete appellate rule, its limits, and reversal with remand.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### A claimant denied the immediate award she sought may appeal a final sentence-four remand

**Controlling proposition:** A sentence-four judgment under 42 U.S.C. §405(g) ends the district-court civil action and is a final decision reviewable under 28 U.S.C. §1291. A claimant who sought an immediate benefits award but received only renewed agency proceedings remains adversely affected by the denied relief and may appeal that judgment.

**Authority:** The unanimous Opinion of the Court directly adopts this rule, its application, limits and remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** Schaefer establishes that a sentence-four remand terminates the action rather than leaving it pending while the agency acts. Finkelstein and Melkonyan distinguish that disposition from the separately authorized sentence-six remand. Nothing in that finality rule depends on whether the Government or the claimant appeals.

Finality does not itself establish that an appellant is adversely affected. Roper supplies the ordinary distinction between complete relief and a remaining personal stake. Forney obtained further proceedings but did not receive the immediate award she requested. Her partial success therefore leaves a concrete disagreement about relief. Treating her as a prevailing party for fee purposes does not erase it. Nor can the prospect of another agency decision turn the terminated district action into an interlocutory proceeding. The Government’s agreement with Forney does not replace the Court’s independent jurisdictional determination.

Allowing review decides no benefits question. The appellate court must assess the District Court’s remedy under the applicable review law; it receives no license to substitute unsupported factfinding. The Court does not decide appeals from sentence-six remands, general agency-remand finality, judgments granting all requested relief, or requests merely for a different explanation of wholly favorable relief. Sentence six retains its own conditions: a Secretary’s pre-answer motion for good cause, or new material evidence with good cause for its earlier omission, followed by retained jurisdiction and return to court.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Shalala v. Schaefer:** Its sentence-four termination rule is applied to claimant appeals; its separate fee and prevailing-party holdings are unchanged.
- **Sullivan v. Finkelstein:** Its final-judgment construction supplies the statutory structure; its government-appellant setting did not resolve this claimant’s denied request for greater relief.
- **Melkonyan v. Sullivan:** Its sentence-four/sentence-six distinction remains operative; the Court creates no third remand category.
- **Deposit Guaranty National Bank v. Roper:** Its distinction between complete relief and a continuing personal stake supports appellate aggrievement here.
- **Director, OWCP v. Newport News Shipbuilding & Dry Dock Co. and Ingalls Shipbuilding, Inc. v. Director, OWCP:** Their limits on general administrative interest do not prevent an actual claimant from appealing denied relief.

#### Law After Decision

A sentence-four benefits remand is subject to an appeal by a claimant who remains aggrieved by denial of the immediate award requested. Finality, appellate aggrievement and entitlement to benefits remain distinct. The decision changes no disability standard, sentence-six condition or fee entitlement.

#### Source Notes

The reported record at [524 U.S. 266](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-524/pdf/USREPORTS-524-266.pdf) supplies the procedural history and requested relief. Sections 405(g) and 1291 supply the review framework. The decision rests on the sentence-four character of this remand, not on a finding that Forney is disabled.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Ninth Circuit must entertain Forney’s appeal and consider the challenge to the remand remedy. The benefits merits remain undecided; no immediate agency award or other outcome on appellate review is directed.

**End of entry: Forney v. Apfel, Decision, June 15, 1998.**

### Hohn v. United States, No. 96-8986

Decision, June 15, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 3, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the Eighth Circuit. The question is whether 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) permits review of the court of appeals’ denial of a certificate of appealability.

Hohn sought §2255 relief from a firearm-use conviction under Bailey. His district-court motion was pending when AEDPA became law on April 24, 1996. After the motion was denied, he filed a notice of appeal on July 29, 1996. A circuit panel treated it as a certificate request and denied it because it regarded the Bailey claim as statutory rather than constitutional. The Government now characterizes the claim as constitutional and supports reconsideration. An appointed advocate contests this Court’s jurisdiction.

Hohn separately argues that Lindh removes the certificate requirement from his pre-enactment motion. The Government contests preservation, scope and that temporal construction; Hohn disputes those objections. The Court resolves the statutory review question without deciding that alternative or its preservation.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Vacated and remanded, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas, who would dismiss for want of statutory jurisdiction.

The Eighth Circuit must reconsider the certificate question in light of the Government’s changed constitutional characterization and applicable law. No certificate, automatic merits appeal, collateral relief, trial-conviction vacatur, hearing, release or acquittal is ordered.

#### Opinions and Joins

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Breyer | Vacatur of the certificate denial and remand | Complete statutory-jurisdiction rule, limited overruling of House, transition reservation and certificate remand. |
| Dissent | Scalia | Thomas throughout; O'Connor in Part I only | Would dismiss for want of statutory jurisdiction | Part I preserves House and rejects §1254 jurisdiction. Part II applies the certificate condition to the post-enactment appellate step. |
| Separate dissent | O'Connor | No other Justice | Would dismiss for want of statutory jurisdiction | Joins Part I of Scalia's dissent; gives a distinct, provision-specific temporal reason for rejecting the no-certificate alternative. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. A judicial certificate-denial proceeding is reviewable under §1254(1)

**Controlling proposition:** An appellate court’s adjudication denying a certificate of appealability is a case in the court of appeals reviewable by certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1), even though the denial prevents the underlying merits appeal from proceeding. House v. Mayo is overruled only insofar as it denies that statutory certiorari jurisdiction; its distinct extraordinary/common-law certiorari discussion under §1651 and other holdings are not enlarged or displaced.

**Authority:** The six-Justice Opinion of the Court directly supports both holdings, their explanations, the reservations and the mandate.

**Controlling explanation:** The certificate request asks a judicial body to decide an asserted legal entitlement adverse to the applicant. Denial of permission does not erase the proceeding that decided permission. Section 1254’s provision for review before or after judgment fits that threshold judicial decision. Section 2253(c)’s condition on taking the merits appeal does not say that the certificate proceeding itself is beyond review. Allocating the determination to circuit judges does not change the judicial character of the actual appellate adjudication.

House identified the case only with an underlying appeal already permitted to proceed. That premise fails to account for the separately adjudicated certificate request. Statutory precedent warrants substantial respect, but the Court must correct that mistaken jurisdictional construction expressly. Its statutory character does not cure weak reasoning about the judicial proceeding Congress made reviewable. Quirin and ordinary review of threshold jurisdictional decisions support treating that judicial business as a case even before the merits appeal proceeds. Lower courts were not free to treat House as silently overruled before this decision.

Felker enforces Congress’s express bar on review of successive-application authorization decisions. Triestman and Dorsainvil apply that bar; none transfers it to a different certificate ruling. Stewart likewise recognizes the importance of identifying the actual threshold act rather than extending a review prohibition by label. Those distinctions support statutory review here, not review of a true authorization refusal. Agreement of the parties creates no jurisdiction, and no extraordinary-writ substitute is used.

This ground establishes review of the actual denial whether the circuit correctly or incorrectly required a certificate. The Court therefore reserves Hohn’s competing Lindh transition argument and the disputed preservation and scope objections. It holds neither that a certificate was required nor that the underlying appeal was automatically perfected.

##### II. Reconsideration of the certificate question does not grant collateral relief

**Controlling proposition:** The certificate denial is vacated and the matter returned for reconsideration in light of the Government’s changed constitutional characterization and governing law. If a certificate is required and is later issued, §2253(c) still requires a substantial showing of the denial of a constitutional right and identification of the qualifying issues; this judgment itself establishes neither condition.

**Controlling explanation:** Bailey defines the substantive reach of firearm use. Bousley distinguishes that statutory meaning from a possible constitutional claim and from satisfaction of the requirements for collateral relief. The statutory origin of an argument therefore does not alone dispose of every constitutional claim it may support. The Government’s changed position warrants reconsideration of the actual claim but proves no constitutional violation. The lower court must apply the proper law to the claim presented, including the unresolved certificate question. The Court finds no default exception, factual innocence, invalid conviction or entitlement to a certificate, and supplies no trial or plea facts not established in this proceeding.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **House v. Mayo:** Its contrary statutory certificate-review holding is expressly overruled; its extraordinary/common-law certiorari discussion under §1651 is neither expanded nor a substitute ground for today’s action.
- **Ex parte Quirin:** Its treatment of judicial business before completion of the ordinary appellate path supports statutory review of the threshold proceeding.
- **Felker v. Turpin:** Its actual authorization-review bar and separately retained original-habeas jurisdiction remain intact, including their independent conditions.
- **Triestman v. United States and In re Dorsainvil:** Their dismissals of ordinary authorization review are distinguished from review of a certificate denial.
- **Stewart v. Martinez-Villareal:** Its identification of an independently reviewable threshold classification supports the act-specific channel inquiry; it did not previously decide certificate review.
- **Lindh v. Murphy, Landgraf v. USI Film Products and Rivers v. Roadway Express:** Their provision-specific temporal inquiry remains governing. This decision reserves the certificate amendment’s application to this pre-enactment motion and later notice.
- **Bailey v. United States and Bousley v. United States:** Their distinction between substantive statutory meaning, a constitutional claim and ultimate relief informs reconsideration without deciding Hohn’s claim.
- **Citizens for a Better Environment v. Steel Co.:** The Court establishes its statutory review authority before granting the bounded vacatur; it does not assume jurisdiction to decide the merits.

#### Law After Decision

A certificate denial is subject to ordinary statutory certiorari review. That rule removes House’s contrary jurisdictional obstacle without weakening the express bar on review of successive-application authorization decisions. It changes neither certificate eligibility nor the requirements for collateral relief, and resolves no general AEDPA transition rule.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Justice Scalia, dissenting, with Justice Thomas throughout and Justice O’Connor in Part I.** Part I would preserve House’s statutory construction. Section 2253 conditions the taking of an appeal on a certificate; until a circuit justice or judge gives that permission, the underlying case has not entered the court of appeals in the sense §1254 requires. The decision of a judge on permission is not a separate appellate case merely because the request involves legal judgment. Congress retained and extended the certificate structure against House’s settled construction, strengthening the statutory-stare-decisis objection. Felker’s express bar concerns permission for initial district consideration of a successive application, a different act for which House supplied no controlling construction; Stewart concerns a different circuit classification. Neither warrants discarding the certificate-specific precedent.

Part II, joined only by Thomas, rejects the no-certificate alternative because the provision regulates the appellate step initiated after AEDPA’s enactment. Applying it to the July 29, 1996 notice is prospective at the relevant activity; it does not impose a new merits rule on the earlier district motion. They make no forfeiture finding. Without statutory review authority they would dismiss, not vacate the denial. They grant no extraordinary writ and do not treat §1651 as an independent source of jurisdiction.

**Justice O’Connor, separately dissenting.** She joins the House and statutory-jurisdiction analysis in Part I but states a narrower temporal ground. Landgraf requires attention first to statutory direction and the particular provision, not a generic procedural label. Lindh’s application-date construction for the amendments governing the pending district application does not settle the separate appellate condition. Section 2253(c) regulates the later appeal initiated on July 29, 1996; applying it there is consistent with Felker’s separately examined post-enactment authorization and review proceedings. She therefore rejects Hohn’s alternative on that provision-specific basis, without resolving every chapter-153 amendment or finding his argument forfeited. She would dismiss for want of statutory jurisdiction.

#### Source Notes

The reported proceeding at [524 U.S. 236](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-524/pdf/USREPORTS-524-236.pdf) supplies the certificate-denial posture and Government’s changed characterization. The original briefs in [the record for No. 96-8986](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385014_0098) contain the competing temporal and preservation arguments; those submissions are not findings of preservation or waiver. Sections 1254(1) and 2253(c) supply the distinct statutory inquiries.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Eighth Circuit’s certificate denial is vacated. It must reconsider under applicable law and the Government’s changed position. The disputed transition argument remains unresolved; the Court neither directs issuance of a certificate nor orders consideration of the underlying appeal as automatically authorized. No collateral merits relief follows from this mandate.

**End of entry: Hohn v. United States, Decision, June 15, 1998.**

### Pennsylvania Department of Corrections v. Yeskey, No. 97-634

Decision, June 15, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 28, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the Third Circuit. The question is whether Title II of the Americans with Disabilities Act covers state prisons and their programs.

Yeskey, sentenced to 18 to 36 months, was recommended for a motivational boot camp whose successful completion could permit parole after six months. He alleges that admission was refused because of his history of hypertension. The District Court dismissed on the ground that the ADA did not apply to state inmates; the Third Circuit reversed that categorical ruling.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The categorical exclusion of state prisons cannot support dismissal. The remaining issues continue in the lower courts; the Court orders neither admission to the program nor early release.

#### Opinions and Joins

Justice O'Connor delivered the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine join the statutory coverage rule, its application to the categorical dismissal, the reservations and affirmance.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Title II covers state prisons and their programs

**Controlling proposition:** A state correctional department is a public entity under 42 U.S.C. §12131, and its prison services, programs and activities fall within §12132. Custody supplies no categorical exception: a qualified individual may not, by reason of disability, be excluded from participation, denied the benefits of those services, programs or activities, or otherwise subjected to discrimination by the public entity.

**Authority:** The unanimous Opinion of the Court directly adopts the complete statutory rule and its limits.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 12131 includes state and local governments and their departments, agencies, special-purpose districts and other instrumentalities. A state correctional department falls within that definition. A prison’s boot-camp opportunity is a program offering a benefit; its custodial setting does not make that description inapplicable. Section 12132 contains no exception for involuntary custody.

Even assuming Gregory’s clear-statement principle applies, the enacted definition and program language supply the required clarity. Gregory addressed an ambiguous express employee exception, not a rule that Congress must list each covered institution by name. Neither the importance of prison administration nor an assertion that Congress failed to anticipate this application authorizes adding an exception to comprehensive text.

Coverage does not establish liability. A qualified individual must meet essential eligibility requirements, with or without reasonable modifications to rules, policies or practices, removal of architectural, communication or transportation barriers, or auxiliary aids and services. The Court decides neither that Yeskey has a statutory disability nor that he meets those requirements. It requires no particular modification or fundamental alteration and displaces no legitimate security or direct-threat defense. The question whether a cause of action or particular relief is available, the constitutional scope of congressional power, abrogation, sovereign immunity and damages are not decided. Those distinct questions cannot be resolved by treating the institution as outside the statutory definition.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Gregory v. Ashcroft:** Assuming its clear-statement principle applies, the comprehensive public-entity definition satisfies it. The Court neither displaces that principle nor requires a prison-specific list.
- **City of Boerne v. Flores:** Its limits on Congress’s Fourteenth Amendment enforcement power remain intact; statutory coverage alone determines no application’s constitutional validity.
- **Seminole Tribe of Florida v. Florida:** Its actual statutory and remedy holdings remain distinct from the coverage question; no controlling direct-State amenability rule is added.
- **Pennsylvania v. Union Gas Co.:** Its existing force is undisturbed; the Court does not decide abrogation or immunity here.

#### Law After Decision

Title II has no categorical state-prison exception. Its ordinary disability, qualification and discrimination requirements remain operative, and its application must respect otherwise governing defenses and remedial rules. The coverage holding determines no constitutional-power or sovereign-immunity question.

#### Source Notes

The reported record at [524 U.S. 206](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-524/pdf/USREPORTS-524-206.pdf) supplies the sentence, boot-camp recommendation, alleged hypertension exclusion and lower-court posture. Sections 12131 and 12132 supply the definitions and operative prohibition. The alleged medical exclusion is not a finding that every statutory element is satisfied.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Third Circuit’s reversal of the categorical dismissal stands. The case returns to the ordinary lower-court proceedings for unresolved matters. No finding of disability, qualification, unlawful exclusion, damages liability or entitlement to early release is made.

**End of entry: Pennsylvania Department of Corrections v. Yeskey, Decision, June 15, 1998.**

### Phillips v. Washington Legal Foundation, No. 96-1578

Decision, June 15, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 13, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the Fifth Circuit. The question is whether interest actually generated by client funds in a mandatory IOLTA account is the private property of the principal’s owner for Takings Clause purposes.

Texas requires lawyers to place client funds in IOLTA when they cannot reasonably be expected to earn interest for the client, or when expected interest would not cover the costs of establishing and maintaining the account, service charges, accounting and tax reporting. The interest goes to the Texas Equal Access to Justice Foundation to finance legal services for persons with low incomes. The District Court granted summary judgment on the ground that clients had no property interest in the proceeds. The Fifth Circuit reversed that determination and left the remaining takings questions for further proceedings.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.
**Opposing:** Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, who would vacate and remand for integrated consideration of property, taking and compensation; if ownership is decided separately, they reject the asserted interest entitlement in the proper no-net-return program setting.

The lower courts must treat the interest as the client’s property while separately addressing taking, compensation, standing and remedy to the extent those questions remain live. The Court awards no compensation and does not determine the program’s ultimate constitutional validity.

#### Opinions and Joins

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Scalia | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas | Affirmance of the ownership determination | Complete ownership rule and qualifications; later constitutional consequences reserved. |
| Dissent | Souter | Stevens, Ginsburg, Breyer | Would vacate and remand | Integrated consideration of property, taking and compensation; also joins Breyer's alternative answer if ownership is reached alone. |
| Separate dissent | Breyer | Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg | Joins the integrated-remand disposition | Alternative objection to client ownership of the otherwise unavailable return. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Actually generated interest follows the client’s principal

**Controlling proposition:** Interest actually generated by client funds in an IOLTA account is the private property of the principal’s owner unless a valid background property rule assigns it elsewhere. The inability to earn a positive net return outside IOLTA does not itself eliminate ownership of the interest once generated; this holding determines title, not whether a taking occurred or compensation is owed.

**Authority:** The five-Justice Opinion of the Court directly adopts the ownership rule, its explanation, limits and affirmance.

**Controlling explanation:** Texas recognizes the clients’ ownership of their principal. Its traditional rule assigns the interest earned on that principal to the same owner. Roth directs attention to those existing rules and understandings; Webb’s forbids turning the resulting private earnings into public property by a new governmental declaration. Lucas preserves the distinction between a genuine antecedent limit on title and a newly imposed public burden. Its separate categorical land-use rule does not decide this case.

The pooled client funds generated the interest. Pooling and the public purpose of the proceeds do not sever that relationship. The absence of a profitable alternative return may matter greatly to the later taking and compensation questions, but value and ownership are not identical. An owner’s interests in possession, control and disposition are not extinguished merely because separately earning the increment would cost more than it produces.

Federal permission for NOW accounts and tax nonattribution do not independently assign title under the background property rule. Individuals are a permitted NOW category; for-profit corporations and partnerships face a distinct restriction, and the Federal Reserve’s charitable-interest interpretation addresses banking eligibility. The Court decides neither the validity of that interpretation nor banking legality by resolving ownership. The IRS’s treatment where clients control neither placement nor recipient likewise does not resolve title.

The Court does not decide whether Texas has taken the interest, whether compensation exceeds zero, public use, standing, First Amendment claims or entitlement to declaratory, injunctive or monetary relief. Nor does it prohibit reasonable charges for actual services or characterize this diversion as such a fee. No finding establishes that the particular funds could earn net client interest, individually or through feasible attorney pooling. Anticipated profits and improperly placed funds raise different questions.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Board of Regents v. Roth:** Existing state-law rules and understandings identify the property interest; the Court decides no procedural-due-process claim.
- **Webb’s Fabulous Pharmacies, Inc. v. Beckwith:** Its interest-follows-principal principle and prohibition on public conversion by assertion apply to the earnings actually generated. Its fee reservation is preserved; this decision does not resolve taking or compensation.
- **Lucas v. South Carolina Coastal Council:** Its distinction between antecedent title limits and new governmental restrictions is applied. Its complete-land-use deprivation rule is not extended to decide the unpresented IOLTA taking question.
- **Penn Central Transportation Co. v. New York City:** Its contextual takings inquiry remains available where applicable below; the Court makes no economic-impact, expectations or character-of-action determination here.

#### Law After Decision

The property element cannot be rejected merely because properly placed IOLTA funds would not generate a positive net return for the client outside the program. Ownership of actually generated interest is established at the stated scope. Taking, loss, compensation, standing and available relief remain separate questions; neither an automatic gross-interest award nor a categorical prohibition on IOLTA follows.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Justice Souter, dissenting, joined by Justices Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer.** The dissent would vacate the limited judgment and remand for the lower courts to consider property, taking and compensation together. The legal and economic arrangement matters to whether an isolated title declaration has any consequence under the Takings Clause. Roth’s reference to existing entitlements does not require examining one incident of ownership apart from the rules making that interest available. Penn Central directs attention to economic impact, reasonable investment-backed expectations and the character of governmental action, while compensation ordinarily concerns the owner’s loss rather than the recipient’s gain. Those matters could defeat a takings claim, but the dissent would have the lower courts decide them rather than declare no taking or zero compensation here.

The dissent accepts that property identification and valuation are distinct, but regards their isolated treatment in this case as liable to distort the full constitutional inquiry. It does not approve confiscation of the principal, which unquestionably belongs to the client. A claim concerning compulsory use of that principal remains available for lawful consideration even under the dissenters’ alternative rejection of the asserted interest entitlement. The four also join Breyer’s alternative analysis if the Court decides ownership alone.

**Justice Breyer, separately dissenting, joined by Justices Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg.** He joins Souter’s proposed integrated remand. If ownership is reached in isolation, he would reject the claimed entitlement for funds properly placed under the no-net-return program rule. The operative arrangement makes a charitable return possible where a client otherwise has no legally and economically realizable net benefit. Webb’s involved an independently productive fund; its protection against public relabeling does not settle ownership of this otherwise unavailable return. Lucas protects established entitlements without deciding how this claimed benefit must be identified.

This argument does not assume that every individual is legally barred from a NOW account. Individual eligibility, restrictions on corporate and partnership funds, administrative and reporting costs, and the distinction between gross and net yield must remain separate. The Federal Reserve interpretation is part of the banking setting, not a holding that its validity has been adjudicated. The reported Texas concession allows assessment of net client earnings through feasible attorney pooling; it establishes no positive alternative yield for these particular funds. Funds capable of earning net client interest belong outside IOLTA, and diversion of such interest or principal presents a different question. The dissent fixes no compensation amount, accepts no public-purpose exemption from the Takings Clause and does not direct final judgment against every claim.

#### Source Notes

The Texas program, reported account mechanics and lower-court posture appear at [524 U.S. 156](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-524/pdf/USREPORTS-524-156.pdf). The [record for No. 96-1578](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385014_0064) supplies the original litigation materials. Texas IOLTA Rule 6, 12 U.S.C. §1832(a)(2), the Federal Reserve’s October 15, 1981 interpretation and Revenue Rulings 81-209 and 87-2 address distinct program, banking and tax conditions. The reported alternative-pooling concession is a party position, not a finding of net yield on the particular funds.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Fifth Circuit’s judgment and existing remand remain in place. Further proceedings must respect client ownership of the generated interest and decide the remaining live questions under their own governing law. No injunction, damages award, particular compensation measure or ultimate constitutional disposition is ordered.

**End of entry: Phillips v. Washington Legal Foundation, Decision, June 15, 1998.**

### Zeran v. America Online, Inc.; Fourth Circuit No. 97-1523, 129 F.3d 327

Decision, June 15, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

On review of the Fourth Circuit's affirmance of judgment on the pleadings. The questions are whether §230(c)(1) bars notice-based distributor liability for third-party information and whether §230 applies when the provider conduct preceded enactment but the action followed it.

Anonymous advertisements for offensive Oklahoma City bombing merchandise listed Zeran's telephone number, bringing threats and harassment. He alleges repeated notice to AOL and failures to remove, retract, investigate or screen the material. The initial posting and notice occurred April 25, 1995; further postings followed. Section 230 became effective February 8, 1996, and this action against AOL began April 23, 1996. The January 4, 1996 action against radio station KRXO was a different suit. The original posting's removal date remains disputed.

All nine Justices participated in the merits determination.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 8–1.**

**Supporting:** Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Stone-Zsela.

Claims based on completed pre-enactment conduct return for consideration under otherwise applicable law. No liability, damages or mandatory trial is ordered, and no newly inferred post-enactment claim is resolved.

#### Opinions and Joins

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | Stevens | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Reversal and remand | Eight join the provision-specific temporal rule and its limits. |
| Opinion of the Court, Part II | Stevens | O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Explanation supporting reversal and remand | Five join the complete Landgraf organizing explanation. |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Scalia | Kennedy, Thomas | Joins Part I and the judgment | Distinct relevant-activity basis; does not join Part II's general framework. |
| Dissent | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Would affirm | Both temporal-direction and publisher-treatment grounds. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Section 230 does not extinguish claims based on completed pre-enactment conduct merely because the action was filed later

**Controlling proposition:** The original 47 U.S.C. §230 does not bar an otherwise legally sufficient state-law claim based on provider conduct completed before February 8, 1996 merely because the plaintiff sued afterward. Its prohibition on bringing inconsistent causes and imposing inconsistent liability does not prescribe that retrospective operation; the judgment on the pleadings must therefore be reversed and the pre-enactment claims considered under otherwise applicable law.

**Authority:** Part I of Stevens's Opinion of the Court is joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Those eight directly adopt the provision-specific temporal holding and bounded remand. Part II's complete Landgraf explanation is joined by O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, giving that explanation five direct votes. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas retain the distinct relevant-activity explanation stated in their concurrence.

**Controlling explanation:** Landgraf first asks whether Congress prescribed temporal reach and otherwise examines new legal consequences for completed conduct. Lindh confirms that context and statutory structure can supply direction without prescribed words. Section 230(d)(3), as enacted, preserves consistent state law and bars inconsistent causes and liability. Those commands state the preemptive effect of the publisher-treatment rule; they do not select commencement or adjudication as the exclusive temporal event. No contrasting pending-case provision or other contextual instruction supplies the missing transition. The findings, policies, good-faith protection, preservation clauses and definitions describe substantive purposes and scope rather than the treatment of accrued claims.

Removing an otherwise sufficient claim changes the substantive legal relation, rather than merely changing forum, requiring exhaustion or withdrawing a litigation subsidy. Hughes Aircraft rejects a later-filing shortcut where the amendment changes a substantive defense. That amendment removed a defense and this provision would add one, but both change substantive rights concerning completed conduct. Rivers rejects a demand for proved subjective reliance and the suggestion that legislative purpose alone determines temporal reach. Whether Congress could constitutionally alter an unfiled claim is distinct from whether it directed that result. No vested-property or final-judgment prerequisite defeats the ordinary statutory inquiry.

Zeran's allegations concern the 1995 postings and publication-related omissions; the AOL action began April 23, 1996. They remain allegations accepted for judgment on the pleadings. The Court orders no liability or damages. The disputed removal date is not resolved, and no continuing post-enactment omission is invented. Independently pleaded and established later conduct would require its own application of the statute. The prospective publisher/distributor question is unnecessary to this judgment and remains unresolved.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court determines no common-law liability, choice of state law, damages or mandatory trial. It neither recognizes a prospective notice exception nor adopts the Fourth Circuit's prospective publisher construction. AOL's own creation or development of information, independent promises, original-author liability and recordkeeping are not adjudicated. Section 230(c)(2)'s distinct protection for good-faith access restrictions and technical means, and the original (d)(1) federal-criminal, (d)(2) intellectual-property and (d)(4) communications-privacy preservation provisions, remain unchanged. The intellectual-property clause neither limits nor expands that law; the communications-privacy clause preserves the Electronic Communications Privacy Act of 1986, its amendments and similar state law. Definitions remain those in the original subsection (e).

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Landgraf v. USI Film Products:** Applied to statutory temporal direction and the impairment of substantive rights concerning completed conduct; its general framework retains its distinct opinion joins.
- **Rivers v. Roadway Express:** Applied to reject actual reliance as an indispensable condition and legislative purpose as sufficient temporal direction; its distinction between legislation and judicial construction remains intact.
- **Hughes Aircraft Co. v. United States ex rel. Schumer:** Applied to reject reliance on later filing and a court-access label where substantive rights or defenses change; the difference between adding and removing a defense does not exempt the claim holder's rights from the inquiry.
- **Lindh v. Murphy:** Its contextual and structural method is preserved. The absence of prescribed temporal words alone is not decisive; this enactment lacks a comparable contextual transition instruction.

#### Law After Decision

Claims based on completed pre-February 8, 1996 conduct cannot be dismissed under §230 solely because suit followed enactment. Otherwise applicable law controls those claims. The meaning of §230(c)(1) for conduct governed by the statute, including notice-based distributor liability, remains open in this Court. No new statutory exception or constitutional rule governing accrued claims is created.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Justice Scalia, concurring in part and in the judgment, joined by Justices Kennedy and Thomas.** They join Part I's temporal holding and remand but not Part II's full organizing explanation. Their relevant-activity inquiry identifies the publication-related conduct whose legal responsibility the statute regulates. The enforcement clause cannot be detached from the substantive publisher-treatment rule and read as though filing were its only regulated event. The text gives no direction to attach the new protection to the completed conduct here. Kennedy's Hughes Aircraft opinion likewise rejects later filing as conclusive when substantive legal consequences change. They adopt no constitutional vested-claim rule and do not decide prospective distributor liability.

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, dissenting.** He would affirm the judgment on the pleadings on two necessary statutory grounds. First, the original §230(d)(3) addresses both causes brought and liability imposed after enactment. In his view, those commands supply the temporal direction Landgraf requires, and applying them to this April 23, 1996 action follows that direction despite the earlier provider conduct. Hughes Aircraft lacked comparable temporal language and therefore does not control the result.

Second, he would hold the pleaded notice-based distributor duties within §230(c)(1). Notice may change a distributor's fault standard, but duties to remove, retract, investigate or screen another person's information remain duties concerning publication. AOL did not become the information's creator or materially develop it merely by receiving notice. He would leave these notice-based claims dismissed while preserving independently enforceable promises and claims not dependent on publisher status under their own elements. His position does not protect the original author, information AOL created or materially developed, conduct outside the statutory definitions, or claims within the statute's express preservation provisions. Recordkeeping remains unresolved. Neither ground of this dissent supplies a holding of the Court.

The allegations, filing dates and Rule 12(c) posture appear in the [Fourth Circuit opinion, 129 F.3d 327](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/129/129.F3d.327.97-1523.html). [Public Law 104-104, §509, 110 Stat. 137–139](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-104publ104/html/PLAW-104publ104.htm) supplies the enacted text and original subsection numbering. No separate Supreme Court docket number or argument date is established in the available public record. The lower appeal excluded the recordkeeping question.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Fourth Circuit's judgment sustaining dismissal under §230 is reversed. The case returns for ordinary further proceedings on the pre-enactment claims, with their state-law sufficiency and available relief unresolved. Any genuinely presented later-conduct issue requires its own statutory analysis; the disputed removal date establishes no such issue by itself.

### Caron v. United States, No. 97-6270

Decision, June 22, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 21, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the First Circuit. The question is whether §921(a)(20) preserves a conviction's federal firearms consequences when state restoration of civil rights retains a restriction on some firearm possession.

Massachusetts restored Caron's civil rights by operation of law. He held the required identification permit and could possess rifles and shotguns because the convictions were more than five years old, but could not possess handguns outside his home or business. A December 1993 search recovered six rifles and shotguns and ammunition. A jury returned four §922(g)(1) convictions. The initial enhancement counted one California and three Massachusetts convictions. After an en banc decision rejected the requirement of individualized restoration, the District Court excluded the Massachusetts convictions; the reviewed First Circuit judgment counted them because a significant firearms restriction remained.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Scalia, Souter and Thomas.

The Massachusetts convictions remain countable under the reviewed restoration question. The Court imposes no new sentence and decides no independent constitutional challenge, other predicate dispute or sentencing issue outside that question.

#### Opinions and Joins

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela delivered the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer. They join the complete conviction-status rule, its application to the actual residual restriction, the reservations and affirmance. Justice Thomas dissents, joined by Justices Scalia and Souter; they would reverse and remand on the express-prohibition construction and, independently, on residual lenity.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### A continuing state firearms restriction retains the conviction's federal effect

**Controlling proposition:** When a State restores civil rights but expressly retains the substantial firearm disability presented here—prohibiting handgun possession outside the home or business—the restoration does not remove the conviction under 18 U.S.C. §921(a)(20). The retained conviction has the federal consequences prescribed by the applicable firearms statutes, including for rifles and shotguns the State permits the person to possess; those statutes' independent offense and enhancement requirements still must be met.

**Authority:** The six-Justice Opinion of the Court directly adopts the statutory rule, its explanation, application, limits and affirmance.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 921(a)(20) excludes a restored conviction unless the restoration expressly retains the identified firearms prohibition. The conviction is the object classified, not a sequence of licenses for individual weapon transactions. Beecham directs the Court to the convicting jurisdiction's law to identify restoration. The federal statute then determines its consequences. Massachusetts's automatic restoration and permission to possess rifles and shotguns are accepted here, but its significant remaining handgun restriction prevents the conviction from being disregarded under the federal unless clause.

The contrary reading correctly emphasizes the actual state permission. It fails, however, to explain why Congress's qualification would disregard a continuing state firearms disability whenever some other possession remained lawful. The State need not prohibit every firearm in every place before the federal qualification has work to do. Congress borrowed state restoration status while prescribing its own broader consequence. That structure resolves the competing constructions; under Beecham and Chapman, lenity does not displace a meaning established by the ordinary interpretive inquiry. The separate statutes still determine which convictions qualify, what conduct and commerce connection are prohibited, and which enhancement applies.

The conclusion rests on the actual Massachusetts restriction, not a finding that Caron is individually dangerous. Love recognizes the personal and incorporated arms guarantee but does not resolve every convicted-person restriction. Neither that constitutional holding nor Staples's offense-specific factual-knowledge rule rewrites this restoration clause. Bryan addresses knowing unlawful dealing under a different provision. No distinct constitutional or mental-state issue is decided.

**Application and limits:** The Massachusetts convictions remain countable against the restoration objection, and the First Circuit's statutory judgment is affirmed. This is not a complete Massachusetts ban on handguns: possession in the home or business remained permitted. The Court accepts the parties' restoration premise without deciding every automatic restoration mechanism. It does not reconsider original violent-felony classifications, separately adjudicate the California predicate, pronounce every firearms disability constitutional, impose a new sentence or resolve an independently cognizable issue outside the presented restoration question. Section 922(g)(1)'s prohibition and commerce predicates remain distinct from §924(e)(1)'s three qualifying convictions, separate-occasion condition and minimum fifteen-year sentence.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Beecham v. United States and Jones v. United States:** Applied to the convicting-jurisdiction selector and connected restoration text; this case decides the residual-restriction question that Beecham left open.
- **Chapman v. United States and United States v. Granderson:** Their contextual interpretation and residual role for lenity remain. The Court finds no unresolved ambiguity after examining this text and structure.
- **Dickerson v. New Banner Institute:** Congress's later state-law selector controls over its former federal conviction-definition rule. A general federal protective purpose cannot create an additional statutory predicate.
- **Love v. Pepersack:** Its personal-right and incorporation holdings remain governing; the validity of a particular firearms restriction requires its own properly presented constitutional question. Lewis's extant-felony disability remains undisturbed.
- **Staples v. United States and Bryan v. United States:** Their distinct, offense-specific knowledge rules do not supply an exception to the restoration provision or a newly adjudicated mental-state issue here.

#### Law After Decision

The Massachusetts convictions remain available for federal firearms consequences despite permission to possess the seized kinds of long guns. Section 921(a)(20) classifies the conviction; the substantive prohibition and enhancement provisions supply their separate legal effects and requirements. State restoration is not a weapon-by-weapon federal exemption. The decision settles no independent constitutional challenge, mental element or remaining sentencing question.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Justice Thomas, dissenting, joined by Justices Scalia and Souter.** The three would reverse and remand because Massachusetts did not expressly prohibit firearm possession in the sense required by the federal exception. It allowed rifles and shotguns and even allowed handguns in the home or business. A restriction on handgun possession elsewhere does not amount to the express prohibition that, on their reading, prevents restored convictions from being excluded.

Their construction also treats the conviction as the statutory unit. It does not make the same conviction exist federally for a forbidden handgun and disappear for a permitted rifle. They choose the defendant's side of the all-or-nothing inquiry: because the restoration does not contain the required complete possession prohibition, these restored convictions are excluded. Beecham's sovereign selector supports consulting the actual restoration but did not decide this partial-restriction question. Federal protective purpose cannot replace Congress's chosen express condition.

Independently, the textually natural restoration reading remains at least sufficiently unresolved after context and purpose are examined to require lenity. Chapman and Granderson reserve that principle for ambiguity surviving ordinary interpretation; the dissent relies on the actual statutory difficulty, not merely the existence of two proposed readings. It would remove this unless-clause basis for counting the Massachusetts convictions and return the reviewed sentencing consequences for lawful disposition. It orders neither acquittal on all counts nor exclusion of every other predicate, and it decides no constitutional challenge. Both the textual ground and residual-lenity ground have the assent of all three dissenters.

#### Source Notes

The restoration mechanism, limited Massachusetts handgun restriction, convictions and procedural sequence appear in the reported record at [524 U.S. 308](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-524/pdf/USREPORTS-524-308.pdf), together with the earlier First Circuit proceeding, 77 F.3d 1, and the District Court disposition, 941 F. Supp. 238, 251–254. The [record for No. 97-6270](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385014_0196) identifies the original litigation materials. Sections 921(a)(20), 922(g)(1) and 924(e)(1) perform distinct statutory functions. Massachusetts General Laws chapter 140 §§121, 123, 129B, 129C and 131 and chapter 269 §10 supply the reported state-law setting.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The First Circuit's statutory judgment remains effective. Any further sentencing proceedings must treat the Massachusetts convictions as retained against this §921(a)(20) objection while respecting the independently applicable offense, enhancement and procedural requirements. This Court orders no new sentence or separate constitutional disposition.

**End of entry: Caron v. United States, Decision, June 22, 1998.**

### Gebser v. Lago Vista Independent School District, No. 96-1866

Decision, June 22, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 25, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the Fifth Circuit. The question is what showing permits private damages against a school district under Title IX for a teacher's sexual harassment of a student. Review concerns only the appealed Title IX judgment against the district.

In October 1992, parents of two other students complained to the principal about Waldrop's classroom comments. The principal met with him, received an apology and assurance against repetition, cautioned him and informed the guidance counselor, but did not transmit the complaint to the superintendent, the Title IX coordinator. Gebser did not report the relationship herself. Police discovered it in January 1993, and the district terminated Waldrop.

The evidence about grievance procedures is disputed. The superintendent's affidavit described a policy, while deposition testimony described no formal policy; she did not remember whether a handbook notifying students of procedures had been distributed. Gebser testified that she probably would have reported had she known what to do, while describing bewilderment and fear. These are evidentiary accounts, not findings of institutional fault or causation. The District Court granted summary judgment for the district, and the Fifth Circuit affirmed under an actual-supervisory-knowledge requirement.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

The reversal affects only the Fifth Circuit's affirmance of the district's Title IX summary judgment. Further proceedings must apply the controlling attribution propositions and determine the unresolved elements, causal facts, damages and defenses. No compensation or compulsory trial is ordered, and no unappealed claim or defendant is restored.

#### Opinions and Joins

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | Stone-Zsela | Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports reversal and remand | Five directly reject actual notice to a corrective official as the exclusive attribution route, with the stated limits. |
| Opinion announcing the judgment, Parts II–III | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Supports reversal and remand on the author's grounds | Part II states the narrower attribution ground with the component-specific Marks force explained below. Part III gives his own record application. The four other reversal supporters do not join either part. |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Breyer | Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg | Joins Part I and supports reversal and remand | Independent actual-authority/material-aid and negligent-correction grounds; does not join Parts II–III. |
| Separate concurrence | Ginsburg | Souter, Breyer | Supports reversal and remand within their broader framework | Proposed, noncontrolling avoidable-harm defense. Stevens reserves it; Stone-Zsela leaves defenses unresolved. |
| Dissent | O'Connor | Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Would affirm | Kinman's exclusive actual-notice rule and its record-specific insufficiency application. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. Actual notice to a corrective official is not the exclusive attribution route

**Controlling proposition:** A funding recipient's responsibility for a teacher's intentional discrimination cannot invariably be defeated solely because no official empowered to correct the harassment had actual notice. Kinman is limited insofar as it made that particular notice route exclusive; employment, ordinary teacher access or a technical regulatory breach, standing alone, still does not establish recipient liability.

**Authority:** The five Justices joining Part I directly adopt this limited negative proposition. They do not adopt a common complete damages framework.

**Controlling explanation:** Recipient responsibility remains distinct from the teacher's intentional wrong. That distinction does not make actual notice to a particular corrective official the only possible means of attribution. The categorical rule would exclude other supported grounds before their predicates and causal connection were examined. Franklin preserves compensation for proved intentional Title IX discrimination while requiring attribution and loss to be established separately. Kinman's actual-notice and deliberate-disregard route remains available, but its former exclusivity does not survive. The Court neither equates teacher employment with recipient responsibility nor treats a violation of grievance regulations as automatic discrimination or damages liability.

##### II. Authority materially enabling discrimination, combined with the recipient's causal fault, supplies a narrower attribution route

**Controlling proposition:** A recipient may be held responsible when school-conferred authority materially enables a teacher's intentional discrimination and the recipient's own actual knowledge or willfully blind, deliberately indifferent reporting failure causes that discrimination to continue. This is a sufficient route for attributing the discrimination beyond the actual-corrective-official-notice route; it establishes neither the underlying discrimination nor causal loss, damages or the defeat of a supported defense.

**Authority:** Part II of Stone-Zsela's opinion has no other formal joins. Its stated attribution component supplies the narrower controlling ground under Marks v. United States and Cipollone's component-specific application. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer independently support attribution whenever actual delegated educational power materially aids intentional harassment, without Stone-Zsela's additional recipient-fault requirement. School-conferred authority must actually and materially enable the wrong; title or opportunity alone is insufficient under either ground. Every case meeting the narrower conjunction therefore meets that independently sufficient broader attribution route. Those judgment-supporting positions total five, without converting the four Justices into joins in Stone-Zsela's opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** Franklin's damages remedy does not make every employee wrong the recipient's own wrong. The actual use of school-conferred authority must materially connect the discrimination to the educational relationship, and this narrower route additionally requires the recipient's own causal fault in its continuation. It does not permit the recipient to defeat attribution merely by deliberately disabling transmission of substantial warning information. Ordinary negligence or a technical policy omission alone does not satisfy this route. The underlying discrimination, institutional fault and causal continuation must still be proved.

Marks permits a narrower judgment-supporting ground without whole-opinion joins; Cipollone applies that inquiry to a particular legal component while leaving distinct liability and remedial questions unresolved. Here the four Justices' independent actual-authority ground contains the narrower attribution category. Their additional negligent-correction route is unnecessary to that inclusion. The proposed avoidable-harm limit supported by three of them concerns the extent of recovery; it does not negate their attribution ground. This limited nesting establishes neither their defense nor a complete damages formula. No general circumstantial-awareness presumption, automatic policy-failure inference or pooled-knowledge rule follows.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The authority, recipient-fault and causal-continuation conditions are conjunctive. The alternative involving reporting failure requires willful blindness and deliberate indifference, not constructive notice or negligence alone. The Court does not adopt ordinary agency liability as a complete general rule, the four-Justice negligence route or every Title VII attribution principle. Discrimination, institutional fault, causation, compensable injury, damages and defenses remain unresolved. Official-policy cases, prospective enforcement, individual liability and state-law remedies are outside this holding. The separate regulatory duties to adopt and publish prompt, equitable grievance procedures under 34 C.F.R. §106.8(b) and give nondiscrimination notice under §106.9(a) are not converted into strict private damages liability.

##### III. The appealed Title IX summary judgment is reversed for ordinary further proceedings

**Judgment and operative remedy:** The Fifth Circuit's judgment is reversed only insofar as it affirms summary judgment for the district on the appealed Title IX claim, and the case is remanded. The lower courts must determine whether school-conferred authority materially enabled the discrimination and whether the recipient's own actual knowledge or willfully blind, deliberately indifferent reporting failure caused it to continue, while preserving the actual-notice route and the remaining elements, ordinary procedures and defenses.

**Authority and application limit:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer support this formal reversal and ordinary remand. Stone-Zsela alone adopts Part III's application of his recipient-fault reasoning to the disputed warning and reporting evidence; the other four support further proceedings on their separately stated grounds. Their judgment agreement is not a five-Justice finding of actual knowledge, deliberate disregard, willful blindness, negligence, material aid, causation or liability. The Court orders neither compensation nor a compulsory trial. The unappealed constitutional, personal and state-law claims are not reopened.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Kinman v. Omaha Public School District:** Limited insofar as actual notice to a corrective official was the exclusive recipient-attribution threshold. Its actual-notice, deliberate and unreasonable disregard and causal-injury route remains available, together with the other Title IX elements. Its rejection of automatic teacher-status attribution and a technical policy breach as sufficient by themselves remains. No earlier sole-Justice awareness inference becomes Court law merely through this decision.
- **Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools:** Preserved as allowing appropriate compensation for proved intentional Title IX discrimination, subject to recipient responsibility, causation and loss. The present attribution holding does not establish a damages award or unintended-effects liability.
- **Cannon v. University of Chicago:** The established private action remains intact; no new regulatory cause of action is recognized.
- **Marks v. United States:** Applied to the particular sufficient attribution ground, without treating every reason or remedy proposed by a judgment supporter as controlling.
- **Cipollone v. Liggett Group:** Its component-specific logical-subset inquiry supports the limited attribution holding despite the absence of whole-opinion joins; separate proof, defenses and damages questions remain distinct.
- **Gade v. National Solid Wastes Management Association:** Its distinction between a directly shared negative proposition and unadopted complete affirmative frameworks supports keeping the exclusivity holding separate from the limited Marks analysis.
- **Meritor Savings Bank v. Vinson, Andrade and Faragher:** Their employment-attribution principles are not transferred wholesale to Title IX. Faragher's unresolved effective-protection/nonuse defense is not resolved here.
- **Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman:** Its funding-notice principle remains; neither it nor Title IX's administrative enforcement sequence becomes a literal exhaustion requirement for this private action.

#### Law After Decision

Actual notice to a corrective official is an available but no longer exclusive route to recipient responsibility. The narrower authority-plus-recipient-fault attribution route stated in Holding II controls at that component-specific scope under Marks. It supplies no comprehensive damages formula, no general awareness presumption and no rule of automatic liability from employment, nonreporting or a policy defect. The broader agency and negligent-correction theories and the three-Justice avoidable-harm defense remain noncontrolling. Kinman's distinct substantive harassment elements and the ordinary requirements of causation, injury, remedy and defense remain material. This record returns for further proceedings without any liability or compensation finding.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's opinion announcing the judgment, Part III.** The complaint to the principal, the internal warning and the evidence about the absence of a functioning grievance route create, in his view, a factual dispute about the recipient's own fault. He would require the lower courts to examine whether school-conferred authority materially enabled the discrimination and whether actual recipient knowledge or willfully blind, deliberately indifferent reporting failure caused its continuation. A district cannot defeat that inquiry through its own failure to transmit substantial warning information. He does not treat the complaint about classroom remarks as an established report of a sexual relationship, resolve the conflicting policy evidence, or find liability. Employment, ordinary negligence and a technical regulatory violation alone remain insufficient. This record-specific application has no other joins; the attribution component of Part II has only the limited Marks force stated above.

**Justice Breyer, concurring in part and in the judgment, joined by Justices Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg.** They join Part I's rejection of exclusivity and support reversal and ordinary remand, but do not join Parts II–III. They renew the two independent attribution routes previously stated in their Kinman position. First, actual delegated educational power materially aiding intentional harassment can attribute the wrong to the recipient without a separate higher-management knowledge requirement. Second, negligent failure to correct after responsible officials knew or reasonably should have known, causing injury or continuation, supplies an independent ground. Teacher title or opportunity alone does not establish actual aid, and a policy breach alone establishes neither negligence nor causation.

Franklin permits compensation for intentional discrimination. These Justices distinguish the teacher's intentional wrong from the agency principles attributing that wrong; their negligence alternative is not compensation for every unintended discriminatory effect. Cannon supplies the established private action, while Title IX's administrative notice and correction process does not require a second intentional wrong by recipient management in every damages case. They acknowledge that Kinman had rejected their broader grounds and would reconsider that exclusivity. Title VII's agency decisions supply a limited analogy, not an automatic transfer of every employment rule.

The actual delegation, material aid, information received, correction, reporting arrangements and causal injury require ordinary further consideration. The affidavit/deposition tension over policy and uncertainty about dissemination are evidence, not conclusive findings. These Justices do not establish liability or compel a trial. Their judgment agreement does not endorse Stone-Zsela's record-specific awareness reasoning. Their broader attribution theories do not become controlling merely because they support the same judgment; only the narrower component identified in Holding II has the stated Marks force.

**Justice Ginsburg, separately concurring, joined by Justices Souter and Breyer.** Within their broader attribution framework, these three would permit a limited avoidable-consequences defense. The district would bear the burden of proving that internal remedies were adequately publicized and likely to provide redress without undue risk, effort or expense. Recovery would be barred only to the extent the plaintiff unreasonably failed to use those measures and consequently suffered avoidable harm. A paper policy, nonreporting alone, an unsafe or ineffective route or eventual correction would not establish the defense. The uncertain policy and dissemination record does not conclusively establish it here. This is a causal limit on avoidable recovery, not automatic complete immunity or an additional Court-adopted attribution condition. Stevens reserves the defense and does not join its terms; Stone-Zsela leaves defenses unresolved.

**Justice O'Connor, dissenting, joined by Justices Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.** They would affirm under Kinman's actual-notice and deliberate-disregard rule. Franklin's availability of compensation does not answer recipient responsibility. Title IX's recipient structure, its administrative correction mechanism and Pennhurst's notice principle support attention to the recipient's own culpable nonresponse rather than ordinary agency attribution or knowledge imputed from the teacher's conduct. They would preserve Kinman's exclusivity and reject the additional attribution route.

Even assuming that the principal had corrective authority, the reported information and response do not, in their view, establish actual knowledge of the relationship or a substantial risk followed by deliberate and unreasonable disregard. The parents complained about classroom comments. The principal met with Waldrop, received an apology and assurance, cautioned him and informed the guidance counselor; termination followed police discovery. These responses are not declared perfect, and a warning does not invariably suffice. But this record does not establish the required culpable refusal to protect.

The possible absence of a functioning complaint procedure is not itself institutional actual notice. The principal's information cannot be pooled with Waldrop's knowledge, and the policy conflict cannot be resolved by assuming that every procedure was absent. Their rule requires neither a written complaint, a victim's personal report, a confession nor proof that a completed sexual relationship was already known. Actual notice of a substantial danger can suffice. Administrative and injunctive enforcement remain distinct, and the district receives no personal qualified immunity. These four oppose both reversal and the limitation of Kinman.

#### Source Notes

The procedural and evidentiary record is described at [524 U.S. 274](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-524/pdf/USREPORTS-524-274.pdf) and in [the original record for No. 96-1866](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385014_0076). App. 44a–45a, 64a–65a and 72a–73a and the identified summary-judgment deposition materials preserve the policy conflict and reporting testimony. A complaint about classroom comments is not a reported sexual relationship. Title IX, 20 U.S.C. §§1681–1682, and 34 C.F.R. §§106.8(b) and 106.9(a) address distinct substantive, administrative and regulatory duties. [Marks v. United States, 430 U.S. 188](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/430/188) supplies the narrowest-ground inquiry.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The appealed Title IX judgment is reversed and the claim returns for ordinary further proceedings. The lower courts must examine the school-conferred authority, material enablement, recipient fault and causal continuation under the route identified above, as well as any actual-corrective-official-notice route and the remaining requirements and supported defenses. The district may prevail if the applicable elements fail or a proper defense defeats relief. Individual, constitutional and state-law claims, administrative enforcement and prospective relief receive no new disposition.

### Pearson v. Hines; Ninth Circuit Nos. 95-17259 and 95-17260, 108 F.3d 265

Decision, June 22, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

On review of the Ninth Circuit’s affirmance of the judgment against Pearson. The questions concern a knowingly false disciplinary accusation retaliating for a prisoner’s lawful grievance, the effect of Superintendent v. Hill’s some-evidence rule, and the asserted need for an additional injury or actual chilling.

The conduct occurred January 19, 1992. Evidence included Hines’s prior grievances, guards’ descriptions of him as a frequent complainer and his statement that morning that he would file a grievance against Pearson. Pearson did not challenge the jury’s finding that his accusation was false. The discipline imposed ten days’ confinement and loss of television. The jury awarded $100 compensatory and $1,000 punitive damages against Pearson. The separate hearing officer’s postverdict judgment is outside this review; qualified immunity is not presented.

All nine Justices participated in the merits determination.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

Pearson’s $100 compensatory and $1,000 punitive judgment remains in force in the reviewed respect. No new damages, injunction, release, expungement, credit restoration or judgment against another officer is ordered.

#### Opinions and Joins

Justice Ginsburg delivered the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer. All nine join the retaliation, Hill, injury, proof and sufficiency reasoning and the bounded affirmance.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. A false retaliatory charge causing concrete discipline violates the protected grievance right

**Controlling proposition:** A correctional officer may not knowingly file a false disciplinary accusation because a prisoner filed or threatened to file a lawful grievance when the accusation causes concrete discipline such as the confinement and television loss established here. Those consequences supply adverse action without additional proof of subjective chilling, abandonment of grievances, a separate due-process deprivation or loss of a nonfrivolous court case.

**Authority:** The unanimous Opinion of the Court directly adopts both holdings, their stated limits and affirmance.

**Controlling explanation:** Lawful use or threatened use of the prison grievance system is protected petitioning. A knowingly false charge motivated by that activity advances no legitimate penological objective. Perry and Umbehr distinguish the absence of an entitlement to a benefit from a constitutional prohibition on retaliatory withdrawal; the prison setting does not make concrete punishment for protected petitioning harmless.

The actual ten-day confinement and television loss establish consequences. Lewis requires claim-specific obstruction for an access-to-courts claim; it does not add a lost-lawsuit element to this different claim. Sandin addresses meaningful liberty under genuine substantive constraints, through a sufficient and nonexclusive rule; it does not supply the elements of First Amendment retaliation. Edwards concerns claims necessarily invalidating outstanding earned-credit deprivation. No credit loss or sentence extension is established here, and this judgment does not invalidate the disciplinary adjudication.

The decision protects no false grievance, threat or independent rule violation, and does not prohibit lawful discipline or valid prison restrictions. It decides no liability for an accusation causing no material consequence, every adverse comment, trivial inconvenience or subjective offense.

##### II. Hill’s review of the adjudication does not shield the accusing officer’s retaliation

**Controlling proposition:** Hill’s some-evidence standard for a disciplinary adjudication does not defeat a claim that the accusing officer knowingly fabricated the charge in retaliation for protected conduct. Under ordinary proof and review rules, the prisoner must establish protected conduct and its substantial or motivating causal role; the officer may defeat the claim by proving by a preponderance that the same action would have occurred without that conduct, but a merely possible lawful explanation is insufficient.

**Controlling explanation:** Hill asks whether evidence supports the disciplinary decision for its due-process purpose. It does not license an official to supply knowingly false accusations for a forbidden reason. Crawford-El rejects special motive-proof barriers and preserves competent circumstantial evidence, while Mt. Healthy supplies the actual same-decision defense.

The jury could consider Hines’s grievance history and reputation, his stated intent to grieve Pearson and the unchallenged falsity finding. Ordinary deference to a jury’s supported findings governs; the Court makes no new credibility determination. Pearson’s proposed some-evidence shortcut cannot replace the actual causation inquiry or supply a special heightened quantum. The verdict stands on the reviewed grounds. The Court decides no qualified-immunity question, converts no later law into notice at the January 1992 conduct date and reopens no separate officer’s judgment.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Superintendent v. Hill:** Its evidentiary minimum for disciplinary adjudication remains intact, but does not shield the accuser’s knowingly false retaliation.
- **Crawford-El v. Britton:** Ordinary direct and circumstantial motive proof governs without a special quantum; its separate conduct-date immunity defense is not adjudicated here.
- **Mt. Healthy City School District Board of Education v. Doyle:** Its motivating-factor inquiry and defendant’s preponderance same-decision defense apply; possible lawful reasons are not proof of that defense.
- **Perry v. Sindermann and Board of County Commissioners v. Umbehr:** Their distinction between benefit entitlement and prohibited retaliation informs this claim, without importing the contractor-specific balance.
- **Lewis v. Casey:** Its court-access hindrance rule is confined to that distinct claim and adds no lost-lawsuit requirement here.
- **Sandin v. Conner:** Its substantive-constraint/meaningful-liberty rule remains distinct from retaliation; no atypical-hardship test is adopted.
- **Edwards v. Balisok:** Its credit-invalidity prerequisite is preserved at its actual scope; the described discipline establishes no credit loss or sentence extension.

#### Law After Decision

A knowingly false retaliatory accusation causing the concrete disciplinary consequences present here cannot be insulated by Hill’s some-evidence rule or a demand that the prisoner actually abandon grievances. Ordinary proof, causation, the actual same-decision defense and lawful prison restrictions remain. No universal code covering consequence-free accusations or every prison inconvenience is adopted.

The [original petition and opposition](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40386013_1111) identify the reviewed injury and Hill questions; the opposition states that qualified immunity is not raised. No separate Supreme Court docket number or argument date is established.

### Pennsylvania Board of Probation & Parole v. Scott, No. 97-581

Decision, June 22, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 30, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the Pennsylvania Supreme Court. The question is whether the federal exclusionary rule applies when parole officers search their known supervisee unconstitutionally and the parole authority seeks to use the evidence in revocation.

Scott’s parole prohibited possession of weapons. Parole officers arrested him, obtained his residence keys and searched without obtaining consent; they found firearms, a bow and arrows. The Board admitted the evidence and recommitted him. The Commonwealth Court reversed, and the Pennsylvania Supreme Court affirmed that reversal on the federal exclusionary ground because the searchers knew his parole status. The Court assumes the Fourth Amendment violation for this remedial question and does not decide the validity or state-law basis of the lower court’s consent construction.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, who would reverse the federal exclusion requirement and remand.

The exclusion and reversal of revocation on the reviewed federal ground remain effective. The state court may determine the lawful further consequence for the revocation judgment. No new release order is entered, and no separate procedural or hearsay ruling outside the reviewed question is disturbed.

#### Opinions and Joins

Justice Souter delivered the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer. They join the complete institutional-deterrence rule, its limits and affirmance. Justice Stevens also writes separately on the constitutional basis for an exclusionary remedy. Justice Kennedy dissents, joined by Justices O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas; they would reverse the federal exclusion requirement and remand on the ordinary-revocation cost-and-deterrence ground.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The parole authority may not use the fruits of its officers’ unconstitutional supervisory search to revoke parole

**Controlling proposition:** When parole officers unconstitutionally search their known supervisee and the parole authority seeks to use the fruits for supervision and revocation, the evidence must be excluded from the resulting revocation proceeding. The rule concerns the direct institutional connection between the officers’ search and that use; it requires no additional showing of a subjective plan to evade criminal-trial suppression.

**Authority:** The five-Justice Opinion of the Court directly adopts the bounded deterrence ground and affirmance. Justice Stevens's separate constitutional-remedy explanation supplies no additional holding of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** The exclusionary remedy must be examined in the proceeding where the government seeks the evidence. Calandra and Janis require attention to deterrence and the costs of excluding relevant proof; neither establishes a categorical exemption for every use outside a criminal trial. Here the officers’ own parole institution seeks the benefit of the search to return its supervisee to custody. Exclusion addresses the officials and use most directly connected to the violation. Exclusion only at a later criminal trial may leave that expected benefit untouched.

Morrissey and Gagnon recognize conditional liberty and the distinctive administration of revocation, including its need for reliable information. Losing relevant evidence and litigating the search impose real costs. Those costs do not outweigh the direct deterrent force of excluding the institution’s own unlawful acquisition in this setting. The rule requires neither all criminal-trial procedures nor proof that revocation and prosecution are identical.

Evans concerns objectively reasonable reliance on a non-law-enforcement court employee’s clerical error. It supplies no immunity for these officers’ own search. Askin and Fields / Crawley keep search legality, justification and suppression distinct; they do not themselves decide this extension. The Court assumes the violation without resolving consent or invalidating otherwise lawful parole-search conditions.

Evidence independently obtained by police, unknown-status searches and other collateral uses remain outside this holding. Good-faith reliance, attenuation, independent source and inevitable discovery retain their own supported predicates; none is established merely by naming it. The Court finds no special evasion scheme, harassment, effective alternative discipline or adequate civil remedy. It decides no independent state-law exclusion rule and enters no new revocation or release order.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **United States v. Calandra and United States v. Janis:** Their setting-specific deterrence inquiry is applied; neither forecloses exclusion in every nontrial proceeding.
- **Morrissey v. Brewer and Gagnon v. Scarpelli:** Their conditional-liberty and administrative-revocation principles are preserved without making revocation equivalent to a criminal trial.
- **Arizona v. Evans:** Its court-employee and objective-reliance exception is distinguished; it does not cover the parole officers’ own unlawful search on these facts.
- **In re Askin and Fields / Crawley:** Their separation of right, justification and remedy is preserved; their distinct facts and reservations establish no suppression exception here.
- **United States v. Leon:** Its actual objective-reliance limits remain; no automatic good-faith finding follows from the officers’ supervisory role.

#### Law After Decision

Federal exclusion applies to the parole authority’s use of its own officers’ unconstitutional search of a known supervisee at the stated scope. The rule neither mandates exclusion for every police search later relevant to parole nor invalidates lawful supervisory searches. Existing remedial exceptions require their own actual predicates.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Justice Stevens, concurring.** He joins the Court’s institutional-deterrence ground and judgment, while also regarding a meaningful exclusionary remedy as constitutionally necessary to make the Fourth Amendment effective. His positions in Leon and Segura treat judicial use of unlawfully obtained evidence as more than a matter of administrative preference. The investigating officers and revoking authority belong to the same enforcement system; allowing that system the ordinary benefit of its unlawful search would impair the right itself. He does not decide consent, require exclusion for every collateral use or impose all criminal-trial procedure on revocation. His additional constitutional characterization is his own, not a ground adopted by the other Justices joining the Court.

**Justice Kennedy, dissenting, joined by Justices O’Connor, Scalia and Thomas.** The dissent would reverse the federal exclusion requirement and remand. A Fourth Amendment violation does not automatically determine the remedy in each later forum. Calandra and Janis require examination of additional deterrence against the costs of withholding relevant evidence. Morrissey and Gagnon recognize a supervisory process concerned with compliance during conditional liberty; exclusion would obstruct that function and require additional search litigation.

The dissent acknowledges that these searchers were parole officers who knew Scott’s status, and that their institution could benefit from revocation use. It nevertheless considers the added deterrence insufficient to justify the evidentiary and administrative costs in the ordinary revocation setting presented. It relies on no finding that parole officers never investigate crime, that this search was lawful, or that discipline and civil damages proved effective. Evans’s specific clerical-reliance exception is not transplanted. The proposed reversal would remove this federal exclusion requirement, not establish admissibility under every other rule, extinguish available state law, order a new revocation or decide exceptional abuse not established here.

#### Source Notes

The reported facts and state rulings appear at [524 U.S. 357](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-524/pdf/USREPORTS-524-357.pdf). The lower court’s consent analysis and the dispute about its state-law source concern search validity; this decision assumes the violation to resolve the separately presented federal remedy. No finding establishes a special evasive purpose or the efficacy of alternative remedies.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The reviewed federal suppression ruling stands. Further state proceedings must respect that exclusion and the existing reversal of revocation, while determining any lawful consequence within the actual posture. The Court resolves no disputed search-validity issue and directs no new custody order.

**End of entry: Pennsylvania Board of Probation & Parole v. Scott, Decision, June 22, 1998.**

### United States v. Bajakajian, No. 96-1487

Decision, June 22, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 4, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit. The question is whether forfeiture of the entire $357,144 transported out of the country without the required report violates the Excessive Fines Clause.

Bajakajian pleaded guilty to willfully failing to report currency under 31 U.S.C. §§5316 and 5322. A separate false-statement count was dismissed by agreement, not decided by acquittal. After a forfeiture trial, the District Court found a lawful source, intended payment of a lawful debt and no connection to another crime. It refused full forfeiture under 18 U.S.C. §982(a)(1), ordered $15,000 forfeited and imposed three years’ probation and a $5,000 fine. Section 5322(a) authorized up to five years and $250,000; the applicable Guidelines maxima were six months and $5,000.

The Ninth Circuit affirmed on the ground that reporting currency was not an instrumentality and thus could not be forfeited, without reaching its proportionality prong. The Government seeks the full fund. Bajakajian did not cross-appeal the $15,000 forfeiture.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia and Kennedy, who would reverse the refusal of full forfeiture.

The existing judgment remains in place. The Court does not decide the constitutional validity of the unappealed $15,000 forfeiture or the statutory authority to substitute that amount. The conviction, probation and ordinary fine are not reopened; no new forfeiture amount is fixed.

#### Opinions and Joins

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | Thomas | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Governing fines framework | Punitive coverage, gross disproportionality and rejection of a categorical instrumentality prerequisite. |
| Opinion of the Court, Part II | Thomas | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Appellate-review rule | Independent review of legal proportionality; clear-error review of supported factual findings. Stone-Zsela does not join. |
| Opinion of the Court, Part III | Thomas | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Affirmance | Actual excessiveness of full forfeiture on these findings and bounded affirmance. |
| Dissent in part | Kennedy | O'Connor, Scalia | Would reverse the refusal of full forfeiture | Joins Parts I–II of the Court's opinion; rejects Part III's application and would permit the full statutory forfeiture. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. A punitive criminal forfeiture must not be grossly disproportionate to its offense

**Controlling proposition:** Section 982(a)(1)’s forfeiture imposed on this convicted offender as punishment is a fine subject to the Excessive Fines Clause. A punitive forfeiture violates that Clause when grossly disproportionate to the gravity of the offense for which it is imposed; statutory property connection alone does not answer excessiveness, and instrumentality is not a categorical prerequisite barring every currency-reporting forfeiture.

**Authority:** Part I commands all nine votes. Parts II and III have the distinct coalitions shown above. Each proposition below has direct majority support at its stated scope.

**Controlling explanation:** Austin recognizes partly punitive forfeitures as fines and leaves excessiveness separate from statutory connection. Alexander requires that independent inquiry for criminal forfeiture. The Government’s exaction here is imposed as punishment for the reporting conviction; it is not exempt because the currency is the property involved in the offense.

Substantial respect for legislative punishment judgments and the imprecision of gravity assessments make gross disproportionality, rather than strict equivalence, the governing boundary. Solem supplies that respect and proportionality analogy without deciding a new imprisonment question. The nature of the offense, culpability, harm, property’s relation to wrongdoing and authorized sanctions inform the comparison where relevant. They are not a compulsory exhaustive checklist, fixed ratio or government-cost formula. Congress may punish a reporting violation through property forfeiture, but its statutory authorization does not foreclose the constitutional inquiry. The Court rejects the circuit’s conclusion that such currency can never be forfeited for want of an instrumentality.

##### II. The legal proportionality judgment receives independent appellate review

**Controlling proposition:** Appellate courts independently review the constitutional determination whether this punitive forfeiture is grossly disproportionate, while reviewing supported underlying factual findings for clear error.

**Authority:** Part II commands the votes of all eight Associate Justices; Chief Justice Stone-Zsela does not join this proposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The facts found and the constitutional evaluation perform different work. Deference to the trial court’s supported findings does not transfer the legal boundary of the Excessive Fines Clause to unrestricted sentencing discretion. Independent review supplies no authority to invent an additional offense or substitute adverse facts for the findings. The Court announces no universal review standard for every mixed question.

##### III. Full forfeiture is grossly disproportionate on these findings

**Controlling proposition:** Forfeiture of the full $357,144 is grossly disproportionate to the willful reporting offense established here, considering the lawful funds and purpose, absence of another-crime connection, limited reporting harm and particular sentencing assessment together. The refusal of full forfeiture is affirmed without reviewing the existing $15,000 amount or setting a constitutional dollar ceiling.

**Authority:** The six Justices joining Part III directly adopt this application and bounded affirmance.

**Controlling explanation:** The reporting duty is serious, and willful failure remains culpable even when transporting the funds would otherwise be lawful. But the proved offense withheld information; the findings establish neither criminal proceeds nor laundering, trafficking, fraud or a separate revenue loss against which the whole fund may be measured. Dismissal of the false-statement count does not establish that no false statement occurred.

The applicable six-month and $5,000 Guidelines maxima reflect the particular offense’s assessed severity. Congress’s broader five-year and $250,000 maxima and the forfeiture authorization deserve weight but do not conclusively make this full exaction proportionate. Treating them as conclusive would deprive independent excessiveness review of work. The lawful source, lawful debt and lack of another-crime connection are relevant together; lawful property is not automatically immune from a fine.

The reporting system’s preventive function and the large unreported transaction supply the strongest contrary argument. They do not overcome the extraordinary mismatch on these supported findings. The Court substitutes no new amount. Because Bajakajian did not cross-appeal, neither $15,000’s validity nor authority to select it is decided; no rule identifies $15,001 as excessive. Wealth, income, livelihood effects, other materially different crime-connected forfeitures and a comprehensive rule for innocent owners or forfeitures directed against property remain unresolved.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Austin v. United States:** Partly punitive coverage and an excessiveness inquiry beyond statutory connection are applied; its holdings are not converted into an instrumentality-only test or a universal historical forfeiture theory.
- **Alexander v. United States:** Its independent offense-related Excessive Fines inquiry for criminal forfeiture is applied and given the gross-disproportionality formulation here.
- **Browning-Ferris Industries v. Kelco Disposal:** Its distinction between governmental punishment and private awards supports fine coverage.
- **Solem v. Helm:** Its legislative-deference and proportionality principles inform this fines rule without a new imprisonment holding.
- **Department of Revenue v. Kurth Ranch, United States v. Ursery and Hudson v. United States:** Their distinct Double Jeopardy classification rules remain intact and supply no automatic Excessive Fines result or universal cost ceiling.
- **One Lot Emerald Cut Stones and One Assortment of 89 Firearms:** Their distinct customs and forfeiture classifications establish no exemption or proportionality answer for this direct criminal reporting fine.

#### Law After Decision

Punitive criminal forfeiture is subject to real but deferential gross-disproportionality review. A statutory connection to the offense is not enough, but currency-reporting forfeiture is not categorically forbidden. The full exaction fails on these findings; the judgment establishes neither a ratio nor a constitutional or statutory approval of the existing partial amount.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Justice Kennedy, dissenting in part, joined by Justices O’Connor and Scalia.** They join punitive coverage, the gross-disproportionality standard, independent legal review and rejection of the circuit’s instrumentality prerequisite. They disagree with the application and would reverse the refusal of full forfeiture.

The amount concealed measures the scale of a deliberate failure to report. Congress may punish that wrong seriously without establishing that the funds were criminal proceeds or proving a second offense. Reporting helps detect financial crime that otherwise may remain hidden; treating the absence of a proved additional crime as decisive undervalues that preventive function. The five-year and $250,000 statutory maxima and the expressly mandated forfeiture demonstrate substantial legislative seriousness. The $5,000 Guidelines fine operates alongside, rather than silently replacing, forfeiture.

In their view the full amount remains within the demanding constitutional boundary despite lawful source and intended use. Their ground adds no laundering or trafficking finding and does not treat dismissal of the false-statement count as a conviction. They reject neither fines coverage nor the need for an individualized constitutional judgment, and they do not reopen the conviction, probation or ordinary fine. The disagreement supplies no ruling on the unappealed $15,000 amount.

#### Source Notes

The reported findings and appellate grounds appear at [524 U.S. 321](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-524/pdf/USREPORTS-524-321.pdf); the [original record for No. 96-1487](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385014_0060) identifies the underlying filings. Sections 5316, 5322(a) and 982(a)(1) supply reporting, statutory punishment and forfeiture provisions. The $250,000 statutory and $5,000 Guidelines maxima are distinct; the false-statement count was dismissed rather than resolved by acquittal.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The judgment refusing the Government’s demand for full forfeiture remains effective. No new calculation or further forfeiture hearing is directed. The unappealed partial forfeiture and other sentence components remain undisturbed without affirmative adjudication of their independent validity.

**End of entry: United States v. Bajakajian, Decision, June 22, 1998.**

### Wisconsin Department of Corrections v. Schacht, No. 97-461

Decision, June 22, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 20, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the Seventh Circuit. The question is whether a claim subject to an Eleventh Amendment bar destroys removal jurisdiction over an entire action containing otherwise removable federal claims.

Schacht sued Wisconsin’s corrections department and employees in personal and official capacities over his dismissal as a prison guard, alleging federal constitutional and civil-rights violations. The defendants removed, then asserted immunity. The District Court dismissed the State and official-capacity claims and entered summary judgment on the personal-capacity claims. Schacht appealed only the latter. The Seventh Circuit independently vacated the federal judgment and ordered the whole action returned to state court because an immune claim was present.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Vacated and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The Seventh Circuit must consider the remaining personal-capacity appeal under the proper jurisdictional rule. The unappealed State and official-capacity dismissals are not reopened, and the District Court’s personal-capacity merits judgment is not automatically restored or affirmed.

#### Opinions and Joins

Justice Thomas delivered the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine join the federal-question removal rule, claim-specific immunity analysis, construction of §1447(c), reservations and vacatur with remand.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Immunity affecting one claim does not eliminate removal jurisdiction over the remaining federal claims

**Controlling proposition:** An action presenting substantial federal claims within §1331 is removable under §1441(a) despite an Eleventh Amendment bar applicable to particular State or official-capacity claims. The bar prevents adjudication of the affected claim when properly asserted, but does not erase otherwise valid original and removal jurisdiction over the remaining federal claims or require remand of the entire action under §1447(c).

**Authority:** The unanimous Opinion of the Court directly adopts the rule, explanation, reservations and appellate remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The complaint itself supplies a federal-question basis independent of the asserted immunity. Hafer distinguishes a claim against a State from a personal-capacity claim for an officer’s own violation. Immunity affecting the former does not make the latter cease to present federal law. City of Chicago likewise separates the federal anchor for removal from questions concerning particular claims and their later treatment.

State immunity can be waived and does not automatically destroy every federal claim in a case. It therefore differs from failure of complete diversity where that is the sole original-jurisdiction basis. That distinction does not classify immunity as ordinary personal jurisdiction in every respect or establish waiver here.

Even assuming immunity counts as subject-matter jurisdiction for this analysis, §1447(c)’s direction to remand a case lacking such jurisdiction does not require erasing the independent federal claims. The statute’s separate thirty-day rule for a nonjurisdictional removal-defect motion creates no new defect or timeliness finding in this litigation. The Court need not decide the general jurisdictional classification of Eleventh Amendment immunity.

Removal authority must be established and immunity and any genuinely remaining remand questions treated at their proper claim-specific scope. The actual complaint contains federal claims; City of Chicago’s supplemental-claim discussion is an analogy, not an additional claim here. Wisconsin’s waiver by removing, the merits of the personal-capacity claims, any new relief against the State and compulsory retention of every hypothetical supplemental claim remain undecided. The unappealed dismissals stay outside the returned appeal.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Hafer v. Melo:** Its personal/official-capacity distinction is applied; state immunity does not independently bar a personally payable judgment, and personal defenses and merits remain separate.
- **City of Chicago v. International College of Surgeons:** Its substantial-federal-claim removal anchor is applied. Its supplemental-jurisdiction and retention rules create no additional actual claim here.
- **Seminole Tribe of Florida v. Florida:** Its actual statutory and remedial holdings and unresolved direct-State rationale remain unchanged; this decision adopts no general abrogation theory.
- **Pennsylvania v. Union Gas Co.:** Its existing force is undisturbed; the Court neither overrules it nor decides direct-State amenability in this case.
- **Gutierrez de Martinez v. Lamagno:** Its distinct certification, substitution and removal scheme is not transplanted into ordinary federal-question removal.

#### Law After Decision

An immune claim does not make State immunity spread to every otherwise removable federal claim in the action. Federal courts must distinguish the statutory jurisdictional anchor from immunity and remaining claim-specific questions. The decision creates no removal-waiver rule, new State liability or automatic merits disposition.

#### Source Notes

The complaint, capacities and scope of the appeal are reported at [524 U.S. 381](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-524/pdf/USREPORTS-524-381.pdf). Sections 1331, 1441(a) and 1447(c) govern the stated jurisdictional question. [Public Law 104-219](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-104publ219/html/PLAW-104publ219.htm), effective October 1, 1996, distinguishes a timely motion based on a defect other than subject-matter jurisdiction from remand for absence of subject-matter jurisdiction. No separate nonjurisdictional defect or timeliness issue is decided.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Seventh Circuit’s whole-action remand is vacated. The personal-capacity appeal returns for consideration under the correct jurisdictional framework and otherwise applicable law. No new merits relief, reinstatement, damages or claim against Wisconsin is ordered.

### Swidler & Berlin v. United States, No. 97-1192

Decision, June 25, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued June 8, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the District of Columbia Circuit. The question is whether the attorney-client privilege survives death and whether later criminal-investigative importance permits a case-specific override.

As investigations concerning the dismissal of White House Travel Office employees began, Vincent Foster consulted attorney Hamilton for legal representation. Hamilton made handwritten notes. Foster died nine days later. A federal grand jury, at the Independent Counsel’s request, subpoenaed the notes in an investigation concerning possible crimes during the earlier investigations. The District Court denied enforcement on privilege grounds. The court of appeals adopted a posthumous exception for communications of substantial relative importance to particular criminal litigation.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas, who would sustain a strictly limited privilege inquiry on remand in the reviewed respect, without ordering disclosure themselves.

The subpoena may not compel these privileged communications merely because they are important to the investigation. The Court does not adjudicate work product or an unestablished separate exception.

#### Opinions and Joins

Justice Stevens delivered the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. They join the complete post-death survival rule, rejection of the asserted need balance, reservations and reversal. Justice O'Connor dissents, joined by Justices Scalia and Thomas; they would permit a strictly confined inquiry into necessary information unavailable elsewhere and a compelling criminal interest, without a final disclosure or work-product determination.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Attorney-client confidentiality survives death without a general investigative-need exception

**Controlling proposition:** The federal attorney-client privilege ordinarily continues after the client’s death. Substantial importance to a criminal investigation alone does not authorize disclosure of protected communications through a case-specific need balance; waiver, crime-fraud and genuinely distinct recognized exceptions retain their separate predicates.

**Authority:** The six-Justice Opinion of the Court directly adopts the rule, its explanation, limits and reversal.

**Controlling explanation:** Rule 501 directs common-law privilege development through reason and experience, subject to the Constitution, Acts of Congress and rules prescribed under statutory authority. Trammel requires attention to function and evidentiary cost; it does not require narrowing an established privilege whenever evidence would be useful. Upjohn explains why clients need predictable confidence when they seek legal advice, while keeping underlying facts distinct from protected communications.

Death ends personal criminal exposure but does not erase the reasons living clients may care about later disclosure. Advice may concern reputation, family, property and other legal interests after death. A client cannot know how a future investigator or judge will assess the importance of a communication. Jaffee’s rejection of routine evidentiary balancing supports that predictability principle without itself deciding this different privilege’s post-death scope.

The strongest objection is the loss of potentially critical information when the client can no longer supply it. The Court accepts that evidentiary cost but rejects later usefulness as the proposed boundary. Glover’s testamentary exception serves the deceased client’s estate-related intention; it is not a general preference for criminal-investigative evidence. Nixon addresses executive privilege in criminal-trial process and supplies no universal balancing requirement for this established relationship privilege. Neither precedent establishes that adding a new exception is costless.

The legal-consultation notes remain protected against the asserted need ground. The Court makes no finding about the notes' contents, waiver, a crime-fraud predicate or this client's subjective intention. Work product, successor authority or waiver, a presented testamentary dispute and an exceptional criminal defendant’s constitutional need are not decided. Independently discoverable facts remain distinct, and a future exception supported by its own legal authority requires a proper record. Rule 501’s separate state-law sentence remains effective; no comprehensive code of privilege exceptions is announced.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Upjohn Co. v. United States:** Predictable confidence at the time legal advice is sought supports survival after death; protection of communications does not conceal independently discoverable facts.
- **Trammel v. United States:** Its reason-and-experience development of privilege is applied with attention to function and evidentiary cost, not an automatic preference for disclosure.
- **Glover v. Patten:** Its client-serving testamentary setting is distinguished from a general criminal-investigative exception; no actual estate dispute is resolved.
- **Jaffee v. Redmond:** Its predictable-relationship principle and rejection of routine need balancing support the reasoning. Its psychotherapy scope and reserved contours remain unchanged.
- **United States v. Nixon:** Its executive-privilege and criminal-trial setting is distinguished; it does not require balancing within every established confidential relationship.

#### Law After Decision

Ordinary attorney-client privilege survives death, and investigative importance alone does not defeat it. Existing and genuinely distinct exceptions remain governed by their own sources and predicates. The decision neither adjudicates work product nor makes every posthumous communication or underlying fact immune from lawful inquiry.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Justice O’Connor, dissenting, joined by Justices Scalia and Thomas.** They agree that attorney-client privilege ordinarily survives death. They would nevertheless permit a narrowly confined inquiry when a criminal defendant’s exculpatory interest or a compelling law-enforcement need concerns necessary factual information unavailable from other sources, and fairness and accuracy outweigh the remaining confidentiality justification. Ordinary usefulness or the client’s death alone is insufficient.

Death removes personal criminal exposure and may diminish other risks while also eliminating the client as an alternative source. In their view Rule 501’s reason-and-experience direction permits that distinct exception. They acknowledge Upjohn’s concern with predictable candor and Jaffee’s rejection of routine evidentiary balancing, but distinguish exceptional post-death scope from ordinary need balancing within a continuing treatment privilege. They neither overrule Jaffee nor regard confidentiality as valueless after death. Existing exceptions show legal limits, not that another exception has no cost.

The notes’ contents, actual necessity, availability elsewhere and ultimate balance remain to be determined. The dissent would sustain the availability of that limited privilege inquiry on remand in the reviewed respect; it does not itself order production, find waiver or crime-fraud, or adjudicate work-product protection. Its proposed exception is not the Court’s rule.

#### Source Notes

The contents of the withheld notes and any waiver, crime-fraud or exceptional constitutional predicate are not established by their investigative importance.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The court of appeals’ privilege-balancing disposition is reversed. Further proceedings must protect the communications against the asserted investigative-need ground. No production order issues, and work product or a genuinely distinct, properly supported exception receives no decision here.
