### Air Line Pilots Association v. Miller, No. 97-428

Plenary merits decision, May 26, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 23, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit, 108 F.3d 1415. The question is whether nonmember employees who have not agreed to arbitrate must exhaust a union's impartial arbitration procedure before bringing their compulsory-fee objections to federal court. Review concerns exhaustion for the 1992 fee-calculation dispute. Earlier facial challenges to the agency-shop clause were resolved against the pilots and were not appealed; they and other fee years are outside this review. The district court required exhaustion; the court of appeals rejected that requirement and remanded the remaining 1992 fee disputes.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg.

**Opposing:** Stevens and Breyer.

The nonconsenting objectors may pursue independent judicial adjudication without completing the union's arbitration as a prerequisite. The D.C. Circuit's bounded remand remains; no refund, final chargeable percentage, particular expenditure ruling or discovery plan is ordered.

#### Opinion Topology

Souter delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Ginsburg. Breyer dissents, joined by Stevens.

#### Holdings

**Holding and operative rule:** Nonmember employees who did not agree to arbitrate need not exhaust this union-provided impartial arbitration procedure before bringing their compulsory-fee claims in federal court. Hudson's requirement that a union offer a prompt impartial opportunity to contest fees does not make that private procedure a compulsory preliminary step; the ruling does not displace express statutory exhaustion requirements, appropriate administrative exhaustion or an actual arbitration agreement.

**Authority:** Souter's opinion for the seven Justices identified in Opinion Topology; all seven adopt the complete rule, its explanation and its limits.

**Controlling explanation:** Hudson protects employees from having to finance activities beyond the lawful scope of compulsory fees. A procedure supplied for that protection does not become a condition of judicial access merely because the union adopts it. AT&T Technologies and First Options require an agreement before contractual arbitration can bind a party; participation solely to protest arbitral authority does not provide that agreement. But nonconsent alone does not answer every exhaustion question. McCarthy requires attention to statutory design and the actual institution involved. Here Congress has not allocated initial decisionmaking to this private arbitrator. The arbitrator does not complete the work of a statutory agency or act as the union's final policymaking authority.

ALPA argues that a nonbinding first step could settle claims, develop facts and reduce expense without displacing later court review. Those possible benefits do not establish a compulsory gate under this arrangement. Objectors must identify the nature of their objections sufficiently for litigation, while the union retains its burden to establish that challenged expenditures are germane to collective representation. Courts retain balanced discretion to defer discovery or other proceedings while prompt arbitration may narrow the actual dispute. Such case management does not make union arbitration a universal prerequisite or give its award contractual finality.

The plaintiffs either did not participate or participated under protest. They remain entitled to independent judicial treatment. The Court does not decide consenting parties' obligations, any particular expenditure's chargeability, audit adequacy or the final amount owed.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Chicago Teachers Union v. Hudson, 475 U.S. 292:** Applied as a requirement to provide an adequate fee explanation, a reasonably prompt impartial opportunity and escrow for reasonably disputed amounts; it is not converted into compulsory exhaustion or preclusive arbitral review.
- **AT&T Technologies v. Communications Workers, 475 U.S. 643:** Applied to preserve agreement as the source of contractual arbitration authority.
- **First Options of Chicago v. Kaplan, May 22, 1995:** Applied to antecedent assent; a protest appearance does not itself establish consent. Its arbitrability rule does not decide every noncontractual exhaustion duty.
- **McCarthy v. Madigan, March 4, 1992:** Applied to the actual statutory scheme and institutional function. Its surviving judicial-exhaustion framework is neither overruled nor expanded into a universal no-consent exception.

#### Law After Decision

The union's impartial procedure remains an available protection, but these nonconsenting fee objectors may seek independent federal adjudication directly. The decision adds this private-union application without altering lawful statutory exhaustion, agreed arbitration, ordinary judicial case management or the union's substantive burden to justify chargeable expenditures. The amount and legality of the disputed fees remain to be determined.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Breyer, joined by Stevens, would reverse the categorical rejection of exhaustion and remand for a bounded initial-recourse rule. Hudson's balance between prompt protection of objectors and the legitimate collection of representative expenses, informed by Street, Allen and Abood, permits an impartial, expeditious and adequate nonbinding procedure to narrow the dispute before court litigation. Their rule does not depend on contractual assent: escrow and genuinely independent judicial adjudication would protect the employees while preliminary arbitration could avoid duplicate expense. It gives the award neither preclusion nor contractual finality. Pilots who already completed arbitration have taken that step; no automatic loss follows for nonparticipants without considering the lawfulness and actual adequacy of the procedure and their posture. Both agree that a discretionary judicial pause for prompt arbitration can address practical duplication.

#### Source Notes

[Miller v. Air Line Pilots Association, 108 F.3d 1415](https://static.case.law/f3d/108/html/1415-01.html) supplies the lower proceedings, the pilots' nonconsent and the remand's scope. The parties do not dispute Hudson's application to this RLA arrangement; that premise does not decide its application to every private-union arrangement. No finding of consent is drawn from participation under protest.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The fee dispute returns under the court of appeals' remand for independent findings and appropriate proceedings on the remaining issues. The exhaustion question is resolved; the Court directs neither a final fee award nor a particular course of discovery.

**End of entry: Air Line Pilots Association v. Miller, Plenary merits decision, May 26, 1998.**

### Calderon v. Ashmus, No. 97-391

Article III threshold decision, May 26, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 24, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, 123 F.3d 1199. The court of appeals affirmed a preliminary injunction barring California officials from invoking chapter 154's special capital-habeas procedures against the prisoner class, with a clarification tied to current state law. Review was granted on Eleventh Amendment and First Amendment questions. The Court resolves the antecedent Article III question and does not reach those granted issues.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded for jurisdictional dismissal, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The declaration that California is unqualified and the injunction preventing officials from asserting chapter 154 cannot remain in force. Dismissal establishes neither California's qualification nor a prisoner's default, and it awards no habeas relief.

#### Opinion Topology

Stone-Zsela delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Breyer concurs, joined by Souter; both also join the Court's opinion in full.

#### Holdings

**Holding and operative rule:** A prisoner class may not obtain a standalone declaration selecting chapter 154 as inapplicable to future habeas litigation when that declaration would settle only an isolated procedural issue and would resolve neither entitlement to habeas relief nor a complete independent legal claim. This action lacks an Article III controversy and must be dismissed; chapter 154's applicability may be determined when properly presented in a concrete habeas proceeding.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's opinion for all nine Justices controls the rule, its application and jurisdictional remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The Declaratory Judgment Act supplies a remedy within an actual controversy, not authority to obtain advance answers to every legal issue that may affect another suit. Aetna permits a conclusive determination of adverse legal relations. Seminole likewise recognizes judicial power to determine an existing statutory duty conclusively even when mediation or executive steps follow. Neither an immediate coercive remedy nor the absence of later proceedings is indispensable.

This complaint does something different. It attempts to settle one procedural feature of the prisoners' eventual custody challenges, leaving the actual habeas controversies to be brought and decided elsewhere. Coffman and Wycoff require attention to that incompleteness rather than merely the importance of the desired advance answer. California's asserted eligibility and the shorter filing regime create serious practical concerns, especially given the reported delays in appointing counsel. But the usefulness of resolving a prospective litigation issue does not make that issue a complete independent legal relation suitable for this declaration. Texas and Ohio Forestry preserve anticipatory adjudication of a sufficiently concrete dispute; they do not dispense with that requirement.

The qualification issue belongs in an actual habeas case with the applicable statutory predicates before the court. The Court decides no individual clock, counsel-system deficiency or claim default. It reaches neither the Eleventh Amendment nor the First Amendment objections, California's actual qualification, underlying habeas merits, §1983 liability or fees. Dismissal does not depend on a categorical objection to declaratory relief or pre-enforcement review.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Aetna Life Insurance Co. v. Haworth, 300 U.S. 227:** Applied for a definite controversy and conclusive declaratory resolution of adverse legal relations.
- **Seminole Tribe of Florida v. Florida, March 27, 1996:** Distinguished because its binding adjudication determines an existing statutory negotiation duty; its rule permitting conclusive declarations despite later statutory steps remains intact.
- **Coffman v. Breeze Corporations, 323 U.S. 316:** Applied to the attempted advance determination of an isolated issue rather than a complete controversy.
- **Public Service Commission v. Wycoff Co., 344 U.S. 237:** Applied to distinguish a conclusive legal relation from a separated issue expected to arise in later litigation.
- **Texas v. United States, March 31, 1998:** Preserved; actual fitness and present hardship govern anticipatory review, without making strategic importance alone sufficient.
- **Ohio Forestry Association v. Sierra Club, May 18, 1998:** Preserved at its concrete-dispute and present-hardship boundary; it does not decide this standalone habeas-procedure action.
- **Citizens for a Better Environment v. Steel Co., June 2, 1997:** Applied in resolving the actual Article III defect before contested merits here, without an exhaustive rule for all threshold sequences.

#### Law After Decision

The decision adds a boundary for standalone chapter-154 qualification litigation. It preserves conclusive declaratory judgments and proper anticipatory challenges, while requiring this procedural issue to arise in an actual controversy. Chapter 154's separate qualification, counsel, transcript-deferral, filing, tolling and claim-scope provisions remain unchanged. Neither prospective-officer doctrine nor §1983 supplies jurisdiction for the same isolated declaration merely under another label. No particular independent surviving claim is identified or sustained.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Breyer, joined by Souter, emphasizes a possible route to prompt determination in actual habeas litigation. A timely petition may present an amendment question for which chapter 154's applicability matters; the ordinary amendment rules and the more restrictive chapter-154 rules remain distinct. An appropriate ruling may also permit an interlocutory appeal under §1292(b), but only if the district court states in writing that there is a controlling legal question, substantial ground for difference of opinion and a prospect of materially advancing the litigation's termination, and the court of appeals permits an application made within ten days. An application does not itself stay proceedings. These possibilities promise no amendment, certification, appeal, exhaustion determination or additional filing time for any prisoner. They explain a concrete adjudicative route without treating convenience or aggregate efficiency as Article III jurisdiction.

#### Source Notes

[Ashmus v. Calderon, 123 F.3d 1199](https://static.case.law/f3d/123/html/1199-01.html) supplies the lower injunction, its interlocutory review and the reported counsel-system findings. Those findings are not a determination by this Court that California fails chapter 154, and the aggregate appointment delay establishes no particular prisoner's filing deadline. The lower court did not decide the merits of §1983 liability on the interlocutory appeal.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns for dismissal of the complaint and removal of the declaration and injunction. The Court orders no habeas filing, deadline extension or adjudication of California's eligibility. Any properly presented qualification issue in an actual habeas proceeding must be resolved under that proceeding's independent statutory and jurisdictional requirements.

**End of entry: Calderon v. Ashmus, Article III threshold decision, May 26, 1998.**

### County of Sacramento v. Lewis, No. 96-1337

Plenary merits decision, May 26, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 9, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, 98 F.3d 434. The case concerns the constitutional culpability required when a rapid police pursuit ends in an unintended fatal collision. After final summary judgment, the court of appeals reversed Deputy Smith's individual qualified-immunity judgment using a deliberate-indifference or reckless-disregard standard. Its affirmance of the municipal judgments and the unappealed state-law dispositions are outside this Court's review. No Fourth or Eighth Amendment claim is presented.

#### Judgment

**Reversed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The case returns for proceedings consistent with termination of the reviewed federal individual claim, leaving the district court's favorable individual judgment in effect. Six Justices adopt the Court's contextual constitutional ground; Scalia and Thomas rely on a distinct constitutional ground, and Stevens relies only on qualified immunity. No damages, trial, new evidentiary hearing, municipal retrial or state-law judgment is directed.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Kennedy | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports reversal | Complete rapid-pursuit culpability rule, application, qualifications and remedy |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Stevens | No other Justice | Supports reversal | Individual immunity because May 22, 1990 law did not make this conduct's federal unlawfulness sufficiently apparent; reserves constitutional merits |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Scalia | Thomas | Supports reversal | Distinct constitutional ground; no join in the Court's conscience-based purpose-to-harm standard |
| Concurrence | Breyer | No other Justice | Fully joins the Court and supports reversal | Preserves flexibility to decide an appropriate case on immunity before difficult constitutional merits |

#### Holdings

**Holding and operative rule:** In the rapidly evolving high-speed pursuit presented here, where officers must make instant choices amid competing enforcement and safety obligations, reckless or deliberately indifferent risk-taking alone does not establish a substantive-due-process violation. A purpose to cause harm unrelated to a legitimate law-enforcement objective is required; because this plaintiff-favorable record does not establish that purpose, the reviewed federal individual claim fails.

**Authority:** Kennedy's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies six direct votes for the complete rule and application. Neither the two distinct constitutional votes nor Stevens's immunity vote supplies additional support for that rule. No combination of separate rationales is needed.

**Controlling explanation:** Rochin identifies the constitutional boundary against arbitrary executive abuse; its conscience-shocking standard must be applied through precedent, constitutional tradition and the actual setting, not a judge's personal reaction. Daniels excludes negligence but left intermediate culpability unresolved. Whitley explains why competing obligations and an emergency matter to fault, while reserving the civilian due-process question. Farmer's actual-awareness standard concerns prison safety with a materially different opportunity to respond; Doe likewise requires knowledge, authority and a reasonable opportunity for protection against deliberate abuse. Those holdings do not mechanically determine culpability in a rapid pursuit.

The officer chose to pursue, and the favorable account includes dangerous speed, close following distance, danger to others, inadequate communication and alleged policy violations. The lack of a serious predicate offense and the ability to abandon pursuit are substantial criticisms. They do not, in this rapidly developing encounter, establish the improper harmful purpose required for constitutional liability. The collision was unintended, and the supported record supplies no purpose to injure unrelated to enforcement. The Court finds neither wise police judgment nor an adjudicated benevolent motive.

Actual opportunity for deliberation remains important. This rule is not a universal intent requirement, an automatic deliberate-indifference rule whenever an official could desist, or permission to manufacture an emergency. Purposeful collisions, deliberate terrorizing conduct, contrived encounters and materially different opportunities for considered action require their own facts and analysis. The decision creates no seconds-based cutoff, broad noncustodial immunity or mandatory order between constitutional merits and qualified immunity. It resolves neither the excluded municipal and state claims nor a procedural-due-process challenge to state remedies.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Rochin v. California, 342 U.S. 165:** Applied as the executive-abuse boundary, with the required culpability identified through the actual emergency setting.
- **Daniels v. Williams, 474 U.S. 327:** Its negligence exclusion remains; the Court resolves the previously open intermediate-culpability question only for this rapid pursuit.
- **Whitley v. Albers, 475 U.S. 312:** Its competing-obligation analysis informs the rule, but its prison-security holding is not treated as already deciding civilian pursuit liability.
- **Farmer v. Brennan, June 6, 1994:** Distinguished by the custodial duty and actual opportunity for response. Its awareness, reasonable-response and permissible-inference rules remain intact.
- **Doe v. Taylor Independent School District, October 16, 1995:** Distinguished because knowing supervisory acquiescence in deliberate abuse requires authority, opportunity and causal participation not supplied merely by continuing this pursuit.
- **Collins v. City of Harker Heights, February 26, 1992:** Its bounded workplace-omission holdings do not automatically resolve affirmative police conduct or merge the underlying right with municipal attribution.
- **Pinder v. Johnson, May 21, 1996:** Its distinction between nonprotection and affirmative deprivation remains; it did not decide this culpability question.
- **Uhlrig v. Harder, February 18, 1997:** Its conduct-time immunity disposition is preserved without importing the lower court's unadopted merits test or a mandatory merits-first sequence.
- **United States v. Lanier, March 31, 1997:** Its rejection of categorical immunity for noncustodial official wrongdoing remains. A governing specific constitutional provision must be used where it covers a claim; that principle does not itself dispose of every due-process claim.
- **Albright v. Oliver, January 24, 1994:** Its fractured judgment supplies no controlling general displacement rationale for this case.
- **Washington v. Glucksberg, June 26, 1997:** Its assistance-in-suicide holding remains limited; no exclusive most-specific-history methodology governing all liberties is established or displaced here.

#### Law After Decision

The decision establishes a purpose-to-harm requirement for the supported rapid-pursuit setting and rejects the circuit's use of reckless disregard or deliberate indifference alone there. Existing deliberate-indifference rules retain their distinct duties, opportunities for response and proof requirements. Constitutional wrongdoing remains separate from qualified immunity, municipal attribution and state-law liability. A purposeful or materially more deliberative encounter is not resolved merely by calling it a pursuit.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stevens concurs only in the judgment. He would resolve the individual claim on qualified immunity without deciding the difficult constitutional standard. All relevant law existing on May 22, 1990 must be considered under the particularized-notice approach. Garner and Brower concern intentional force or intentionally applied stopping means; Checki concerns intentional misuse of police power to terrorize; Wood II concerns affirmative interference with protective circumstances. The reported pursuit decisions did not establish this claim's constitutional boundary merely by recognizing a possible egregious case, and Daniels reserved intermediate culpability. Neither the absence of an identical case nor a tally of judicial disagreement decides immunity. On the complete favorable account, the existing law did not make this pursuit's federal unlawfulness sufficiently apparent. Later decisions cannot supply retrospective notice. He makes no finding that the conduct was constitutional.

Scalia, joined by Thomas, concurs in the judgment on a different constitutional ground. The carefully described asserted right against this reckless pursuit and unintended collision lacks sufficient textual, historical or precedential support. A general protection of life or bodily security does not by itself define the constitutional tort. They reject the Court's reliance on an open-ended conscience standard and do not join its affirmative purpose-to-harm definition, although they agree that these facts establish no constitutional violation. Glucksberg does not impose a universally exclusive history method; their objection rests on the absence of adequate support for this particular asserted right and on the limits of judicially defined constitutional tort liability. Their position does not categorically exclude direct intentional assaults outside custody.

Breyer fully joins the Court. He separately preserves flexibility to decide a suitable case on qualified immunity before resolving a difficult or poorly presented constitutional question. That approach is consistent with Uhlrig and the distinction between actual wrongdoing and conduct-time notice. Today's choice to resolve the pursuit standard is not a compulsory merits-first sequence for every later case.

#### Source Notes

[Lewis v. Sacramento County, 98 F.3d 434](https://static.case.law/f3d/98/html/0434-01.html) supplies the final-judgment posture, favorable pursuit facts and the undisputed absence of an intended collision. Alleged departmental violations remain distinct from federal constitutional culpability. The lower account does not establish that the riders were dangerous felons or that Smith had a purpose to injure. The immunity notice date is May 22, 1990.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Ninth Circuit must give effect to reversal of its ruling against Smith and termination of the reviewed federal individual claim. The district court's favorable individual judgment remains in effect. The municipal judgments and unappealed state-law dispositions are not reopened, and no additional evidentiary proceeding or relief is ordered.

**End of entry: County of Sacramento v. Lewis, Plenary merits decision, May 26, 1998.**

### Kiowa Tribe of Oklahoma v. Manufacturing Technologies, Inc., No. 96-1037

Plenary merits decision, May 26, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 12, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the Oklahoma Court of Civil Appeals, First Division, after the Oklahoma Supreme Court declined review. The intermediate court affirmed a contract money judgment against the Tribe under an off-reservation commercial-activity exception to immunity. The question is whether federal tribal immunity permits this direct contract suit without a clear waiver or congressional authorization.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded with instructions to dismiss, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Stevens, Thomas and Ginsburg.

The direct contract money claim against the Tribe must be dismissed. The three opposing Justices would remand under a limited off-territory commercial exception, with its factual predicate determined from the actual record; they do not unconditionally sustain a money judgment on an assumed location finding.

#### Opinion Topology

Breyer delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter. Stevens dissents, joined in full by Thomas and Ginsburg. All six members of the Court's opinion adopt the complete contract-immunity rule, its application, qualifications and dismissal mandate.

#### Holdings

**Holding and operative rule:** A federally recognized Indian tribe retains sovereign immunity from a direct suit on its contract, whether the activity is governmental or commercial and whether the contract concerns activity on or off reservation land, unless the tribe clearly waives immunity or Congress unequivocally authorizes the suit. No such waiver or authorization supports this action, so the direct contract claim against the Kiowa Tribe must be dismissed without deciding the debt's merits or resolving the disputed place of execution.

**Authority:** Breyer's opinion for the six Justices identified in Opinion Topology controls the entire rule and its application.

**Controlling explanation:** Tribal immunity and the substantive obligations a tribe may owe are different questions. Potawatomi preserves that distinction even when the underlying activity is commercial and the State has a valid substantive interest. Santa Clara Pueblo requires an unequivocal waiver; Puyallup distinguishes relief against the tribal sovereign from claims against members. Those cases do not already decide every off-reservation contract, but their federal immunity framework provides no adequate basis for the geographical and commercial exception adopted below.

The proposed exception has substantial practical appeal. Modern tribal commerce can affect nonmembers far from tribal land, and immunity can leave a creditor without the ordinary means of enforcing a debt. The doctrine's early foundations and present policy costs merit attention. But determining which commercial activities, locations, counterparties and reliance interests warrant an exception requires choices intertwined with Congress's treatment of tribal sovereign status. Congress can revise the rule and specify its boundaries. The Court declines to create this new exception through a direct contract action.

Mescalero and Chickasaw Nation address the substantive operation of state law; they do not establish that a court may enter a money judgment against an immune tribe. Here the note preserves sovereign rights, and neither signing it nor defaulting clearly consents to suit. Whether execution occurred in Carnegie or Oklahoma City therefore does not affect the immunity result. The decision does not extinguish contractual obligations, automatically protect individual agents, decide a tort claim, determine an asset's immunity from execution or establish that some adequate alternative remedy exists. A clear waiver or valid congressional authorization would present a different case.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Citizen Band Potawatomi Indian Tribe, 498 U.S. 505:** Applied for immunity distinct from substantive obligation and its rejection of a commercial-business limitation in the trust-land setting; the present holding extends the direct contract-immunity rule to the off-reservation commercial setting.
- **Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez, 436 U.S. 49:** Applied to the requirement of an unequivocal waiver and Congress's authority over the immunity rule.
- **Puyallup Tribe, Inc. v. Department of Game of Washington, 433 U.S. 165:** Applied to the distinction between the tribal sovereign and individual members; jurisdiction over others supplies no automatic authority to bind the Tribe.
- **Mescalero Apache Tribe v. Jones, 411 U.S. 145:** Distinguished as a rule concerning substantive state-law application outside reservation borders, not a direct contract-suit immunity exception.
- **Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Chickasaw Nation, June 14, 1995:** Its territorial and tax-incidence holdings remain intact and create no automatic contract remedy against the Tribe.
- **Alaska v. Native Village of Venetie Tribal Government, February 25, 1998:** Its separation of tribal recognition from Indian-country land status is preserved; loss of a territorial classification does not itself eliminate sovereign status or decide suit immunity.
- **Montana v. Crow Tribe of Indians, May 18, 1998:** Its requirement of an independently grounded restitutionary remedy is preserved; it establishes neither automatic tribal recovery nor an automatic private contract action.

#### Law After Decision

Direct contract immunity now expressly covers the off-reservation commercial transaction presented. It remains subject to clear tribal waiver and unequivocal congressional authorization. Substantive contract obligations and ordinary state-law applicability are separate from the power to adjudicate this direct money claim. Claims against nonimmune parties, collateral security and property enforcement depend on their own actual legal bases; the Court recognizes no particular such remedy here and decides no general tort-immunity question.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stevens, joined by Thomas and Ginsburg, would decline to extend tribal immunity to genuinely off-reservation commercial conduct unrelated to tribal land or sovereign functions. Potawatomi resolved a trust-land operation, and Puyallup protected the tribal defendant in its own setting; neither requires the broader extension. Mescalero's ordinary-state-law baseline informs, but does not itself decide, the distinct amenability question. The dissent regards the extension as new federal common-law displacement of state adjudicatory authority without an identified federal interest sufficient to justify it. Congress's power to legislate does not compel courts to enlarge the received doctrine meanwhile, and unusually broad immunity for outside commerce burdens parties dealing beyond tribal territory.

The dissent preserves established immunity within its received scope and infers no waiver from commerce or a promise to pay. It would remand for application of its limited rule to the actual transaction and valid existing findings. The note's Carnegie recital and the creditor's Oklahoma City account remain distinct; if existing findings settle the legally material location and commercial predicates, no new factfinding is needed. Only a genuinely unresolved material predicate would require further ordinary proceedings. The dissent does not convert the location dispute into a finding against the Tribe or direct an unconditional money judgment.

#### Source Notes

The reported transaction and procedural history appear in [the official United States Reports, 523 U.S. 751](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-523/pdf/USREPORTS-523-751.pdf). The note recites Carnegie, while the creditor asserts Oklahoma City execution, delivery and payment. Neither account becomes a new factual finding, and the controlling immunity rule makes that dispute immaterial to dismissal. No separate defendant, actual alternative forum or available secured remedy is established here.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The state courts must dismiss the presented direct contract action against the Tribe on immunity grounds. They need not resolve the competing execution-location accounts to implement that direction. Breach, damages, another defendant's liability, particular security or execution measures, and any actually authorized waiver-based or statutory action are not adjudicated by this mandate.

**End of entry: Kiowa Tribe of Oklahoma v. Manufacturing Technologies, Inc., Plenary merits decision, May 26, 1998.**

### New Jersey v. New York, No. 120, Original

Original-jurisdiction merits decision on exceptions, May 26, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 12, 1998. The Court considers both States' exceptions to the Special Master's March 31, 1997 Final Report and May 30, 1997 Supplement after trial and full briefing. The questions concern the 1834 Compact's treatment of later artificial fill at Ellis Island, prescription and acquiescence, laches, the original island's tide line, historical filled-pier land and a proposed practical adjustment of the boundary. New Jersey claims the later fill; New York claims the enlarged island. There is no lower appellate judgment.

#### Judgment

| Exception or judgment component | Disposition and actual votes | Supporting Justices | Opposing or other Justice positions | Remedy or consequence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Territorial entitlement | New Jersey retains the later artificial fill; New York retains the original island, 6–3 | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Stevens, Scalia and Thomas would recognize New York over the enlarged island on their separate grounds | Recommit for a proposed conforming decree; no final geographic decree is entered. |
| New York: Compact interpretation | Overruled, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Scalia and Thomas would sustain | The Compact itself preserves New Jersey's original entitlement to later artificial fill. |
| New York: prescription and acquiescence | Overruled, 6–1; two do not reach the ground | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Stevens would sustain; Scalia and Thomas do not need prescription under their Compact construction | New York has not proved later acquisition of the fill. |
| New York: laches | Overruled by six; three do not need this determination | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Stevens, Scalia and Thomas reach whole-island New York entitlement on other grounds | No independent laches bar defeats New Jersey's entitlement. Stone-Zsela agrees in this disposition only. |
| New Jersey: mean-high-water boundary | Overruled by six; three do not need a divided-island boundary | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Stevens, Scalia and Thomas favor whole-island New York entitlement | The original island is located to its 1834 mean low-water line. #### Holdings

##### 1. The Compact leaves the later artificial fill in New Jersey

**Holding and operative rule:** The 1834 Compact preserves New York's entitlement to the original Ellis Island but does not transfer New Jersey's surrounding sovereign territory whenever artificial fill enlarges that island. The later artificial additions therefore remain in New Jersey unless a legally sufficient subsequent change of entitlement is established.

**Authority:** Part I of Souter's opinion, joined by all five full joiners identified in Opinion Topology, Stone-Zsela and Stevens: seven direct votes.

**Controlling explanation:** Article First sets the midwater sovereignty line, west of which Ellis Island lies; Article Second reserves the original island; Article Third gives New York distinct authority serving commerce and navigation. Central Railroad distinguishes that authority from sovereignty. Reading the agreement as a whole against ordinary artificial-change principles preserves the original island without allowing federal construction to transfer another State's territory.

The geographic conclusion does not rest on the word present alone as though it were a metes-and-bounds description. Foreseeable reclamation, earlier filling practices and omission of an acreage limit do not establish an agreement that every later addition would become New York territory. Nor does continuing to call the enlarged physical feature Ellis Island decide sovereign entitlement. Georgia distinguishes artificial or avulsive change from natural accretion, but its different reservation and naturally formed-island questions do not settle this Compact. Here the substantial additions are artificial.

The Compact supplies no relevant displacement of the ordinary boundary consequence. The Court does not establish a rule for every federal dredging project or every subsequently connected feature. The United States' express jurisdictional reservation remains, and sovereignty does not itself decide federal or private ownership.

##### 2. New York has not established prescription and acquiescence

**Holding and operative rule:** A State claiming territory by prescription must prove by a preponderance of the evidence long, continuous possession and assertion of sovereignty over the actual disputed land, together with the other State's acquiescence. That acquiescence requires knowledge of the adverse claim or adverse acts sufficiently open, notorious, visible and uninterrupted to support the inference; New York's cumulative proof does not establish acquisition of this fill.

**Authority:** Part II, joined by the five full joiners and Stone-Zsela: six direct votes. Stevens disagrees with the evidentiary conclusion; Scalia and Thomas do not reach prescription as a necessary ground.

**Controlling explanation:** Illinois requires proof directed to the disputed territory, and Georgia makes notice and acquiescence depend on the actual sovereign conduct. The record includes specifically identified electoral registrations of fill residents and hospital records on the fill, as well as census treatment, labor standards, compensation practice and limited civil services. Those records are not dismissed as merely generic island references. But their intermittent character and the mixed governmental understandings do not establish the necessary continuous adverse assertion and notice.

New York's conceded authority over the original island makes generic references equivocal. Federal administration limited both ordinary state activity and the notice or protest reasonably expected of New Jersey. The 1904 deed is qualified, and the 1933 permit must be assessed by its actual object. Federal or local action is not automatically state action. The 1935 federal return to New York's position and the 1949 wage reversal are considered; they prevent treating earlier contrary federal acts as permanent New Jersey control, but do not eliminate the underlying uncertainty.

New York argues that modest surviving records reflect the only sovereign acts possible on a federal installation and that contrary episodes concerned property or were abandoned. Their cumulative force remains insufficient under the ordinary burden, without demanding every municipal service or a formal state protest. A late protest could not undo title already acquired; the necessary earlier acquisition has not been proved. The Court adopts no fixed prescriptive period and leaves open whether federal occupation could categorically prevent prescription in another case. The Court draws no unsupported inference for either side from missing papers or unavailable witnesses.

##### 3. Laches does not excuse proof of affirmative territorial acquisition

**Holding and operative rule:** New York cannot use laches based on evidentiary losses affecting its own prescriptive claim to replace proof of that affirmative acquisition. Although prescription is pleaded as a defense, New York bears the claimant's burden on that entitlement; the asserted losses do not establish a laches defense to New Jersey's Compact-based boundary.

**Authority:** Part III, joined by the five full joiners: five direct rationale votes. Stone-Zsela agrees only in overruling the exception.

**Controlling explanation:** New York points to deteriorated and lost records, expired retention periods and deceased witnesses, contending that New Jersey delayed suit until acquisition became harder to prove. Even assuming those circumstances impaired its prescriptive proof, the asserted defense cannot relieve New York of the affirmative burden to establish that it acquired the territory. It identifies no lost proof defeating New Jersey's underlying entitlement under the Compact and the artificial-change rule.

Illinois's boundary-repose analysis supplies context, while Kansas leaves general laches availability in compact litigation open. Neither requires a finding here that New York suffered no evidentiary prejudice. The Court rejects the proposed legal use of that loss rather than deciding whether every asserted loss occurred or whether another interstate compact controversy could support laches. The ruling does not infer the contents of missing documents, decide an independent lack-of-diligence issue or turn dismissal on laches into a new sovereignty award.

##### 4. The original island extends to its mean low-water line in 1834

**Holding and operative rule:** Article Second's retained original island is bounded by its mean low-water line as it stood in 1834. New Jersey's proposed mean-high-water boundary is rejected; ordinary property conveyances and approximate deed acreage do not independently fix the interstate sovereign line.

**Authority:** Part IV, joined by the five full joiners: five direct rationale votes. Stone-Zsela agrees only in overruling the high-water exception; conditional views of the whole-island dissenters are not counted.

**Controlling explanation:** Handly's Lessee and Vermont supply the general sovereign-boundary rule against which the island reservation is read. Their river settings differ from this tidal island, but the territorial boundary principle explains the reservation's reach without treating ordinary private ownership as sovereignty. The Court does not enact language omitted from the unsuccessful 1827 proposal.

Article Third's express low-water limit along the New Jersey shore answers a distinct question: how far New York's authority over waters and submerged lands could move inland with the rising tide. That problem differs from identifying the edge of an island already reserved to New York. Express wording for the former does not require high water for the latter. The 1808 conveyance's high-water limit and approximate acreage concern the property conveyed, not an agreed interstate decree.

Louisiana established continuity even under a proposed high-water test and expressly declined a universal island definition; it therefore does not select high water here. The interpretive rule does not require proof that modern access has been obstructed. The resulting historical line must still be located accurately for the decree.

##### 5. The historical filled-pier land belongs within the retained original island

**Holding and operative rule:** On independent evaluation of the circumstantial evidence, the historical pier rested on filled land before 1834 and that land belongs within New York's retained original island. New Jersey's exception is overruled, but the implementing description must include the accepted historical land only once and reconcile its acreage; no exact additional 0.2-acre award or final numerical total is entered.

**Authority:** Part V, joined by the five full joiners: five direct rationale votes. Stone-Zsela agrees only in the exception's disposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The identified 1819 map appears to show filled material around the pier location. The credited evidence about piling practice and the relative ease of shallow-water filling supports the likely inference that the pier stood on fill. The alternative possibility of a pile-supported structure, its changing shape, objections to the witness's engineering qualifications and uncertain ammunition-load assumptions do not defeat that narrower map-and-construction inference. A structure projecting over water is not automatically land.

Louisiana requires independent original-action review and appropriate evidentiary weight, not appellate deference under Rule 52. The Court applies that distinction and does not presume that every detail of the Master's description is correct. His reference to an 1839 chart is not treated as a new exhibit; the identified map supporting the inference is from 1819.

The Master explains his 0.2-acre allowance as land omitted from the 4.69-acre base; New Jersey disputes duplication. Acceptance of the filled-pier finding does not independently measure or resolve that numerical disagreement. The proposed decree must locate the historical land once, explain the pier's treatment and reconcile the description with its acreage. Existing supported work may supply that technical implementation without reopening the pier finding or requiring an unnecessary new trial.

##### 6. Implementation must locate the Compact boundary, not exchange territory

**Holding and operative rule:** The Court may not replace the established Compact boundary with different parcels merely to simplify administration or preserve an approximate acreage. New Jersey's exception to the practical reconfiguration is sustained, and the matter is recommitted for preparation of an authenticated, internally consistent proposed decree implementing the 1834 mean-low-water boundary and accepted historical filled-pier land; this Court must separately consider and enter the decree.

**Authority:** Part VI, joined by the five full joiners and Stone-Zsela: six direct votes for this implementation and recommittal. The dissenters' rejection of judicial exchange does not make them supporters of the divided-island mandate.

**Controlling explanation:** Cuyler establishes the Compact's federal-law force. Texas v. New Mexico and Kansas require enforcement of its bargain rather than a judicially preferred substitute. Georgia's treatment of an undefined maritime line does not authorize transferring territory already allocated by this Compact. Louisiana permits the technical work necessary to locate an adjudicated line and requires reliable geographical implementation.

The Master's modified 5.1-acre Survey A moves the line to accommodate buildings, margins, corridor turns, ferry access and staircase treatment. Those are territorial reallocations, not simply identification of the historical tide line. Survey B's 4.89 acres is not lawful merely because it matches an approximate area. The parties' survey assistance, lack of objection to execution of a survey or preferences among proposed options do not establish consent to exchange sovereignty.

Administrative convenience and uncertain old measurements justify careful location, not substitute parcels. Existing supported survey work may be used, and neither a new full survey nor a new merits trial is required merely by recommittal. The Court fixes no exact coordinates, final acreage or decree date and enters no presently operative injunction. Federal jurisdiction expressly reserved by Congress, ownership and unpresented municipal, regulatory and tax consequences remain distinct.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Central Railroad Co. of New Jersey v. Jersey City, 209 U.S. 473:** Applied to distinguish Article First sovereignty from Article Third commerce/navigation jurisdiction; it is not a prior judgment binding these States on the precise island boundary.
- **Georgia v. South Carolina, 497 U.S. 376:** Applied at its prescription, notice and artificial-change principles; its different island reservation, natural formations and undefined maritime line do not decide this Compact or authorize a territorial exchange.
- **Illinois v. Kentucky, 500 U.S. 380:** Applied for the preponderance burden, adverse control over the actual disputed land and acquiescence. Its boundary-repose discussion is not enlarged into an absolute laches rule for every compact case.
- **Handly's Lessee v. Anthony, 18 U.S. 374:** Applied for the sovereign low-water boundary principle, without confusing it with ordinary title to tidelands.
- **Vermont v. New Hampshire, 289 U.S. 593:** Applied to the low-water construction of the territorial reservation; the different river setting does not establish a high-water rule here.
- **Cuyler v. Adams, 449 U.S. 433:** Applied to the congressionally approved Compact's status as federal law.
- **Texas v. New Mexico, 462 U.S. 554:** Applied to forbid relief inconsistent with the actual compact bargain.
- **Kansas v. Colorado, May 15, 1995:** Applied to faithful compact enforcement, independent original review and the distinction between entitlement and remedial flexibility; its reservation of general laches availability remains.
- **Louisiana v. Mississippi, October 31 and December 4, 1995:** Applied to independent evidentiary review and an authenticated, internally consistent implementing decree. Its continuity holding supplies no universal island definition or automatic high-water boundary.
- **Mississippi v. Louisiana, December 14, 1992:** Preserved at the distinction between this Court's exclusive interstate adjudication and otherwise lawful private-title litigation.
- **Hess v. Port Authority Trans-Hudson Corp., November 14, 1994:** Its actual-compact, entity-specific immunity inquiry remains distinct from territorial sovereignty and supplies no power to rewrite this boundary.

#### Law After Decision

New Jersey retains sovereignty over the later artificial additions; New York retains the original island to its 1834 mean low-water boundary, including the accepted historical filled-pier land. New York has not proved a later prescriptive acquisition, and its asserted laches theory supplies no substitute for that proof. The Court adds the Compact-specific artificial-fill allocation and shore/pier determinations while preserving ordinary prescriptive burdens, independent original review and the prohibition on judicially replacing a compact bargain. The exact geographic decree remains to be prepared and entered. Sovereignty does not adjudicate federal or private ownership, the precise extent of federal enclave jurisdiction, every aspect of New York's retained jurisdiction, or unpresented municipal, regulatory and tax issues.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Breyer, joined by Ginsburg, fully joins the Court. They find no relevant ambiguity in Articles First and Second that displaces the background artificial-change rule, without declaring every Compact clause unambiguous or denying all contextual interpretation of silence. Federal control also matters to acquiescence. Modest civil acts may be all that a State can perform on a federally administered installation, but that does not make them adequate notice of an adverse claim or require the neighboring State to mount a major protest against each one. The contrary inference would make loss of sovereignty too easy. Conflicting federal understandings, including the Wyzanski statement and later reversals, are evidence to evaluate, not correct legal holdings that the island lies outside every State.

Stevens agrees with Part I's original Compact allocation but would find New York acquired the enlarged island by prescription. The identified fill-resident electoral and hospital records, civil administration, census treatment and public affiliation cumulatively show a continuing sovereign understanding appropriate to a federal installation. Sparse surviving examples need not negate consistent practice. He infers notice from the public character and significance of those acts and treats contrary episodes as isolated, property-related, attributable to different actors or abandoned. He does not equate every registration with an actual vote, locate every marriage on the fill or treat every federal wage choice as compulsory state law. A late protest cannot undo an acquisition already complete. The difficulties of dividing buildings reinforce his evidentiary criticism but supply no independent power to exchange territory. His sufficient prescription ground would eliminate the need for a divided-island decree.

Scalia, joined by Thomas, would recognize New York's whole-island entitlement through practical construction of the Compact, without deciding that prescription has been proved. Articles First through Third form an integrated settlement; the retained island's scope is sufficiently ambiguous for the parties' administration to inform its meaning. Article Third helps interpret the agreement even though New York advances no preserved independent claim of title under that article. Air France v. Saks, Choctaw Nation and Vermont support consulting practical construction where the agreement is ambiguous. The long treatment of the enlarged island as one New York jurisdiction, without comparable New Jersey island administration, supports their construction. Interpreting an ambiguous reservation differs from dispossessing an otherwise undoubted owner by prescription. They do not read Article Second as a transfer clause for every conceivable future fill and do not authorize exchanging a boundary once its meaning is settled. Their ground makes prescription, laches and divided-island implementation unnecessary to their preferred judgment.

### Waste Management, Inc. of Tennessee v. Metropolitan Government of Nashville and Davidson County; Sixth Circuit Nos. 95-5863 and 95-5900, 130 F.3d 731

Merits decision on submission, May 27, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

On full merits submission. Review under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) concerns the Sixth Circuit's November 5, 1997 Commerce Clause determinations against compulsory routing to Metro's waste-to-energy facility and the separate fee on disposal outside its system. The court of appeals exercised §1292(a)(1) jurisdiction over the injunction rulings, reversing denial of the routing injunction and affirming the fee injunction. Its separate passenger-car/pickup takings and ripeness remand is outside this review.

Metro owns the facility; Nashville Thermal Transfer Corporation operates it. Residential routing permits the Director's specific written authorization for another destination. Nonresidential routing is limited to allocations needed for the reported 6,300-ton weekly requirement, based on monthly reporting, at-least-quarterly pro rata calculations using the best available data, and advance facility and collector notices. The separate charge is $2 per cubic yard or $8 per ton on the specified waste generated or collected within Metro and disposed at non-Metro facilities inside or outside Metro; NTTC and other Metro-owned sites are exempt.

#### Judgment

The two reviewed Commerce Clause determinations are vacated, and the case is remanded for separate application of the Court's public-facility routing rule and diversion-cost requirement. Neither enacted measure is held valid or invalid. The takings and ripeness rulings outside the grant remain undisturbed.

#### Holdings

**Common authority:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, controls each of the following four holdings by five direct votes.

##### 1. Genuine public routing with equal treatment of private processors is not facial discrimination

**Holding and operative rule:** Routing locally generated waste to a genuinely public facility does not facially discriminate against interstate commerce when local private processors and out-of-state processors are treated alike. Whether the facility is genuinely public turns on its actual ownership, control, financing, public-service responsibility and revenues, and the absence of concealed private favoritism; public title alone does not settle the question.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court distinguishes public provision of a waste-disposal service from a command reserving private business for a favored local enterprise. Carbone condemns the latter and leaves the genuinely public category open; excluding local private competitors does not cure favoritism toward a selected private processor. That reasoning does not compel the same classification when the service is actually public and all private processors are treated alike. Tracy requires attention to the actual service and its obligations, rather than the physical process alone. Camps likewise preserves genuinely different public systems while refusing to let a public-benefit label justify private commercial discrimination.

The inquiry therefore concerns who actually owns and controls the facility, how its financing and financial risks are allocated, who bears responsibility for providing the public service, where its revenues go, and whether the arrangement conceals private favoritism. These considerations implement the public-service distinction; a deed is not conclusive, and employing a private operator does not itself establish either genuine public operation or a private preference. Metro owns the plant and NTTC operates it, but the supplied record does not settle the operator's economics, risk allocation or every contractual obligation. The Court supplies none of those facts. The lower court erred by treating public ownership as categorically immaterial under Carbone; on remand it must apply the genuine-public inquiry rather than presume either its satisfaction or its failure. This holding addresses facial discrimination, not blanket immunity from every Commerce Clause claim.

##### 2. A diversion fee must correspond to diversion-related costs, independently of the routing rule

**Holding and operative rule:** A diversion fee survives only insofar as its actual incidence and amount are genuinely tied to diversion-related costs rather than interstate origin or protection of local commerce. A permissible public routing distinction does not establish that connection, and a compensatory-tax defense separately requires an identified actual intrastate burden, rough approximation without excess, and substantially equivalent taxable events reasonably treated as mutually exclusive proxies.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court examines the fee itself, not merely the facility it finances. A charge that applies without regard to interstate origin and genuinely corresponds to diversion-related costs is distinct from a surcharge protecting local commerce. Equal nominal treatment of private destinations cannot conceal a different actual incidence, and a revenue shortfall does not by itself prove costs caused by diversion. The Court adopts no accounting formula, assumed cost amount or finding that this charge satisfies the required relationship.

Oregon Waste and Fulton supply the cumulative compensation requirements; neither a financing need nor aggregate public expense replaces an identified matching intrastate burden. Metro's existing compensation defense fails because no corresponding burden is identified. That failure does not establish that Metro incurs no costs, nor does it decide whether this enacted fee fits the separate diversion-cost rule. The lower courts must test the $2-per-cubic-yard or $8-per-ton charge on specified Metro-generated or collected waste sent to non-Metro facilities, including destinations inside Metro, while accounting for the NTTC and Metro-owned-site exemptions. The inquiry must fit that incidence and the actual record; the Court does not rewrite the enactment into a different fee. An origin-based or protectionist charge remains subject to the governing discrimination rule, and public ownership supplies no excuse for it.

##### 3. Public ownership is distinct from government purchase of private transactions

**Holding and operative rule:** Ownership of a disposal facility does not establish that the government purchases independent generators' and haulers' transactions compelled by a routing requirement or burdened by a diversion fee. SSC's government-purchase protection therefore cannot rest on public title alone, although the genuine public character of a facility remains independently material to discrimination analysis.

**Controlling explanation:** SSC protects collection and disposal that the government actually purchases; it separately applies Carbone to regulation reserving independent private business. Metro's ownership does not show a municipal purchase of every private transaction its rules reach. Neither an operator's performance nor the government's investment supplies the missing purchase. The public-facility rule concerns the character of the favored service and equal treatment of private alternatives, not a fictional purchase of all their transactions. Actual government procurement retains its existing protection, and no unproved procurement contract is supplied here.

##### 4. Both claims return for application of the rule and the appropriate remaining scrutiny

**Holding and operative rule:** The lower courts must separately reconsider routing and the diversion fee under the public-facility and diversion-cost rules. A genuinely evenhanded measure remains subject to Pike's inquiry whether its supported interstate burden is clearly excessive in relation to local benefits; where discrimination is established, the government must show a legitimate local purpose that reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives cannot adequately serve, rather than rely on Pike balancing.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court's classification rule resolves the governing legal question without deciding the missing factual predicates or either measure's ultimate validity. The routing requirement includes the Director's specific written-authorization exception for residential waste and the reported nonresidential allocations needed for the 6,300-ton weekly requirement, with monthly reporting, at-least-quarterly pro rata calculations from the best available data, and advance notices. The fee has its own incidence and cost inquiry. Neither may be treated as an unconditional all-waste command or an automatically valid public financing measure.

Pike supplies the rule for an evenhanded burden; Oregon Waste and Camps require the distinct legitimate-purpose and adequate-alternatives inquiry for discrimination. Reliable disposal, fuel supply and the State's 25-percent reduction requirement are supported objectives. The reported alternatives—competitive charges, increased municipal collection or contracts, and a generator/container tax—are not assumed costless, immediate or equally profitable, and the law does not demand disproof of every imaginable substitute. Vacatur permits proper application of these rules without requiring an automatic evidentiary hearing, deciding a monetary remedy or prescribing a replacement program. The separate passenger-car/pickup takings and ripeness rulings were outside the grant and remain untouched.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Carbone: preserves the rule against compulsory patronage of a favored local private processor and resolves its reserved genuine-public category through the public-facility rule. Equal exclusion of local private competitors still does not cure a private preference.
- SSC Corp.: preserves protection for performance actually purchased by government; municipal ownership, financing and operator contracts do not establish purchase of independent transactions. Its private-service rule remains distinct from the genuine-public classification adopted here.
- Tracy: applies its comparison of actual offerings and supported obligations to the genuine-public inquiry; institutional title alone and physical similarity alone are insufficient. Its record-specific holding remains, without general utility immunity.
- Camps: preserves scrutiny of discriminatory private-service restrictions dressed as public benefits and gives effect to its reservation of genuinely different public systems. The actual public arrangement, not the challenged preference's label, must establish the distinction.
- Oregon Waste: preserves the rule for established discrimination and the cumulative compensation requirements; a diversion-cost charge is not presumed valid merely because a public system receives its proceeds.
- Fulton: applies the identified-burden, rough nonexcessive equivalence and substantially equivalent taxable-event requirements; the existing compensation defense fails without the identified burden, independently of possible lawful diversion-cost treatment.
- Pike: preserves review of genuinely evenhanded measures for interstate burdens clearly excessive in relation to local benefits; it does not replace the rule governing established discrimination.

No precedent is overruled. The genuine-public question reserved in the earlier waste-service decisions is resolved at the stated scope.

#### Law After Decision

Routing locally generated waste to a genuinely public facility is not facial discrimination when local private and out-of-state processors receive equal treatment. Actual ownership, control, financing, public-service responsibility, revenues and the absence of concealed private favoritism determine genuine public status; title alone is insufficient. Diversion fees require an independent, genuine relationship between their incidence and amount and diversion-related costs, without interstate-origin discrimination or protectionism. The decision supplies neither blanket public-system immunity nor a finding that Metro's measures meet these conditions.

Public ownership does not establish a government purchase, and the existing compensation defense fails for want of an identified corresponding intrastate burden. The appropriate evenhanded-burden or discrimination rules remain available according to the actual classification. Both reviewed determinations are vacated for separate application of this law. The excluded takings and ripeness rulings remain undisturbed. The Court's five-Justice rule controls directly; the concurrence and dissents do not qualify its authority.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Stone-Zsela, concurring.** The Chief Justice joins the full remand judgment and the Court's entire rule. He separately states that he would reverse outright on the view that routing to an actual public facility does not facially discriminate merely by excluding local and out-of-state private alternatives alike, and that a diversion charge is permissible when its incidence is nondiscriminatory and it genuinely corresponds to diversion-related costs. Public title cannot conceal private favoritism, and a protectionist or origin-based fee remains impermissible. His preference adds no finding about NTTC's economics, actual control, cost correspondence or the permissible amount of this fee. It does not change the Court's remand or its refusal to declare either enacted measure valid or invalid. He expresses no view on the excluded takings questions.

**Kennedy, joined by Stevens, dissenting.** The dissent would affirm both determinations. Actual public ownership does not convert generators' and haulers' independent purchases into government purchases. Carbone protects competition in those compelled transactions; SSC's protection extends to performance the government actually buys. The dissent confronts the reserved public-system category but finds no supported difference in this offering sufficient to remove the compulsory transactions from discrimination scrutiny. Tracy and Camps do not make public title or stated fuel and environmental purposes decisive.

Once discrimination is found, the reported alternatives defeat the claimed necessity of these mechanisms under the legitimate-purpose and adequate-nondiscriminatory-alternatives rule. The dissent does not assume that every substitute is free, immediate or equally profitable. The fee separately lacks the identified equivalent intrastate burden required by Oregon Waste and Fulton. Affirmance would preserve lawful licensing, actual public purchases and unreviewed claims; it would prescribe no replacement tax, collection program or monetary relief.

**Thomas, joined by Scalia, dissenting.** The dissent would reverse and terminate both reviewed Commerce Clause grounds. As in their Camps constitutional position, the affirmative grant of congressional commerce power does not, in their view, authorize courts to impose this negative restriction in the absence of governing congressional legislation. They question the inference of exclusivity from that grant, the conversion of congressional silence into a prohibition, and judicial policy balancing. They expressly seek displacement of contrary precedent; they do not contend it has already been overruled.

Their comparative observations under existing precedent do not supply assent to renewed service classification, discrimination justification or Pike balancing. Ordinary mandate proceedings on independently preserved matters may continue, but these Commerce claims would end. Ginsburg's narrower comparison position does not join this constitutional repudiation. Thomas's tentative Import-Export alternative in Camps supplies no disposition of these routing and per-ton measures: no preserved presentation, established import/export status or definitive applicable rule is supplied here.

#### Source Notes

The complete [Sixth Circuit opinion, 130 F.3d 731](https://static.case.law/f3d/130/html/0731-01.html), including its footnotes, supplies the stipulated operational facts, enactment qualifications, injunction posture and reported alternatives. The appellate narrative's $36 tipping fee is distinct from the reproduced $44 enacted rate effective January 1, 1995; neither establishes a quantified interstate burden. The absence of an identified compensating intrastate burden does not establish that Metro incurs no public-service costs. #### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Any injunction modification must implement that mandate; no interim enforcement status or permission pending mandate is determined here. The passenger-car/pickup takings and ripeness remand remains undisturbed. No additional Supreme Court event or return date is set.

### DeCarlo v. Fry; Second Circuit Nos. 97-7702, 97-7704 and 97-7754, 141 F.3d 56

Plenary merits decision on submission, May 28, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

On review of the Second Circuit's April 6, 1998 decision after trial, summary judgments and cross-appeals. The questions concern Astle and Blank's personal qualified immunity and the distinct official- and personal-capacity proceedings involving DuRose. This is not an interlocutory appeal from denial of immunity. Turner's favorable personal jury verdict and the unappealed Perales official declaration are outside review.

#### Judgment

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|
| Astle and Blank personally | Affirm the immunity disposition, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | Their qualified-immunity summary judgments remain. |
| DuRose officially | Affirm the appellate vacatur and municipal remand, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | The district's $300,000 entity judgment and dependent $70,324.47 fee/cost award cannot stand on the mistaken premise; the actual trial record must receive proper Rule 50 and municipal-liability analysis. |
| DuRose personally, post-expungement | Affirm the clarification and adjudication remand, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | Identify and explain any actual disposition, or decide the pending request under its proper procedural standard. |
| Pre-expungement amendment permission | Preserve existing leave to amend, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | The affected claims against DuRose, Hendricks and Kraus retain that opportunity; no amended claim's merits are established. |

The Supreme Court affirms the appellate decision; it does not reinstate the vacated district judgment. No recertification, liability finding, new monetary amount, automatic trial or specified discovery follows.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Breyer throughout | Supports every reviewed disposition | I: personal immunity; II: official-capacity judgment and Rule 50 remand; III: later personal claim; IV: existing amendment permission |
| Same opinion, partial join | Ginsburg | Stone-Zsela | Supports every reviewed disposition | Part I only insofar as superior approval supplies no automatic defense; Part II's entity-capacity, separate-deprivation/policy/causation and no-personal-immunity propositions; disposition only on the additional Rule 50 ground, Part III and Part IV |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Supports the immunity result and the bounded remands | Proposed contextual reasonable-mistake defense; preserves the distinct county and personal claims |

The eight Associates adopt the Court's full rationale. Stone-Zsela's additional limited joins do not adopt the Harlow/Anderson immunity rationale or the further procedural grounds identified above.

#### Holdings

##### 1. The two subordinates receive personal immunity on the actual notice and role record

**Holding and operative rule:** Astle and Blank receive qualified immunity because, during the 1988–1989 processing at issue, the law did not make their own conduct's federal unlawfulness sufficiently apparent in the supported administrative circumstances. Consultation with a superior is relevant to those circumstances but is not an automatic defense, and a state regulatory deadline violation does not alone establish a clearly established federal violation.

**Authority:** Part I of Ginsburg's opinion for all eight Associate Justices controls the complete ground. Stone-Zsela also joins the no-automatic-defense proposition but agrees with the immunity judgment on a different ground.

**Controlling explanation:** Harlow and Anderson require objective, particularized conduct-time notice. Elder requires consideration of all relevant authority; Lanier permits an obviously covered application without an identical prior licensing case. Neither those later decisions nor Lewis can supply notice retrospectively. Davis v. Scherer separates a state-law breach from the required federal obligation.

The earlier revocation was judicially sustained, while later administrative proceedings expunged the underlying indications. Expungement did not itself adjudicate automatic reinstatement. Astle sought Blank's advice about that relationship; Blank consulted Turner, and the consultation reached state regional official Klump. Klump's direction to decide went to Turner. The record does not establish that Astle or Blank personally received and defied that instruction or possessed final authority to resolve the conflict.

Prolonged inaction may deny the practical benefit of a hearing available only after denial, and asking a supervisor cannot excuse knowingly refusing a plainly required act within one's control. Those concerns do not establish these two defendants' personal liability on this supported role and notice record. The state rules required investigation within 75 days of a valid application and notification within 30 days after completion, with a hearing after denial; those commands do not themselves decide the federal process claim or entitlement to certification. The Court resolves immunity without deciding the ultimate deprivation or excusing every administrative delay.

##### 2. The official-capacity judgment requires an actual Rule 50 and municipal-liability determination

**Holding and operative rule:** Denial of a defendant's summary-judgment motion is not a judgment establishing the plaintiff's liability case and cannot, merely as law of the case, justify a directed liability judgment at trial. DuRose's official-capacity judgment must instead be evaluated on the actual trial evidence under Rule 50 and the requirements of an attributable county policy or authorized final decision causing an established federal deprivation; personal qualified immunity supplies no county defense.

**Authority:** Part II has all eight Associate Justices for the complete Rule 50 correction and bounded remand. Stone-Zsela additionally joins the entity-capacity, separate-deprivation/policy/causation and no-personal-immunity propositions, but not the additional Rule 50 rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** Kentucky v. Graham identifies the official-capacity judgment as the entity's judgment. Monell excludes automatic employer liability, and Pembaur requires an actual authorized final policy decision when that is the asserted basis. McMillian directs attention to the entity and the challenged function under applicable law and legally effective usage. A title, operational discretion, employment or absence of immediate supervision alone does not establish final policymaking; its Alabama holding identifies no Oneida policymaker. Collins keeps the underlying federal deprivation distinct from its municipal attribution.

The trial court treated an earlier denial of DuRose's summary-judgment request as though liability already had been established. That premise bypassed the required trial-record determination. The familiar district court must now evaluate that record and explain any supported Rule 50 ruling. Judgment for plaintiffs, judgment for the county or factual issues for a jury remain possible; the Court selects none in advance and orders no automatic new trial. The complete trial record is not reproduced here, and that limitation supplies no adverse factual finding. Neither the $300,000 judgment nor its dependent fee/cost award can remain on the erroneous premise, but the Court fixes no proper amount or independent fee entitlement.

##### 3. The distinct later personal claim requires its own identified disposition

**Holding and operative rule:** An order addressing the original pre-expungement pleading cannot dispose of a distinct post-expungement personal claim added later merely by an informal assertion that all personal claims were dismissed. The district court must identify any actual ruling and its basis, or adjudicate the pending request under the standard governing its actual procedural stage.

**Authority:** Part III, joined by all eight Associate Justices, controls this procedural ground. Stone-Zsela supports the remand disposition without joining the additional rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** The original complaint concerned earlier acts, while the amended pleading added the later processing claim. Treating an earlier order as a disposition of a not-yet-pleaded claim fails to identify what was decided. An informal letter does not fill that gap. If a ruling occurred, its actual basis must be explained; otherwise the pending request requires decision. Pleading review cannot demand trial proof, and summary judgment must use its own record and standard. Crawford-El preserves ordinary procedural burdens and case management rather than a special evidentiary barrier. Nothing in this remand finds DuRose personally aware of the consultation chain, responsible for every subordinate act or liable for a federal violation. The Article 78 adequate-process contention remains undecided for appropriate consideration below.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Harlow v. Fitzgerald:** Applied to the two subordinates' objective conduct-time immunity; its defense remains governing law.
- **Anderson v. Creighton:** Applied to particularized apparent unlawfulness without demanding a factual twin.
- **Davis v. Scherer:** Applied to distinguish violation of state procedural duties from violation of a clearly established federal obligation.
- **Elder v. Holloway, February 23, 1994:** Applied to consideration of all relevant law while keeping notice fixed to the actual conduct date.
- **United States v. Lanier, March 31, 1997:** Applied to reject a Supreme-Court-only or identical-facts prerequisite; it creates no retrospective notice.
- **Kentucky v. Graham:** Applied to the entity character of the official-capacity judgment and the separation from personal defenses.
- **Monell v. Department of Social Services:** Applied to policy and causation requirements without automatic liability for an employee's act.
- **Pembaur v. Cincinnati:** Applied to the need for actual final authority when a single decision is asserted to be municipal policy.
- **McMillian v. Monroe County, June 2, 1997:** Applied to function-specific identification of the responsible government and final policymaker; the Alabama result is not transferred to Oneida County.
- **Collins v. City of Harker Heights, February 26, 1992:** Applied to the independent inquiries into a federal deprivation and municipal responsibility.
- **Crawford-El v. Britton, June 16, 1997:** Preserved at ordinary proof, lawful case management and independent defenses; it adopted no replacement for Harlow/Anderson and makes motive no element of every process claim.
- **County of Sacramento v. Lewis, May 26, 1998:** Distinguished as a rapid-pursuit substantive-due-process decision; its harmful-purpose rule does not govern licensing procedure, and its later date supplies no earlier notice.

#### Law After Decision

The decision applies personal qualified immunity to these two officials without a superior-approval safe harbor or a merits ruling on every licensing delay. County liability remains independent of that personal defense. A denial of summary judgment cannot establish trial liability for the opposing party, and distinct later claims require an actual stage-appropriate disposition. Existing amendment permission creates no merits entitlement. No automatic reinstatement follows from expungement, and no general rule requiring or excusing Article 78 recourse is adopted.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stone-Zsela agrees with the judgment but would use a contextual reasonable-mistake defense borne by the official, preserving his proposed change to the existing immunity framework. Accurate consultation about genuinely conflicting administrative obligations, followed by a legally plausible resolution, can support that defense. Superior approval alone is insufficient and cannot provide a duplicative excuse for deliberate violations or indefinite inaction. He supports the limited protection of Astle and Blank on their actual circumstances without treating expungement as automatic reinstatement.

He also emphasizes that DuRose's official-capacity judgment concerns the county. A personal reasonable-mistake defense cannot excuse an attributable county policy. The actual federal deprivation, policymaker's role, municipal responsibility and trial judgment require their own analysis. His proposed personal defense is not adopted by the Court, and his concurrence supplies no new liability or damages finding on either DuRose claim.

### United States v. Lacy; Ninth Circuit No. 95-30370, 119 F.3d 742

Plenary merits decision on submission, May 28, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

On review of the Ninth Circuit's July 10, 1997 affirmance of the possession conviction after a jury trial. The two questions are whether the affidavit's ten-month-old download evidence was stale and whether the authorization for equipment, storage media and related records was sufficiently particular. The lower court's scienter, meaning-of-matter, interstate-commerce-element and plain-error decisions are outside the grant.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

All nine reject both the staleness and facial-particularity challenges. The conviction remains in force without a new hearing, search, sentence or remedial instruction. Previously suppressed documents remain suppressed, and evidence obtained in the separate consent search is not reclassified as a product of the apartment warrant.

#### Opinion Topology

Thomas delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine adopt both holdings and their qualifications. No separate writing is filed.

#### Holdings

##### 1. Actual acquisition and supported retention sustain probable cause over this interval

**Holding and operative rule:** A ten-month interval does not defeat probable cause to search for downloaded child-pornography files when the affidavit establishes actual acquisition, durable electronic storage and a supported likelihood of continued retention. On this affidavit, those facts gave the issuing magistrate a substantial basis to find a present fair probability that the material remained at Lacy's apartment; neither elapsed time alone nor a generalized collector profile decides the issue.

**Authority:** Thomas's opinion for all nine Justices controls the complete rule and application.

**Controlling explanation:** Sgro requires facts sufficiently related to issuance to support a present probability. Gates asks for a practical assessment of the total circumstances and substantial-basis review of the issuing magistrate. Ornelas preserves that institutional distinction from appellate review of a warrantless search.

The affidavit described completed downloads, not a subscription, inquiry or attempted order. BAMSE records identified the number and downloaded files, and telephone records linked matching calls to the apartment. At least two specifically described files supplied acquisition evidence. The experienced agent explained that possessors commonly value such material and retain it for long periods in secure places, often at home. That account had an individual foundation here: the corroborated receipt of durable files.

The competing argument correctly warns that generalized retention habits cannot make a home perpetually searchable. This result depends on the established acquisition, the property involved and this ten-month interval. The Court assumes neither permanent possession nor an ongoing distribution business. An affidavit resting only on generalized habits without actual acquisition presents a different question. Subsequent discoveries and trial admissions supply no retrospective justification.

##### 2. The reported system and records categories are sufficiently particular

**Holding and operative rule:** This warrant adequately confines the equipment, storage-media and related-record search because probable cause ties the apartment system to the downloaded material, the affidavit explains the storage and retrieval uncertainty, and the authorization supplies objective offense-related document categories, including the BAMSE connection. The ruling permits neither a general inventory of unrelated data nor unrestricted examination of everything later found in a seized device.

**Authority:** The same unanimous opinion controls this independently resolved particularity question.

**Controlling explanation:** Andresen and Coolidge require limits that prevent a general exploratory search. The necessary specificity depends on the supported offense, the objects sought and information reasonably available at issuance. Inability to name hardware more precisely cannot justify unrelated categories, but a warrant need not supply knowledge investigators could not reasonably possess.

Here the reported affidavit tied the system to actual downloads and explained why investigators could not tell whether the files were on the hard drive or disks or what equipment and software accurate retrieval would require. The authorization's objective records limits answered the separate risk of taking unrelated papers. The circuit's contrast with the unrestricted all-records warrant in Kow is persuasive on those actual differences; its three considerations are not adopted as a new mandatory national checklist.

Pinson and Myers protect concealed domestic information through lawful authority and do not create a digital exception. No antecedent unauthorized thermal or comparable search is established here. The Court decides the sufficiency of this warrant's reported categories, not an unpreserved execution challenge, an unquoted clause, incorporation of a separate instrument or every later examination after seizure. It prescribes no forensic protocol and decides no remote account, cloud or other technologically different search.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Sgro v. United States, 287 U.S. 206:** Applied to require a present probable-cause basis, with time assessed in relation to the evidence's nature and supported retention.
- **Illinois v. Gates, 462 U.S. 213:** Applied for practical totality and substantial-basis review of the issuing magistrate, without retrospective supplementation.
- **Ornelas v. United States, May 28, 1996:** Its separate nonwarrant appellate rule is preserved; it does not replace Gates review of warrant issuance.
- **Andresen v. Maryland, 427 U.S. 463:** Applied to objective offense-related limits that prevent an exploratory records search.
- **Coolidge v. New Hampshire, 403 U.S. 443:** Applied to the prohibition on general searches, without making investigative uncertainty permission to take unrelated material.
- **United States v. Pinson, May 8, 1995:** Its protection of concealed home information remains; it neither invalidates an adequately supported warrant nor licenses unrelated digital examination.
- **United States v. Myers, June 11, 1996:** Its distinction between authority for an intrusion and derivative remedies remains. No earlier unauthorized intrusion is found in this case.
- **United States v. Leon, 468 U.S. 897:** Not reached. No good-faith exception or reliance finding supplies an alternative holding.

#### Law After Decision

The decision supplies a bounded application of existing probable-cause and particularity principles to durable downloaded files and the associated system. It creates no automatic expiration period, indefinite-retention presumption, collector-profile shortcut or general computer-search exception. Actual acquisition, supported retention and objective offense-related limits remain essential to this result. Execution and exclusion questions require their own properly presented facts and law.

#### Source Notes

[United States v. Lacy, 119 F.3d 742](https://static.case.law/f3d/119/html/0742-01.html) supplies the corroborated downloads, ten-month interval, retention account, reported equipment and document limits, partial suppression and consent-search distinction. The published account does not reproduce the complete original warrant and affidavit or establish every warrant clause, an exact warrant date or item-level execution findings. Later discoveries do not establish original probable cause.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The conviction judgment remains in force on both reviewed warrant grounds. The receipt acquittal and dismissed importation charge are not revived. The excluded instructional and statutory issues remain outside this decision. Existing suppression of the inconsequential documents and the separate consent-search basis for some disks are undisturbed. No new hearing, search, acquittal, release, retrial or altered sentence is directed.

### City of Florence v. Chipman / Stemler v. City of Florence; Sixth Circuit Nos. 96-5993 and 96-5996, 126 F.3d 856

Plenary merits decision on submission, June 1, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

Review under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) concerns the Sixth Circuit's October 8, 1997 reinstatement of Black's individual substantive-due-process claim and Stemler's individual equal-protection claim; rehearing was denied November 13, 1997. The plaintiffs appealed final individual dismissals and municipal summary judgments. This is not an interlocutory officer appeal. The questions concern compelled dangerous placement, purposeful discriminatory selection despite probable cause, and qualified immunity for conduct on February 18–19, 1994.

#### Judgment

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|
| Black's individual substantive-due-process claim | Reinstatement affirmed, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | The claim may proceed under the stated individual-proof and immunity limits. |
| Stemler's individual equal-protection claim | Reinstatement affirmed, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | The claim may proceed under the stated comparison, purpose, individual-proof and immunity limits. |

The decision determines legal sufficiency on the proper favorable account, not final wrongdoing, damages or every defendant's ultimate immunity. It orders no automatic trial, discovery program or new hearing. Municipal and official-capacity judgments and the other excluded claims are undisturbed.

#### Opinion Topology

O'Connor delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine adopt the narrow restraint, arbitrary-selection and conduct-time immunity holdings with their individual-proof limits. Scalia files a concurrence, joined by Thomas, preserving their direct-restraint basis and rejecting any implication that they adopt a broader conscience framework. Their concurrence withholds assent from no proposition actually adopted by the Court.

#### Holdings

##### 1. Knowing, involuntary transfer into danger states a bounded due-process claim

**Holding and operative rule:** An officer who actually restrains a person's ability to protect herself and personally directs or carries out her involuntary transfer into a serious danger the officer actually appreciates may not deliberately and unreasonably disregard that danger when the officer has authority and a real opportunity to respond. The claim requires injury attributable to that defendant's conduct; negligence, constructive knowledge, another officer's knowledge, assistance in a genuinely voluntary departure or a reasonable protective response that fails does not suffice.

**Authority:** O'Connor's opinion for all nine Justices controls this direct-restraint application. It adopts no broader free-standing conscience framework or general prison-to-roadside rule.

**Controlling explanation:** DeShaney distinguishes failure to protect from the State's affirmative restraint of a person's ability to protect herself. Youngberg supplies the custody-related protection principle, Daniels excludes negligence, and Collins distinguishes actual compulsion from voluntary exposure. Pinder's noncoercive assurances did not create custody and do not foreclose this alleged compelled transfer. Farmer, Doe and Lewis preserve actual awareness, individual responsibility, reasonable response and genuine opportunity; their distinct settings do not supply a mechanical roadside checklist.

The favorable account alleges specific warnings of Kritis's violence and intoxication, a threat of arrest if Black did not leave with him, and physical removal and placement in his truck. Those successive actions differ from Lewis's rapidly unfolding pursuit. Neither the earlier private chase nor the later crash turns the roadside transfer into a police-pursuit case. No fixed number of seconds establishes opportunity or culpability.

Dusing allegedly received warnings and directed the threat; Dolan and Alsip allegedly removed Black, and Alsip placed her in the truck. Reuthe's alcohol report is not itself culpable placement. Alsip's limited information and the competing voluntary-departure account remain material. Knowledge and responsibility cannot be pooled. Obvious danger may support an inference of actual awareness but does not compel it. The fatal crash permits the causal theory, not a finding against every officer; the renewed fight, uncertain steering cause and legally proper scope of the state causal ruling remain relevant. The Court creates no general duty to investigate every warning, arrest Kritis or prevent private violence, and does not reach noncustodial state-created danger.

##### 2. The favorable knowing-coercion account cannot be dismissed categorically on qualified-immunity grounds

**Holding and operative rule:** Qualified immunity does not categorically bar Black's claim on the favorable allegation that an officer knowingly used actual police restraint to compel her transfer into an appreciated serious danger. The relevant clarity is that of February 18–19, 1994, assessed for each officer's own information, authority and acts; the ruling leaves supported factual and legal defenses available at their proper stage.

**Authority:** The same unanimous opinion controls this separate conduct-time immunity holding; it neither finds every defendant liable nor substitutes a new defense for Harlow and Anderson.

**Controlling explanation:** Harlow and Anderson ask whether existing law made the actual conduct's federal unlawfulness sufficiently apparent. DeShaney, Youngberg, Daniels and Collins supplied the restraint/direct-deprivation distinction before this encounter. Meador and Heflin applied custodial deliberate indifference. Walton's absence of police custody distinguishes its immunity result. Nishiyama's intentional unreasonable exposure to a known danger supplies relevant warning, but its gross-negligence language and contested noncustodial reach are neither adopted nor indispensable.

An exact roadside factual twin is unnecessary, while a general assertion of bodily security would be insufficient by itself. The alleged knowing use of police control supplies the particular connection. Neither uncertainty about noncustodial protection nor disagreement about every officer's information justifies dismissing that favorable account categorically.

Elder's February 23, 1994 decision, Farmer and later decisions including Pinder, Doe, Lanier, Lewis and DeCarlo may clarify present analysis but cannot supply missing notice retroactively. DeCarlo reinforces personal role and stage-specific adjudication; its different administrative setting does not compel immunity here. There is no mandatory order between difficult constitutional merits and immunity. The Court resolves this properly presented narrow claim without determining disputed knowledge, actual opportunity, causation or the outcome of a later supported motion.

##### 3. Probable cause does not defeat the pleaded arbitrary-selection claim

**Holding and operative rule:** Probable cause to arrest does not defeat an equal-protection claim that officers deliberately selected an otherwise chargeable driver solely to impose arbitrary group hostility while deliberately leaving an actually comparable driver unenforced on that basis. The claimant must establish discriminatory purpose and effect, relevant comparison, each defendant's participation and attributable injury; a supported legitimate enforcement distinction defeats the asserted arbitrary comparison.

**Authority:** The same unanimous opinion controls this narrow selective-enforcement rule, without an orientation-based scrutiny tier.

**Controlling explanation:** Yick Wo prohibits arbitrary discriminatory enforcement; Oyler preserves enforcement discretion while rejecting selection on an unjustifiable arbitrary basis; Wayte requires purpose and effect. Moreno excludes bare group hostility as a legitimate governmental end. Whren preserves an independent equality claim despite objective Fourth Amendment authority, and Romer applies ordinary review's prohibition on bare hostility without creating a new tier.

Stemler alleges that officers deliberately arrested her while leaving the visibly intoxicated Kritis unenforced because they perceived her as a lesbian and sought to impose arbitrary harm. Her denial of that orientation does not erase the alleged perceived classification. Wince arrested her; Wince, Dusing and Dolan are her individual defendants. Other defendants on Black's claim are not automatically defendants on hers.

Probable cause, a hostile epithet, knowledge of Kritis's characterization, differential arrest or later acquittal alone proves neither purpose nor the necessary comparison. The favorable account permits the claim, but legitimate differences and each defendant's actual responsibility remain for proof. Bowers's particular conduct holding does not authorize hostile use of unrelated DUI laws. The Court imposes no duty to arrest every offender, imports no Armstrong prosecutor-discovery threshold and prescribes no universal new burden sequence from Crawford-El. It resolves neither every kind of personal animosity nor every orientation classification.

##### 4. The narrow arbitrary-selection prohibition predates this encounter

**Holding and operative rule:** The existing prohibition against purposeful arbitrary group-based enforcement permits Stemler's narrow claim to proceed despite qualified immunity, subject to proof of the actual comparator, arbitrary purpose, effect and each officer's conduct. The Court relies on law existing before February 18–19, 1994, not later recognition of a new protected classification or retroactive notice from Romer or Whren.

**Authority:** The same unanimous opinion controls this distinct immunity application and its proof limits.

**Controlling explanation:** Yick Wo, Oyler, Wayte and Moreno already prohibited the particular arbitrary selection alleged here. Oyler did not make every selective enforcement choice unconstitutional; its rejection of mere selectivity and insufficient statistics remains. The claim rests on deliberate selection for bare group harm, supported by an actual comparator, rather than a demand for heightened scrutiny or an inference from nonarrest alone.

The competing contention that no established orientation tier existed fails to answer that narrower prohibition. Bowers did not exempt unrelated enforcement from equal protection. Romer and Whren clarify present doctrine but supply no February 1994 notice. Harlow and Anderson still require each defendant's particular conduct to fall within the established prohibition. The decision permits ordinary proof and defenses; it does not adjudicate motive, comparative intoxication or responsibility as established facts.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **DeShaney v. Winnebago County:** Applied to actual restraint rather than a general failure to protect; noncustodial state-created danger remains unaddressed.
- **Youngberg v. Romeo:** Applied for the protection principle arising from actual custody, without mechanically transferring every institutional standard.
- **Daniels v. Williams:** Applied to exclude negligence; the separate actual-awareness requirement is preserved by the custody authorities and this direct-restraint application.
- **Collins v. City of Harker Heights, February 26, 1992:** Its distinction between voluntary exposure and actual compulsion is applied; its separate municipal-attribution inquiry remains intact.
- **Pinder v. Johnson, May 21, 1996:** Distinguished because assurances and a noncoercive undertaking did not restrain the claimant there; its immunity result and reservation of distinct affirmative deprivation remain.
- **Farmer v. Brennan, June 6, 1994:** Its actual-awareness, permissible-inference and reasonable-response limits inform the present analysis; it supplies no earlier notice or wholesale roadside Eighth Amendment framework.
- **Doe v. Taylor Independent School District, October 16, 1995:** Its personal knowledge, authority, opportunity and causal-role limits inform the analysis without becoming a mechanically transplanted checklist.
- **County of Sacramento v. Lewis, May 26, 1998:** Distinguished from alleged knowing roadside restraint and transfer; its rapid-pursuit harmful-purpose requirement is not extended to every vehicle-related injury.
- **Harlow v. Fitzgerald:** Applied to preserve personal qualified immunity and the conduct-time inquiry.
- **Anderson v. Creighton:** Applied to require particularized apparent unlawfulness without an identical factual precedent.
- **Elder v. Holloway, February 23, 1994:** Its all-relevant-law method is preserved; its date postdates the encounter and supplies no earlier notice.
- **United States v. Lanier, March 31, 1997:** Its rejection of a factual-twin prerequisite informs present review without retrospective notice or substitution of an unpresented Fourth Amendment claim.
- **DeCarlo v. Fry, May 28, 1998:** Applied to preserve personal role, actual information and the proper procedural stage; its administrative immunity result does not decide this coercive-transfer claim.
- **Meador v. Cabinet for Human Resources:** Its pre-event custodial deliberate-indifference application supports notice at the bounded restraint level.
- **Heflin v. Stewart County:** Its pre-event custodial protection rule supports the same particularized notice inquiry.
- **Walton v. Southfield:** Distinguished because the absence of police custody mattered to its immunity result.
- **Nishiyama v. Dickson County:** Considered for intentional unreasonable exposure to known danger; its gross-negligence language and disputed noncustodial reach are not adopted.
- **Yick Wo v. Hopkins:** Applied to arbitrary discriminatory enforcement despite formally lawful authority.
- **Oyler v. Boles:** Applied while preserving discretion, the insufficiency of mere selectivity and the need for an unjustifiable discriminatory basis.
- **Wayte v. United States:** Applied to require discriminatory purpose and effect.

- **Bowers v. Hardwick:** Distinguished as addressing a particular substantive conduct question, not authorization for arbitrary unrelated DUI enforcement.

- **United States v. Armstrong, May 13, 1996:** Its prosecutor-discovery threshold is not extended to this police-enforcement merits claim.

#### Law After Decision

The decision permits the two individual claims on knowing coercive restraint and purposeful arbitrary selection, while retaining actual awareness, genuine opportunity, comparison, personal responsibility, causation and particularized immunity. It establishes no general police-protection duty, universal conscience framework, orientation scrutiny tier or collective-knowledge rule. A lawful basis to arrest answers neither every equality objection nor the separate dangerous-transfer claim. Current constitutional law and conduct-time notice remain different inquiries. No municipal liability, final constitutional tort or damages award follows from reinstatement.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia, joined by Thomas, concurs fully in the narrow opinion. DeShaney's direct-restraint principle and Collins's distinction between voluntary exposure and compulsion support this alleged affirmative deprivation. They do not adopt a free-standing judicial-shock inquiry or a general duty to rescue persons from private danger. Lewis's distinct reckless-pursuit claim therefore does not compel the same merits result here. Thomas's disagreement with Farmer's Eighth Amendment punishment predicate remains; today's direct-deprivation holding neither converts that judgment concurrence into a full Farmer join nor transplants every prison rule to roadside custody.

They also join the narrow equality and immunity holdings. Moreno's bare-harm prohibition, preserved in their Romer position, does not depend on a suspect classification. Bowers does not license deliberate hostile selection under unrelated traffic laws. Actual comparison, purpose, effect, personal conduct and the old prohibition must do the work. Their concurrence supports no broader framework than the Court adopts and no finding that the disputed allegations have been proved.

### United States v. Cabrales, No. 97-643

Merits decision, June 1, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 29, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit, 109 F.3d 471, as amended at 115 F.3d 621. The Government appealed pretrial dismissal of two substantive money-laundering counts for improper venue, and the court of appeals affirmed. The question is whether Missouri's connection to the criminal source of the funds permits trial there when all charged financial transactions occurred in Florida. Count II invokes 18 U.S.C. §1956(a)(1)(B)(ii), the reporting-avoidance branch; Count III invokes §1957, concerning a monetary transaction in criminally derived property exceeding $10,000. Count I, conspiracy, was not dismissed and is outside this appeal.

#### Judgment

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|
| Count II: §1956(a)(1)(B)(ii), reporting avoidance | Dismissal for improper Missouri venue affirmed, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | Count II remains dismissed for improper venue. |
| Count III: §1957, transaction in criminally derived property exceeding $10,000 | Dismissal for improper Missouri venue affirmed, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | Count III remains dismissed for improper venue. |

This is not an acquittal or immunity from prosecution in a proper district. The Court directs no new indictment, hearing, sentencing action or prosecution elsewhere and adjudicates no part of Count I.

#### Opinion Topology

Stone-Zsela delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine adopt the common venue rule, its application to both counts and its limits. No separate writing is filed.

#### Holdings

##### 1. The source of the proceeds does not relocate these Florida transactions

**Holding and operative rule:** When the charged conduct under §§1956(a)(1)(B)(ii) and 1957 consists entirely of financial transactions begun, conducted and completed in Florida, Missouri does not become a proper venue solely because prior Missouri crimes generated the funds. Illicit origin remains a necessary statutory circumstance, but these counts allege no Missouri laundering act or transportation by Cabrales that would make her charged offenses begin, continue or end there; the two Missouri counts must therefore be dismissed for venue.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's unanimous opinion controls the complete rule, both counts' application and the reserved charging distinctions.

**Controlling explanation:** Article III, the Sixth Amendment and Rule 18 locate trial where the charged crime was committed. Anderson directs attention to the offense's nature and constituting acts. Subject to contrary congressional provision, §3237(a) permits trial where an offense actually begins, continues or is completed; its transportation paragraph does not turn every commerce-linked transaction into transportation through the place where funds arose.

The January 1991 Florida deposit and withdrawals are the charged transactions. Missouri drug sales supplied the property's criminal origin. The Government alleges no transport of those funds from Missouri by Cabrales and no act of hers constituting these laundering transactions there.

The Government argues that illicit origin is indispensable and therefore begins the laundering offense where the predicate crime occurred. But an indispensable attribute of the transaction's object need not be prohibited conduct by this defendant at that location. Gaudin preserves every statutory element and its factfinder; it does not transform each circumstance into a geographical act. The knowledge and unlawful-source elements retain their full force. Practical access to Missouri evidence and a description of an integrated scheme cannot add charged conduct.

United States v. Thomas involved actual authorized interstate transportation completed at its recipient, not this antecedent connection. Beddow's predicate participation and transport likewise present different allegations. A source-of-funds theory alone, as suggested by Sax, cannot supply venue for these counts. Genuine acquisition and transportation, participation in the predicate crime, aiding and abetting or a differently charged conspiracy require their own allegations and law. No universal bar to multistate laundering venue follows. Count I's venue, agreement, overt acts and guilt remain undecided; the Court inserts none of those matters into Counts II and III.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **United States v. Anderson, 328 U.S. 699:** Applied to identify the charged offense's nature and the acts constituting it, rather than every related historical circumstance.
- **United States v. Gaudin, June 19, 1995:** Preserved as requiring full force and proper factfinding for every statutory element; it does not make every circumstance element a separate geographical act.
- **United States v. Thomas, May 19, 1997:** Distinguished because authorized interstate transportation actually ended at the recipient there; its venue rule and limits on remote effects remain intact.
- **United States v. Beddow, 957 F.2d 1330:** Distinguished by the defendant's predicate participation and acquisition and transport of funds; no decision about such allegations is necessary here.
- **United States v. Sax, 39 F.3d 1380:** Its suggestion that the predicate crime's location alone supplies laundering venue is rejected for the transactions and charges presented here.

#### Law After Decision

Venue for these substantive laundering counts follows the charged financial conduct. Proof that the funds arose from crime in another district does not by itself relocate that conduct. Every statutory element remains, and actual multistate offenses remain subject to their own venue rules. A conspiracy, aiding-and-abetting or transportation theory cannot be assumed to repair the present indictment. The result leaves properly venued prosecution available without resolving another charge or the defendant's guilt.

#### Source Notes

The [Eighth Circuit opinion, 109 F.3d 471](https://static.case.law/f3d/109/html/0471-01.html), read with its [June 12, 1997 amendment, 115 F.3d 621](https://static.case.law/f3d/115/html/0621-01.html), establishes the charged Florida transactions and the two-count venue disposition. The operative account establishes only that Count I was not dismissed and is outside this appeal; it establishes no Missouri overt act, valid conspiracy venue or conspiracy guilt. The Government alleges neither Cabrales's transportation from Missouri nor a Missouri act constituting either substantive transaction.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The dismissal of Counts II and III for improper Missouri venue remains effective. Count I is outside this decision; its venue, factual predicates and merits are not determined. There is no remand to decide guilt, ordered new prosecution or sentencing consequence from this ruling.

**End of entry: United States v. Cabrales, Merits decision, June 1, 1998.**

### Duffy v. Wolle; Eighth Circuit No. 96-3210, 123 F.3d 1026

Plenary merits decision on submission, June 3, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

Review under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) concerns the Eighth Circuit's August 19, 1997 affirmance of final summary judgment for Wolle, Vietor and Longstaff. Duffy challenges the selection of McPhillips as chief probation officer and seeks further proceedings. The questions concern the received individual constitutional damages action despite an internal judicial EEO plan, absolute immunity for administrative hiring, and the summary-judgment and Rule 56(f) treatment. This is not an interlocutory officer appeal.

#### Judgment

**Vacated and remanded, 8–1 as to the operative mandate.**

**Supporting:** Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Stone-Zsela, who would reverse and require appropriately limited intent-and-pretext discovery before resolving merits, damages or qualified immunity.

All nine reject the offered internal-plan bar and absolute judicial immunity and agree that the existing final judgment cannot stand; that agreement does not make the two remand instructions identical.

The Court orders genuine reconsideration under ordinary procedural rules, with no guaranteed deposition, evidentiary hearing, trial, appointment, backpay, damages or fee award. A properly supported denial or limitation of discovery and renewed dispositive judgment remain available. Each defendant's knowledge, participation, purpose and defenses require their own basis.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stevens | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports vacatur and the ordinary reconsideration mandate | I: received individual action and offered plan bar; II: administrative hiring and absolute immunity; III: Rule 56(f), summary judgment and bounded remedy |
| Same opinion, partial join | Stevens | Stone-Zsela | Agrees that the judgment cannot stand but seeks a different mandate | Parts I and II; Part III only on the absence of special evidentiary protection from judicial status or favorable affidavits and the prematurity of the existing judgment |
| Concurrence in part and dissent in part | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Would reverse with required limited discovery | Rejects the Court's permission to deny discovery on reconsideration and resolve a dispositive issue before that required inquiry |

#### Holdings

##### 1. The internal plan does not establish the asserted categorical bar

**Holding and operative rule:** The existence of the Judicial Conference's nonstatutory internal EEO procedure does not, on the supported remedial record, displace this received individual Fifth Amendment employment-discrimination damages action under Davis v. Passman. The decision establishes no direct action against the court as an agency, no Title VII coverage and no rule foreclosing consideration of a different congressional remedial scheme or another properly supported special factor.

**Authority:** Part I of Stevens's opinion has all nine Justices for this bounded rejection of the offered bar.

**Controlling explanation:** Davis recognizes the individual constitutional employment action. Meyer rejects a new direct federal-agency action while preserving received individual actions at their own scope; its agency holding cannot simply be transferred to these defendants. Bush requires attention to an actual congressional remedial design. The reported plan originated with the Judicial Conference, and no supported congressional direction makes it the asserted exclusive substitute.

The judicial-employment setting and absence of Title VII coverage warrant attention to the real remedial arrangement, not an inference that any internal procedure necessarily extinguishes the received action. An unquoted plan term or unsupported statutory scheme cannot supply the missing bar. The Court does not hold that only an express exclusivity sentence could ever matter or that internal procedures are always irrelevant.

ALPA likewise requires examination of the institution and the authority assigned to it; it supplies no universal consent exception or blanket rule against preliminary recourse. Proper case management remains, and a materially different exhaustion question requires its own supported record. This holding neither grants automatic equitable relief nor decides the plaintiff's ultimate discrimination case.

##### 2. Selecting the chief probation officer is administrative hiring

**Holding and operative rule:** Selection of a chief probation officer is personnel administration, not adjudication, and receives no absolute judicial immunity merely because judges make the choice under statutory appointment authority or embody it in an order. Rejecting that absolute defense leaves qualified immunity, personal responsibility, the constitutional elements and the legal basis for each requested remedy distinct.

**Authority:** Part II has all nine Justices for the functional classification and rejection of absolute immunity.

**Controlling explanation:** Forrester distinguishes personnel administration from judging disputes. Antoine requires historical and functional justification for absolute immunity; importance to judicial work does not suffice. Bogan protects prospective legislative policy while expressly preserving the difference from individualized administrative personnel decisions.

The chief probation officer performs important duties assisting the courts. Neither those duties nor section 3602's placement of appointment authority in judges transforms selecting an employee into deciding a case. Judicial title, operational significance and the formal appointment instrument therefore do not answer the functional question. No legislative abolition or adjudicative act is presented.

The ruling does not declare discrimination or remove every personal defense. Harlow and Anderson govern the actual conduct-time notice and individual conduct questions. Davis and Forrester predate the 1993–1994 events; later decisions such as DeCarlo and Florence guide present adjudication but cannot create earlier notice. The Court neither grants nor conclusively rejects every defendant's qualified-immunity defense on an undeveloped record.

##### 3. The supported request requires genuine procedural reconsideration

**Holding and operative rule:** Before sustaining summary judgment on this record, the lower courts must assess Duffy's specific Rule 56(f) request and the material evidence under ordinary standards, without treating the defendants' favorable affidavits or judicial status as conclusive proof of lawful motive. Specific nonconclusory support remains necessary; the court may clarify, tailor or deny an inadequately supported request, resolve an actually dispositive legal issue first, and enter renewed summary judgment when the properly assessed record warrants it.

**Authority:** Part III's complete rule and remedy have the eight Associate Justices. Stone-Zsela joins the rejection of special evidentiary protection and the prematurity conclusion but would require discovery before further dispositive rulings; that additional mandate is not the Court's holding.

**Controlling explanation:** Crawford-El preserves ordinary motive proof, necessary tailored inquiry, early legal defenses and protective case management; neither unavailable proof demanded in advance nor a general hope of impeachment decides every request. Hicks leaves ultimate persuasion with the plaintiff: rejection of an explanation may support an inference but neither compels liability nor requires a special additional category of evidence. DeCarlo reinforces individual responsibility and decision at the proper procedural stage.

McPhillips had substantial experience, education, favorable references and interview assessments. Those are genuine lawful explanations, not a finding of discriminatory pretext. Inclusive recruitment and a neutral vacancy notice are lawful. The law-clerk comparison concerns different work and inadequately described pools and may remain weak or immaterial.

But Ailts's account refers to interest in appointing a female, while Wolle describes recruiting a diverse qualified pool. Ailts's uncertain source and inferred position limit the inference; they do not conclusively rewrite his account as recruitment alone. No panel policy or adoption by any defendant is found. Counsel's actual request sought to test the stated selection reasons. The district court's reliance on favorable defense and screener affidavits requires a genuine assessment of that request rather than treating production as conclusive proof of motive. An all-male panel carries no special presumption against discrimination.

The Court does not order any particular deposition, a broad personnel inquiry or a trial. More precise identification of sought facts, written inquiry, sequencing and protection of unrelated judicial work remain available. Each defendant's knowledge, participation and purpose must be assessed separately. No clear-and-convincing rule was imposed below; the correction concerns application of ordinary rules to this record.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Davis v. Passman:** Applied to preserve the received individual constitutional employment-discrimination action at its existing scope.
- **FDIC v. Meyer, February 23, 1994:** Distinguished at its direct-agency boundary; its preservation of received individual actions governs here.
- **Bush v. Lucas:** Applied to require examination of actual congressional remedial design, without treating any internal procedure as necessarily exclusive.
- **Air Line Pilots Association v. Miller, May 26, 1998:** Its institution-specific analysis is preserved; no universal consent or no-exhaustion rule is imported.
- **Forrester v. White:** Applied to classify personnel selection as administration rather than adjudication.
- **Antoine v. Byers & Anderson, June 7, 1993:** Applied to require historical and functional justification for absolute immunity, not importance alone.
- **Bogan v. Scott-Harris, March 3, 1998:** Distinguished as prospective legislation; its exclusion of individualized administrative decisions remains operative.
- **Harlow v. Fitzgerald:** Preserved for the separate personal qualified-immunity inquiry, without a judicial-office evidentiary exemption.
- **Anderson v. Creighton:** Preserved for particularized conduct-time notice; later law supplies no retrospective clarity.
- **McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green:** Its production framework informs the evidentiary setting without making every constitutional claim identical to a Title VII action.
- **Texas Department of Community Affairs v. Burdine:** Its ultimate persuasion allocation remains; production of legitimate reasons is not conclusive proof of actual motive.

- **St. Mary's Honor Center v. Hicks, June 25, 1993:** Applied to preserve permissible circumstantial inference while rejecting automatic liability and a mandatory extra category of direct or independent evidence.

#### Law After Decision

The received individual action remains available against the three officials on this supported record, and administrative appointment receives no absolute judicial immunity. Ordinary proof, personal defenses and properly managed procedure govern. Legitimate qualifications remain material evidence, but neither a judge's denial of discriminatory motive nor the panel's sex supplies special evidentiary protection. The judgment requires proper reconsideration of the concrete request; it creates no categorical entitlement to discovery, no liability finding and no general new discrimination burden sequence.

He agrees that judicial status and supportive affidavits cannot decide disputed motive by themselves. His required-discovery conclusion remains distinct from the Court's permission to refine, limit or deny an insufficient request or resolve an actually dispositive legal issue first. He finds no discrimination, orders no appointment and decides neither damages nor qualified immunity on a developed record. A congressionally enacted exclusive scheme could present a different case.

### United States v. Beggerly, No. 97-731

Merits decision, June 8, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 27, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit, 114 F.3d 484, decided May 29, 1997 and corrected July 28, 1997. The district court dismissed the independent action, denied the claimants' summary-judgment request and denied amendment asserting Quiet Title Act/Tucker Act jurisdiction. The court of appeals vacated the 1982 consent judgment as to these claimants and directed title for them. The questions concern authority to consider correction of that decree, extraordinary equitable relief and the alternative statutory title route; reopening, title and compensation remain distinct.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The appellate vacatur and title direction cannot stand; the 1982 consent judgment remains operative. No new title trial, extraordinary-relief hearing, valuation, repayment or second payment is ordered. The proposed district-court Tucker Act/inverse-condemnation route exceeding $10,000 supplies no new jurisdiction or amendment relief. Stone-Zsela agrees with that latter disposition without adding a ground.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Breyer | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | Supports the complete reversal and remand | I: same-court corrective jurisdiction; II: extraordinary-action threshold; III: QTA notice, period and statutory rejection of additional equitable tolling; IV: title and monetary consequences |
| Same opinion, partial join | Breyer | Stone-Zsela | Supports the complete disposition | Parts I and II and Part IV's reversal/consent-judgment consequences; disposition only on Part III and the separate Tucker Act/inverse-condemnation route, without joining their additional rationales |
| Concurrence | Stevens | Souter | Fully joins the Court and supports reversal | Distinguishes conceivable fraudulent-concealment or equitable-estoppel questions from the additional equitable tolling rejected here; grants no exception or relief |

#### Holdings

##### 1. The original court may consider the narrowly corrective independent action

**Holding and operative rule:** When the United States invoked a court's jurisdiction and obtained the challenged decree there, that same court may entertain a traditional independent action directed to relief from that decree without requiring a second independent jurisdictional grant merely because the request takes a new action's form. That corrective authority does not itself establish a new title action, general subject-matter jurisdiction or sovereign consent to affirmative monetary relief.

**Authority:** Part I of Breyer's opinion has all nine Justices for bounded corrective jurisdiction. Part IV's additional monetary-route rationale is addressed separately below.

**Controlling explanation:** Rule 60(b)'s savings language preserves an existing equitable procedure. Pacific Railroad illustrates its ancillary character: correction of the court's own decree continues the original controversy for jurisdictional purposes. The Government's invocation of that court permits this limited attack on the judgment it obtained; power to entertain the request does not establish entitlement to reopen.

Kokkonen restricts enforcement of a separate settlement contract and expressly reserves reopening. Peacock distinguishes effectuation of a decree from a new action imposing another person's debt. Neither requires the Government's blanket rule that every independent corrective action needs a new jurisdictional grant. The original court's power is actually resolved, not assumed merely to reject the merits.

The warning against new substantive relief remains valid: the authority decided here concerns correction of the same decree, not an independent authorization of every form of relief that might follow. A distinct title or monetary demand must satisfy its own lawful route.

##### 2. The alleged search and disclosure failure does not satisfy the extraordinary threshold

**Holding and operative rule:** An independent action relieving a completed judgment is available only to prevent a grave miscarriage of justice; newly discovered favorable evidence or an allegedly incomplete search and disclosure does not alone establish that exceptional entitlement. On the supported allegations here, the independent action fails, leaving the consent judgment operative without deciding the discovered document's ultimate effect on title.

**Authority:** Part II has all nine Justices for the extraordinary threshold, application and stated reservations.

**Controlling explanation:** Hazel-Atlas supplies the exceptional-injustice principle; Marshall's alleged forged-document case illustrates its force without making forgery indispensable. The traditional conditions reflected in Bankers Mortgage and National Surety—an inequitable judgment, a good defense, fraud, accident or mistake preventing its use, absence of claimant fault or negligence, and absence of an adequate legal remedy—must establish that exceptional need together. They do not provide an ordinary second chance whenever better evidence appears.

Rule 60's independent action must coexist with its ordinary motions and limits, including the one-year limit for the relevant mistake, newly discovered evidence and misconduct motions. Treating the alleged failure to provide relevant information alone as sufficient would bypass those limits. The Court does not decide whether a timely Rule 60(b)(3) motion could have succeeded.

The claimants emphasize the Government's inaccurate representation, its influence on settlement and their sustained efforts to locate records. Those matters are accepted at their supported level. They do not make the alleged incomplete search and disclosure approach the required extraordinary injustice. The Court neither finds the Government's search adequate nor faults the claimants for failing to find the material earlier. A document's presence in federal archives does not establish intentional concealment or obstruction.

Morgan Stanley and Plaut permit expressly bounded legislative reinstatement, with independent judicial eligibility and merits decisions. No comparable legislative command governs this decree; those decisions do not authorize unrestricted judicial reconsideration. The ruling creates no absolute ban on correcting consent judgments, universal intentional-fraud requirement or general archive-search immunity. It makes no separate adverse factual findings on each traditional condition and does not decide whether the new material would have changed the original result.

##### 3. The QTA's notice-based period excludes the additional equitable tolling asserted here

**Holding and operative rule:** For the private title claim presented, §2409a(g)'s twelve-year period runs from when the claimant or predecessor knew or should have known of the United States' adverse claim, not from discovery of convincing evidence supporting the claimant's own title. Additional nonstatutory equitable tolling is incompatible with that notice-triggered, unusually generous period and the Act's protection of certainty in land ownership; this 1994 alternative title action is untimely.

**Authority:** Part III has the eight Associate Justices for the complete statutory rule and application. Stone-Zsela agrees with the QTA disposition without joining that additional statutory rationale. Stevens and Souter fully join while separately addressing distinct doctrines the Court does not decide.

**Controlling explanation:** Irwin supplies a rebuttable equitable presumption, not an additional interval regardless of the enacted scheme. The QTA already accounts for lack of knowledge through its accrual rule. Twelve years to act after actual or constructive notice, together with the special need for predictable land-title repose, rebuts additional nonstatutory tolling of the kind asserted.

Brockamp requires examination of the particular statute's operation; its tax clauses are not imported here. Beach likewise demands provision-specific analysis without converting the QTA period into the separate substantive-right expiration rule governing rescission there. Morgan Stanley and Plaut authorize their defined legislative reinstatement procedure, not a general suspension of this statutory clock.

The claimants knew the adverse claim by 1979 at the latest; the lower court used 1976. Either date places the June 1, 1994 action beyond twelve years. Continued searching and the 1991 discovery neither move accrual nor authorize tolling from the original search until the favorable document emerged. The result needs no adverse diligence or affirmative bad-faith finding.

The Court does not classify every QTA timing provision as jurisdictional, decide unpresented special claimant categories or create a universal rule against every doctrine called equitable. A materially different fraudulent-concealment or equitable-estoppel question is not resolved. No such doctrine is found applicable here, and no new hearing or amendment entitlement follows from leaving it open.

##### 4. Correction, title and monetary relief require separate authority

**Holding and operative rule:** Rejection of both asserted routes removes the appellate title award and leaves the 1982 consent judgment operative, so §2409a(b)'s possession-or-compensation option is not activated. Bounded corrective authority supplies no new district-court Tucker Act jurisdiction or amendment entitlement for the inverse-condemnation claim exceeding $10,000, and no repayment, second payment or compensation valuation is ordered.

**Authority:** Part IV's complete remedial rationale has the eight Associate Justices. Stone-Zsela joins removal of the title award, preservation of the consent judgment and the resulting nonactivation of the possession-or-compensation option; he agrees only in disposition on the separate monetary-jurisdiction route and adds no ground there.

**Controlling explanation:** Nordic Village and Department of Energy v. Ohio require consent to the particular affirmative monetary remedy. Neither an equitable label nor authority over an existing decree creates that consent or enlarges district-court Tucker Act jurisdiction. The proposed amendment cannot obtain it merely by attaching a compensation demand to this unsuccessful corrective action.

The lower court's possession-or-compensation option depended on its title award, which no longer stands. Even permission to reopen would not itself establish the disputed grant's validity or a complete title chain; after rejection of the actual routes, no alternative title trial or valuation is required. The 1975 canceled agreement and the payment under the 1982 settlement remain distinct. The Court creates no obligation to repay that settlement, pay it again or recognize a new recovery forum. A genuinely distinct permitted claim remains unadjudicated, not established.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Pacific Railroad v. Missouri Pacific Railway:** Applied to the ancillary character of an independent action correcting the same court's decree; it supplies no general new-claim jurisdiction.
- **Kokkonen v. Guardian Life Insurance Co., May 16, 1994:** Its separate contract-enforcement limit is distinguished and its reopening reservation respected.
- **Peacock v. Thomas, February 21, 1996:** Distinguished at the boundary between decree effectuation and a new substantive obligation; same-decree correction is not foreclosed.
- **United States v. Nordic Village, February 25, 1992:** Applied to preserve the need for consent to the particular affirmative monetary remedy.
- **United States Department of Energy v. Ohio, April 21, 1992:** Applied to the same remedy-specific consent requirement; an equitable label does not create monetary liability.
- **Hazel-Atlas Glass Co. v. Hartford-Empire Co.:** Applied for exceptional relief against grave injustice, without making every later evidentiary discovery sufficient.
- **Marshall v. Holmes:** Its alleged forged-document circumstances illustrate extraordinary equitable relief, without imposing universal intentional forgery as an element.
- **Bankers Mortgage Co. v. United States:** Its traditional independent-action conditions are preserved within the demanding extraordinary-relief threshold.
- **National Surety Co. v. State Bank:** Its traditional equitable conditions are preserved without separate adverse factual findings on each condition.

#### Law After Decision

A court may consider a traditional independent attack on the very decree it entered, but relief requires a grave miscarriage of justice. Power, extraordinary entitlement, statutory title authority and monetary consent remain separate. The QTA period for this private claim begins with notice of the Government's adverse claim, and the asserted additional equitable tolling cannot extend it until favorable title evidence is found. The 1982 settlement remains operative. No general archive-search immunity, definitive concealment or estoppel exception, new title chain or alternative recovery right is established.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stevens, joined by Souter, fully joins the Court. He distinguishes additional equitable tolling from fraudulent concealment or equitable estoppel. Outrageous governmental misconduct that prevents a person already aware of the Government's adverse claim from discovering her own claim might conceivably present one of those distinct doctrines. That possibility does not replace or qualify their assent to the statutory tolling rule adopted today.

The concurrence neither recognizes a definitive exception nor finds this hypothetical established. It makes no fraud finding, grants no estoppel, postpones no accrual and orders no hearing or amendment. The other Justices' adoption of the Court's bounded statutory ground likewise does not decide that distinct hypothetical adversely. The present reversal and preservation of the settlement remain unanimous.
