## Camilo-Robles v. Hoyos

### Corrected review posture and components

The First Circuit's June 29, 1998 judgment **affirmed** the district court's denial of four summary-judgment motions brought by five appellants. The docket span is Nos. 97-2260, 97-2261, 97-2262 and 97-2264. It awarded ordinary appellate costs to the appellee. The Supreme Court review is of that circuit judgment, not an unexplained direct review of a trial-court denial. The appeal preserves both the psychiatrists' claimed Puerto Rico statutory immunity and the five appellants' federal qualified-immunity positions; its federal-contractor eligibility discussion is a distinct issue, not proof that state action automatically confers immunity.

The underlying claims allege unreasonable arrest/detention and police violence under §1983, attributable to the appellants' own culpable participation through evaluations, rearming or deployment. The arrest/force facts implicate the Fourth Amendment as applied to state officials through the Fourteenth. Do not reproduce the lower court's suggestion that the Fifth Amendment constrains Puerto Rico police through the Fourteenth. Whether a genuinely distinct substantive-due-process claim is preserved, and whether any later custody stage requires a different source, must be identified before adopting a separate constitutional holding. *Graham* supplies the arrest-force framework; the abstract right to bodily security alone does not resolve the supervisory notice inquiry.

The district court set its schedule February 21, 1996; summary-judgment motions followed in December. Camilo-Robles opposed the psychiatrists on the merits and sought Rule 56(f) time as to the police officials, citing obstructed discovery. The court granted the extension without a time limit and referred discovery disputes. The magistrate first ruled August 26, 1997; the district judge denied summary judgment two days later in a two-page order before receiving all oppositions. The circuit reconstructed likely plaintiff-favorable assumptions from material already in the trial record, including the joint psychiatric motion, its opposition, other defendants' submissions and earlier evidence. It expressly excluded extra-record material referenced on appeal. Lack of an opposition was neither default nor an evidentiary admission. A clarification remand remained available if the assumptions could not responsibly be identified; factual sufficiency cannot be relabeled a legal issue.

### Supported factual account and individual attribution

On the favorable account, on May 13, 1994, Officer Miguel Diaz-Martinez tried to park in the judges' area at the Bayamon Judicial Center. Security guard Grancid Camilo-Robles told him not to. Diaz-Martinez put a hand on his gun, arrested and handcuffed him, shoved him into the cruiser, punched his stomach and slapped his face while taking him to the station, and detained him after removing belt and shoes. He was released uncharged approximately three hours later. These are summary-judgment assumptions, not a verdict.

Diaz-Martinez's reported history included at least eighteen disciplinary infractions involving violent/threatening conduct; an August 1989 incident in which he assaulted his wife, took a police shotgun, and held officers including the acting superintendent hostage; involuntary hospitalization and a schizophrenia diagnosis; a recommendation for civilian work; suspension in 1990 and expulsion in 1991. Following a successful appeal he was reinstated in May 1993. He assaulted a civilian on desk duty, received unrestricted psychiatric clearance in August 1993, and after rearming shot two unarmed residents on September 8, killing one. His weapon was taken away; he returned to desk work in November. On January 20, 1994 he threatened to kill another officer; he transferred to CIC six days later. On February 28 the psychiatrists again cleared him for unrestricted armed duty. The later August 1994 expulsion, manslaughter plea and sentence are subsequent history, not notice the appellants possessed before Camilo-Robles's injury. Mental illness alone does not establish dangerousness, constitutional culpability or liability; the concrete history and the defendant's actual information do the work.

### Distinct defenses and lawful alternatives

**Puerto Rico statute.** The psychiatrists invoked P.R. Laws Ann. tit. 26, §4105 (1994), which protects a health professional acting as a Commonwealth/dependency/instrumentality/municipal employee in the performance of professional duties against damages litigation for malpractice. Their contract purported to invoke the provision (misnumbering it §5105), while describing them as independent contractors. The lower court identified unresolved employee-status facts and held separately that a state immunity cannot defeat §1983 under *Martinez v. California*, 444 U.S. 277, 284 n.8 (1980). It did **not** decide §4105's effect on pendent Puerto Rico claims. This statutory defense, federal immunity and any state claims must receive separate treatment. No general federal absolute immunity for medical evaluation is established.

**Contractor eligibility.** The lower court treated contractual integration in police evaluations as state action and extended qualified-immunity eligibility. Current *Richardson*, June 23, 1997, separates state attribution, eligibility and a possible independent good-faith defense. Its commercial-prison holding expressly reserves brief assistance, adjunct service, close official supervision and governmental integration. One lawful approach assumes eligibility arguendo and resolves the particular legal immunity question; another reaches preserved eligibility through the actual historical/functional record; a third returns an inadequately supported eligibility issue without declaring either all contractors immune or none immune. The reported record establishes a professional contract and recurring evaluations, but not market competition, full-time commercial prison management or all terms of official supervision. Those features must not be invented. A separate private good-faith defense remains undecided under Richardson and cannot silently replace immunity.

**Culpability and notice.** *Doe* (October 16, 1995) supplies a current, bounded school-authority knowing-acquiescence rule; *Farmer* (June 6, 1994) supplies actual-awareness, permissible circumstantial inference and reasonable-response principles in prison conditions. Neither licenses a general constructive-knowledge supervisory rule, neither retrospectively gives these defendants notice for pre-May 13, 1994 conduct, and neither may be transplanted mechanically. Current *Chipman* reinforces individual awareness and genuine opportunity without adopting a general failure-to-protect rule. The lower opinion's actual-or-constructive formulation must therefore be separated from the actual-knowledge facts it identifies.

The strongest claimant path is a narrow application to each defendant's own admitted warning information, practical authority and causally effective decision or omission, preserving proof and later defenses. The counterargument is that even serious known danger does not establish the exact federal supervisory/professional obligation at the relevant conduct date, that medical judgment or reasonable reliance could be mistaken without deliberate disregard, and that rearming/public deployment must be connected to this seizure and beating. The strongest immunity path is individualized notice or objectively reasonable response on the proper assumptions; its counterargument is that no factual twin is required and an officer's known repeated violence plus direct rearming/deployment powers may make the particular conduct plainly unlawful. A narrower jurisdictional path dismisses a purely factual-sufficiency portion or seeks clarification where assumptions cannot be reconstructed. None permits choosing the defense account, pooling knowledge, finding liability from immunity denial, or commanding a trial regardless of remaining legal defenses.
