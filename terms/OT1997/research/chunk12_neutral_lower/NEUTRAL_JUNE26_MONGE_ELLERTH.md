## Monge v. California - No.97-6146

Argued April28,1998; decision-event anchor June26,1998. Review of the California Supreme Court's judgment permitting renewed proof of the serious-prior-felony allegation, 16 Cal.4th826,941P.2d1121(1997). The precise question is whether double jeopardy bars another attempt after the first record failed to establish the prior conviction's necessary personal-conduct qualification. The ordinary drug convictions and the separate prior-prison-term enhancement are not merged into that question.

**Record and burdens.** The information gave notice of both enhancement allegations; Monge waived a jury on them. California supplied trial-like safeguards and required proof beyond a reasonable doubt. The state conceded insufficiency of the first proof of personal use/infliction, and the prosecutor's assertion about a stick is not missing evidence. The prison record proved assault conviction/imprisonment, not necessarily the personal use of a deadly weapon or personal infliction of great bodily injury required to make that prior serious under the provision at issue. A prior conviction itself may remain valid while its necessary classification for another statute is unproved. The five-year selected term, seven-year unenhanced maximum, fourteen-year enhanced maximum, actual doubling and independent one-year enhancement must remain separate.

**Current law checked.** Caspari is a habeas new-rule/finality decision expressly reserving the constitutional merits on direct review; it does not predecide Monge against either side. Witte permits consideration of relevant conduct within the conviction-authorized range and expressly reserves maximum-increasing facts. The actual Almendarez-Torres holding treats section1326(b)(2)'s prior conviction as an aggravated-offense element, with indictment/proof consequences, but has no majority general constitutional rule for all maximum-increasing facts and no general constitutional recidivism exception. Dixon retains Grady, yet same-offense protection matters only after classifying this proceeding as one to which jeopardy attaches. Hinds and Burks prohibit supplying missing prosecution proof through a second trial after actual insufficiency; the contested issue here is whether the recidivism proceeding receives that acquittal-like constitutional protection. Neither the word sentencing nor the trial-like procedure label by itself supplies an answer. Current Hudson retains its distinct sanctions framework; it does not automatically classify every enhancement as a second prosecution.

**Open lawful paths.** Examine the argument that a trial-like adjudication needed to authorize a higher maximum is sufficiently analogous to an acquittal to trigger Bullington/Rumsey and Burks, and the contrary argument that noncapital recidivism remains a sentencing-status determination under DiFrancesco and the traditional distinction between an offense acquittal and a sentencing finding. Bullington's capital setting and its reasons both require attention; it should be neither erased nor automatically generalized. Compare the actual effect of the serious-felony finding, California's procedural safeguards and the separate prior-conviction/underlying-conduct questions. Use the actual Almendarez-Torres statutory scope without importing later sentencing law or turning one Justice's separate constitutional view into controlling law.

**Remedy.** A holding barring renewed proof must identify the affected enhancement and consequent lawful sentencing process rather than erase the drug convictions or assume the independent prison-term enhancement invalid. A holding allowing renewed proof creates no true finding, automatic doubling, conviction of a new offense or right to introduce an invented assault fact. A narrower lawful remand must specify what remains open and respect the conceded insufficiency of the first record. The actual personal conduct underlying the old assault is not known from the prison record. Its retrieval is unnecessary to answer whether the State may try to prove it again; if a proposed disposition instead finds that conduct, the actual record is required.

## Burlington Industries, Inc. v. Ellerth - No.97-569

Argued April22,1998; decision-event anchor June26,1998. Review of the Seventh Circuit en banc reversal of employer summary judgment, Jansen v. Packing Corp. of America,123F.3d490(1997). The reviewed issue is employer responsibility for supervisor harassment involving unfulfilled threats, where the employee neither submitted nor suffered the described tangible retaliation. The hostile-environment allegations are viewed favorably to Ellerth on employer summary judgment. The lower treatment of severe/pervasive conduct is not a new Supreme Court factfinding.

**Record boundaries.** Slowik's actual authority to make hiring/promotion decisions was subject to approval/signature above him; he was not alleged to be the company's alter ego. The threat during an interview accompanied an actual promotion, not a denied promotion. The later alleged logo-related threat concerned obtaining work authorization. Both supported delegated power and its actual use matter; title alone is insufficient. Ellerth knew a policy existed but did not complain to higher authority during employment. She first resigned for other stated reasons and later attributed departure to harassment. Those facts do not conclusively establish an effective policy, an unreasonable failure to report, a constructive discharge or any defense. The separately dismissed constructive-discharge claim is not automatically revived by general employer-attribution review; preservation must be established before deciding it. No party relied on an alter-ego or nondelegable-duty theory.

**Current entering law controls.** Andrade, Faragher and Harrison already require the plaintiff to prove by a preponderance that actual delegated supervisory power materially aided creation or maintenance of an actionable hostile environment. Higher-management notice, hire/fire authority, an express threat, completed personnel action or economic loss is not invariably necessary; rank, employment or proximity alone is insufficient. Ordinary scope, negligence and apparent authority remain distinct. Harrison gives apparent authority its own employer-attributable manifestation and reasonable-reliance requirements; those are not extra elements of actual-authority attribution. Harris/DeAngelis and Oncale preserve separate objective/subjective severe-or-pervasive environment and discriminatory-causation requirements. The lower court's notice-only ground cannot be treated as the sole available attribution rule.

**Defense reservation.** Faragher rejected the ineffective-policy/noncomplaint contention on its established facts, while explicitly leaving open whether genuinely effective protection and unreasonable employee nonuse could supply a complete defense. Harrison preserved that reservation, including the elements, burdens, relation to tangible employment action and distinct relief limitations. This Court has NOT already adopted a universal two-element affirmative defense, a tangible-action exception to such a defense, a new burden shift, a reporting duty, a policy-publication duty or automatic policy immunity. Nor did it categorically reject every defense. Whether to resolve an appropriately presented part of that reserved question here is a lawful issue for modeling, not a premise fixed by the historical case title.

**Open lawful paths.** The Court may apply the existing material-aid rule to the plaintiff-favorable record and determine the necessary remand, examine a properly presented reserved defense without contradicting the existing attribution rule, or resolve a narrower supported question. Analyze whether actual authority made threats effective in the employment setting, not simply whether threats were fulfilled. Compare the employer's agency/avoidability argument and the employee's argument that private purposes, a written prohibition and lack of management notice do not erase material aid. If a newly defined defense is adopted, its source, trigger, both parties' burdens, relation to any tangible action and application to this undeveloped record must all be stated; the packet presumes none. No allegation proves either party's complete case as a matter of law.

**Remedy and source limits.** Reversing a notice-only summary judgment need not direct liability, damages, trial or a second agency factfinding proceeding; ordinary lower-court resolution on supported independent grounds remains possible. Affirming a defendant's legal defense requires its actual source and sufficient supported facts, not a presumption that every known written policy is effective. The complete policy and underlying appendix/briefs were not recovered by control. A finding about exact reporting routes, prevention/reasonable-care practices, employee nonuse, constructive-discharge preservation or a factual concession beyond the recitation requires the corresponding source. Missing source files are not litigation omissions. Conduct was1993-94; the 1991 Act's new-remedy temporal bar in Landgraf is not automatically triggered, but damages/fees/jury entitlement have independent conditions and are not decided merely by attribution.

## Monge v. California — No. 97-6146

Source: [official report, 524 U.S. 721](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-524/pdf/USREPORTS-524-721.pdf), factual and lower-court account at 724–727; statutory sentencing ranges at 729. Raw PDF and full text are `USREPORTS-524-721.pdf` and `.txt` here; control-only originals.

- The jury convicted Monge of using a minor to sell marijuana, selling or transporting marijuana, and possessing marijuana for sale, under California Health and Safety Code §§11361(a), 11360(a), and 11359. The information also gave notice of two sentence-enhancement allegations: a prior assault conviction and a prison term served for it. The challenged prior conviction's serious-felony qualification and the separate prior-prison-term enhancement must not be collapsed.
- California's three-strikes provisions double the term for a current felony when there is one qualifying prior serious felony, and prescribe 25 years to life when there are two. An assault qualifies under the provisions at issue if the defendant personally used a dangerous or deadly weapon or personally inflicted great bodily injury. A generic assault-with-a-deadly-weapon conviction does not alone establish that personal-conduct requirement. California afforded a jury, confrontation, self-incrimination protection, rules of evidence, and proof beyond a reasonable doubt for the allegations.
- Monge waived a jury on the enhancement allegations and obtained bifurcation. At the subsequent bench proceeding, the prosecutor asserted personal use of a stick but introduced only a prison record showing conviction for assault with a deadly weapon and imprisonment. The personal-use assertion was not itself the missing evidentiary proof. The trial judge found both allegations true and imposed 11 years: a five-year sentence on count one doubled to ten, plus one year for the prior prison term. A three-year sentence on count two was stayed; a two-year sentence on count three ran concurrently.
- The Court of Appeal requested supplemental briefing on evidentiary sufficiency. The State conceded that the sentencing record failed to prove beyond a reasonable doubt personal use of a weapon or infliction of great bodily injury, and requested a new opportunity on remand. The appellate court found the evidence insufficient and held retrial barred by double jeopardy. The California Supreme Court reversed the double-jeopardy ruling. 16 Cal.4th 826, 941 P.2d 1121 (1997). Its judgment allowed another attempt to prove the prior-conviction allegation; it did not erase the conceded insufficiency of the first record.
- The published account gives the first-offender maximum for count one as seven years and the one-prior qualifying maximum as fourteen years. The actual sentence selected before doubling was five years. Keep the authorized maximum, selected term, and separate one-year enhancement distinct.

Source limit: the appendix prison record and complete California opinions were not separately recovered. The reproduced account establishes the State's concession, evidence introduced, and disposition below without requiring an invented new fact about the underlying assault. Any later attempt to identify the assault's actual personal conduct would require the underlying record; it is not supplied by the prosecutor's assertion.

# Burlington Industries v. Ellerth: factual source extraction

Objective facts and lower-court posture only. Historical Supreme Court reasoning, result and lineup are excluded. Source: fully read official [524 U.S. 742-774](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-524/pdf/USREPORTS-524-742.pdf), including every writing and footnote. Raw source must be withheld from independent modeling. Fresh neutral validation required. Evidentiary cutoff: June 26, 1998.

* Ellerth worked as a Chicago salesperson from March 1993 until May 1994. Slowik, a vice president within a business unit, was a midlevel manager above her immediate supervisor. He could make hiring/promotion decisions subject to his superior's approval and signature; the record described him as outside the upper policymaking hierarchy. (747.)
* Ellerth alleged repeated sexual comments/gestures, and three episodes involving threats associated with job benefits: a business-trip lounge conversation; remarks and touching during a promotion interview followed by an actual promotion; and sexual remarks while she sought permission to use a customer logo in a sample. She rejected his advances. The threats were not carried out through a denial of promotion or other tangible retaliation. Because the employer won summary judgment, the allegations were taken favorably to her for review; they are not trial findings. (747-749.)
* After her immediate supervisor criticized delayed customer callbacks, Ellerth resigned with a letter stating reasons unrelated to sexual harassment. About three weeks later she sent a letter attributing her resignation to Slowik. She knew Burlington had an antiharassment policy but did not report Slowik to an authority during employment. She once told Slowik a remark was inappropriate. (748-749.) Those reported facts do not by themselves resolve the legal reasonableness of her conduct or Burlington's prevention efforts.
* After an EEOC right-to-sue letter, she filed in the Northern District of Illinois in October 1994. The district court found the alleged conduct sufficiently severe/pervasive to create a hostile environment, but entered summary judgment for Burlington because there was no triable evidence that it knew or should have known of the harassment. It noted the unused complaint procedures and separately dismissed constructive discharge. 912 F. Supp. 1101 (N.D. Ill. 1996). (749.)
* The Seventh Circuit en banc reversed the summary judgment in Jansen v. Packing Corp. of America, 123 F.3d 490 (1997). Eight opinions disclosed disagreement on attribution of supervisor conduct to an employer; the dispute on appeal was vicarious liability rather than proof of independent employer negligence. Some judges treated unfulfilled threats as sufficient; others required a company act or employer negligence. (749-751.) These are lower-court approaches, not source-established statutory rules.
* The petition's quoted question asks whether Title VII permits the claim where the employee neither submitted to the sexual advances nor suffered tangible effects in compensation, terms, conditions or privileges as a consequence of refusing. (753, quoting petition i.) No party argued that Slowik's rank made him the employer's alter ego or that a nondelegable duty supplied liability. (758.)
