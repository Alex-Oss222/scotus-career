ARGUMENT
In opposing review respondent attempts to support the deci-
sion below by ignoring traditional rules of statutory construc-
tion and proposing a strained reading of § (e)(7) of the Privacy
Act (“Act”) which is unrealistic and contrary to Congress’ in-
tent. Respondent then argues against a proper construction of
the Act by recasting the legitimate requirements of § (e)(7) into
imaginary and unwarranted burdens. Finally, the FBI miscon-
strues both the facts and the reasoning of the Seventh Circuit’s
opposing precedent, Becker v. I.R.S., 34 F.3d 398 (7th Cir. 1994).
1. As a preliminary matter, the government has misrepre-
sented petitioner’s position as allowing the maintenance of First
Amendment information only if there is “‘an active or ongoing
investigation.” Brief for the Respondent In Opposition, 7 (““Opp.
Cert. __”). That is not the case. What the Privacy Act does
demand, as stated by Circuit Judge Tatel, is a “present, articulable
use” for keeping this sensitive and constitutionally chilling in-
formation. Pet. App. 15a. It is precisely this showing that the
FBI is unwilling and apparently unable to make.
According to the FBI, the requirements of § (e)(7) are satis-
fied as long as it can show that information describing an
individual’s First Amendment activities was pertinent when col-
lected, no matter how long ago collected or how irrelevant to
present concerns of national security. Though petitioner ac-
knowledges that § (e) (7)’s concept of authorized law enforce-
ment activity may not be limited to an active or ongoing investi-
gation, he vigorously asserts that § (e)(7) requires an agency to
show that keeping First Amendment-related information matters
to the agency, and the country, now.
2. Respondent refuses to address any of the applicable rules
of statutory construction. Nor does it address Congress’ ex-
plicit language in the preamble of the Act which prohibits fed-
eral agencies from collecting or keeping information “in a man-


[[PDF PAGE 7 OF 11; reply 3]]

ner” that is contrary to the Act. This language makes clear that
Congress was concerned with how agencies keep First Amend-
ment information, as well as how they collect it. Pet. App. 16-
17. This explicit proscription in the preamble is directly at
odds with the appeals court’s conclusion that § (e)(7) requires
only that information be pertinent and not that keeping such in-
formation be pertinent.
Respondent argues only that the decision of the court of ap-
peals did not render the definition of “maintain” superfluous,
because § (e)(7) “makes clear that an agency’s retention of those
records is a continuing Privacy Act violation.” Opp. Cert. 8-9.
But, this is an unrealistic and implausible purpose for the lan-
guage at issue. It requires Congress to have felt the need to
inform federal agencies like the FBI that they must not keep
information they unlawfully collected. To say the least, such a
construction imputes a sorry lack of common sense to federal
agencies. And to the extent that it does not impute such igno-
rance, the government’s reading is entirely impractical in in-
structing agencies to do what they already must do. See, e.g.,
Peters v. Hobby, 349 U.S. 331, 348-49 (1955)(ordering
expungement of Loyalty Review Board findings from federal
employee’s personnel records on showing that Board acted with-
out jurisdiction).'
3. The government erroneously asserts that petitioner’s ap-
proach would result in “severe” administrative burdens on law
enforcement agencies by requiring agencies “continually to re-
view” records for pertinence, and, as part of any such review,
“to consult with all of [their] field offices and with other law
enforcement and investigative agencies.” Opp. Cert. 11.
' Neither does the government's reliance on the Federal Records Act of 1950, 44
U.S.C. § 3101 et seg. (Opp. Cert. 8 n.5), provide support for its interpretation of
§ (e)(7). The Federal Records Act requires only that records be destroyed in compli-
ance with regulations promulgated by the Archivist. See 44 U.S.C. § 3302. Nothing
in the Federal Records Act requires the indefinite retention of information on Ameri-
cans’ First Amendment activities.

But petitioner does not seek “continual review” by federal
agencies. To the contrary, there are a number of administra-
tively convenient occasions when agencies can easily comply
with their § (e)(7) duties. At the time of closing an investiga-
tion, for example, it would not be unduly burdensome to deter-
mine the need to keep information relating to First Amendment
activities. Similarly, reviewing for pertinence when process-
ing a Privacy Act request would be no undue burden. Under any
such request, an agency is required by the Act to notify its local
offices—at least to find out if files on the requester are held in
any of them. In addition, the agency already must inspect any
files on the requester in great detail in order to determine what
materials it will release. Indeed, government submissions to
the district court in this very case show that at least two agents
reviewed petitioner’s files in their entirety. [J.A. 59, 96] The
government's assertion that the burden involved in doing a similar
evaluation as to the pertinency of First Amendment information
at the time of the request is consequently insupportable.
4. The government continues to rely on its “mosaic” theory.
It argues that, due to the special needs present in intelligence
operations, agencies like the FBI must be able to retain infor-
mation even when they cannot show the current pertinence of
the information. Opp. Cert. 10. This effectively means that the
FBI must be permitted to forever retain every single intelli-
gence file it compiles, even ones describing First Amendment
activities, no matter how innocent the information, because the
FBI can never be sure that the information will not prove valu-
able in the future.”
2 The government cites C./.A. v. Sims, 471 U.S. 159 (1985) in support of this
position. Opp. Cert. 10. But the case is of no relevance here. Sims is a FOIA case
concerning whether to make publicly available, information concerning intelligence
sources—information that the CIA was concemed might fall into the hands of foreign
agents. There is nothing inthe Court’s opinion that provides meaningful support for the
FBI’s apparent practice
of retaining any information itpleases—including information
describing the First Amendment activities of U.S. citizens—regardless of how irrel-
evant to any present interests.

The FBI’s own conduct, however, belies this assertion. For
example, at issue in Patterson by Patterson v. F.B.1., 893 F.2d
595 (3d Cir. 1990), was a name-retrievable file describing cer-
tain of appellant’s First Amendment activities. The court indi-
cated that the FBI had offered, as late as at oral argument, to
expunge appellant’s name from its records. /d. at 598 & n.5.
Apparently, the FBI was quite able to make a determination that
there was no need, in the case of a particular person, to con-
tinue to maintain a name-retrievable file.
Obviously, making such a determination did not impose a daunt-
ing substantive burden on the FBI. It is very hard indeed to see
how making such an individualized determination regarding
petitioner’s records would be any harder. This is all petitioner
would have the agency do as a condition of retaining informa-
tion about his lawful and protected associational activities.
5. The government asserts that the decision in MacArthur
does not conflict with Becker v. ILR.S., 34 F.3d 398, 407-09 (7th
Cir. 1994), because “the factual setting addressed [in Becker]
is markedly different from that at issue here.” Opp. Cert. 12.
This is incorrect.
In the first place, the government attempts to distinguish Becker
on the ground that the Becker court never “squarely address[ed]
the question whether the files in question had ever been perti-
nent to an authorized law enforcement activity,” (Opp. Cert. 12-
13), while Lindblom has conceded the propriety of the collec-
tion of his record. But even a cursory reading of the Becker
opinion reveals that the court resolved the point in issue on the
basis that the Beckers’ files were pertinent at the time of collec-
tion. As the reviewing court recounted, the district court had
made such a finding. 34 F.3d at 408. And the Becker court’s
criticism of the district court was not with this finding, but rather
with its inference that “maintenance”—i.e., retention—of the
files was thereby justified. Id. The court essentially reasoned
that itdoesn’t follow from the assumed pertinence of the files at
the time of collection that the retention of the files was justified.


[[PDF PAGE 10 OF 11; reply 6]]

Id. at 408-09.
In the second place, the government tries to distinguish Becker
on the basis that the case did not involve counterintelligence
operations while MacArthur does. Opp. Cert. 12. But if this
makes a difference in the applicability of § (e)(7), then the JRS
is obliged under § (e)(7) to independently justify the retention
of properly collected records, but agencies (like the FBI) that
are engaged in counterintelligence operations are not. It is clear,
however, that this distinction cannot possibly be extracted from
§ (e)(7), a statute that is addressed quite simply to every agency
that maintains a system of records.
In short, neither of the government’s asserted distinctions be-
tween MacArthur and Becker does away with a clear circuit
split on what § (e)(7) requires. The truth of the matter is this:
the Seventh Circuit requires federal agencies to independently
justify the retention of First Amendment information that they
have properly collected, while the District of Columbia Circuit
does not.
