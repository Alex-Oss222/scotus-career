JURISDICTION 


On November 4, 1996, the court of appeals denied 
Mr. Hohn a certificate of appealability, and on February 
10, 1997 denied his petition for rehearing. On May 12, 
1997, Mr. Hofn filed a petition for a writ of certiorari, 
trying to invoke jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. 1254(1). 
Because no judge or justice has granted him leave to 
appeal, as required under 28 U.S.C. 2253(c)(1), because 
his case was never “in” that court, as required under 
§ 1254(1), and because his case does not otherwise meet 
the requirements of 28 U.S.C. 1651, he may not rely on 
either the statutory or common law writ. The Court 
accordingly lacks jurisdiction to grant certiorari, vacate, 
and remand the case, and should dismiss the petition.

STATEMENT 


This case concerns the one aspect of the Court's 
jurisprudence that perhaps rivals the importance of its 
authority to engage in judicial review, namely the sensi- 
tive determination of when such power exists. At issue 
are three lines of federal legislation regarding the Court's 
jurisdiction —- one concerning its power to review habeas 
corpus claims, another addressing its authority to exer- 
cise appellate jurisdiction through a writ of certiorari, the 


last addressing its power under the All Writs Act. 
Because the issues raised by Mr. Hohn’s certiorari peti- 
tion implicate all three pieces of legislation and because 
the background of each law bears on this dispute, it is 
useful at the outset to set forth the history of the three 
statutes as well as the circumstances that make them 
relevant to Mr. Hohn. 


A. The Gatekeeping Requirement for Habeas 
Corpus Appeals. 


Since the turn of the century, Congress has limited 
the rights of habeas corpus claimants to seek appellate 
review of their claims. “In 1908, concerned with the 
increasing number of frivolous habeas corpus petitions 
challenging capital sentences which delayed execution 
pending completion of the appellate process, Congress 
inserted the requirement that a prisoner first obtain a 
certificate of probable cause to appeal before being enti- 
tled to do so.” Barefoot v. Estelle, 463 U.S. 880, 893 n.3 
(1983) (citation omitted). To this end, Congress enacted 
the following requirement: 


That from a final decision by a court of the 
United States in a proceeding in habeas corpus 
where the detention complained of is by virtue 
of process issued out of a State court no appeal to 
the Supreme Ccurt shall be allowed unless the 
United States court by which the final decision was 
rendered or a justice of the Supreme Court shall be of 
opinion that there exists probable cause for an 
appeal, in which event, on allowing the same, the 
said court or justice shall certify that there is 
probable cause for such allowance. 


Act of March 10, 1908, ch. 76, 35 Stat. 40 (emphasis 
added). 


Entitled “An Act Restricting in Certain Cases the 
Right of Appeal to the Supreme Court in Habeas Corpus 
Proceedings,” the statute had a straightforward goal. It 
sought to make “it impossible to continue th[e] vicious 
practice” of delaying the execution of sentences and 
undermining the finality of criminal sentences, “as under 
it no appeal can be prosecuted unless either the United 
States court making the final decision or a justice of the 
Supreme Court shall be of the opinion that there exists 
probable cause for such appeal.” H.R. Rep. No. 23, 60th 
Cong., 1st Sess., 2 (1908); 42 Cong. Rec. 608-09 (1908). 


In 1925, while enacting legislation designed to curb 
the Court’s obligatory jurisdiction, Congress modified the 
gatekeeping requirement. Instead of allowing unsuccess- 
ful habeas claimants to seek leave to appeal from a justice 
of the Court, the amendment allowed applicants only to 
seek leave from the district court judge who denied the 
claim or from a judge of the court of appeals. Act of 
February 13, 1925, ch. 229, §§ 6(d), 13, 43 Stat. 940, 942; 28 
U.S.C. 466; Appendix. 


Twenty-three years later, Congress amended the stat- 
ute again, this time giving the habeas claimant three 
sources for obtaining a certificate of probable cause — the 
district court, the court of appeals or the Supreme Court. 
Accordingly, starting in 1948, the statute provided that 
“an appeal may not be taken to the court of appeals . . . 
unless the justice or judge who rendered the order or a 
circuit justice or judge issues a certificate of probable 


cause.” Act of June 25, 1948, ch. 646, § 2253, 62 Stat. 967, 
28 U.S.C. 2253; Appendix. 


Finally, in 1996, Congress reenforced the leave-to- 
appeal requirement with passage of The Anti-Terrorism 
and Effective Death Penalty Act (the “Act” or “AEDPA”). 
The legislature gave the gatekeeping requirement a more 
explicit name (a “certifieate of appealability”), extended 
the requirement to habeas claims brought by federal pris- 
oners under § 2255, and required all claimants (federal or 
State) to demonstrate “a substantial showing of the denial 
of a constitutional right.” In its current form, the require- 
ment thus provides that “an appeal may not be taken” 
“[ujnless a circuit justice or judge issues a certificate of 
appealability.” 28 U.S.C. 2253(c)(1). 


B. The Certiorari Statute. 


In contrast to the gatekeeping requirement, the story 
of congressional legislation regarding the Court's cer- 
tiorari jurisdiction has primarily been one of continuity, 
not change. Congress first vested the Court with cer- 
tiorari power to review federal courts of appeals’ deci- 
sions in 1891. In the same Act that established these 
appellate courts, Congress gave the Court the following 
power: 


And excepting also that in any such case as 
is hereinbefore made final in the circuit court of 
appeals it shall be competent for the Supreme 
Court to require, by certiorari or otherwise, any 
such case to be certified to the Supreme Court 
for its review and determination with the same 
power and authority in the case as if it had been 


carried by appeal or writ of error to the 
Supreme Court. 


Act of March 3, 1891, ch. 517, § 6, 26 Stat. 828. 


After making a slight modification to the statute in 
1911, see Appendix, Congress enacted legislation in 1925 
designed “to curtail the Court’s obligatory jurisdiction by 
substituting, for the appeal as of right, discretionary 
review by certiorari in many classes of cases.” Ex Parte 
Peru, 318 U.S. 578, 585 n.4 (1942). In doing so, Congress 
made clear that the Court’s certiorari power extends to 
“any case, civil or criminal, in a circuit court of appeals.” 
Act of February 13, 1925, ch. 229, § 240(a), 43 Stat. 938; 
Appendix. 


Amendments to the provision in 1948 and again in 
1988 brought the provision to its current form, which 
now reads: 


Cases in the courts of appeals may be 
reviewed by the Supreme Court by .. . writ of 
certiorari granted upon the petition of any party 
to any civil or criminal case, before or after 
rendition of judgment or decree. 


28 U.S.C. 1254(1) (emphasis added). 


Cc. The All Writs Act. 


The All Writs Act has long been a constant on the 
judicial landscape. First enacted as part of the Judiciary 
Act of 1789, the “extraordinary writ” has always required 
at a minimum some form of necessity before it could be 
deployed. See Pennsylvania Bureau of Correction v. United 


States Marshals Service, 474 U.S. 34, 43 (1985). In its initial 
form, the law provided as follows: 


That all the before-mentioned courts of the 
United States shall have power to issue writs of 
scire facias [to enforce a judgment], habeas 
corpus, and all other writs not specially pro- 
vided for by statute, which may be necessary for 
the exercise of their respective jurisdictions, and 
agreeable to the principles and usages of law. 


Judiciary Act of 1789, § 14, 1 Stat. 81. 


Several modest changes to the provision occurred 
over the next 120 years, the primary one being the sever- 
ance of the writ of habeas corpus from the All Writs Act. 
See Carbo v. United States, 364 U.S. 611, 615 & n.6 (1961). 
Congress did not modify the provision again until 1911, 
when it altered the statute to provide: 


The Supreme Court and the district courts 
shall have power to issue writs of scire facias. 
The Supreme Court, the circuit courts of 
appeals, and the district courts shall have power 
to issue all writs not specifically provided for by 
statute, which may be necessary for the exercise 
of their respective jurisdictions, and agreeable to 
the usages and principles of law. 


Act of March 3, 1911, ch. 231, § 262, 36 Stat. 1162. 


Finally, in 1948 (coupled with a minor alteration in 
1949), Congress amended the provision to its current 
form, which now reads: 


The Supreme Court and all courts estab- 
lished by Act of Congress may issue all writs 


necessary or appropriate in aid of their respec- 
tive jurisdictions and agreeable to the usages 
and principles of law. 


28 U.S.C. 1651(a); see Act of June 25, 1948, ch. 646, 62 Stat. 
944; Act of May 24, 1949, ch. 139, § 90, 63 Stat. 102. In 
making these changes, Congress merely “consolidated 
various provisions into § 1651 and made ‘necessary 
changes in phraseology’ without substantive amend- 
ment.” Pennsylvania Bureau of Correction v. U.S. Marshals, 
474 U.S. 34, 41 (1985) (quoting H.R. Rep. No. 308, 80th 
Cong., 1st Sess. A144). 


D. The Underlying Proceedings. 


These statutes, together with the history behind 
them, became relevant to Arnold Hohn when he chose to 
distribute methamphetamine from his home in Omaha, 
Nebraska. After learning from an informant that Mr. 
Hohn was selling drugs from his residence and that he 
always had at least one gun on his person, the police 
obtained a “no knock” warrant to search his house. On 
June 20, 1990, they conducted the search, which uncov- 
ered 16 grams of methamphetamine, drug paraphernalia, 
several weapons and money. The police found the drugs 
and three loaded weapons (two in holsters) within a few 
feet of each other on the kitchen counter. Elsewhere in the 
kitchen and the house, they found three other firearms, 
one of them loaded. J.A. 34, 43. 


The police promptly arrested Mr. Hohn. They 
charged him with using and carrying a firearm in relation 
to a drug offense under 18 U.S.C. 924(c)(1), possession 
with intent to distribute methamphetamine within 1000 


feet of a public school under 21 U.S.C. 845a and being a 
felon in possession of a firearm in violation of 18 U.S.C. 
922(g) and 924(a). 


A jury convicted Mr. Hohn on all three counts, and 
he was sentenced to 90 months in prison. He appealed to 
the Eighth Circuit, raising three claims — that the federal 
government violated the Speedy Trial Act, that his 
motion to suppress evidence from the search of his resi- 
dence should have been granted, and that the charge 
concerning drug distribution within the vicinity of a 
schoolyard should have been dismissed. J.A. 12-26. 
Nowhere in his appeal did he challenge the sufficiency of 
the evidence to support his § 924(c)(1) conviction for 
using or carrying a firearm in relation to a drug offense. 
Id. On November 8, 1993, the court of appeals affirmed. 
Id. 


On January 24, 1996, Mr. Hohn filed a motion in the 
United States District Court for the District of Nebraska 
to vacate, set aside or correct his sentence under 28 U.S.C. 
2255. Relying on this Court’s decision in Bailey v. United 
States, 116 S. Ct. 501 (1995), he argued that his conduct 
did not violate § 924(c)(1) because the government failed 
to show that he was “using” a firearm in the course of a 
drug transaction. In Bailey, the Court determined that 
“use” requires the government to prove “an active 
employment of the firearm by the defendant, a use that 
makes the firearm an operative factor in relation to the 
predicate offense.” Id. at 505. The decision overruled 
prior lower-court authority to the effect that “ ‘use’ may 
be inferred from the availability and presence of the 
firearm.” United States v. Jones, 990 F.2d 1047, 1050 (8th 


Cir. 1993), cert. denied, 510 U.S. 1048 (1994), denial of habeas 
corpus aff'd, 39 F.3d 1184 (8th Cir. 1994). 


On April 24, 1996, while the district court was con- 
sidering Mr. Hohn’s § 2255 petition, the President signed 
into law the Anti-Terrorism and Effective Death Penalty 
Act. The new law modified the process for appealing a 
§ 2255 motion, requiring the inmate to obtain a “certifi- 
cate of appealability” as a prerequisite to an “appeal- 

.. from... the final order in a proceeding under section 
2255.” 28 U. S.C. 2253(c)(1). Such a certificate may issue, 
the Act provided, only upon a “substantial showing of 
the denial of a constitutional right.” 28 U.S.C. 2253(c)(2). 


On July 18, 1996, the district court rejected Mr. 
Hohn’'s § 2255 motion. The court concluded that he had 
waived any claim to challenge the jury instructions 
regarding “use” because he did not object to the instruc- 
tions on direct appeal. J.A. 37. 


On July 29, 1996, Mr. Hohn filed a notice of appeal. 
Treating the notice as a certificate of appealability under 
the three-month-old AEDPA, the Eighth Circuit denied 
Mr. Hohn leave to appeal. It reasoned that at most he was 
“making a claim to a federal statutory right,” not a “con- 
stitutional claim” as required under the statute. J.A. 42. 
“Bailey did no more than interpret a statute,” the court 
concluded, “and an incorrect application of a statute by a 
district court, or any other court, does not violate the 
Constitution.” Id. Because “petitioner makes no due pro- 
cess claim with respect to the trial judge’s action” and 
because in any event “trial errors hardly ever rise to the 
level of a due process violation,” the court found that Mr. 


10 


Hohn had not made a “substantial showing of the denial 
of a constitutional right” as required under § 2253(c)(2). 
Id. 


At that point, Mr. Hohn did not seek leave to appeal 
from a justice of the Supreme Court, as permitted under 
§ 2253(c)(1). Instead, he filed a petition for a writ of 
certiorari, attempting to invoke jurisdiction under 28 
U.S.C. 1254(1). The petition sought review of the court of 
appeals’ determination that he was not entitled to a cer- 
tificate of appealability because his claim raises a statu- 
tory, not a constitutional, question. In response, the 
government agreed that Mr. Hohn’s claim could be char- 
acterized as constitutional, and that the court of appeals 
erred in reaching a contrary conclusion. The government 
suggested that the petition should be granted, the lower 
court decision vacated, and the case remanded for further 
consideration whether a certificate of appealability 
should issue. 


On October 31, 1997, the Court granted certiorari. In 
light of the court of appeals’ denial of Mr. Hohn’s request 
for a certificate of appealability, the Court limited cer- 
tiorari to whether it had jurisdiction to act on the sugges- 
tion of the Solicitor General of the United States to grant, 
vacate and remand the case. 


° 


SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


The Court lacks jurisdiction over the petition. Con- 
gress has precluded the Court from exercising statutory 
or common law certiorari power over a court of appeals’ 


11 


denial of an inmate’s application for a certificate of 
appealability. 


1. Under the exceptions and regulations clause (U.S. 
Const., Art. III, § 2), Congress may streamline collateral 
review of requests for release filed by federal and State 
prisoners. It thus may prohibit appellate review of — and 
terminate - a rejected habeas corpus petition in the event 
the applicant fails to obtain leave to appeal. Nothing 
about such legislation tests the borders of Congress’s 
power under the clause. Because the claimant remains 
free to file an “original” habeas corpus application in this 
Court under 28 U.S.C. 2241, or an application for a certifi- 
cate of appealability with a justice of the Court, the law 
does not exceed congressional power. 


2. Congress not only has the power to regulate the 
Court's appellate jurisdiction in this way, but it also has 
unmistakably done so in this instance. In unyielding 
terms, § 2253(c)(1) says that “an appeal may not be taken | 
to the court of appeals” in the absence of a “certificate of 
appealability” from an Article III judge or justice. The 
plain import of the language of the provision is to expe- 
dite review by setting a specific course for considering 
requests for leave to appeal and by finalizing those cases 
that fail to obtain the required certificate. 


The language of §§ 1254(1) and 1651 cements this 
conclusion. Section 1254 requires a “case” to be “in” the 
court of appeals before certiorari may be sought. Denial 
of leave to appeal prevents the case from ever entering 
the court of appeals, and it defies common sense to 
conclude that a certificate-to-appeal-a-case is itself a case. 
Because Mr. Hohn retains discretion to seek a certificate 


12 


of appealability from this Court, he also cannot credibly 
argue that a common law writ of certiorari is “necessary” 
or “appropriate” “in aid” of the Court's jurisdiction. The 
certificate-of-appealability option does everything that 
§ 1651 could possibly do for him. It allows the Court to 
dispense individual justice (by granting leave to appeal 
when due), and to provide guidance regarding the gate- 
keeping standard (by interpreting the standard in review- 
ing an application itself). Nor would a common law writ 
be used “in aid” of the Court’s jurisdiction. Quite to the 
contrary, he is looking to the writ precisely to evade the 
express requirements of §§ 2253(c)(1) and 1254(1), not to 
supplement them. 


3. The Court’s decisions all respect the plain lan- 
guage of these jurisdictional limitations. 


a. Since 1908, when Congress first imposed a 
gatekeeping standard on appellate review of collateral 
challenges to a criminal sentence, the Court’s opinions 
have unanimously precluded inmates from invoking 
§ 1254(1) (or its predecessors) to seek review of a denied 
leave to appeal. In House v. Mayo, 324 U.S. 42 (1945), for 
example, the Court prevented an inmate from doing just 
that. Because jurisdiction under the certiorari statute 
“extends only to cases ‘in a circuit court of appeals,’ ” 
and because “the case was never ‘in’ the court of appeals, 
for want of a certificate of probable cause,” the Court 
refused to exercise its statutory certiorari powers over the 
petition. Id. at 44. Other opinions follow the same course. 
See, e.g., Davis v. Jacobs, 454 U.S. 911 (1981). 


b. Case law also prevents litigants from using 
the All Writs Act to escape the detailed jurisdictional 


13 


requirements of §§ 2253 and 1254(1). Because the Act is a 
“residual source of authority,” not a free flowing source 
of jurisdiction, because it only permits the Court “to issue 
writs that are not otherwise covered by statute,” Carlisle 
v. United States, 116 S. Ct. 1460, 1467 (1996) (quotation 
omitted), and because it requires necessity of some kind, 
the Act has no application here. Mr. Hohn self-evidently 
is trying to use the Act to avoid the consequences of 
“express statutory provision[s]” that prohibit jurisdiction. 
Pennsylvania Bureau of Correction v. United States Marshals 
Service, 474 U.S. 34, 42 (1985). And, even if this were 
permissible, the option of applying for a certificate of 
appealability to the Court dooms any claim that extraor- 
dinary or exceptional circumstances require relief in this 
instance. 


House v. Mayo does not alter this conclusion. When 
the Court decided House in 1945, Congress did not permit 
habeas litigants to seek leave to appeal from this Court. 
Accordingly, the inmate faced a predicament then - no 
recourse for a mistaken denial of leave to appeal — that 
Mr. Hohn can only conjure today. No urgency, in short, 
supports his reliance on the Act under these circum- 
stances. At the same time, the increasingly “express statu- 
tory provisions,” established in 1948 and 1996, 
streamlining the appellate rights of habeas claimants pro- 
hibit continued reliance on the common law writ of cer- 
tiorari in this setting.

ARGUMENT 


I. Article III Vests Congress With Power To Streamline 
Appellate Review Of Habeas Corpus Cases. 


As a preliminary matter, Congress has authority to 
limit the Supreme Court’s appellate “judicial Power” 
(U.S. Const., Art. III, § 1) in the way it did here. Apart 
from three discrete classes of original jurisdiction cases, 
the Constitution provides that “in all other Cases . . . the 
supreme Court shall have appellate Jurisdiction, both as 
to Law and Fact, with such Exceptions, and under such 
Regulations as the Congress shall make.” Art. III, § 2. 
While the Court’s “appellate powers ‘are given by the 
constitution,’” they thus ” ‘are limited and regu- 
lated . . . by such [congressional] acts as have been passed 
on the subject.’” Felker v. Turpin, 116 S. Ct. 2333, 2339 
(1996) (quoting Durousseau v. United States, 10 U.S. (6 
Cranch) 307, 314 (1810)). 


No doubt some jurisdictional statutes might test the 
reach of Congress's power under the exceptions clause to 
close off all review of a habeas petitioner’s complaint. But 
this is not one of them. For every appellate door that 
§ 2253(c)(1) closes, it leaves open several others that 
allow inmates to seek relief from the Court. In this 
respect, the provision bears a similarity to the sections of 
the AEDPA at issue in Felker v. Turpin. Congress, there, 
also precluded a petition for a writ of certiorari to review 
a decision of a court of appeals exercising its gatekeeping 
function over a successive habeas petition, but at the 
same time did not repeal the Court's authority to enter- 
tain an original petition for habeas corpus. Under those 
statutory circumstances, the Court concluded, there “can 


15 


be no plausible argument that the Act has deprived this 
Court of appellate jurisdiction in violation of Article III, 
§ 2.” Id. at 2339. 


The same conclusion applies with equal — indeed, 
more — force here. Section 2253(c)(1) not only leaves open 
the Court’s authority to entertain an original habeas 
action, see 28 U.S.C. 2241, but also allows the Court itself 
to entertain an application for a certificate of appeal- 
ability. Authority to petition the Court directly for a 
certificate also defuses the risk that the Court might 
somehow lack authority to review lower-court decisions 
that “adopted divergent interpretations of the gatekeeper 
standard.” Felker, 116 S. Ct. at 2342 (Souter, J., joined by 
Stevens, J., and Breyer, J., concurring). See id. (noting that, 
in the absence of such review, “the question whether the 
statute exceeded Congress’s Exceptions Clause power 
would be open”). Because the same standard that governs 
lower-court gatekeeping decisions also governs the 
Court’s decision, the Court necessarily will resolve such 
disputes through its own review of an application. As in 
Felker, in short, there is “no plausible argument” that 
Congress has exceeded its power under the Constitution 
in this instance. Suggestions to the contrary (Pet. Br. 
39-40) should be rejected. 


II. Under The Plain Terms Of Sections 1254(1), 1651, 
And 2253(c)(1), Congress Has Not Given The 
Supreme Court Certiorari Power To Review Unsuc- 
cessful Applications For A Certificate Of Appeal- 
ability. 


In addition to having the constitutional authority to 
foreclose certiorari review of habeas corpus gatekeeping 


16 


decisions, Congress has in fact exercised that power here. 
The relevant statutes provide as follows: 


The Certificate-Of-Appealability Requirement: 
“Unless a circuit justice or judge issues a certifi- 
cate of appealability, an appeal may not be taken 
to the court of appeals from - (A) the final order 
in a habeas corpus proceeding in which the 
detention complained of arises out of process 
issued by a State court; or (B) the final order in a 
proceeding under section 2255.” 28 U.S.C. 
2253(c)(1). 


The Certiorari Statute: “Cases in the courts of 
appeals may be reviewed by the Supreme Court 
by . . . writ of certiorari... . ” 28 U.S.C. 1254(1). 


The All Writs Act: “The Supreme Court and all 
courts established by Act of Congress may issue 
all writs necessary or appropriate in aid of their 
respective jurisdictions and agreeable to the 
usages and principles of law.” 28 U.S.C. 1651(a). 


In word and design, this trio of laws prevents the 
Court from exercising either statutory or so-called com- 
mon law certiorari jurisdiction over Mr. Hohn’s petition. 
First, in unbending terms, § 2253 erects a jurisdictional 
gate to appellate review of all failed habeas petitions. 
Such “an appeal may not be taken to the court of 
appeals,” Congress has unequivocally mandated, save 
when a judge or justice gives the inmate permission to do 
so. The prerequisite of a certificate of appealability thus 
prohibits any “case” from entering the appellate court 
unless the right judge authorizes it. In the absence of 
such a certificate, the case never enters - and therefore is 
never “in” — the appellate court as required by § 1254(1). 


17 


Even aside from the prerequisite that a case be “in” 
the court of appeals, the requirement of a certificate of 
appealability under § 2253(c)(1) by itself ends all habeas 
cases that do not obtain the requisite leave. The provision 
is at once jurisdiction stripping and case ending. Con- 
gress purposefully set up a two-step process for review- 
ing district court habeas decisions, the first to screen 
appeals that warrant further review, the second to review 
deserving claims on the merits. The failure to meet the 
threshold requirement both terminates the federal courts’ 
power to review the matter and the inmate’s power to 
seek review of the district court decision elsewhere. The 
case is over. The inmate cannot go back to district court to 
obtain a final order; and he cannot correct any of the 
other jurisdictional defects that might initially have pre- 
cluded review. The manifest point of the statute is to 
screen out meritless habeas appeals and finalize them. 


Second, while it might be argued that Mr. Hohn’s 
application for a certificate came into the court of appeals, 
that is not the same as doing what Congress required of 
him - to obtain permission to get his “case” in the appel- 
late court. That, of course, never happened. And, to say 
that Congress meant to use the word “case” in § 1254 to 
allow certiorari review of certificate applications asks too 
much of the operative words. Not only is it awkward to 
think of a certificate-to-appeal-a-case as itself a “case,” 
but that view makes even less sense in this statutory 
setting. The same provision (§ 2253(c)(1)) that allows Mr. 
Hohn to seek a certificate of appealability from the appel- 
late court also allows him to seek one from the Supreme 
Court. Having once permitted Mr. Hohn to seek review in 


18 


this Court via a certificate application, no rational expla- 
nation exists for authorizing Court review of the court of 
appeals’ certificate decision by certiorari under § 1254(1) 
or for that matter under § 1651. The former option does 
everything that the latter two options could possibly do 
for him. 


Apparently recognizing that a denied certificate at 
the court of appeals level prevents the inmate from seek- 
ing certiorari, Congress wisely created a safety valve. It 
gave inmates permission to ask a justice (or the whole 
Court if the justice refers the matter) for a certificate of 
appealability, making certain that Congress’s efforts to 
finalize meritless habeas appeals did just that, but only 
that. 


Third, still another component of the statutory 
scheme bolsters this reading. When a prisoner seeks a 
certificate of appealability, he must do so issue by issue. 
That is to say, the application must detail “which specific 
issue or issues” meet the gatekeeping requirement of “a 
substantial showing of the denial of a constitutional 
right.” 28 U.S.C. 2253(c)(2) and (3). The court of appeals 
therefore may grant inmates permission to appeal some 
issues but not others. Yet, under that scenario, what hap- 
pens next? May the inmate then seek statutory (or com- 
mon law) certiorari of the denied issues while the court of 
appeals proceeds to review the accepted issues? If so, that 
imposes a unique two-track system of appellate review 
that will spawn a host of administrative oddities that 
Congress could not possibly have intended. 


Take the problem of timing. Since petitioners have 90 
days to file a writ, as well as ample time for opposition 


19 


and reply briefs, review of a certiorari petition custom- 
arily takes a minimum of 5-6 months from the date of 
decision, a minimum of 7-8 months when the petition 
overlaps with the summer recess, and occasionally longer 
(consider Mr. Hohn’s petition, which took almost 9 
months). Similar delay would presumably plague a 
§ 1651 common law writ of certiorari, particularly one 
that (like Mr. Hohn’s) was conceived as and then filed in 
the form of a statutory certiorari petition. See S. Ct. Rule 
20. 


In this setting, a court of appeals would face an 
awkward dilemma: either wait for this Court’s certiorari 
decision before proceeding with all issues entitled to 
review, or bifurcate the case into the court-of-appeals- 
approved issues first with the Supreme-Court-approved 
issues to follow. No such dilemma arises, in conspicuous 
contrast, when litigants hew to the application process 
that Congress expressly created. Applications to the 
Court customarily take days or at most weeks to review, 
may be reviewed promptly during the summer recess, 
and accordingly would not compel a two-track system of 
review. While Congress perhaps could impose a bifur- 
cated system of review on courts and litigants, it makes 
little sense to credit the legislature with that disfiguring 
intention when a more natural reading of the laws elimi- 
nates any such requirement. 


Fourth, Mr. Hohn cannot tenably shoehorn his case 
into the language of the All Writs Act, which requires a 
common law writ of certiorari to be “necessary” or 
“appropriate” as well as to be “in aid” of the Court's 
jurisdiction. Surely it is not necessary to use § 1651 here. 
As Mr. Hohn well knows, indeed has now shown he 


20 


knows (Pet. Br. 45 n.34; Application by Arnold F. Hohn 
for a Certificate of Appealability (Jan. 20, 1998)), an 
inmate may permissibly file an application under 
§ 2253(c)(1) to obtain a certificate of appealability from a 
justice or (if referred) the Court. See Beavers v. Pruett, 118 
S. Ct. 621 (1997) (referring application for a certificate of 
appealability and a stay of execution to the Court). Just as 
it is not necessary to review Mr. Hohn’s § 1651 request, it 
is not appropriate to do so either. Congress already gives 
inmates a procedure for ensuring that the certificate- 
application process goes fairly. He has now taken advan- 
tage of that option, making his efforts to expand § 1651 
jurisdiction as inappropriate as they are gratuitous. 


In addition to his inability to satisfy the necessary / 
appropriate requirement, Mr. Hohn cannot show that his 
§ 1651 writ is “in aid” of jurisdiction. That remains 
implausible here since Mr. Hohn self-evidently is using 
§ 1651 to create jurisdiction, not supplement it. It can 
never be true that § 1651 is “in aid” of jurisdiction that 
Congress elsewhere has expressly regulated and indeed 
taken away. Finally, in light of the availability of the 
certificate option, Mr. Hohn cannot meet another essen- 
tial requirement. Under Supreme Court Rule 20(1), the 
applicant must show something that Mr. Hohn has not - 
“that adequate relief cannot be obtained in any other 
form.”

A. Longstanding Precedent Emphasizes That 
Leave-To-Appeal Decisions May Not Be 
Reviewed Under § 1254(1). 


1. Several Decisions of The Court Support 
This Interpretation. 


House v. Mayo, 324 U.S. 42 (1945) (per curiam), 
addressed this issue in a context similar to this one. 
Confined in a State prison, the inmate filed a petition for 
habeas corpus in federal district court. The district court 
denied the petition, then denied a certificate of probable 
cause for an appeal. The inmate next filed an application 
for an appeal in forma pauperis but without requesting a 
certificate of probable cause. The appellate court denied 
the application, after which the inmate sought leave in 
this Court to file a petition for certiorari. 


The Court determined that it lacked power to issue a 
statutory writ of certiorari. “Our authority under that 
section,” the Court observed, “extends only to cases ‘in a 
circuit court of appeals, or in the United States Court of 
Appeals for the District of Columbia.’ ” Id. at 44. Because 
“the case was never ‘in’ the court of appeals, for want of 
a certificate of probable cause,” the Court continued, it 
lacked jurisdiction to review the case under Congress’s 
jurisdictional requirements. Id. 


Most recently, in Davis v. Jacobs, 454 U.S. 911 (1981), 
two members of the Court exchanged views about this 
same question. While the dialogue between Justices 
Rehnquist and Stevens failed to resolve whether the

grant a writ of certiorari where both the District Court 
and the Court of Appeals have denied a habeas corpus 
petitioner a certificate of probable cause to appeal.” Id. at 
915-19 (Rehnquist, J., joined by Burger, C.J., and Powell, 
J., dissenting from the denial of certiorari). And in the

Barksdale, 453 U.S. 914 (1981) (“Since there was no certifi-

In several other decisions, the Court has followed 
this course. In Bilik v. Strassheim, 212 U.S. 551 (1908), and 
Ex Parte Patrick, 212 U.S. 555 (1908), the Court disposed of 
two cases without opimion under a forerunner of § 2253. 
At the time, Congress did not allow the Court to review 
an unsuccessful habeas petition in the absence of a certifi- 
cate of probablie cause issued by a justice of the Court or 
of the federal district court that had denied the petition. 
In the words of the 1908 statute, “no appeal to the 
Supreme Court shall be allowed unless” the petitioner

Court concluded that it lacked jurisdiction to allow direct 
appeals in cases in which no certificate had issued. 


Ferguson v. District of Columbia, 270 U.S. 633 (1926) 
(per curiam), reached a like conclusion. At stake was 
whether the Court had jurisdiction to review the refusal 
of a justice of the court of appeals of the District of 
Columbia to allow a writ of error to the police court of 
the District. No such authority existed, the Court held, 
and therefore denied a petition for a writ of certiorari 
“for want of jurisdiction in this [CJourt.” Id. at 634. And 
in In re 620 Church Street Bldg. Corp., 299 U.S. 24 (1936), 
the Court declined to exercise its statutory certiorari 
powers over a bankruptcy confirmation plan that the 
court of appeals had declined leave to appeal. Because 
the “Circuit Court of Appeals declined jurisdiction,” it 
concluded that the case involved an “instance[ }] not cov- 
ered by” the Court's jurisdiction under the certiorari 
statute. Id. at 26. Cf. Kennedy v. Lainson, 323 U.S. 780 
(1944) (denying “for want of jurisdiction” a petition for a 
writ of certiorari to a district court). In the last analysis, 
no opinion of the Court, nor for that matter a single 
separate opinion of a justice, to amicus curiae’s knowl- 
edge, has ever stated that the Court may use its statutory 
certiorari power to review decisions denying leave to 
appeal. 

Nor has Congress made any changes to the pertinent 
laws that could possibly alter these conclusions. For 
example, in House and Davis, the statutory provision 
regarding certiorari required the “case” to be “in” the 
court of appeals, just as it does today. See Appendix. And 
the relevant portions of the certificate-of-probable-cause

cate-of-appealability prerequisite. Both say that “an 
appeal may not be taken” in the absence of permission 
from the right judge. See Appendix. 


What changes there have been, moreover, cement 
these conclusions. In its current form, § 2253(c)(1) 
requires a certificate of appealability, not one of probable 
cause. Yet that only goes to emphasize the severity of the 
screening function Congress has mandated. Likewise, the 
predecessor of § 2253 at issue in House did not permit an 
application to be filed with a justice of this Court, while 
the modern version does. But that, too, offers little solace 
to Mr. Hohn. That the inmate today may seek a certificate 
of appealability from the Court under § 2253(c)(1) makes 
all the more unnecessary the stretched reading of 
§ 1254(1) that Mr. Hohn asks the Court to embrace. 


2. Mr. Hohn’s Contrary Arguments Are Mis- 
taken. 


To all of this, Mr. Hohn offers several responses, each 
unpersuasive. He first claims (Pet. 15-16) that the Court 
in practice has not always adhered to these requirements. 
There are several decisions, he points out, that issue writs 
of certiorari to review denials of certificates of probable 
cause; and there is one case (In re Burwell, 350 U.S. 521 
(1956)), he adds, in which the Court exercised jurisdiction 
over questions certified by the Ninth Circuit under the 
predecessor to § 1254(2), even though the statute required 
a “case” to be “in” the court of appeals and the appellate 
court had denied leave to appeal. Yet neither this alleged 
general practice nor the specifics of Burwell supply the 


25 


support Mr. Hohn needs. It is a basic principle of this 
Court’s jurisprudence that “when questions of jurisdic- 
tion have been passed on in prior decisions sub silentio, 
this Court has never considered itself bound when a 
subsequent case finally brings the jurisdictional issue 
before us.” Hagans v. Lavine, 415 U.S. 528, 532 n.5 (1974). 
See also FEC v. NRA Political Victory Fund, 115 S. Ct. 537, 
542 (1994); Will v. Michigan Dept. of State Police, 491 U.S. 
58, 63 n.4 (1989). 


As the federal government acknowledges (Br. 26), 
this venerable principle disposes of Mr. Hohn’s argu- 
ment. Not one of the cases Mr. Hohn cites, including 
Burwell, discusses the jurisdictional question, to say noth- 
ing of mentioning the statutory language that governs it. 
Any way one looks at it, Mr. Hohn’s reliance on a series 
of orders and decisions that fail to address the jurisdic- 
tional issue cannot support his position, let alone show 
that the Court has indiscreetly overruled these earlier 
rulings. 


Next, Mr. Hohn argues (Pet. 35-36) that in Congress's 
efforts to place additional restrictions on the appeal of 
meritless habeas claims through passage of the AEDPA, it 
somehow altered the House conclusion that inmates may 
not seek certiorari under § 1254 to review a denied leave- 
to-appeal. Another provision of the AEDPA, he points 
out, specifically prohibits an unsuccessful petitioner from 
filing a writ of certiorari, while section 2253 does not - 
leading to the inference (he claims) that Congress meant 
to allow certiorari review in one setting but not the other. 
True, the AEDPA sets up a gatekeeping requirement for 
first and successive federal habeas petitions. True also, 


26 


Congress specified that certiorari review was not avail- 
able for successive gatekeeping decisions while declining 
to reiterate the House conclusion for initial gatekeeping 
decisions. 


There, however, the comparison ends. The two set- 
tings serve utterly distinct functions. With respect to the 
first federal habeas setting, the gatekeeping provision 
serves the certificate-of-probable-cause function 
addressed in House: Does the inmate’s claim have suffi- 
cient merit to warrant further review? In the successive 
habeas setting, the gatekeeping provision serves a differ- 
ent function. It asks not whether an appeal may proceed 
but whether any petition at all may be filed. Under these 
circumstances, Congress acted sensibly in tying a double, 
as opposed to a single, statutory knot precluding cer- 
tiorari review. While prior successive federal habeas peti- 
tioners often faced stringent requirements, they never 
faced a hurdle to any district court consideration at all. 


Just as consequentially, the inmate seeking leave to 
file a successive habeas petition has no option of filing an 
application to the Supreme Court in the event the court of 
appeals denies his or her request. In stark contrast, first 
federal habeas petitioners may freely file an application 
to the Court when the lower court denies leave. In the 
first federal habeas setting, in other words, Congress had 
little reason to worry about confirming that certiorari was 
precluded. Not only did House already establish the 
point, but the express provision for relief from the Court 
through a certificate application supplied ample clarifica- 
tion that the certificate, not certiorari, was the appropri- 
ate vehicle for responding to a denied leave-to-appeal.

Far from supporting Mr. Hohn’s position, Lindh v. 
Murphy, 117 S. Ct. 2059 (1997), undermines it. Lindh asked 
whether a specific retroactivity provision in Chapter 154 
of the Act (regarding capital cases) implied a lack of 
retroactivity for a provision in Chapter 153 regarding the 
standard for obtaining habeas relief. That was an apples- 
to-apples comparison. For in that setting nothing about 
the difference between the two provisions offered a 
meaningful reason for Congress to specify a date-of- 
application in one section but not the other, except to 
imply (as Lindh concluded) that the new rules apply to 
pending cases in the first but not the second setting. The 
same cannot be said here. In the context of first federal 
habeas, Congress clearly spelled out a procedure for 
seeking relief from the Court when leave to appeal was 
denied. Yet, in the successive habeas context, it chose not 
to do so, making the drafter’s repetition wholly appropri- 
ate and wholly without implication for the other setting. 


That leaves a few other contentions that Mr. Hohn 
has raised. What of the Court’s cases concluding that it 
possesses jurisdiction to review decisions over which the 
court of appeals may have lacked jurisdiction? Does not 
review in that setting show that the Court has jurisdiction 
to decide a case that has not been permitted “in” the 
court of appeals? See, e.g., Pet. Br. 23-26; Nixon v. 
Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 731, 743 & n.23 (1982); United States v. 
Nixon, 418 U.S. 683 (1974). 


The analogy does not hold. When it comes to the 
requirement of a final order, Congress has established a 
policy of avoiding piecemeal appeals. That policy, how- 
ever, only goes to the efficiency of appellate review, not 
to the objective of terminating it altogether. In telling

contrast, the whole point of the gatekeeping requirement, 
from its initial appearance in 1908 to the present, has 
been to curb “frivolous habeas corpus petitions” by 
requiring that a “prisoner first obtain a certificate of

Barefoot v. Estelle, 463 U.S. 880, 893 n.3 (1983). See id. at 892 
(“Congress established the requirement that a prisoner

The Nixon cases merely teach that the Court has 
power to assess its own jurisdiction. 457 U.S. at 743 n.23,

then to review the court of appeals’ interpretations of the

now to assess its authority to review Mr. Hohn’s petition. 
But that does not say anything about whether jurisdiction 
over the lower-court decision in fact exists - whether, 
here, the Court in fact may review the court of appeals’ 
denial of Mr. Hohn’s application for a certificate of 
appealability. Nothing about the Nixon cases undermines, 
let alone overrules, House v. Mayo’s conclusion that the 
Court lacks statutory-certiorari jurisdiction to review a

claimant’s failure to obtain leave to appeal. Still less 
helpful are the cases reviewing denials of leave to appeal 
in forma pauperis. See, e.g, Adkins v. E.I. DuPont de Nemours

the jurisidictional issue; but they relate to a statute that 
by its terms is not designed to streamline and terminate 
appeals, but rather to facilitate them. 


As a final reason for overruling House v. Mayo, Mr. 
Hohn argues (Pet. Br. 17-21) that certificate-of-appeal- 
ability applications fall within the broad definition of

application-to-appeal-a-case itself a case. Nor at any rate

words, could there possibly be for permitting applica-

two-track review of denials for leave to appeal not only 
would fail to curb the lack-of-finality problem that 
prompted the legislation, Barefoot v. Estelle, 463 U.S. at 
892-93, but would positively exacerbate the law’s delays - 
by forcing governments and courts to consider two

multi-track review of the court-of-appeals-approved- 


issues and the Supreme-Court-approved issues. See infra. 
The national legislature deserves more credit than that.

B. Precedent Demonstrates That The Court May 
Not Review An Application For A Certificate 
Of Appealability Under The All Writs Act. 


Besides lacking power to review Mr. Hohn’s petition 
under its statutory certiorari authority, the Court also 
lacks power to do so under the All Writs Act. Only the 
most extraordinary circumstances warrant review under 
the All Writs Act, and there is nothing extraordinary 
about Mr. Hohn’s situation. Whatever the scope of the so- 
called common law certiorari power under § 1651, more- 
over, it does not empower the Court to create jurisdiction 
that Congress has expressly addressed elsewhere or 
indeed taken away. 


1. Mr. Hohn’s Petition Raises No Urgency And 
Asks The Court To Construe § 1651 To Cre- 
ate Jurisdiction That Congress Has 
Expressly Addressed Elsewhere. 


Consistent with the text of the All Writs Act, the 
Court has made clear that necessity remains a threshold 
requirement for invoking power under the provision. The 
“ ‘Act is a residual source of authority to issue writs that 
are not otherwise covered by statute.’ ” Carlisle v. United 
States, 116 S. Ct. 1460, 1467 (1996) (quoting Pennsylvania 
Bureau of Correction v. United States Marshals Service, 474 
U.S. 34, 43 (1985)). The Act at most “empowers federal 
courts to fashion extraordinary remedies when the need 
arises,” United States Marshals, 474 U.S. at 43; it does not 
allow courts to use it “whenever compliance with statu- 
tory procedures appears inconvenient or less appropri- 
ate,” id. For these reasons, one seeking the writ must

One test for discerning such urgency, the Court has

words, “may not be done with it.” Bankers Life & Casualty 
Co. v. Holland, 346 U.S. 379, 383 (1953). See also Ex Parte 
Peru, 318 U.S. 578, 584 (1943) (“[t]he common law writs, 
like equitable remedies, . . . are usually denied where 
another adequate remedy is available”). In accordance 
with this requirement, the Supreme Court’s Rules warn 
litigants that “[t]o justify the granting of any such writ, 
the petition must show,” among other things, “that ade- 
quate relief cannot be obtained in any other form or from 
any other court.” S. Ct. Rule 20(1). 


Whether one considers the general requirement of 
“exceptional circumstances” or the more specific one of 
“alternative” avenues of relief, Mr. Hohn comes up short 
in either event. Above all else, Mr. Hohn’s position is 
neither unusual nor dire because he may file an applica- 
tion for a certificate of appealability with a justice of the 
Court. This device provides all the individual relief any 
inmate could need, most notably because it empowers the 
Court to allow the appeal to proceed. The device also 
authorizes the Court to clarify the meaning of the gate- 
keeping standard, as the Court is free to (indeed must) 
interpret the test in reviewing the application itself. In the 
last analysis, while the All Writs Act no doubt provides 
“extraordinary remedies,” they “are reserved for really 


32 


extraordinary causes.” Bankers Life & Casualty Co. v. Hol- 
land, 346 U.S. 379, 385 (1953) (quotation omitted). This 
simply is not one of them. 


Mr. Hohn’s reliance on the All Writs Act fails for a 
second, independent reason. While urgency may be a 
necessary precondition for invoking the Act, the Court 
has made clear that it is not by itself a sufficient explana- 
tion for exercising power under the Act. Section 1651 thus 
is a “residual source of authority to issue writs that are 
not otherwise covered by statute. Where a statute speci- 
fically addresses the particular issue at hand, it is that 
authority, and not the All Writs Act, that is controlling.” 
Carlisle v. United States, 116 S. Ct. at 1467 (1996) (quota- 
tion omitted). The Court therefore has “rejected use of the 
all writs provision to enable the Court to review a lower 
court’s determination where jurisdiction did not lie under 
an express statutory provision.” United States Marshals, 
474 US. at 41. 


Consistent with these views, Carlisle refused to allow 
a litigant to rely on the All Writs Act to file a motion for a 
judgment of acquittal one day after the time limit pre- 
scribed by the Rules of Criminal Procedure. Even though 
the delay was counsel’s fault, not the defendant's, and 
even though the district court determined that it would 
acquit the individual, the Court concluded that since Rule 
29 “specifically addresses the particular issue at hand,” 
the All Writs Act does not apply. 116 S. Ct. at 1467 
(quotation omitted). In like manner, United States Mar- 
shals declined to authorize a district court judge under 
the Act to order federal marshals to transport State pris- 
oners to testify as witnesses at a § 1983 hearing. Because 
several federal statutes established procedures relating to 


33 


the “issue at hand,” the Court reversed the lower court 
decision, relying on case law “reject[ing] use of the all 
writs provision to enable the Court to review a lower 
court’s determination where jurisdiction did not lie under 
an express statutory provision.” 474 U.S. at 41 (citation 
omitted). 


This modern view rests on the shoulders of several 
earlier cases. In U.S. Alkali Export Assn. v. United States, 
325 U.S. 196, 203 (1945), for example, the Court came to a 
similar conclusion: 


The writs may not be used as a substitute 
for an authorized appeal; and where, as here, 
the statutory scheme permits appellate review 
of interlocutory orders only on appeal from the 
final judgment, review by certiorari or other 
extraordinary writ is not permissible in the face 
of the plain indication of the legislative purpose 
to avoid piecemeal reviews. 


U.S. Alkali, it is true, ultimately allowed review of a 
non-final order by writ of certiorari. But it did so not 
because the All Writs Act may be “used as a substitute for 
an authorized appeal” but because the district court was 
charged with exceeding a limitation placed on its jurisdic- 
tion by Congress. Id. at 203-04. That explanation may just 
as easily justify a writ of prohibition as distinct from one 
of certiorari. At any rate, the exception is sound. For like 
reasons, this Court could exercise its all writs power to 
prevent a court of appeals from reviewing a first federal 
habeas petition after leave to appeal had been denied. The 
U.S. Alkali exception for lower courts that overstep their 
jurisdictional boundaries, in other words, simply proves 
the general rule. 


34 


Other cases invoke the general rule as well. See, ¢.g., 
De Beers Consolidated Mines, Ltd. v. United States, 325 U.S. 
212, 217 (1945) (“When Congress withholds interlocutory 
reviews, [the All Writs Act] can, of course, not be availed 
of to correct a mere error in the exercise of conceded 
judicial power”); Roche v. Evaporated Milk Assn., 319 USS. 
21, 27-28 (1943) (the All Writs Act “may not be resorted to 
as a mode of review where a statutory method of appeal 
has been prescribed”) (“Where the appeal statutes estab- 
lish the conditions of appellate review, an appellate court 
cannot rightly exercise its discretion to issue a writ whose 
only effect would be to avoid those conditions and thwart 
Congressional policy”); United States v. Beatty, 232 U.S. 
463, 467-68 (1914) (the common law writ of certiorari may 
not be used as a substitute for an authorized appeal); In re 
Commonwealth of Massachusetts, 197 U.S. 482, 488 (1905) 
(“in cases over which we possess neither original nor 
appellate jurisdiction we cannot grant . . . certiorari as 
ancillary thereto”); McClung v. Silliman, 19 U.S. (6 Wheat.) 
598, 601-02 (1821) (the Act was “intended” only to “vest 
the power . . . in cases where the jurisdiction already 
exists, and not where it is to be courted or acquired, by 
means of the writ proposed to be sued”); McIntire v. 
Wood, 11 U.S. (7 Cranch) 504 (1813). As these cases illus- 
trate, the Act does not empower the Court to exercise 
jurisdiction that Congress has elsewhere expressly 
addressed. 


In light of this case law, Mr. Hohn may not use the 
All Writs Act to escape the requirements of §§ 2253 and 
1254. From 1908 forward, Congress has been steadfast in 


35 


curbing frivolous habeas cases by enacting and reenact- 
ing legislation that denies inmates leave to appeal merit- 
less claims. Plainly, this is a case in which there is not just 
one, but two, “express statutory provision[s]” that 
already address the jurisdictional issue. Just as Mr. Hohn 
could not use the All Writs Act to sidestep the 90-day 
limitation for filing a statutory petition for a writ of 
certiorari, see 28 U.S.C. 2101(c), he may not use it to 
circumvent the requirements of §§ 2253 and 1254. 


A reading of § 1651 that allows inmates to escape the 
jurisdictional imperatives of these specific provisions also 
slights the singlemindedness of Congress’s efforts to curb 
endless habeas review. It presumes that the same govern- 
ment body with stewardship over §§ 2253(c)(1) and 
1254(1) somehow meant to defeat these efforts through 
§ 1651. And, worse, it transforms the All Writs Act from a 
“sparingly exercised” (Parr v. United States, 351 U.S. 513, 
520 (1956)) “residual source of authority” concerning 
cases within the Court’s jurisdiction (Carlisle v. United 
States, 116 S. Ct. 1460, 1467 (1996)) into a roving “source 
of jurisdiction.” No cases support this view, and several 
positively reject it. The invitation should be rejected, the 
Court’s cases preserved, and Congress’s sensible and 
express legislative design left intact. 


2. Neither House v. Mayo Nor Any Other Deci- 
sion Alters This Conclusion. 


Neither House v. Mayo nor any of the other cases 
upon which Mr. Hohn and the federal government have 
relied alter this conclusion. No doubt, from all that 
appears on the face of House v. Mayo, there would seem to 


36 


be little reason to adhere to the first half of the opinion 
(which precludes jurisdiction under § 1254(1)) while dis- 
regarding the second (which permits jurisdiction under 
§ 1651). But an examination of the statutes at issue in 
House eliminates any such inconsistency. 


When the Court decided House in 1945, all three 
pieces of this statutory puzzle were in place - the gate- 
keeping requirement, the All Writs Act and the certiorari 
statute. Yet, while the All Writs Act and § 1254 paralleled 
in meaningful respects the language of the statutes today, 
see Appendix, the same cannot be said about the gate- 
keeping requirement. Unlike the modern version of 
§ 2253, the 1945 law did not permit an application to a 
justice of the Supreme Court. It allowed leave only from 
“the United States court by which the final decision was 
rendered or a judge of the circuit court of appeals.” 28 
U.S.C. 466 (1940); see Appendix. 


The change in law is dispositive, first because it 
eliminates any urgency, and second because it (together 
with the AEDPA) supplies the kind of “express statutory” 
direction that precludes reliance on the All Writs Act. 
Nothing about Mr. Hohn’s situation today compares with 
the predicament that Mr. Mayo faced in 1945. Once the 
court of appeals denied leave to appeal, Mr. Mayo had 
nowhere to go but the All Writs Act to correct a seem- 
ingly mistaken interpretation of the gatekeeping require- 
ment. Mr. Hohn, of course, has no such problem. He may 
file an application with a justice of the Court, which at 
once provides a congressionally-approved method for 
dispensing individual justice and a vehicle for ensuring 
uniform treatment of the gatekeeping standard. Under 
these circumstances, Mr. Hohn cannot tenably claim that 


37 


he is asking the Court to do something Congress has 
authorized under the Act, which is to say “fashion 
extraordinary remedies when the need arises.” United 
States Marshals, 474 U.S. at 43. Whether such urgency 
existed in 1945 or not, it plainly does not exist today. 


Likewise, by specifically granting inmates the option 
of seeking leave to appeal from this Court in 1948 and by 
enacting the detailed - and more stringent — provisions of 
the AEDPA in 1996, Congress supplied the kind of delib- 
erate statutory direction that precludes resort to the All 
Writs Act. As the cases frequently repeat, the Act may not 
“enable the Court to review a lower court's determination 
where jurisdiction did not lie under an express statutory 
provision.” United States Marshals, 474 U.S. at 41. The 
specifics of § 2253 regarding the path for reviewing 
habeas corpus petitions, coupled with the prescribed 
route for seeking review under § 1254, make continued 
existence of a statutory-common-law writ of certiorari as 
anachronistic in this setting as it is confusing. 


Like House v. Mayo, each of the other cases upon 
which the federal government and Mr. Hohn rely either 
fail to address the jurisdictional issue or involve a situa- 
tion in which the requirements of § 1651 had been met. In 
In re 620 Church Street, 299 U.S. 24 (1936), for instance, the 
Court invoked the Act to review a district court’s con- 
firmation of a plan of bankruptcy reorganization. But it 
did so not just because statutory certiorari jurisdiction 
did not exist (that would vest jurisdiction over any lower 
court order), but because the “auxiliary process” of the 
writ was “a means of giving full force and effect to 
existing appellate authority and of furthering justice in 
other kindred ways.” Id. at 26 (emphasis added) (internal 


38 


quotation omitted). Unlike a bankruptcy reorganization 
plan, which the Court may later review after a final order 
has been obtained, no “existing appellate authority” is 
available over the denial of a leave to appeal — unless or 
until a judge or justice grants leave. Without such leave, 
in other words, a habeas case terminates under federal 
law, while a bankruptcy case does not. 


Nor, under these circumstances, may even such an 
agreeable standard as “furthering justice” assist Mr. 
Hohn. He cannot show that a single injustice has come 
his way until the Court denies his application. The 
greater the injustice he claims at the hands of the court of 
appeals, moreover, the less likely the Court will deny an 
application. The same problem exists under — and defeats 
- other broad characterizations of the standard for using 
the writ - e.g., “its use must only be ‘reasonably neces- 
sary in the interest of justice.’ ” U.S. Br. 32 (quoting Adams 
v. United States ex rel. McCann, 317 U.S. 269, 274 (1942)). 
Even a test of reasonable necessity must meet the Court's 
own rules for granting extraordinary writs, which require 
a showing that “adequate relief cannot be obtained in any 
other form.” S. Ct. R. 20(1). The certificate option defeats 
any such showing. 


Policy considerations also do not favor altering this 
congressional framework. Surely a court of appeals’ gate- 
keeping decision “in violation of equal protection or 
other fundamental constitutional principles” (U.S. Br. 30) 
will not stand in the way of an application decision by 
this Court reminding lower courts of the correct stan- 
dard. Contrary to Mr. Hohn’s suggestion (Br. 30), more- 
over, once a justice refers such an application to the 
whole Court, the Court plainly may issue a decision that 


39 


constitutes “binding precedent.” Nor is the application 
process that Congress created “a clumsy and burden- 
some” way (U.S. Br. 32) for ensuring uniform interpreta- 
tion of the gatekeeping standard or for preserving the 
Court's ultimate right to review the merits of a habeas 
petition. In this case, for example, how does the applica- 
tion relief to which Mr. Hohn may be entitled differ in 
any meaningful way from the grant-vacate-remand order 
that the Solicitor General has proposed? Both simply 
prevent the case from being terminated, and both orders 
may convey as little or as much direction as the Court 
wishes. Such concerns at any rate are best addressed to 
the members of Congress, who are sure to reply that the 
system of review they have allowed represents a vast 
improvement on the two-track system that review by 
common law certiorari and certificate application would 
compel. 


IV. Mr. Hohn’s Other Arguments Exceed The Scope Of 
The Question Presented, And Have Been Waived. 


Mr. Hohn also asks (Br. 40-49) the Court to treat his 
petition as an application for a certificate of appealability, 
and to consider whether the certificate requirement 
should apply to him even though he filed his appeal after 
the AEDPA became effective. Both requests should be 


rejected. 


In each respect, Mr. Hohn seeks to expand the scope 
of the narrow jurisdictional question that the Court asked 
to be briefed and argued. Neither one of these conten- 
tions, moreover, was mentioned in the petition for a writ 
of certiorari, and one (application of the gatekeeping 


40 


standard to the appeal) was not even raised below. Mr. 
Hohn’s petition plainly assumed that the gatekeeping 
requirement applies. Indeed, the thrust of the petition 
was that the Court should grant certiorari to resolve a 
lower-court division of authority over the meaning of the 
test. On this record, the Court properly granted certiorari 
to review a jurisdictional question that assumes the appli- 
cability of the Act to this appeal. It is wholly appropriate 
to continue to resolve the petition on that assumption, 
since Mr. Hohn initiated it and the Court had every 
reason to accept it. See Bray v. Alexandria Women’s Health 
Clinic, 506 U.S. 263, 280 (1993) (observing that “the Peti- 
tion for Certiorari (not the Brief in Opposition and later 
briefs) . . . determines” scope of the petition); Parker v. 
Randolph, 442 U.S. 62, 77 (1979) (plurality) (“Although 
petitioner sought review of this ruling, our grant of cer- 
tiorari was limited to the [one] issue”). 


Nor will Mr. Hohn suffer any discernible harm if the 
Court declines to convert the petition into an application 
to a justice or the Court. No time restrictions curb Mr. 
Hohn’s authority to file an application. He remains free 
to file one after the jurisdictional issue has been resolved, 
or he may file a protective application (as he now has 
done) in the event he loses the jurisdictional issue. 
Because he was recently placed on parole (Pet. Br. 4), he 
also has regained one of the most vital interests that a 
§ 2255 motion seeks to vindicate. At all events, because 
Mr. Hohn now has filed an application to Justice Thomas 
for a Certificate of Appealability, amicus curiae intends to 
seek leave of Court to file a response to the application, 
which would seem to be the proper jurisdictional route 
for addressing these matters. 


41 


One might fairly ask what difference all this makes. 
Does it really matter whether the Court has jurisdiction to 
review this matter under its statutory certiorari powers, 
as Mr. Hohn claims, under its common-law certiorari 
powers, as Mr. Hohn and the federal government claim, 
or under its certificate-of-appealability powers, as the 
parties and amicus curiae agree? 


The question, of course, does matter: It implicates the 
Court’s unique and most essential power. At stake is 
whether Congress vested the Court with jurisdiction to 
review Mr. Hohn’s petition, then (if it did) whether fed- 
eral prosecutors violated the United States Constitution 
in convicting Mr. Hohn. Rightly appreciating that 
“[flederal courts are courts of limited jurisdiction” that 
“possess only that power authorized by Constitution and 
statute,” Kokkonen v. Guardian Life Ins. Co. of America, 511 
U.S. 375, 377 (1994), the Court long has been careful to 
avoid “expand|[ing] by judicial decree” the statutory juris- 
diction that Congress has given it, id. 


In modestly “presum[ing] that a cause lies outside 
this limited jurisdiction,” the Court has placed the “bur- 
den of establishing the contrary” “upon the party assert- 
ing jurisdiction.” Kokkonen, 511 U.S. at 377 (citing McNutt 
v. General Motors Acceptance Corp., 298 U.S. 178, 182-83 
(1936)). See also Durousseau v. United States, 10 US. (6 
Cranch) 307, 314 (1810). This self-deprecating “practice of 
strict construction of statutes authorizing appeals,” the 
Court has emphasized, “dictates that we not give an 
expansive interpretation” of such laws. Fornaris v. Ridge 
Tool Co., 400 U.S. 41, 42 n.1 (1976) (per curiam). Even 


42 


when “there is a present need to expand the jurisdiction 
of [the federal] courts,” the Court has reminded litigants 
that it will not “overlook the fact that the Constitution 
specifically vests that power in the Congress, not in the 
courts.” Snyder v. Harris, 394 U.S. 332, 341-42 (1969). 


This custom of jurisdictional self-restraint makes 
sense for the branch of government responsible for 
reviewing every other branch’s compliance with the rule 
of law. By erring on the side of denying for itself rather 
than seizing power, by ensuring in other words compli- 
ance with the Constitution’s delegation of authority to 
Congress to regulate the Court’s appellate jurisdiction, 
the Court “teaches the whole people by its example.” 
Olmstead v. United States, 277 U.S. 438, 485 (1928) 
(Brandeis, J., dissenting). 


More than just example, however, explains the 
Court’s discretion in this area. When the Court interprets 
grants of authority from Congress to itself, it wears two 
hats: It not only must construe the reach of power, but it 
then may exercise it as well. That distinguishes the dis- 
pute from all others in which the Court referees the 
extent of legislative or constitutional delegations of 
power. For example, when the Court construes a grant of 
power, say, to executive agencies, it remains appropriate 
to draw inferences from congressional ambiguity in favor 
of jurisdiction for the executive branch. See Chevron, 
U.S.A. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, 467 U.S. 837 
(1984). But, when the Court construes congressional 
grants of authority to itself, it acts as a referee and a 
player, making reticence uniquely appropriate. In the 
end, no rule of construction in favor of jurisdiction for the 
Court, or any other type of default rule (compare Pet. Br. 


43 


34-35, 40; U.S. Br. 14), applies here. If anything, the 
Court’s careful practices point precisely in the opposite 
direction. 

In the final analysis, however, default rules and other 
ambiguity crutches are not needed to resolve this dispute. 
The unmistakable words of the provisions themselves 
make clear that Mr. Hohn has just one mechanism, not 
three, for obtaining leave to appeal. It is the certificate of 


appealability that Congress explicitly provided by enact- 
ing 28 U.S.C. 2253(c)(1).

CONCLUSION 


For the foregoing reasons, the Court should dismiss 
the petition for lack of jurisdiction.

IV. Supreme Court Rule 20.1 


Issuance by the Court of an extraordinary writ autho- 
rized by 28 U.S.C. 1651(a) is not a matter of right, but of 
discretion sparingly exercised. To justify the granting of 
any writ, the petition must show that the writ will be in 
aid of the Court’s appellate jurisdiction, that exceptional 
circumstances warrant the exercise of the Court's discre- 
tionary powers, and that adequate relief cannot be 
obtained in any other form or from any other court.
