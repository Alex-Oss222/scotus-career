Statement of the Case 


This case raises important questions relating to the 
power of this Court to entertain petitions for certiorari from 
state and federal prisoners who have been denied Certificates of 
Appealability from federal courts of appeals in seeking review 
of the denial of habeas corpus relief by the district courts. 
Historically, it had been only state prisoners seeking to appeal 
who were required to obtain certificates of probable cause to 
bring their cases before federal courts of appeals. See Ira P. 
Robbins, The Habeas Corpus Certificate of Probable Cause, 44 
Ohio St. L.J. 307, 313-15 (1983). 


However, the passage of the Antiterrorism and Effective 
Death Penalty Act of 1996, Pub. L. 104-132, 110 Stat. 1214, 
effective April 24, 1996, newly codified at 28 U.S.C. §2253, 
now requires federal prisoners to obtain Certificates of 
Appealability in order to receive plenary appellate review of 
their §2255 motions. Under AEDPA, these certificates may be 
issued only where the petitioner makes “a substantial showing 
of the denial of a constitutional night.” 28 U.S.C. §2253(c). 


The significance of the denial of this certificate is that 
courts of appeals summarily dismiss habeas appeals with the 
consequence that the litigant’s case is not deemed to have 
properly been “in” the court of appeals for certiorari purposes 
under this Court’s relevant jurisdictional statute. 28 U.S.C. 
§1254(1). See, eg., Davis v. Jacobs, 454 U.S. 911, 912-19 
(1981)(Stevens, J., opinion respecting denial of certiorari); id. 
at 920-22 (Rehnquist, J., joined by Burger, C.J., and Powell, J., 
dissenting from denial of certiorari); Jeffries v. Barksdale, 453 
US. 914, 915-16 (1981)(Rehnquist, J., joined by Burger, C.J., 
and Powell, J., dissenting from denial of certiorari). See 
especially, Robbins, 44 Ohio St. LJ. at 308-12 & n27

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


1. This Court has statutory jurisdiction (28 U.S.C. 
§1254(1)) by which to entertain a federal petitioner’s post- 
conviction certiorari petition absent a certificate of appeal- 
ability from the court of appeals (28 U.S.C. §2253(c)). This 
Court clearly has the power to review courts of appeals’ 
jurisdictional determinations. For those jurisdictional purposes, 
cases are deemed sufficiently “in” the court of appeals, under 
§1254, even where the court of appeals has determined that it 
lacked appellate jurisdiction. Moreover, where the court of 
appeals has combined merits review alongside its jurisdictional 
gatekeeper function, there can be no serious question that the 
case was effectively “in” the court of appeals sufficiently for this 
Court to exercise statutory certiorari jurisdiction. 


2. This Court routinely invokes its common law certiorari 
authority under the All Writs Act (28 U.S.C. §1651) where the 
Court lacks statutory jurisdiction because the court of appeals 
declined to issue the necessary leave to appeal. This practice 
dates back to House v. Mayo, 324 U.S. 42 (1945) where the 
Court determined it had the power under common law cer- 
tiorari not only to review the refusal to allow the appeal, but 
also to review the question on the merits raised by the appeal. 
Nothing contained in AEDPA’s recent revision of the habeas 
statutes in any respect interferes with this Court’s common law 
certiorari authority in this case. 


3. The newly-enacted appellate certification requirement 
(§2253(c)(1)) limits the authorities empowered to issue a 
certificate to “a circuit justice or judge”. Accordingly, plenary 


prisoners by itself mandates that Hohn’s case be remanded to the court of 
appeals for plenary merits review on appeal. 


6 


appellate review should be afforded whenever either an 
individual circuit justice (of this Court) or a circuit judge (of 
the court of appeals) certifies that the habeas petitioner has 
made “a substantial showing of the denial of a constitutional 
right”. §2253(c)(2). In this case, a dissenting circuit judge in 
the court of appeals has implicitly made this certification. A 
member of this Court, nonetheless, is also empowered to issue 
similar certification, which would have the effect of remanding 
this case to the court of appeals for plenary merits review. 


4. AEDPA’s appellate certification requirement does not 
apply retroactively to federal petitioners whose §2255 motions 
were pending in the district court prior to the date of the 
statute’s enactment -- April 24, 1996. Lindh v. Murphy, 117 
S.Ct. 2059 (1997). Since §2255 petitioners were entitled to 
appeal as of right under the predecessor statute to §2253, this 
case must be remanded to the court of appeals for plenary 
merits review. 


ARGUMENT 


I. THIS COURT HAS STATUTORY JURISDICTION 
BY WHICH TO ENTERTAIN A FEDERAL PRI- 
SONER’S CERTIORARI PETITION ABSENT A 

CERTIFICATE OF APPEALABILITY FROM THE 
COURT OF APPEALS, WHERE THE LOWER 
COURT HAS NEVERTHELESS ADDRESSED 
THE MERITS OF HIS §2255 CLAIMS 


The threshold question raised in this case is whether, in 
light of the court of appeals’ denial of a certificate of 
appealability, this case was adequately “in” the court of appeals 
to properly invoke this Court’s certiorari jurisdiction. 28 
U.S.C. §1254(1). 


7 


While it is clear, as discussed in Points II and III, infra, 
that this Court has several alternative bases upon which to 
exercise jurisdiction in this matter -- under the All Writs Act (28 
U.S.C. §1651) as well as the habeas statutes themselves (28 
U.S.C. §§2241(a) & 2253(c)(1)) -- this Court also possesses its 
customary statutory jurisdiction under §1254. See, e.g., Nixon 
vy. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 731, 741-43 & n.23 (1982); United 
States v. Nixon, 418 U.S. 683, 690 (1974). Moreover, in light 
of the court of appeals in this case having, in effect, conducted 
plenary merits review along with its decision to deny the 
certificate -- a split decision rendered by a divided court -- this 
case was sufficiently “in” the court of appeals notwithstanding 
its denial of a certificate to entitle this Court to exercise its 
statutory jurisdiction. Barefoot v. Estelle, 463 U.S. 880, 889- 
92, 894 (1983). 


As one set of commentators has candidly observed, 
“[p]eculiar difficulties have been encountered in establishing 
review of cases that arguably are not ‘in’ the court of appeals 
because of failure to secure a required permission for appeal.” 
17 Charles A. Wright, Arthur R. Miller, Edward H. Cooper, 
Federal Practice and Procedure §4036 at 14 (2d ed. 1988); see 
generally id. at 10-19. 


Nevertheless, this Court has determined that even where 
the court of appeals had erroneously dismissed an appeal “ 
lack of jurisdiction, the case was deemed sufficiently “in” 
court of appeals for purposes of §1254 that it was aiety 
within its certiorari jurisdiction. Nixon v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 
at 743 n.23 (“There can be no serious doubt concerning our 
power to review a court of appeals’ decision to dismiss for 
lack of jurisdiction -- a power we have exercised routinely”). 
Indeed, Fitzgerald confirmed that this Court may review a 
ruling of the court of appeals that it lacked jurisdiction even if 
the result is to affirm the ruling as correct. Id. at 741-43. 


In the case at bar, where the court of appeals -- 
obviously taking its cue from Barefoot -- has combined merits 
review alongside its quasi-jurisdictional gatekeeper function, 
there can be no serious question that for all practical purposes, 
this case was effectively “in” the court of appeals sufficient to 
pass muster under §1254(1). See 17 Wright et al., §4036 at 16 
(“[S}tatutory certiorari should be available. Leave to appeal can 
be denied only after the court of appeals has so far considered 
the merits of the questions presented as to have brought the 
case within any reasonable purpose of the requirement that it be 
in that court. Indeed, statutory certiorari should be available in 
any other case in which it is claimed that a court of appeals has 
improperly denied leave to appeal” )(and authorities collected 
therein). 


It is axiomatic, then, that where the court of appeals has 
combined merits review alongside its quasi-jurisdictional 
gatekeeping function, it would be the ultimate exaltation of 
form over substance to assert seriously that this case was not 
adequately “in” the court of appeals sufficiently to qualify for 
statutory certiorari. Particularly where the lower court itself 
divided over the very merits of Hohn’s Bailey claim, it may 
hardly be gainsaid that that court had, in effect, already granted 
him precisely the sort of plenary merits review to which a 
certificate of appealability would have otherwise entitled him. 
That being the case, Hohn should be entitled to invoke directly 
this Court’s statutory certiorari jurisdiction. 


Il. THIS COURT MAY EXERCISE JURISDICTION 
BY INVOKING COMMON LAW CERTIORARI 
UNDER THE ALL WRITS ACT WHERE STATU- 
TORY CERTIORARI IS UNAVAILABLE 


As Justice Stevens noted in Davis, 454 U.S. at 912-14, 


9 


this Court may (and routinely does) invoke its common law 
certiorari authority under the All Writs Act (28 U.S.C. §1651) 
in circumstances virtually identical to those in the case at bar -- 
where the Court lacks statutory certiorari jurisdiction because 
the court of appeals declined to issue the necessary leave to 
appeal. See Robbins, 44 Ohio St. L.J. at 312 n.27. 


In House v. Mayo, 324 U.S. 42 (1945), this Court 
determined that it lacked certiorari jurisdiction under the 
predecessor statute to §1254 where the lower court had denied 
a certificate of probable cause to appeal. Nevertheless, relying 
on prior Court decisions defining the scope of the common law 
writ of certiorari under the All Writs Act, the Court found the 
power not only to review the refusal to allow the appeal, but 
also to review the question on the merits raised by the appeal. 
324 US. at 44-45.‘ 


Accordingly, since House, the arguable unavailability of 
statutory certiorari has routinely been cured by use of common 
law certiorari, which is now the customary vehicle invoked 
where a petitioner has been unable to secure permission to 
appeal or leave to appeal in forma pauperis from the court of 
appeals. See 17 Wright, §4036 at 14-16 (and cases collected 
therein); see also id., §4005 at 111-12. Indeed, the practice has 
become so commonplace that the Court no longer distinguishes 
simple denials of statutory certiorari from refusals to invoke 
common law certiorari. Davis, 454 U.S. at 914; cf Head v. 
California, 374 U.S. 509 (1963)(application for common law 
certiorari granted as petition for statutory certiorari); Carafas v. 
LaVallee, 391 U.S. 234 (1968)(accord); as well as the cases 
cited earlier in this brief, ante, at 3 n.2. 


4 Professor Oaks provides the most thorough discussion of the 
evolution of common law certiorari culminating in House and Holiday v. 
Johnston, 313 U.S. 342 (1941). See Oaks, 1962 Sup. Ct. Rev. at 182-88. 


10 


Nor have Congress’ recent habeas revisions under 
AEDPA in any respect abrogated this Court’s statutory (or 
common law) certiorari jurisdiction. In Felker v. Turpin, 116 
S.Ct. 2333 (1996), this Court determined that while newly- 
amended §2244(b)(3)(E) explicitly precluded this Court from 
reviewing by way of certiorari a court of appeals’ refusal to 
entertain a successive writ application, it did not otherwise 
interfere with this Court’s power to entertain a successive writ 
application as an original matter in the Court. Id. at 2338-39. 


Certainly, if amended §2244(b) -- which explicitly 
abrogated statutory certiorari in successive writ cases -- was not 
deemed a repeal by implication of this Court’s common law 
habeas authority, then a fortion §2253(c)(1) -- which contains 
no language respecting the Court’s certiorari authority and, 
indeed, explicitly authorizes “a circuit justice” to issue a 
certificate of appealability -- may hardly be construed to abolish 
or in any respect interfere with this Court’s common law (or 
statutory) certiorari jurisdiction. As long ago as 1821, in Chief 
Justice Marshall’s opinion in Cohens v. Virginia, 19 U.S. (6 
Wheat.) 264 (1821), this Court required a clear statement 
before concluding that Congress intended to affect this Court’s 
appellate jurisdiction. Id. at 379-80.° 


Accordingly, this Court remains free to exercise its 
common law certiorari authority under the All Writs Act and 
remand this case to the court of appeals, as suggested by the 
Acting Solicitor General.

Ill. A CERTIFICATE OF APPEALABILITY MAY BE 
ISSUED BY AN INDIVIDUAL CIRCUIT JUSTICE 
OR CIRCUIT JUDGE 


The newly-enacted appellate certification requirement 
(§2253(c)(1)) limits the authorities empowered to issue a 
certificate to “a circuit justice or judge”, thereby leaving an 
open question whether the certificate is to be issued by an 
individual justice or circuit judge as opposed to the Court itself 
or a three-judge panel. The distinction is not a trivial one -- 
compare §2253(c\(1)(“a circuit justice or judge”) with 
§2244(b)\(3)(B)(authorization for permission to file second or 
successive petition must be granted by “a three-judge panel of 
the court of appeals”). 


Congress’ choice of different language for different 
provisions of the AEDPA clearly manifests an intention to 
create different procedures for separate provisions of the Act. 
See Lindh, 117 S.Ct. at 2063 (chapter 153 interpreted 
prospectively in the absence of chapter 154's specific 
retroactivity language). While a court of appeals may choose 
to assign such motions to three-judge panels, see In re Burwell, 
350 U.S. at 522, it cannot thereby repeal petitioner’s statutory 
night to appeal with the permission of a single judge. Compare 


Ojeda Rios v. Wigen, 863 F.2d 196, 199-200 & n.3 (2d Cir. 
1988)(Newman, J., in chambers)(discussing relevance and 


importance of distinctions between “judges” and “courts” in 
context of habeas corpus).°

IV. AEDPA’S APPELLATE CERTIFICATION 
REQUIREMENT DOES NOT APPLY RET- 
ROACTIVELY TO CASES COMMENCED 
PRIOR TO ITS EFFECTIVE DATE 


In Lindh vy. Murphy, this Court held that AEDPA’s 
amendments to Chapter 153 of Title 28, United States Code -- 
including §2253(c), the provision requiring certificates of 
appealability -- do not apply to cases that were already pending 
on the date AEDPA was signed into law -- April 24, 1996. 117 
S.Ct. at 2063 (“The statute reveals Congress’s intent to apply 
the amendments to chapter 153 only to such cases as were filed 
after the statute’s enactment * * * We read this provision ... as 
indicating implicitly that the amendments to chapter 153 were 
assumed and meant to apply to the general run of habeas cases 
only when those cases had been filed after the date of the Act”). 
The government concedes here that Hohn’s §2255 petition was 
already pending in the district court prior to AEDPA’s enact- 
ment. 


AEDPA’s nonretroactivity is significant in the context 
of this case. Under prior law, federal §2255 petitioners were 
not required to obtain permission to appeal to the court of 
appeals, their habeas appeals, unlike those of state prisoners, 
were heard as a matter of right. See Robbins, 44 Ohio St. L_J. 
at 313-15. 


Accordingly, under Lindh, federal petitioners such as 
Hohn are entitled to automatic plenary merits review in the 
court of appeals where their §2255 petitions were pending in 
the district court prior to April 24, 1996. Independent of all 
other grounds for relief in this case, the court of appeals’ retro- 
active misapplication of the appellate certification procedure to 
Hohn has created the anomalous situation in which it may have 
improperly deprived this Court of its own statutory jurisdiction 


14 


to review a jurisdictional error committed by the lower court 
insofar as it declined to exercise mandatory jurisdiction over an 
appeal it was legally required to accord plenary merits review. 
See Kerr v. U.S. District Court, 426 U.S. 394 (1976)(common 
law writs used to correct lower courts’ jurisdictional errors), 
Will v. United States, 389 U.S. 90, 95 (1967)(common law writ
