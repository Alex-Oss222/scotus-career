QUESTION PRESENTED 

Whether petitioner's motion under 28 U.S.C. 2255, which 
alleged that his conviction under 18 U.S.C. 924(c) for using and 
carrying a firearm during and relation to a drug trafficking crime 
could not stand under this Court's later decision narrowing the 
"use" element in Bailey v. United States, 116 S. Ct. 501 (1995), 
made a purely statutory claim, or made a "substantial showing of 
the denial of a constitutional right" as required to obtain a 


certificate of appealability under 28 U.S.C. 2253(c) (2).

- On December 6, 1995, this Court held, in Bailey v. United 
States, 116 S. Ct. 501 (1995), that a conviction for use of a 
firearm under Section 924(c) "requires evidence sufficient to show 
an active employment of the firearm by the defendant." Id. at 505. 
Active employment includes uses such as "brandishing, displaying, 
bartering, striking with, and most obviously, firing or attempting 
to fire" the weapon. Jd. at 508. By contrast, possession of a 
firearm -- without more -- does not constitute a "use," nor does 
"placement of a firearm to provide a sense of security or to 
embolden[.}]" JIbid. 

4. Petitioner filed a pro se motion under 28 U.S.C. 2255 
making two claims. First, he contended that, under Bailey, the 
facts established at his trial were insufficient to support a 
conviction for violating Section 924(c)(1). Petitioner acknowl- 
edged that he had not raised such an insufficiency claim on direct 
appeal, but argued that his default should be excused because his 
conduct “was mot a crime” and, therefore, it would be a 
"fundamental miscarriage of justice” not to review his claim. 
Mc-ion to Vacate, Set Aside or Correct Sentence Pursuant to 28 


S.C. § 2255, at 8. Second, petitioner challenged the jury 


4

did not properly define the phrase “use a firearm" to require proof 
of active use, and that correct jury instructions would likely have 
produced an acquittal. Jg. at 11. 

In response, the government contended that petitioner was not 
entitled to relief under Bailey because the evidence at trial was 
sufficient to show that petitioner had "carried" a firearm. Noting 
that the police found weapons in holsters within arm's reach of 
methamphetamine in petitioner's kitchen, the government argued that 
the jury could infer that petitioner had “entered his residence and 
placed his belongings, which included the methamphetamine and the 
firearms, onto the counter." Pet. App. 25a-26a. The government 
acknowledged that, under Bailey, the jury instructions at 
petitioner's trial erroneously defined the word "use," but it 
contended that the error was harmless because "(t]he evidence 
obviously establishes ‘carrying.'" Jd. at 22a-26a. 

The district court held that petitioner had waived his Bailey 
claim by failing to raise it on direct appeal. Pet. App. 8a-9a. 
In so holding, the court relied on United States v. McKinney, 79 
F.3d 105, 109 (8th Cir. 1996), in which the Eighth Circuit held 
that a defendant who had not raised a Bailey claim at trial or in 
his initial brief on direct appeal had waived it. This Court 
recently vacated and remanded McKinney for further consideration in 
light of Johnson v. Wnited States, 117 S. Ct. 1544 (1997).

DISCUSSION 
Petitioner seeks review of the court of appeals' holding that 


he was not entitled to a certificate of appealability because his 


Claim -- that he is presently incarcerated for conduct that, under 
Bailey, is not criminal -- raises a statutory, not a constitu- 
tional, issue. We agree that claims of the kind raised by 


petitioner in his motion under Section 2255 may fairly be 
characterized as "constitutional," and that the court of appeals 
erred in concluding otherwise. Accordingly, we believe that the 
judgment of the court of appeals should be vacated and the case 
remanded for further consideration of whether a certificate of 
appealability should be granted here. 

1. In Bailey, this Court "clarif[ied) the meaning of ‘use’ 
under § 924(c)(1)" by looking to "the language of the statute" and 
"consider[ing) not only the bare meaning of the word ['use'] but 
also its placement and purpose in the statutory scheme." 116 S. 
Ct. at 506. That holding resolved a question of statutory 
construction. The Court did not purport to apply or construe the 
Constitution, but rather "determine[d)]) the meaning Congress 
intended." Id. at 505-506. Thus, "Bailey did not establish a new 
rule of constitutional law, but simply interpreted a substantive 
criminal statute." In re Dorsainvil, No. 96-8074, 1997 WL 409442 
De (3d Cir. July 23, 1997) (citing cases from the Fourth, 
Seventh, Tenth, and Eleventh Circuits agreeing with that 
proposition). 


The fact that petitioner's Section 2255 motion invoked a 


8 
statutory holding of this Court, however, does not mean that his 
claim is purely a statutory one. In bringing a collateral 
challenge to the sufficiency of the evidence and the adequacy of 


the jury instructions, a movant under Section 2255 is entitled to 


rely on the statutory meaning of the word "use" as later : 


interpreted by this Court in Bailey.* A contention that, in light 
of Bailey, the evidence was insufficient to support a conviction 
under Section 924(c)(1) may state a claim under the Due Process 
Clause. See Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 307, 313-316 (1979) (Due 
Process Clause requires that a criminal conviction rest on evidence 
sufficient to support a conclusion by a rational trier of fact that 
every element of the offense has been established beyond a 
reasonable doubt). A contention that the jury instructions failed 
to require the jury to make findings on every essential element of 
the offense under Section 924(c), as construed in Bailey, may state 
a claim under the Fifth and Sixth Amendments. See United States v. 
Gaudin, 115 S. Ct. 2310, 2320 (1995) ("The Constitution gives a 
criminal defendant the right to have a jury determine, beyond a 
reasonable doubt, his guilt of every element of the crime with 
which he is charged."). Petitioner raised claims of both varieties

Claims of the sort that petitioner raised do not automatically 
satisfy Section 2253(c) (2)'s requirement of a "gubstantial showing 
of the denial of a constitutional right." For example, a claim 
under Section 924(c) that the evidence was insufficient to show a 
"use" of a firearm in light of Bailey would not constitute a 
substantial showing of the denial of a constitutional right absent 
an indication that the evidence was insufficient to show a 
"carrying." See United States v. Eyer, 113 F.3d 470, 475-476 (3d 
Cir. 1997) (Section 924(c) conviction can be sustained based on 
evidence of "carry" without regard to "“use”). But Section 
2253(c)(3) does not require certainty that the collateral 
petitioner would prevail. See Barefoot v. Estelle, 463 U.S. 880, 
893 n.4 (1983) (to make a substantial showing, a petitioner must 
demonstrate that the issues are "'debatable among jurists of 
reason; that a court could resolve the issues [in a different 
manner); or that the questions are adequate to deserve 
encouragement to proceed further.'") (quoting Gordon v. Willis, 516 
F. Supp. 911, 913 (N.D. Ga. 1980) (emphasis in original; internal 


quotation marks omitted)). The court of appeals did not evaluate

2. Although we believe that a remand is required to 
reconsider whether petitioner can make a "substantial showing of 
the denial of a constitutional right," no remand is needed to 
reconsider the threshold issue of whether petitioner was required 
to obtain a certificate of appealability. The petition does not 
ask this Court to review whether the certificate-of -appealability 
requirement applies in this case. In Lindh v. Murphy, 117 S. Ct. 
2059 (1997), however, which was decided after the petition was 
filed, this Court concluded that Congress generally intended "to 
apply the [AEDPA]) amendments to chapter 153 only to such cases as 
were filed after the statute's enactment." Id. at 2063, see id. at 
2068. In so holding, the Court reasoned that the express provision 
that the Act's amendments to Chapter 154 of Title 28 "shall apply 
to cases pending," AEDPA § 107(c), implied that the Act's 
amendments to Chapter 153 of Title 28 should not apply to pending 
cases. Id. at 2063. 

The Act's provisicn requiring a certificate of appealability 
for federal prisoners is an amendment to Chapter 153 of Title 28; 
accordingly, under Lindh, that provision does not apply to "cases 
pending" on April 24, 1996, the effective date of the Act. The 
facts of Lindh, however, did not involve a petition for post- 


conviction relief in which the appeal was filed after the effective 


11 

date of the Act. See id. at 2061-2062. And the Court did not 
address whether, for purposes of the applicability of the 
certificate-of-appealability requirement, the "case" that has to be 
pending on the effective date of the Act is the "case" on appeal or 
the original "case" filed in the district court. In this instance, 
petitioner's motion for relief was filed in the district court 
before the effective date of the Act, but his case on appeal was 
filed after the effective date of the Act. We believe that such a 
case is subject to the Act's certificate of appealability 
requirement because the appeal -- the relevant case -- was neither 
"filed" nor "pending" on April 24, 1996.4

court of appeals, Tiedeman contended that Section 2253(c)(1)'s 
certificate-of-appealability requirement did not apply in his case
