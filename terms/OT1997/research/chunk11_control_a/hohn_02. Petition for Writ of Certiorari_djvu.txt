STATUTORY PROVISIONS INVOLVED 

Section 2253(c) of Title 28 of the United States Code provides, in pertinent part: “Unless 
a Circuit justice or judge issues a certificate of appealability, an appeal may not be taken to the 
court of appeals from * * * the final order in a proceeding under Section 2255. * * * A 
certificate of appealability may issue * * * only if the applicant has made a substantial showing 
of the denial of a constitutional right.” 

The Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment of the Constitution of the United States 
provides, in pertinent part: “No person shall * * * be deprived of * * * liberty * * * without 
due process of law * * *.” 

Section 924(c)(1) of Title 18 of the United States Code provides, in pertinent part: 


“Whoever, during and in relation to any crime of violence or drug trafficking crime * * * uses

or carries a firearm, shall, in addition to the punishment provided for such crime * * *, be 
sentenced to imprisonment for five years * * *, nor shall the term of imprisonment imposed 
under this subsection run concurrently with any other term of imprisonment * * *.” 
STATEMENT 

Arnold Hohn was convicted and is presently incarcerated for conduct — purportedly 
“using” a firearm by merely having it “available” to him during his possession of a controlled 
substance — which this Court held in Bailey v. United States, 116 S. Ct. 501 (1995), is not a 
crime. After learning of the Bailey decision, Hohn filed a motion secking collateral relief 
pursuant to 18 U.S.C. § 2255 from his unlawful incarceration. 

The district court denied Hohn’s collateral challenge, finding that he had “waived” it by 
failing to prognosticate that this Court, several years after Hohn’s conviction had become final, 
would reverse well-settled precedent and redefine the meaning of the criminal statute under 
which he was charged. Pet. App. 8a-9a. The court of appeals refused to accept Hohn's appeal, 
relying on a provision of the new Anti-Terrorism and Effective Death Penalty Act (AEDPA), 
28 U.S.C. 2253(c), which allows an appeal to be taken from the denial of a Section 2255 motion 
only upon the issuance of a “certificate of appealability” (which in turn requires the movant to 
make “a substantial showing of the denial of a constitutional right”). 28 U.S.C. § 2253(c). In 
the court of appeal’s view, Hohn’s claim that he was incarcerated for conduct that was not a 
crime did not meet that standard because Hohn was asserting the denial of a “statutory” rather 
than a “constitutional” right.

REASONS FOR GRANTING THE PETITION 

I, THE QUESTION WHETHER A CERTIFICATE OF APPEALABILITY SHOULD 

ISSUE UPON A SECTION 2255 PETITIONER’S SUBSTANTIAL SHOWING 

THAT HE IS INCARCERATED FOR CONDUCT THIS COURT HAS HELD IS 

NOT A CRIME IS IMPORTANT AND RECURRING 

A. The Proper Interpretation of Section 2253(c)’s Appealability Requirement of 

a “Substantial Showing of the Denial of a Constitutional Right” Is a Question 
of Great Importance and Will Affect Countless Cases 

Section 2253(c)(2) of the AEDPA is the first limitation Congress has imposed on the 
availability of appellate review of federal prisoners’ collateral challenges to their convictions 
under Section 2255 since that statute was adopted almost fifty years ago. The proper scope of 
this new limitation on appellate review is an important and recurring issue of federal law that 
will significantly affect the proper administration of justice in the federal courts. 

It cannot seriously be disputed that the federal courts will frequently be required to 
interpret and apply Section 2253(c)(2). As this Court is well aware, innumerable Section 2255 
petitions are filed every year. In every Section 2255 case in which relief is denied, the 


petitioner will have an incentive to take an appeal; and, under the AEDPA, no appeal will lie 


unless a certificate of appealability is issued. The question presented in this case — whether 


Section 2253(c)’s reference to “constitutional rights” restricts the type of cases that may be 
appealed, and in particular forecloses an appeal such as petitioner's — therefore can be expected 
to arise with great regularity in the federal courts. 

This Court often renders substantive law decisions narrowing the perceived reach of 
criminal statutes.’ Each such decision calls into question the validity of hundreds of convictions 
premised on the lower courts’ erroneously broad constructions.* Section 2255 motions are the 
primary avenue of relief for such wrongfully incarcerated persons. Literally hundreds of Section 
2255 challenges have been filed as a consequence of this Court's decision in Bailey alone. Thus, 
the question whether Section 2253(c) forecloses appeal as an avenue of relief for persons who 
are incarcerated for engaging in lawful conduct, as the court below held, will arise often. The 
same question of appealability presented here also will arise in al/ unsuccessful collateral 
challenges premised on statutory grounds. 

In addition to arising with great frequency, the proper meaning of Section 2253(c) is an

c. The Courts of Appeals Are Deeply Divided Over Whether Section 2253(c) 
Permits Appeal of Meritorious Federal Statutory Claims 


“There is a difference of opinion” among the federal courts of appeal on the question 
presented here: “whether § 2253(c), which provides that a certificate of appealability may issue 
‘only if the applicant has made a substantial showing of the denial of a constitutional right,’ 


modifies preexisting law, which defined the required showing as, a ‘substantial showing of the 


denial of [a] federal right.” Hunter v. Bailey, 101 F.3d 1565, 1571 (11th Cir. 1996). “It is 
undecided” among the federal courts “whether Congress intended to change the standard, along 
with the name, for granting a certificate of appealability,” thereby foreclosing appeals by persons 
who claim to be held in violation of federal statutory rights. Santana, 98 F.3d at 757. 

The Ninth Circuit has concluded that the prerequisite showing of a “denial of a 
constitutional right” in Section 2253(c) is “more demanding” than the “federal right” standard 
this Court set forth in Barefoot. Williams v. Calderon, 83 F.3d 281, 286 (9th Cir. 1996). In 
contrast, the Second, Fifth, and Tenth Circuits have concluded that Congress intended to codify, 
and did not alter, the Barefoot “federal right” standard. See Drinkard v. Johnson, 97 F.3d 752, 
756 (Sth Cir. 1996), cert. denied, 117 S. Ct. 1114 (1997); Reyes v. Keane, 90 F.3d 676, 679- 
680 (2d Cir. 1996); Lennox v. Evans, 87 F.3d 431, 434 (10th Cir. 1996), cert. denied, 117 S. 
Ct. 746 (1997). Each of those circuits, however, appears to interpret that standard as 
foreclosing appeals of statutory claims. See Drinkard, 97 F.3d at 756; Reyes, 90 F.3d at 679- 
680; Lennox, 87 F.3d at 434. The Third Circuit has suggested an entirely different 
interpretation, directly at odds with the Eighth Circuit's decision in Hohn: that Congress’ use 
of the term “constitutional right” is attributable to “imperfect draftsmanship,” and that Section 
2253(c) may properly be read to permit appeals of statutory claims. Santana, 98 F.3d at 757.

without handling it, and a firearm can be carried without being used, e.g., when an offender 
keeps a gun hidden in his clothing throughout a drug transaction.” Bailey, 116 S. Ct. at 507 
(emphasis added). As the highlighted language makes clear, this Court in Bailey had in mind 
the ordinary meaning of “carrying”; and the opinion’s clear intimation that a firearm that is 
bartered for drugs would not be carried if it is not handled, confirms that having a firearm in 
one’s hands or one’s person is a necessary precondition to carrying it. Also significant is this 
Court's rejection in Bailey of the government's contention that there could be liability under 
Section 924(c) merely by virtue of a defendant's “placement of a firearm to provide a sense of 


security or to embolden.” 116 S. Ct. at 508. The Court explained: 


38 


It follows, according to this argument, that a gun placed in a closet is “used,” because 
its mere presence emboldens or protects its owner. We disagree. Under this argument, 
mere possession of a firearm by a drug offender, at or near the site of a drug crime or 
its proceeds or paraphernalia, is a “use” by the offender, because its availability for 
intimidation, attack, or defense would always, presumably, embolden or comfort the 
offender. But the mere inert presence of a firearm, without more, is not enough to 
trigger § 924(c)(1). Perhaps the nonactive nature of this asserted “use” is clearer if a 
synonym is used: storage. A defendant cannot be charged under § 924(c)(1) merely for 
storing a weapon near drugs or drug proceeds. Storage of a fiream, without its more 
active employment, is not reasonably distinguishable from possession. 
Ibid. (emphasis added). Although the foregoing discussion was directed at “use” liability, there 
is no reason why a similarly broad reading of “carrying” liability — to include mere possession 
of a firearm in proximity to drugs — would “be reasonably distinguishable from possession” 
either. 

Finally, even if the Court were to conclude that the language, structure, and legislative 
history of Section 924(c)(1) do not compel the interpretation of “carry” suggested above, there 
can be no serious doubt that our interpretation is a reasonable one, and that the statute is 
therefore ar least ambiguous on this score. Under the rule of lenity, “ambiguity concerning the 
ambit of criminal statutes should be resolved in favor of lenity.” Rewis v. United States, 401 
U.S. 808, 812 (1971) (citing Bell v. United States, 349 U.S. 81, 83 (1955)). That principle is 
dispositive here. 

In sum, the crime of “carrying” a firearm under Section 924(c) requires that a defendant 
bear the weapon on his person. There was no evidence in this case that Hohn “carried” the 
firearms in this sense, much less that he carried them “during” the predicate possessory offense. 
Although the government argued below that a fair inference of such conduct could have been 
drawn from the facts, that submission was simply incorrect. There was no evidence presented 


that Hohn ever carried the firearms located in his kitchen and in his bedroom drawers. In any 


39 
event, even if there were proof of carrying at some time by Hohn, there was no proof (much 
less proof beyond a reasonable doubt) that Hohn carried the firearms during the predicate 
offense. See United States v. Harrison, 103 F.3d 986, 990-92 (D.C. Cir. 1997). For all we 
know, any carrying that occurred could well have preceded Hohn’s coming into possession of 
the methamphetamine he was charged with possessing with intent to distribute. If so, then Hohn 
plainly did not violate Section 924(c) — under any view of the evidence. 


D. Because The Jury Instruction Grossly Misstated The Law, Petitioner Is 
Entitled, At A Minimum, To A New Trial 


As previously explained, Hohn is entitled to have his conviction set aside on the Section 
924(c) count because of this Court's decision in Bailey. But even if that were not the case, and 
the government had presented sufficient evidence from which the jury could conclude beyond 
a reasonable doubt that Hohn “carried” a firearm in violation of Section 924(c), he would be 
entitled to a new trial because of fundamental defects in the jury instructions. The jury was 
charged that 

[t}he phrase “used a firearm” means having a firearm available to aid in the commission 

of possession of Methamphetamine with intent to distribute. Similarly, the phrase 

“carried a firearm” does not require proof of actual possession of a firearm or use of it 

in any affirmative manner, but does require proof beyond a reasonable doubt that the 

firearm was available to provide protection in connection with the possession of 

Methamphetamine with intent to distribute or facilitate success. 
Pet. App. 3la (emphasis added). As previously noted (at note 23, supra), the government 
acknowledged that the part of the chagge, solating to firearm “use” was erroneous and would 
have qualified as “plain error” if use were the only basis for liability charged to the jury. 

The jury's instruction on the meaning of “carrying” liability, however, suffers from the 


same flaw as the “use” instruction. Indeed, “use” and “carry” are defined in the Eighth 


40 
Circuit's model instruction in a virtually identical fashion: both permit a finding of liability based 
solely on whether the firearm is “available” to assist the defendant during the predicate act. 
That theory of liability, however, was categorically rejected by this Court in Bailey. See 116 
S. Ct. at 508 (explaining that government's “possession for protection” theory “is not reasonably 
distinguishable from possession,” which Section 924(c) does not reach). Nor does the Eighth 
Circuit's instruction for “carrying” even remotely resemble the ordinary meaning of that term, 
when used in conjunction with weapons. Hohn could easily have “carried” the firearms within 
the meaning of the Eighth Circuit's instruction without bearing them on his person, without 
transporting them within immediate reach in a vehicle, and even without transporting them in 
an inaccessible place in a vehicle.” Indeed, the jury instruction did not even require a finding 
that Hohn posessed the firearms for a finding of “carrying.” At a minimum, petitioner is 
entitled to a new trial.
