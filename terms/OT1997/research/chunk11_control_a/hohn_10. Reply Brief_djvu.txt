REPLY BRIEF FOR THE UNITED STATES 


All of the participants in this case agree that, in 
House v. Mayo, 324 U.S. 42 (1945) (per curiam), this 
Court held that it lacked statutory certiorari juris- 
diction to review the refusal by a court of appeals to 
issue a certificate of probable cause but that the 
Court could nonetheless issue a common-law writ of 
certiorari under the All Writs Act, 28 U.S.C. 1651. 
U.S. Br. 21; Pet. Br. 13-15; Court-Appointed Amicus 
Curiae Br. (Amicus) 21, 35-36. In our view, the hold- 
ing in House controls this case. There is no meaning- 
ful distinction between the Court’s power at the time 
of House to review the decision of a court of appeals 
denying a certificate of probable cause and its power 


(1) 


2 


today to review the decision of a court of appeals 
denying a certificate of appealability. 

l. a. Petitioner requests (Pet. Br. 9) that this 
Court disregard stare decisis and overrule House’s 
holding that the Court lacks statutory certiorari 
jurisdiction on the grounds that the holding is incor- 
rect and “appears to have been renounced by this 
Court in subsequent decisions.” The basic premise of 
stare decisis, however, is that a prior decision is not 
to be disregarded merely because the Court now 
believes that it was wrongly decided. II]t is more 
important that the applicable rule of law be settled 
than that it be settled right.” Burnet v. Coronado 
Oil & Gas Co., 285 U.S. 393, 406 (1932) (Brandeis, J., 
dissenting). “Who ignores [stare decisis] must give 
reasons, and reasons that go beyond mere demonstra- 
tion that the overruled opinion was wrong (otherwise 
the doctrine would be no doctrine at all).” Hubbard v. 
United States, 514 U.S. 695, 716 (1995) (Scalia, J., 
joined by Kennedy, J., concurring in part and concur- 
ring in the judgment). Respect for precedent has 
“special force in the area of statutory interpretation, 
for * * * Congress remains free to alter what [this 
Court] hals] done.” Patterson v. McLean Credit 
Union, 491 U.S. 164, 172-173 (1989). Despite peti- 
tioner’s contention that later cases “appear{] to have 
* * * renounced” House’s holding (Pet. Br. 9), the 
Court’s opinions in those cases are silent on the 
jurisdictional question and therefore cannot be read 
to have overruled House sub silentio. See U.S. Br. 26. 

b. Petitioner also raises two issues as insurance, 
in case the Court overrules the second component of 
the holding in House and rejects its power to review 
the decision of the court of appeals by common-law 
certiorari. Petitioner first contends (Pet. Br. 40-44) 


3 


that he does not in fact need a certificate of appealabil- 
ity in order to appeal the merits of his Section 2255 
motion. That claim is not properly before the Court, 
because petitioner failed to raise the claim in the 
court of appeals or in his petition for certiorari, and 
because the claim is not fairly subsumed in the 
question on which the Court granted review. See 
Heckler v. Campbell, 461 U.S. 458, 468 n.12 (1983); 
Capital Cities Cable, Inc. v. Crisp, 467 U.S. 691, 697 
(1984); Yee v. City of Escondido, 503 U.S. 519, 535 
(1992). 

Petitioner’s claim lacks merit in any event. As we 
explained in our response to the petition for certio- 
rari (at 10-12), petitioner’s notice of appeal had not 
been filed at the time Congress enacted the Anti- 
Terrorism and Effective Death Penalty Act of 1996 
(AEDPA), Pub. L. No. 104-132, 110 Stat. 1214. There- 
fore, Lindh v. Murphy, 117 S. Ct. 2059, 2063 (1997), 
does not control petitioner’s case. Nor do the Court’s 
precedents regarding retroactive application of 
statutes, see, e.g., Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 
511 U.S. 244 (1994), prevent application to petitioner’s 
appeal of the requirement that petitioner obtain a 
certificate of appealability. That requirement is a 
jurisdictional prerequisite to consideration of the 
merits of petitioner’s Section 2255 motion by the 
court of appeals. See id. at 274 (noting that the Court 
has regularly applied statutes ousting jurisdiction to 
pending litigation). Moreover, petitioner’s Section 
2255 motion is a form of prospective relief. See ibid. 
(explaining that an individual does not have a “vested 
right” to relief that operates “in futuro”). The cer- 
tificate requirement is purely procedural and regu- 
lates “secondary rather than primary conduct.” Id. at 
275 (opining that procedural requirements “may often 


1 


be applied in suits arising before their enactment 
without raising concerns about retroactivity”). Fi- 
nally, because the district court had not ruled on 
petitioner’s motion when the AEDPA was enacted, 
the requirement that petitioner obtain a certificate 
was in place well before the time at which petitioner 
was obliged to seek one. Application of the require- 
ment to petitioner’s appeal is therefore prospective, 
and raises no retroactivity concerns. 

Petitioner’s additional fall-back claim, that this 
Court should itself grant a certificate of appealability 
(Pet. Br. 44-49), is similarly not properly before the 
Court in the present case. Petitioner raised that 
claim as well for the first time in his merits brief, and 
it too is not subsumec in the question on which the 
Court granted review. Even were the issue properly 
before the Court, petitioner is not entitled to a 
certificate from this Court or a Justice of the Court, 
as we have explained in our response to the applica- 
tion for a certificite of appealability that petitioner 
submitted on January 21, 1998. See U.S. Mem. in 
Opp., No. A-527 (filed Jan. 29, 1998). 

2. Like petitioner, amicus requests (Br. 35-36) that 
this Court disregard stare decisis and part of its 
holding in House. Unlike petitioner, however, amicus 
would have the Court jettison the part of House's 
holding that made clear that the Court may use 
common-law certiorari under the All Writs Act to 
review a court of appeals’ denial of a certificate 
authorizing appeal. Amicus offers three reasons for 
the Court to disregard House, none of which is 
persuasive. 

a. First, amicus attempts to distinguish House by 
asserting (Br. 3-4, 13, 24, 35-37) that a Justice of this 
Court may issue a certificate authorizing appeal un- 


5 


der current law but lacked that authority at the time 
House was decided. That assertion is incorrect. 
Moreover, even if it were correct, it would not deprive 
this Court of the power under the All Writs Act to 
review the decision of a court of appeals denying a 
certificate. 

Amicus bases his assertion that a Justice of this 
Court could not have issued a certificate at the time 
House was decided on 28 U.S.C. 466 (1940). See Br. 36, 
Al. That Section provided: 


From a final decision by a court of the United 
States in a proceeding in habeas corpus where the 
detention complained of is by virtue of process 
issued out of a State court no appeal to the circuit 
court of appeals shall be allowed unless the United 
States court by which the final decision was 
rendered or a judge of the circuit court of appeals 
shall be of opinion that there exists probable cause 
for an appeal, in which event, on allowing the same, 
the said court or judge shall certify that there is 
probable cause for such allowance. 


28 U.S.C. 466 (1940). The 1940 Code, however, used 
the term “judge of the circuit court of appeals” to 
include the Justice of this Court allotted to that 
circuit. See 28 U.S.C. 217 (1940) (“The words ‘circuit 
justice’ and ‘justice of a circuit’ shall be understood to 
designate the justice of the Supreme Court who is 
allotted to any circuit; but the word ‘judge’, when 
applied generally to any circuit, shall be understood 
to include such justice.”). See also In re Neagle, 135 
U.S. 1, 54 (1890) (Justices of this Court “are also 
judges of the Circuit Courts of the United States.”). 
Thus, the distinction that amicus would find between 


6 


the circumstances of this case and House simply does 
not exist. 

The power of a circuit justice at the time House was 
decided to issue a certificate is confirmed by review of 
Section 6(d) of the Act of February 13, 1925, ch. 229, 43 
Stat. 940, the statute on which the codifiers based the 
language of Section 466. That Section of the United 
States Code was not enacted into positive law, so it is 
only “prima facie” evidence of the law, which must be 
determined by reference to the language of the 
statute that Congress actually passed. See United 
States National Bank of Oregon v. Independent 
Insurance Agents of America, Inc., 508 U.S. 439, 448 
(1993); see also 1 U.S.C. 204(a). 

The Act, which changed the law to provide that 
appeals from habeas corpus cases would go to the 
circuit courts of appeals rather than to the Supreme 
Court, stated, in relevant part, that “the provisions of 
an Act entitled ‘An Act restricting in certain cases 
the right of appeal to the Supreme Court in habeas 
corpus proceedings,’ approved March 10, 1908, shall 
apply to appellate proceedings [in the circuit courts of 
appeal] as they heretofore have applied to direct 
appeals to the Supreme Court.” Act of Feb. 13, 1925, 
ch. 229, § 6(d), 3 Stat. 940. The Act of March 10, 1908, 
ch. 76, 35 Stat. 40, in turn, provided: 


That from a final decision by a court of the United 
States in a proceeding in habeas corpus where the 
detention complained of is by virtue of process 
issued out of a State court no appeal to the 
Supreme Court shall be allowed unless the United 
States court by which the final decision was 
rendered or a justice of the Supreme Court shall 
be of opinion that there exists probable cause 


7 


for an appeal, in which event, on allowing the same, 
the said court or justice shall certify that there is 
probable cause for such allowance. 


Section 6(d) clearly intended to make a certificate 
of probable cause a prerequisite to appeal to the 
circuit courts of appeals, but its intent to deprive a 
Justice of this Court of the ability to issue a 
certificate is far from clear. Tellingly, we could find 
nothing in the legislative history of the 1925 Act, 
which included an extensive list of the intended 
changes to existing law, to suggest that Congress 
meant for Section 6(d) to divest a Justice of the pre- 
existing power to grant a certificate. See American 
Hospital Ass’n v. NLRB, 499 U.S. 606, 613-614 (1991); 
Edmonds v. Compagnie Generale Transatlantique, 
443 U.S. 256, 266-267 (1979); Ex parte Peru, 318 U.S. 
578, 585 n. 4 (1943) (finding it “hardly conceivable” 
that the legislative history of the 1925 Act would lack 
any indication of an intent to limit this Court’s 
jurisdiction to issue extraordinary writs if the Act 
did so). Repeals of jurisdiction by implication are not 
favored. See Felker v. Turpin, 116 S. Ct. 2333, 2338- 
2339 (1996). Because Supreme Court Justices were 
also members of the Circuit Courts of Appeals when 
Section 6(d) was enacted, see Act of March 3, 1911, ch. 
231, S 117-121, 36 Stat. 1131-1132, the best reading of 
Section 6(d) is that all circuit judges, including the 
circuit justice, had the power to issue a certificate. 
Indeed, Congress confirmed that interpretation when 
it enacted Title 28 of the United States Code into 
positive law and explicitly provided that a “circuit 
justice or judge” could issue a certificate. See Act of 


8 


June 25, 1948, ch. 646, § 2253, 62 Stat. 967 (codified at 
28 U.S.C. 2253 (1952)). 

Even if it were true that a Justice of this Court can 
issue a certificate of appealability under current law 
but could not issue one when House was decided, 
there would be no reason to disregard the holding in 
House. Amicus contends (Br. 12-13, 19-20, 30-31, 35- 
37) that the power to issue a certificate makes using a 
common-law writ neither “necessary” nor “appropri- 
ate,” because granting the certificate directly is an 
“alternative” remedy. Amicus ignores, however, that 


The House Committee Report accompanying the 1948 Act 
does not indicate that the relevant Section of the Act made any 
substantive change but states rather that “[cJhanges were made 
in phraseology.” H.R. Rep. No. 308, 80th Cong., Ist Sess. A180 
(1947). We acknowledge that the proper interpretation of 28 
U.S.C. 217 (1940), which clarifies the meaning of the term 
“judge of the circuit court of appeals” in 28 U.S.C. 466 (1940), 
is not free from all doubt. Section 217 of the Code is based on 
Section 121 of the Act of March 3, 1911, ch. 231, 36 Stat. 1132. 
The statutory provision is identical to the code provision 
except that the statutory provision contains the phrase “when 
used in this title” after “justice of a circuit.” That phrase sug- 
gests that the provision was designed to clarify the terminology 
used in the “title” of which it was a part. Because neither 
Section 6(d) of the 1925 Act nor 28 U.S.C. 466 (1940) existed in 
1911, it is unclear whether they should be considered en- 
compassed in the same “title” as Section 121 of the 1911 Act. 
Clarification is provided, however, by the fact that Section 121 
of the 1911 Act reproduced verbatim Section 605 of the Revised 
Statutes. Title XIII of the Revised Statutes contained all the 
laws of the United States related to the judiciary, including 
Section 605 and those Sections governing habeas corpus. See 
Rev. Stat., Tit. XIII, ch. 6 (1874). Thus, it is reasonable to 
conclude that Congress intended “judge of the circuit court of 
appeals” to include the “circuit justice” in all laws related to 
the judiciary, including those governing habeas corpus.

another “alternative” remedy—an original petition in 
this Court for habeas corpus—indisputably existed at 
the time this Court decided Howse. See 28 U.S.C. 451 
(1940). As we have explained (U.S. Br. 31-34), the 
existence of some other means by which the Court 
could “physically discharge its appellate duties,” 
Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann, 317 U.S. 
269, 273 (1942), does not prevent use of an extraordi- 
nary writ. See, e.g., U.S. Alkali Export Ass’n v. 
United States, 325 U.S. 196, 204 (1945) (using com- 
mon-law certiorari to review interlocutory order even 
though final judgment was subject to review on 
appeal); DeBeers Consolidated Mines, Ltd. v. United 
States, 325 U.S. 212, 217 (1945) (same with respect to 
preliminary injunction); Far East Conference v. 
United States, 342 U.S. 570, 572 (1952) (same with 
respect to denial of motion to dismiss); Felker, 116 S. 
Ct. at 2341 (Stevens, J., joined by Souter & Breyer, 
JJ., concurring) (explaining that the Court has 
jurisdiction both to consider an original petition for 
habeas corpus and to review, pursuant to the All 
Writs Act, an order denying a prisoner the right to 
file a successive habeas petition). 

Use of a common-law writ in this case is “rea- 
sonably necessary in the interest of justice,” Adams, 
317 U.S. at 274, and “appropriate,” 28 U.S.C. 1651(a). 
Reliance on an application for a certificate of ap- 
pealability or an original petition for habeas corpus 
would be an unduly cumbersome way for this Court to 
preserve the potential to review the merits of 
petitioner’s Section 2255 motion or to supervise the 
interpretation by the courts of appeals of the standard 
for issuing certificates. The Court can use common- 
law certiorari to consider only those specific and 
discrete legal issues of public importance that it 


10 


believes warrant its attention. Cf. Layne & Bowler 
Corp. v. Western Well Works, Inc., 261 U.S. 387, 393 
(1923) (explaining that Court should confine its 
attention to issues of broad public importance). Pass- 
ing on an application for a certificate or granting an 
original habeas petition, in contrast, would require 
the Court to review the entire case, to determine the 
appropriate legal standards, and to apply the applica- 
ble legal standards to the facts of the case. In 
addition, the decision whether to issue a certificate or 
grant a petition for habeas corpus requires detailed 
consideration of the particular facts of the case, a 
task more easily and more appropriately performed by 
a court or judge closer to the record. See Lyons v. 
Ohio Adult Parole Auth., 105 F.3d 1063, 1072 (6th 
Cir.), cert. denied, 117 S. Ct. 1724 (1997); Hunter v. 
United States, 101 F.3d 1565, 1580-1581 (11th Cir. 
1996) (en banc), cert. denied, 117 S. Ct. 1695 (1997); 
D. Oaks, The “Original” Writ of Habeas Corpus in 
the Supreme Court, 1962 Sup. Ct. Rev. 153, 192-194 
(identifying difficulty in resolving factual issues as a 
reason for this Court’s reluctance to consider 
original petitions for habeas corpus). Thus, common- 
law certiorari would be a more focused and efficient 
use of the Court’s resources than the highly case- 
specific alternatives that might be invoked.’ See U.S. 
Br. 32-34.

c. The final reason proffered by amicus (Br. 18-19) 
for disregarding House is that allowing review by 
certiorari of the denial of an application for a certifi- 
cate would result in an unworkable “two-track sys- 
tem of appellate review.” The possibility of two 
tracks of review arises because a court of appeals may 
deny a certificate as to one or more issues raised by a 
prisoner but grant a certificate as to one or more 
other issues. As amicus acknowledges (id. at 38-39), 
however, “policy considerations” are “best addressed 
to members of Congress.” 

In any event, the policy concerns expressed by 
amicus are not affected by the outcome of the 
question presented in this case. The potential for two 
tracks of appellate review exists whether a prisoner 
seeks review of the denial by a court of appeals of a 
certificate by petitioning for certiorari or by filing an 
application for a certificate with a Justice of this 
Court. Amicus suggests (Br. 19) that two-track re- 
view will present less of a problem if this Court’s 
review is limited to an application for a certificate, 
because individual Justices historically have disposed 
of applications more speedily than the Court has 
disposed of cases on certiorari. Historical experience 
with other applications, however, is not necessarily a 
good guide. As discussed at pages 9-10 & note 2, 
supra, deciding an application for a certificate could 
require a Justice or the Court to pass on a wide range 
of issues. As in this case, an application could require

resolution of more legal and factual issues than the 
Court would address if it limited review to important 
legal issues through the certiorari process.’ Thus, it 
is speculative whether applications for certificates 
will be resolved more quickly than petitions for 
certiorari. Indeed, because certificates of appealabil- 
ity may be filed at any time, using them rather than 
common-law writs of certiorari may actually result in 
more disruption of the appellate process. Compare 28 
U.S.C. 2101(c) (any petition for certiorari must be 
filed within 90 days after the entry of the judgment of 
which review is sought unless the time is extended 
for good cause). In any event, the Court could avoid 
the two-track review problem by deciding that it will 
ordinarily deny certiorari when a court of appeals has 
granted authorization to appeal some issues but not 
others. The Court could instead consider whether to 
review the entire case after the court of appeals has 
passed on those issues for which it authorized appeal.
