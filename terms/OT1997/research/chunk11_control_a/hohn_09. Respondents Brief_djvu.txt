STATEMENT 


1. On June 20, 1990, police officers executed a 
search warrant at petitioner’s house. The police re- 
covered approximately 16 grams of methamphetamine, 
most of which was in a brown case on the kitchen 
counter. Within arm’s reach of the drugs on the 
kitchen counter, police found three loaded firearms, 
two of them in holsters. During the search, the police 
also found a grinder bearing methamphetamine resi- 
due, three other firearms, one of which was loaded, 
and $293 in currency. J. A. 34, 43; Gov't C.A. Br. in 
Opp. to Pet. for Rehearing 2. Following a jury trial in 
the United States District Court for the District of 
Nebraska, petitioner was convicted of, inter alia, 
using and carrying a firearm during and in relation to 
a drug trafficking offense, in violation of 18 U.S.C. 
924(c)(1). J.A. 27. Petitioner appealed but did not 
challenge the sufficiency of the evidence to support 
his conviction under Section 924(c)(1). The court of 
appeals affirmed. J.A. 12-26. 

2.a. On December 6, 1995, this Court held in Bai- 
ley v. United States, 516 U.S. 137, that a conviction 
for use of a firearm under Section 924(c)(1) requires 
the government to “show active employment of 


3 


the firearm.” Id. at 144. Active employment includes 
uses such as “brandishing, displaying, bartering, 
striking with, and most obviously, firing or attempt- 
ing to fire“ the weapon. Id. at 148. By contrast, mere 
possession of a firearm does not constitute “use.” Id. 
at 149. 

b. Following the decision in Bailey, petitioner 
filed a pro se motion seeking to vacate his sentence 
under 28 U.S.C. 2255 on two grounds. First, he con- 
tended that the evidence at his trial was insufficient 
under Bailey to support his conviction under Section 
924(c)(1). Petitioner contended that his failure to 
raise the insufficiency claim on direct appeal should 
be excused because his conduct “was not a crime,” and 
it would therefore be a “fundamental miscarriage of 
justice” not to review his claim. Motion to Vacate, 
Set Aside or Correct Sentence Pursuant to 28 U.S.C. 
§ 2255, at 8. Second, petitioner challenged the jury 
instructions at his trial for improperly defining the 
phrase “use a firearm” and argued that correct jury 
instructions would likely have produced an acquittal. 
Id. at 11. The government responded that petitioner 
was not entitled to relief because the evidence at trial 
was sufficient to show that he had “carried” a fire- 
arm. Noting that the police found weapons in holsters 
next to large amounts of methamphetamine on the 
counter in petitioner’s kitchen, the government ar- 
gued that the jury could infer that petitioner had 
“entered his residence and placed his belongings, 
which included the methamphetamine and firearms, 
onto the counter.” J. A. 34-35. The government ac- 
knowledged that, under Bailey, the jury instructions 
at petitioner’s trial erroneously defined the word 
“use,” but contended that the error was harmless 
because “[tJhe evidence obviously establishes ‘carry- 


4

had “waived” his Bailey claim by failing to raise it on 
direct appeal. J. A. 37-38.

a certificate of appealability, an appeal may not be

b. On July 29, 1996, petitioner filed a notice of ap- 
peal from the district court’s decision denying his

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


This Court has jurisdiction under the All Writs 
Act to grant a common-law writ of certiorari to 
review the court of appeals’ denial of petitioner’s 
application for a certificate of appealability, vacate the 
judgment of the court of appeals, and remand for fur- 
ther consideration whether a certificate should issue. 
The Court first held in House v. Mayo, 324 U.S. 42 
(1945) (per curiam), that it had jurisdiction to review 
the denial of an application for a certificate of probable 
cause—the predecessor and functional equivalent of a 
certificate of appealability. The Court has exercised 
certiorari jurisdiction in that context at least 14 
times since House. 

The Court’s jurisdiction was not affected by the 
Anti-Terrorism and Effective Death Penalty Act of 
1996 (AEDPA), Pub. L. No. 104-132, 110 Stat. 1214. In 
the AEDPA, Congress extended to Section 2255 mo- 
tions filed by federal prisoners, such as petitioner, the 
requirement that state prisoners petitioning for 
habeas corpus under 28 U.S.C. 2254 must obtain a 
certificate authorizing appeal before they may appeal 
the denial of their petitions. Congress thus elimi- 
nated prisoners’ appeals as of right from denials of 
Section 2255 motions. Congress did not, however, 


8 


limit this Court’s discretion to review by writ of 
certiorari the denial of an application for a certificate 
of appealability. 

There are two possible bases for the Court’s power 
to review denials of certificates of appealability— 
the statutory certiorari jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. 
1254(1) and the authority to issue common-law writs 
of certiorari under the All Writs Act, 28 U.S.C. 
165l(a). The Court lacks jurisdiction under Section 
1254(1) to review the merits of an underlying Section 
2255 motion until a certificate of appealability has 
issued. Absent a certificate, a court of appeals lacks 
jurisdiction to review the merits of a Section 2255 
motion, and the motion is therefore not “in” the court 
of appeals within the meaning of Section 1254(1). 
The Court arguably has jurisdiction under Section 
1254(1), however, to review the denial by a court of 
appeals of an application for a certificate of appeal- 
ability. Applications for certificates of appealability, 
which the courts of appeals have jurisdiction to decide 
under 28 U.S.C. 2253(c) (Supp. II 1996), see Jn re 
Burwell, 350 U.S. 521, 522 (1956) (per curiam), could 
be viewed as “cases in the courts of appeals.” 

Nonetheless, jurisdiction under Section 1254(1) ap- 
pears to be foreclosed by this Court’s precedent. See, 
e.g., House v. Mayo, supra. On the assumption that 
jurisdiction is unavailable under Section 1254(1), how- 
ever, this Court’s precedent makes it clear that the 
Court has jurisdiction to review the denial of a cer- 
tificate in petitioner’s case by a common-law writ of 
certiorari under the All Writs Act.

BY COMMON-LAW WRIT OF CERTIORARI THE 
ORDER OF THE COURT OF APPEALS DENYING 
PETITIONER'S APPLICATION FOR A CERTIFI- 
CATE OF APPEALABILITY 


This Court’s jurisdiction to review the denial by a 
court of appeals of a certificate authorizing appeal 
from a decision on collateral review was explored 
most recently in an exchange of opinions between 
Justices Rehnquist and Stevens in Davis v. Jacobs, 
454 U.S. 911 (1981). Justice Stevens contended that 
this Court’s jurisdiction under the All Writs Act, 28 
U.S.C. 1651(a), to review denials of certificates was 
settled in House v. Mayo, 324 U.S. 42 (1945) (per 
curiam). See 454 U.S. at 912-914 (Stevens, J., respect- 
ing the denial of certiorari). Justice Rehnquist main- 
tained that House was inconsistent with the princi- 
ples governing the exercise of All Writs Act jurisdic- 
tion and with Congress’s decision to make a certifi- 
cate a prerequisite to appellate consideration of 
a petition for collateral review. See id. at 915-919 
(Rehnquist, J., dissenting from the denial of certio- 
rari); see also Jeffries v. Barksdale, 453 U.S. 914 
(1981) (Rehnquist, J., dissenting from the denial of 
certiorari). 

In our view, House and later cases firmly establish 
this Court’s jurisdiction under the All Writs Act to 
review a court of appeals’ denial of a certificate grant- 
ing leave to appeal a petition for habeas corpus under 
28 U.S.C. 2254. That jurisdiction was not revoked 
when Congress in the Anti-Terrorism and Effective 
Death Penalty Act of 1996 (AEDPA), Pub. L. No. 104- 
132, 110 Stat. 1214, extended the certificate require-

ment to appeals from denials of Section 2255 motions, 
such as petitioner’s. Although 28 U.S.C. 1254(1) could 
be read to provide jurisdiction to review the denial of a 
certificate, that reading of Section 1254(1) appears 
foreclosed by this Court’s precedent, which instead 
bases jurisdiction on the All Writs Act. That use of 
the All Writs Act is fully consistent with the cases 
and principles defining the scope of this Court’s 
jurisdiction under that Act.

B. The Court’s Precedent Supports Certiorari 
Jurisdiction Under The All Writs Act 


1. There are two possible bases for the Court’s 
jurisdiction to review denials of certificates of ap- 
pealability. One possible basis is the Court’s statu- 
tory certiorari power under 28 U.S.C. 1254(1), and the 
other is its power to issue common-law writs of cer- 
tiorari under the All Writs Act, 28 U.S.C. 1651(a). 
Section 1254(1) authorizes the Court to grant certio- 
rari to review “cases in the courts of appeals.” Sec- 
tion 1651(a) authorizes the Court to grant “all writs 
necessary or appropriate in aid of [its] jurisdiction[] 
and agreeable to the usages and principles of law.” 

A case is “in” the court of appeals within the mean- 
ing of Section 1254(1) only if the case is docketed and 
pending in the court of appeals. See Gay v. Ruff, 292 
U.S. 25, 30 (1934). In addition, this Court may review 
the merits of the case only if the court of appeals 
properly has jurisdiction to do so. See Nixon v. Fitz- 


16 


gerald, 457 U.S. 731, 742-743 & n.23 (1982); United 
States v. Nixon, 418 U.S. 683, 690 (1974). A court of 
appeals lacks jurisdiction to review the merits of a 
prisoner’s Section 2255 motion unless the prisoner 
first obtains a certificate authorizing an appeal. See 
§ 102, 110 Stat. 1217, 28 U.S.C. 2253(c)(1) (Supp. II 
1996) (“Unless a circuit justice or judge issues a 
certificate of appealability, an appeal may not be taken 
to the court of appeals”). Therefore, in the absence of 
a certificate of appealability, the Supreme Court lacks 
jurisdiction to issue a writ of certiorari under Sec- 
tion 1254(1) to review the merits of a Section 2255 
motion even if the court of appeals has passed on the 
merits. See McCarthy v. Harper, 449 U.S. 1309, 1310 
(1981) (Rehnquist, J., in chambers).

The All Writs Act authorizes this Court to issue 
extraordinary or common-law writs when (1) “neces-

See 28 U.S.C. 1651(a). Using a common-law writ of 
certiorari to review the court of appeals’ denial to

Although “sparingly exercised,” Parr v. United

Howes Leather Co., 352 U.S. 249, 254-255 (1957); 
Roche v. Evaporated Milk Ass’n, 319 U.S. 21, 25

conclusion which would deprive the [Clourt of the

suggestion, therefore, that this case should be dis-

This Court’s decisions in U.S. Alkali, supra, and 
De Beers, supra, are not inconsistent with use of the 
common-law writ in this case. Indeed, they support 
its use here. In those cases, the petitioners sought 
interlocutory review of questions arising under a 
statute that provided for direct appeal to this Court 
but did not provide for interlocutory appeals. The 
Court noted in U.S. Alkali that use of the common- 
law writ would generally be inappropriate under those 
circumstances because of the plain intent to preclude 
review on appeal. See 325 U.S. at 203. In both cases, 
however, the Court allowed review because the peti- 
tioners alleged that the district court had exceeded 
its jurisdiction in a manner that could not adequately 
be corrected except by use of the common-law writ. 
See id. at 203-204; De Beers, 325 U.S. at 217. Unlike 
U.S. Alkali and De Beers, this case poses no threat to 
the final judgment rule. Nor, as discussed above, is 
there any indication here of congressional intent to 
preclude this Court’s review. Finally, review is justi- 
fied for reasons parallel to those justifying review in 
the earlier cases: The court of appeals here has re- 
fused to exercise its jurisdiction in a manner that 
cannot be corrected other than by use of the common- 
law writ.”
