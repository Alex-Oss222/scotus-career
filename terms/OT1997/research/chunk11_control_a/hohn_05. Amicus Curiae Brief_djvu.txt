INTEREST OF AMICUS* 


The National Association of Criminal Defense Lawyers 
(NACDL) is a non-profit corporation with a membership of 
more than 9,000 attorneys and 28,000 affiliate members in 50 
states. The American Bar Association recognizes NACDL as 
an affiliate organization and awards it full representation in its 
House of Delegates. 


NACDL was founded over 35 years ago to promote 
study and research in the field of criminal law, to disseminate 
and advance knowledge of the law in the area of criminal 
practice; and to encourage the integrity, independence, and 
expertise of defense lawyers in criminal cases. NACDL seeks 
to defend individual liberties guaranteed by the Bill of Rights 
and has a keen interest in the correctness and fairness of 
criminal convictions. Among NACDL’s objectives are to 
promote the proper administration of justice and to ensure that 
Statutes are construed and applied in accordance with due 
process.

Since Congress enacted the AEDPA just over a year

§2253(c\2) CANNOT BE RECONCILED WITH OTHER 
PROVISIONS OF THE AEDPA AND THE ACT AS A 
WHOLE 


Other key provisions of the Act also employ the 
adjective “constitutional.” If that term is understood in those 


instances to mean what the circuit court misconstrues it to 
mean in §2253(c)(2), the various working parts of the full Act 
cannot function, individually or together in an integrated 
system. 


According to unamended language in §2255, a federal 
prisoner is entitled to seek relief on the ground that he or she is 
in custody under a sentence that was imposed in violation of 
the Constitution “or laws” of the United States, a sentence “in 
excess of the maximum allowed by law,” or a sentence 
“otherwise subject to collateral attack.” And according to 
similarly unamended text in §2241(c)(3) and §2254(a), a state 
prisoner is also entitled to seek habeas corpus relief on the 
ground that he or she is in custody in violation of the “laws” of 
the United States. Clearly, then, the new Act makes no attempt 
to banish federal nonconstitutional claims from the purview of 
federal collateral remedies.’ 


By the circuit court’s account, however, the Act draws 
a clear constitutional/nonconstitutional distinction to control 
the way in which cases are processed after they enter the 
federal courthouse door. That position is untenable. The Act 
uses the “constitutional” designation in some contexts in which 
it cannot possibly mean to exclude nonconstitutional matters. 
Thus the Act plainly uses the modifier “constitutional” 
interchangeably with “federal” or, perhaps, as a synonym for 
“fundamental” or “cognizable.”

To sum up, the circuit’s interpretation of the adjective 
“constitutional” ascribes to the AEDPA a purpose to create a 
series of arbitrary, Byzantine snarls in the federal courts’ 
handling of claims that plainly are cognizable in collateral 
proceedings. By the circuit’s account, a petitioner who 
advances a purely “constitutional” claim is free to file a 
collateral petition, so long as he meets one of the filing 
deadlines established by the Act, and is equally entitled to 
federal fact-finding, a stay of execution, and (if necessary) 
appellate review—provided he meets the standards prescribed 
for those ordinary tools of effective adjudication. 


By contrast, according to the circuit, a petitioner who 
advances a nonconstitutional c/aim faces an entirely different 
landscape. He is free to file a petition, but he must meet a 
filing deadline that takes no account of how recently his claim 
materialized. This is so not because anyone thinks the 
petitioner should have anticipated a “new” nonconstitutional 
claim, but only because his claim is, by nature, 
nonconstitutional. If the facts supporting his claim were not 
developed in state court, the petitioner cannot obtain a federal 
hearing even if he meets the standards the Act establishes for 
determining whether procedural default in state court should be 
excused. This is so not because anyone thinks that the 
petitioner could have developed the facts previously, but only

statute meant to bring good sense, order, and efficiency to the

The only sensible construction that can be placed on the 
Act is that it employs the adjective “constitutional” to denote 
matters that are cognizable in federal collateral 
proceedings—not formalistically to foreclose matters that are 
cognizable, but are nonetheless nonconstitutional in character. 
The reason is plain enough. The primary purpose of the Act is 
to make federal habeas litigation more efficient, virtually all 
the claims raised by state prisoners are constitutional in nature, 
and the Act simply employs that designation because it is 
usually accurate. 


IL THE CIRCUIT’S CONSTRUCTION OF 
§2253(c)(2) RAISES SERIOUS CONSTITUTIONAL 


QUESTIONS 


Just as it makes sense to construe the AEDPA in a way 
that avoids irrational, freakish results, it equally makes sense 
to read the new Act in a way that avoids serious constitutional 
questions under Article Il. Yet the circuit court’s 


10 


interpretation bristles with constitutional difficulty. By the 
circuit court’s account of §2253(c)(2), all prisoners, state and 
federal, can press nonconstitutional claims at the district level, 
but not higher. District court judgments on nonconstitutional 
federal claims are, then, effectively final in some instances. 


Making a single district judge the sole arbiter of 
nonconstitutional federal claims would not only be novel. It 
would invite serious constitutional objections. Appellate 
review at the circuit level and, indeed, in this Court, serves 
both to correct errors below and to ensure a uniform 
interpretation of federal law. If, in habeas and §2255 cases 
invulnerable to review, both those values are sacrificed. In the 
case of §2255 motions, the claims at stake were not typically 
available at trial or on appeal, but are entirely 
fresh—cognizable for the first time in collateral proceedings. 
Making district court judgments final with respect to claims of 
that kind places the responsibility of giving federal law its 
authoritative interpretation in the hands of a single individual.* 


Moreover, the arrangement the circuit court ascribes to 
§2253(c)(2) is not outcome-neutral. For §2253 does not 
require the respondent (i.e., the state or the federal government) 
to obtain a certificate before appealing a judgment in the 
prisoner's favor. If §2253 permits government appeals in cases
