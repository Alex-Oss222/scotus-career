REPLY BRIEF FOR PETITIONER 


The parties in this case are now in agreement: petitioner’s claim that he is incarcerated 
for conduct that does not constitute a crime “may fairly be characterized as ‘constitutional,’ and 
* * * the court of appeals erred in concluding otherwise.” U.S. Br. 7. After confessing error 
on the primary point in dispute, however, the government suggests a remand to determine 
whether petitioner Hohn can make a “substantial showing” within the meaning of the Anti- 
Terrorism and Effective Death Penalty Act (AEDPA), 28 U.S.C. § 2253(c)(2), concerning his 
admittedly constitutional claim. For three reasons, a remand for that purpose is unnecessary and 
the Court should enter an order summarily reversing the decision below. 

First, with respect to one of the two constitutional grounds for relief advanced by Hohn, 
the government fails to provide any reason (and there is none) why the extensive showing 
already made in Hohn’s petition for certiorari might fail to qualify as “substantial.” Second, 
although the government mentions a theory under which claimants who advance arguments 
analogous to Hohn’s second constitutional challenge might fail to make the “substantial showing” 
required to obtain a certificate of appealability, it cannot bring itself to suggest that Hohn himself 
falls within this category (and he does not). And finally, after Hohn’s petition for certiorari was 
filed, this Court issued its decision in Lindh v. Murphy, 117 S. Ct. 2059, 2063 (1997), which 
conclusively establishes that AEDPA’s certificate-of-appealability requirement may not be 
applied retroactively to this case. For all of these reasons, this Court, in light of the 
government’s confession of error, should summarily reverse the Eighth Circuit’s decision and 


remand with instructions to the court of appeals to proceed to the merits of petitioner’s appeal. 


I 

As explained in the petition (at 33-34), Hohn is presently incarcerated for conduct that 
this Court unanimously held ia Bailey v. United States, 116 S. Ct. 501 (1995), is not a federal 
crime. The government admits that Hohn’s continued incarceration could violate the 
Constitution in at least two different ways. First, “[a] contention that, in light of Bailey, the 
evidence was insufficient to support a conviction under Section 924(c)(1) may state a claim under 
the Due Process Clause.” See U.S. Br. 8 (citing Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 307, 313-16 
(1979)). Second, “[a] contention that the jury instructions failed to require the jury to make 
findings on every essential element of the offense under Section 924(c), as construed in Bailey, 
may state a claim under the Fifth and Sixth Amendments.” U.S. Br. 8 (citing United States v. 
Gaudin, 115 S. Ct. 2310, 2320 (1995)). The government acknowledges that Hohn has “raised 
claims of both varieties.” Jbid. 

These concessions should definitively resolve in petitioner's favor the first question 
presented in the petition for certiorari. The government professes to harbor doubts, however, 
as to whether “[c]laims of the sort that petitioner raised * * * setisfy Section 2253(c)(2)'s 
requirement of a ‘substantial showing of the denial of a constitutional right."” U.S. Br. 9 
(quoting 28 U.S.C. § 2253(c)(2)) (emphasis in government's brief). Of course, in order to 
justify a remand for consideration of the question of substantiality, the government would need 
to show that some doubt exists on the substantiality question with respect to both constitutional 
violations. The government, however, has not even attempted to make such a showing. 

A. This Court has recently reaffirmed the principle that “[{t}he Constitution gives a 


criminal defendant the right to have a jury determine, beyond a reasonable doubt, his guilt of 


every element of the crime with which he is charged.” United States v. Gaudin, 115 S. Ct. 
2310, 2320 (1995). As we have shown (Pet. 29-30 & n.21, 39-40), the jury instructions on the 
18 U.S.C. § 924(c) count at Hohn’s trial grossly misstated the law, and Hohn was accordingly 
deprived of his constitutional right to have a jury determine his guilt beyond a reasonable doubt 
of every element of that crime. The jury was charged that 
[t}he phrase “used a firearm” means having a firearm available to aid in the commission 
of possession of Methamphetamine with intent to distribute. Similarly, the phrase 
“carried a firearm” does not require proof of actual possession of a firearm or use of it 
in any affirmative manner, but does require proof beyond a reasonable doubt that the 
firearm was available to provide protection in connection with the possession of 
Methamphetamine with intent to distribute or facilitate success. 
Pet. App. 31a (emphasis added). The government itself conceded below (see Pet. 5, 30 n.21, 
34 n.24, 39; Pet. App. 23a) that this instruction allowed the jury to convict Hohn of “using a 
firearm” under a definition of that crime that is manifestly erroneous in light of Bailey. 
Although we contend that the jury instructions’ definition of “carrying a firearm” is 
similarly unsupportable (Pet. 39-40), it is important to bear in mind that Hohn’s constitutional 
rights would be violated even if the “carrying” instruction were entirely proper. As the Eighth 
Circuit, sitting en banc, has explained, “a verdict based on jury instructions that provided 
alternative bases for a finding, one of which is legally inadequate, is presumed to have rested 
on the insufficient basis.” United States v. Ryan, 41 F.3d 361, 365 (1994). Indeed, that is the 
holding of this Court's decisions in Yates v. United States, 354 U.S. 298, 312 (1957), and 
Stromberg v. California, 283 U.S. 359, 368 (1931). Not surprisingly, the government makes 
no attempt to dispute this controlling principle of law, or to suggest that it is inapplicable here. 
Thus, under established law it must be presumed that Hohn is currently incarcerated for “using 


a firearm” even though no jury has ever found beyond a reasonable doubt that his conduct 
violated 18 U.S.C. § 924(c), as construed by this Court in Bailey. 

The government concedes (U.S. Br. 8) that Hohn’s claim based on the erroneous jury 
instruction for “using a firearm” qualifies as a claim under the Fifth and Sixth Amendments. 
Significantly, though, the government offers not the slightest hint of a reason why Hohn has 
failed to make a “substantial showing” with respect to this constitutional claim. The reason is 
simple: Hohn’s unanswered and undisputed arguments in the petition for certiorari easily satisfy 
the “substantial showing” standard, which, as the government itself is constrained to admit (U.S. 
Br. 9), requires no more than a demonstration that the issues are “debatable among jurists of 
reason; that a court could resolve the issues [in a different manner]; or that the questions are 
adequate to deserve encouragement to proceed further.” Barefoot v. Estelle, 463 U.S. 880, 893 
n.4 (1983) (internal quotation marks omitted) (emphasis added). Hohn’s arguments based on 
the concededly erroneous “use” instruction and the Stromberg- Yates principle amount to much 
more: they conclusively demonstrate that Hohn is entitled to relief. 

Although this should be enough to warrant a summary reversal, there is more. The 
government also pointedly fails to suggest that Hohn has not made a “substantial showing” that 
the “carrying” instruction given at his trial was legally defective. Here again, the reason is 
straightforward: the petition for certiorari (Pet. 39-40) clearly demonstrates that the “carrying” 


instruction largely mirrored the “use” instruction and thus was invalid under Bailey. There 


being no dispute about the substantiality of Hohn’s showing concerning either of his Fifth and 
Sixth Amendment claims, summary reversal is warranted. ' 

B. As we previously explained (Pet. 34-39), there is a second, independent ground 
for Hohn’s constitutional challenge to his continued incarceration: the evidence presented at 
his trial was insufficient to support a conviction under Section 924(c)(1). The government 
acknowledges (U.S. Br. 8) that this shortcoming gives rise to a claim under the Due Process 
Clause. With respect to this constitutional challenge, the government does at least articulate a 
reason why — in the government's carefully chosen words — “[c]laims of the sort that petitioner 
raised do not automatically satisfy” the “substantial showing” requirement of Section 2253(c)(2). 
U.S. Br. 9 (emphasis added). The government states that “a claim under Section 924(c) that the 
evidence was insufficient to show a ‘use’ of a firearm in light of Bailey would not constitute a 
substantial showing of the denial of a constitutional right absent an indication that the evidence 
was insufficient to show a ‘carrying.’” U.S. Br. 9. 

Whatever the truth of these generalizations, they have no bearing on this case. It may 
well be that “[c]laims of the sort that petitioner raised” concerning the sufficiency of evidence 
in his case “do not automatically satisfy” the “substantial showing” requirement. But the issue 
here is whether there is any reason to think that Hohn’s claim is not supported by a “substantial 
showing.” Tellingly, the government cannot even bring itself to say that Hohn’s sufficiency 


argument, as set forth in detail in our certiorari petition, is not “debatable among jurists of

It indicates that the government harbors no real doubt that Hohn will easily qualify for a certificate of

further.” Barefoot, 463 U.S. at 893 n.4. In the absence of even an assertion by the government 
that Hohn has failed to make a substantial showing concerning his sufficiency argument, there 
is no good reason to remand this issue to the court of appeals. 

The government's reluctance to assert that Hohn has failed to make such a “substantial 
showing” is unsurprising, as such an assertion would be wholly implausible. As we explained 
at length in the ~etition for certiorari (at 35-41), the evidence was woefully insufficient to show 
beyond a reasonable doubt that Hohn violated Section 924(c) by “carrying” a firearm “during” 
the predicate offense. Indeed, we are aware of no post-Bailey decision of any court of appeals 
under which the facts of this case would support a conviction for “carrying” a firearm under 
Section 924(c). 

Any doubt about the outcome in the Eighth Circuit, moreover, has been erased by that 
court’s recent decision in United States v. McKinney, 1997 U.S. App. LEXIS 17912 (8th Cir. 
July 16, 1997), on remand, 117 S. Ct. 1544 (1997).? In McKinney, the defendant had been 
convicted of using or carrying a firearm under Section 924(c) based on the following facts: 

McKinney kept several guns in his heavily fortified compound in a room from which he 

monitored video cameras that were placed strategically so as to allow him to observe 

anyone approaching his building. As the government puts it in its supplemental brief, 

“the four assault weapons from the control room formed the basis for defendant's 


conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)” because he was operating “an armed drug 
fortress” on the day that he was raided. 


? Not only does McKinney establish that there was insufficient evidence that Hohn “carried” a firearm, 
it also repudiates the Eighth Circuit's misguided waiver doctrine (upon which the district court relied in 
denying Hohn relief). See Pet. 20-27. Thus, after McKinney there can be no serious question both that 
Hohn is actually innocent of the federal crime for which he is currently serving time and that the district 
court’s reasons for denying him relief were legally erroneous.

1997 U.S. App. LEXIS 17912, at *3. The Eighth Circuit concluded that these facts did not 
support a conviction under Section 924(c) either for “using” or for “carrying” a firearm. In 
fact, the court of appeals stated that it was plain error for the trial court to submit the case to 
the jury on the “carrying” theory, because “[hjaving a gun available for use, even an immediate 
use, is simply not the equivalent of carrying it.” Jd. at *5; see also id. at *4-*5S (“In all of the 
cases in which we have upheld convictions for carrying guns during or in relation to a drug- 
trafficking offense, the defendant has had the weapon either in a car’s hatchback, in the 
passenger compartment of a car, or on his person, and was thus carrying the offending weapons 
in an obvious, literal way.”). If it was plain error for the district court to submit a “carrying” 
charge to the jury in McKinney, the same is true a fortiori in this case. 

Because Hohn has easily made a “substantial showing” with respect to his Due Process 
Clause claim concerning the insufficiency of evidence that he “carried” a firearm, a remand to 
the court of appeals for a resolution of that issue is unnecessary.’ For this reason as well, 


summary reversal is warranted.‘

After the petition for certiorari was filed, this Court issued its decision in Lindh v. 
Murphy, 117 S. Ct. 2059 (1997). Lindh held that AEDPA’s amendments to Chapter 154 of Title 
28 (which includes Section 2253(c), the provision requiring certificates of appealability) do not 
apply to cases that were pending on the date AEDPA was signed into law. 117 S. Ct. at 2063. 
The government acknowledges, as it must, that this is the clear holding of Lindh. See U.S. Br. 
10 (“[U}nder Lindh, that provision [requiring certificates of appealability] does not apply to 
‘cases pending’ on April 24, 1996, the effective date of the Act.”). The government further 
admits that “petitioner's Section 2255 motion was pending in the district court” on “April 24, 
1996.” U.S. Br. 5 (emphasis added). Rather than draw from these two concessions the 
inexorable conclusion that AEDPA has no application to this case, Louse, the government 
maintains that Lindh is not controlling and, even more remarkably, urges this Court to limit 
Hohn’s ability to argue otherwise on remand to the Eighth Circuit. The government's request 
for a limitation on any remand order should be rejected out of hand. Indeed, this Court's 
decision in Lindh provides yet another reason why summary reversal is appropriate. 

According to the government (U.S. Br. 10-11), this Court’s holding in Lindh that 
AEDPA'’s amendments to Chapter 154 of Title 28 apply only to “cases pending” on the date 
AEDPA was enacted is not controlling here because the appellate phase of the proceedings below 
qualifies as a different “case” from the trial phase. Since what the government calls the “case 
on appeal” was commenced only after the passage of AEDPA (with the filing by Hohn of a 
notice of appeal), it does not matter, says the government, that Hohn’s request for Section 2255 


relief was pending in the district court before the statute’s enactment. The government cites no 


authority to support the novel proposition that the appellate phase of litigation constitutes a 
separate “case,” and our research has uncovered none. Indeed, the only circuit that has adopted 
the government's position — the Eighth Circuit in Tiedman v. Benson, 1997 WL 437181 (Aug. 
6, 1997) — did so not because the appellate phase constitutes a different “case” but because it 
could “think of no reason why a new provision exclusively directed towards appeal procedures 
would depend for its effective date on the filing of a case in a trial court.” Jd. at *6. The 
dispositive “reason,” of course, is supplied by this Court’s decision in Lindh. Not surprisingly, 
at least six circuits have concluded that no certificate of appealability is necessary under Lindh 
if the Section 2255 petition was filed in the district court prior to AEDPA’s enactment. See 
United States v. Carter, 117 F.5d 262, 264 (Sth Cir. 1997); United States v. Gutierrez, 1997 
U.S. App. LEXIS 17430, at *2-*3 & n.1 (10th Cir. July 11, 1997) (unpublished opinion); 
Nelson v. Walker, 1997 WL 442686, at *14 (2d Cir. Aug. 7, 1997); Arredondo v. United States, 
1997 WL 459716, at *2 (6th Cir. Aug. 13, 1997); Koo v. McBride, 1997 WL 549557, at *1-*2 
& n.3 (7th Cir. Sept. 3, 1997); Hardwick v. Singletary, 1997 WL 555715, at *1-*2 (11th Cir. 
Sept. 4, 1997). 

To correct the Eighth Circuit’s obvious misreading of Lindh, and to bring it into line with 


the unanimous conclusion of other circuits, summary reversal is also warranted.° 


>’ At a minimum, if the Court decides to vacate and remand for a determination of substantiality, it should 
not preclude Hohn from raising the Lindh argument. Although the government is correct in observing 
that the petition for certiorari “does not ask this Court to review whether the certificate-of-appealability 
issue applied in this case” (U.S. Br. 10), it is clear that the retroactivity question, which is jurisdictional, 
arose only after the certiorari petition was filed when this Court issued its decision in Lindh. See also 
S. Ct. Rule 14.1(a) (issues “fairly included” in questions presented may be considered).
