QUESTIONS PRESENTED 


1. Whether a federal prisoner who demonstrates 
that he is incarcerated for conduct that this Court con- 
clusively determined does not constitute a federal crime 
has made “a substantial showing of the denial of a consti- 
tutional right” warranting issuance of a certificate of 
appealability under 28 U.S.C. § 2255. 


2. Whether a defendant's failure to raise an argu- 
ment on direct appeal, when that argument was fore- 
closed by binding circuit precedent and had been 
uniformly rejected by other circuits, constitutes a know- 
ing and intelligent “waiver,” precluding any showing of 
“cause and prejudice” or other excuse for the procedural 
default in a collateral review proceeding. 


3. Whether a defendant who possesses a firearm 
and drugs in close proximity to each other, but neither 
bears the firearm on his person, nor otherwise transports 
it, commits the crime under 18 U.S.C. § 924(c) of 
“carr[ying]” a firearm “during and in relation to” the 
predicate offense of possession of the drugs with intent to 
distribute them.

FAMILIES AGAINST MANDATORY MINIMUMS 
FOUNDATION (FAMM) files this amicus brief pursuant 
to this Court’s Rule 37.2(a) in support of Arnold Hohn’s 
petition for writ of certiorari to the United States Court of 
Appeals for the Eighth Circuit.' Both petitioner and 
respondent have granted amicus consent to file this brief, 
and letters of consent have been filed with the Clerk of 
this Court.

INTEREST OF AMICUS 


FAMM is a nonprofit, nonpartisan, educational orga- 
nization that conducts research and advocacy regarding 
the cost of mandatory minimum sentencing in terms of 
public expenditures, perpetuation of unwarranted and 
unjust sentencing disparities, and the transfer of the sen- 
tencing function from the judiciary to the prosecution. 
Founded in 1991, FAMM has over 33,000 members 
nationwide with 30 chapters in 24 states and the District 
of Columbia. FAMM conducts sentencing workshops for

its members, publishes a newsletter, serves as a sentenc- 
ing clearinghouse for the media, and researches sentenc- 
ing cases for pro bono litigation. FAMM does not argue 
that crime should go unpunished, but that the punish- 
ment should fit the crime. 


FAMM is deeply concerned about the injustice 
wrought by the mandatory minimum statute under which 
Arnold F. Hohn was convicted, 18 U.S.C. § 924(c). Before 
Bailey v. United States, 116 S. Ct. 501 (1995), untold hun- 
dreds of prisoners received mandatory five-year sen- 
tences for lawful conduct. Prisoners such as petitioner 
Hohn remain incarcerated; others, like Herman McGee, 
have found courts responsive to their plight. United States 
v. Herman McGee, No. 90-137CR(3) (E.D. Mo.). Mr. McGee 
lawfully kept five guns in his home when he was arrested 
for cocaine. Although no drugs were found and no evi- 
dence suggested the guns were used or carried or con- 
nected to the cocaine offense, Mr. McGee was convicted 
of violating § 924(c) and sentenced to five consecutive 
five-year sentences. The district court judge questioned, 
as have most federal judges, the wisdom of shifting sen- 
tencing discretion from the judiciary to the prosecution. 
After this Court handed down Bailey, Mr. McGee's con- 
viction was vacated and his sentence reduced by twenty- 
five years. FAMM submits this brief on behalf of all 
prisoners still serving mandatory minimum sentences for 
conduct that is not a crime.

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


A federal prisoner whose motion to vacate sentence 
under 28 U.S.C. § 2255 has been denied is entitled to 
appeal if a single district or circuit judge, or the Circuit 
Justice, finds that the petitioner has made a “substantial 
showing of the denial of a constitutional right.” 28 U.S.C. 
§ 2253(c)(2).2 The circuit court below wrongly concluded 
that petitioner had not made such a showing, despite his 
conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 924(c) for conduct this Court 
held in Bailey v. United States, 116 S. Ct. 501 (1995), did 
not violate that statute. 


Petitioner's pro se § 2255 motion, liberally read, pre- 
sented the claim that petitioner was convicted without 
proof beyond a reasonable doubt (or, indeed, any proof at 
all) that he was guilty of violating § 924(c), properly 
construed. Thus, that motion presented a constitutional 
claim that was cognizable on federal collateral attack. 


? Although the petitioner has not raised this point, the 
statute entitled him to a certificate of appealability by virtue of 
Judge McMillian’s dissenting vote. Section 2253(c)(1) states that 
the appeal may proceed if “a circuit justice or judge” issues a 
certificate of appealability, not the court itself or a three-judge 
panel; compare 28 U.S.C. § 2244(b)(3)(B) (under same Act, 
authorization for permission to file second or successive 
petition must be granted by “a three-judge panel of the court of 
appeals”). Congress’s choice of different language for different 
provisions of the AEDPA is determinative of its intent to create a 
different meaning. Lindh v. Murphy, 138 L. Ed. 2d 481 (1997). 
While a Circuit may chose to assign such motions to three-judge 
panels, see In re Burwell, 350 U.S. 521, 522 (1956), the court 
cannot thereby repeal the petitioner's right to appeal with the 
permission of a single judge. See 4th Cir. R. 22(a); proposed 3d 
Cir. R. 22.3 (adopting this reading). 


Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 307 (1979); cf. Davis v. United 
States, 417 U.S. 333 (1974). His showing in support of that 
claim was, to say the least, “substantial.” The decision 
below refusing him any right to appeal from the erro- 
neous denial of his § 2255 motion is based on a simple but 
grievous mistake in identifying the constitutional right at 
issue. This Court should grant certiorari and reverse. 


The Eighth Circuit's refusal to hear petitioner Hohnr’s 
appeal stacks injustice upon injustice. The district cowrt 
decision that is insulated from appellate review is prem- 
ised upon an absolute “waiver” rule that the Eighth Cir- 
cuit would now renounce. The district court in this case 
denied § 2255 relief on the basis that petitioner Hohn 
“waived” his Bailey-based claim by not raising it on direct 
appeal. The district court did not consider that “waiver” 
to be a procedural default that could be excused on the 
basis of “cause and prejudice,” as it should have under 
this Court’s cases. United States v. Frady, 456 U.S. 152 
(1982); Davis v. United States, 411 U.S. 233 (1973). The 
Solicitor General has confessed that this approach, taken 
in this and other Eighth Circuit cases, is indefensible. See 
Brief for the United States, Bousley v. Brooks, No. 96-8516 
(filed June 6, 1996). Above all, the district court’s decision 
is groundless because it explicitly rests upon overturned 
law. After vacatur and remand by this Court, the Eighth 
Circuit reversed its decision that announced the absolute 
“waiver” rule. United States v. McKinney, 79 F.3d 105 (8th 
Cir. 1996), vacated, No. 96-6348, 1997 U.S. LEXIS 3099 
(U.S. May 19, 1997), on remand, No. 95-2132, 1997 U.S. 
App. LEXIS 17912 (8th Cir. July 16, 1997).

ARGUMENT 


1. Conviction without proof beyond a reasonable 
doubt of each element of the offense of conviction, 
properly construed, is a due process violation, cog- 
nizable on federal collateral attack and appealable 
under 28 U.S.C. § 2253(c). 


Petitioner Hohn was convicted after trial of violating 
18 U.S.C. § 924(c) without any evidence, much less proof 
beyond a reasonable doubt, that he “used or carried” a 
firearm “during and in relation to” a drug trafficking 
offense. See Bailey v. United States, 116 S. Ct. 501 (1995) 
(authoritatively construing § 924(c) as not penalizing con- 
duct forming the basis of petitioner Hohn’s conviction). 
Were it to reach the issue, the Eighth Circuit itself would 
certainly conclude that petitioner Hohn was wrongfully 
convicted. United States v. McKinney, No. 95-2132, 1997 
U.S. App. LEXIS 17912, at *5 (8th Cir. 1997) (stating that 
having gun available for immediate use does not consti- 
tute using or carrying). 


Yet after petitioner’s motion under 28 U.S.C. § 2255 
to vacate that conviction was denied, the Eighth Circuit 
refused a certificate of appealability, reasoning that: 


[T]he petitioner is not making a constitutional 
claim: He is making a claim to federal statutory 
right. Bailey did no more than interpret a statute, 
and an incorrect application of a statute by a 
district court, or any other court, does not vio- 
late the Constitution. The Constitution does not 
guarantee that judges will always be right. It 
does guarantee, it is true, that persons accused 
of crimes will receive due process; but the peti- 
tion makes no due process claim with respect to 
the trial judge’s actions, and trial errors hardly 


ever rise to the level of a due process violation 
in any case. 


Hohn v. United States, 99 F.3d 892, 893 (8th Cir. 1996) (per 
curiam). The Eighth Circuit thus misconstrued petitioner 
Hohn’s constitutional claim. 


Petitioner was not trying to manufacture a constitu- 
tional claim out of a statutory violation by asserting that 
judicial error is a per se violation of due process. His claim 
is that misconstruction of the statute at his trial led to his 
conviction without proof of guilt, which is a genuine due 
process violation. All sufficiency-of-the-evidence analysis 
necessarily rests upon prior statutory construction of the 
elements of the offense, but the ultimate question of 
sufficiency is no less a constitutional question on that 
account. See, e.g., United States v. Kozminski, 487 U.S. 931, 
953 (1988) (considering whether record contains sufficient 
evidence of offense, as newly construed in that case, as 
basis for deciding whether to remand for new trial or for 
judgment of acquittal); cf. Hudson v. Louisiana, 450 U.S. 40 
(1981); Burks v. United States, 437 U.S. 1 (1978). 


Conviction without proof of every element of the 
offense, properly construed, violates the Fifth Amend- 
ment Due Process rights of federal defendants, just as it 
violates the Fourteenth Amendment Due Process rights of 
state defendants. In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358 (1970). As 
required by the Constitution, the burden of proving 
beyond a reasonable doubt every element of the charged 
offense, as defined by state or federal law, rests upon the 
prosecution. Mullaney v. Wilbur, 421 U.S. 684 (1975). Vio- 
lations of this fundamental principle, if not corrected on 
direct appeal, are cognizable in petitions for federal 


habeas corpus, whether by state or federal prisoners, 
because they work a denial of a constitutional right — the 
right to due process of law. Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 
307 (1979). 


The court below entirely overlooked this basic and 
simple point (perhaps because it elected to interpret a 
new statute without the benefit of adversarial briefing). 
Instead, it ruled that a prisoner advancing such a claim is 
complaining only of “an incorrect application of a stat- 
ute....” Hohn v. United States, 99 F.3d 892, 893 (8th Cir. 
1996). Another Circuit has expressed possible agreement 
with the decision below, indicating that the Eighth Cir- 
cuit’s decision is not the kind of merely-erroneous prece- 
dent that inevitably crops up and must sometimes be 
ignored; rather, it threatens to become influential. See 
United States v. Eyer, 113 F.3d 470, 474 (3d Cir. 1997) 
(dictum).* This Court should grant the petition to clarify 
that a conviction on evidence insufficient to prove each 
element is unconstitutional, even though a court must 
first construe the statute so as to know what the elements 
are and how they can be proven. Kozminski, 487 U.S. at 
953. 


Even if it could be said that the petitioner is incarcer- 
ated in violation of the statute rather than the Constitu- 
tion, this statutory violation creates a “complete 
miscarriage of justice” and is therefore “subject to collat- 
eral attack.” Davis v. United States, 417 U.S. 333 (1974). 
Moreover, the contention that the prisoner’s conviction

violates the statute hardly proves that his confinement is 
not unconstitutional as well — a point this Court did not 
have to reach in Davis. See United States v. McPhail, 112 
F.3d 197 (Sth Cir. 1997) (recognizing, on appeal from 
denial of § 2255 motion, that Bailey announced a non- 
constitutional rule, to wit, a statutory construction, but 
that as a result appellant’s conviction was supported by 
insufficient evidence, requiring vacatur of conviction on 
that count). 


For this reason, petitioner’s application for a certifi- 
cate of appealability should have been granted, regard- 
less of how one construes the term “substantial showing 
of the denial of a constitutional right,” as used in 28 
U.S.C. § 2253. 


2. A certificate of appealability must be granted if the 
appellant has made a “substantial showing of the 
denial of a constitutional right” with reference to an 
issue raised in the § 2255 motion or concerning the 
disposition of that motion. 


In one way, the court below was correct: In asking 
whether petitioner had made a “substantial showing of 
the denial of a constitutional right,” the court looked past 
the issue that petitioner would presumably have to raise 
on appeal to the issue actually presented in petitioner's 
§ 2255 motion. The district court had not reached the 
merits of petitioner’s § 2255 motion but had denied it on 
the basis that petitioner “waived” his Bailey claim by not 
raising it on direct appeal. Thus, the appeal that peti- 
tioner Hohn was seeking leave to pursue would no doubt 
have turned on whether an absolute “waiver” rule 


applies in this situation, or whether, on the other hand, 
the failure to raise the issue on appeal presents a pre- 
sumptive procedural default that must be overcome with 
a showing of “cause and prejudice.”4 


The denial of petitioner’s § 2255 motion based upon 
this erroneous “waiver” rule itself enables petitioner to 
make a “substantial showing” of denial of his rights, but 
not of his constitutional rights. Thus, unless Congress 
carelessly employed the phrase “constitutional right” in 
§ 2253(c) but really meant “federal right,”5 discernment 
of the proceeding in which the statute requires the “sub- 
stantial showing” determines appealability in petitioner’s 
case. Although his constitutional rights were violated in 
the trial court by his improper conviction, the rights 
denied in the § 2255 case, at the district court level, were 
his statutory, procedural rights.

Section 2253(c)(2), like so many other sections of the 
AEDPA, is not clear®: It does not state whether the 
required “substantial showing” was meant to refer to the 
issue(s) to be raised on appeal, or to the issue(s) on the 
underlying merits, or to either of them, or to both. 
Clearly, an appeal is equally worthy of going forward in 
any of these cases. This Court should resolve that ques- 
tion as well in its disposition of petitioner’s case and note 
that a would-be appellant in a habeas or § 2255 matter 
“has made a substantial showing of the denial of a consti- 
tutional right” by demonstrating that such a violation 
occurred within the underlying criminal proceeding or 
within the disposition of the § 2255 motion (or § 2254 
petition) itself. 


In failing to make this point clear, the statute, as 
drafted, seems to incorporate the assumption that every 
appeal from the denial of a § 2255 motion will pursue the 
merits of the underlying claim. Since that often is not and 
cannot be the case, the Court should clarify that 
§ 2253(c)(2) does not require that each issue raised on 
appeal present a question of constitutional law. See United 
States v. Lozada, 107 F.3d 1011, 1013 (2d Cir. 1997) (seem- 
ingly assuming that issue with respect to which showing 
must be made is issue to be raised on appeal); United 
States v. Orozco, 103 F.3d 389, 393 (5th Cir. 1997) (same). 


To hold otherwise would bar prisoner appeals of 
such issues as whether the petition was sufficiently speci- 
fic to withstand summary dismissal, or whether a certain

kind of non-constitutional claim is cognizable in a § 2255 
proceeding, or a denial of the right to counsel guaranteed 
by Fed. R. Gov. § 2255 Proc. 8(c), or the standard of 
harmless error, or whether there was a procedural default 
(or a basis for relief from any default, as in this case), or 
any of a host of other important issues. The statute does 
not compel such a result, and it should not lightly be 
assumed that Congress intended to insulate district court 
decisions from review, or to freeze the development of 
the law, to this radical extent. 


In this case, petitioner has a “substantial” issue to 
raise on appeal concerning the denial of his § 2255 
motion, which he was entitled to have a certificate to 
pursue, on either of two grounds. First, if “constitutional” 
means “federal” in § 2253(c)(2), he could appeal because 
he made a substantial showing that his federal (albeit 
non-constitutional) right to fair consideration of his 
§ 2255 motion was wrongly denied through misapplica- 
tion of the procedural default rule. Second, looking past 
the issue to be raised on appeal to the ultimate question 
about petitioner Hohn’s conviction, he met every defini- 
tion of a “substantial showing of the denial of a constitu- 
tional right,” as discussed under Point 1 of this brief. 


Nor does the district court’s procedural rationale for 
dismissing the § 2255 motion present an alternate ground 
of affirmance. To the contrary, that disposition was as 
flagrantly wrong as that of the court of appeals, as both 
the Eighth Circuit and the Government would now con- 
cede. See United States v. McKinney, 79 F.3d 105 (8th Cir. 
1996), vacated, No. 96-6348, 1997 U.S. LEXIS 3099 (U.S. 
May 19, 1997), on remand, No. 95-2132, 1997 U.S. App. 
LEXIS 17912 (8th Cir. July 16, 1997); Brief for the United

States, Bousley v. Brooks, No. 96-8516 (filed June 6, 1996), 
supra. As this Court has recognized, “the principles of 
comity and finality that inform the concepts of cause and 
prejudice ‘must yield to the imperative of correcting a 
fundamentally unjust incarceration.’ ” Murray v. Carrier, 
477 U.S. 478, 495 (1986) (quoting Engle v. Isaac, 456 U.S. 
107, 135 (1982)). 


Indeed, the Solicitor General’s confession in Bousley 
overlooks one of the two forms of “cause” for relief from 
the procedural default that exist in post-Bailey cases. Not 
only does petitioner have a good claim of innocence, 
giving rise to a “fundamental miscarriage of justice,” 
Schlup v. Delo, 115 S. Ct. 851, 861 (1995); Murray v. Carrier, 
477 U.S. at 496,” he also has cause for relief from default 
by virtue of the novelty of the Bailey ruling and its 
essential “unavailability” to attorneys in the Eighth Cir- 
cuit in the face of controlling, adverse precedent. Smith ». 
Murray, 477 U.S. 527, 536-37 (1986) (discussing Reed »v. 
Ross, 468 U.S. 1, 13-16 (1984)). 


For all these reasons, as well as those discussed in the 
petition itself and by amicus NACDL, the petition for writ 
of certiorari should be granted.
