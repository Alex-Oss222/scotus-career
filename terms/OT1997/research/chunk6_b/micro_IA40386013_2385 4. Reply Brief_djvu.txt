STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


Petitioners stand by the statement of the case set forth in the petition 
for writ of certiorari. Contrary to Carriger’s implication, Petitioners’ 
statement of the facts is not solely dependent upon the trial testimony of 
Robert Dunbar. It is overwhelmingly supported by the physical and 
circumstantial evidence. The “facts” set forth by Carriger, obtained 
verbatim from the majority opinion, contorts the trial evidence by 
interspersing either subsequent “testimony” by Carriger (at a state post- 
conviction relief hearing) or merely Carriger’s purported theories in an 
attempt to explain away the evidence establishing his guilt. (Brief in 
Opposition (B.1.0.) at 1-8) It is a “hopelessly one-sided review of the 
record” (Appendix B at 17), viewing all of the evidence through a 
subjective Carriger-colored prism. 

REASONS FOR GRANTING THE WRIT 

Carriger claims that the en banc decision below is not worthy of 
certiorari review because it does not conflict with the law of other federal 
or state courts, does not announce a novel or important rule of federal law, 
and does not err in its application of the law. Nothing could be further 
from the truth. As pointed out in the petition for writ of certiorari, the 
majority below: (1) effectively eviscerated the 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d) 
presumption of correctness in the Ninth Circuit by refusing to apply it to 
a trial judge’s finding of witness “credibility”; (2) lowered and misapplied 
the Schlup' “actual innocence” standard: and (3) held, contrary to every 
other federal circuit, that, pursuant to Brady,’ a prosecutor must obtain 
from government agencies not involved in the investigation or prosecution 
of the case potential impeachment evidence known to the defense, and hand 
it over to the defense. Petitioners will briefly respond to the arguments 
made by Carriger on each issue.’

THE MAJORITY REFUSED TO APPLY THE 28 U.S.C. 

§ 2254(D) PRESUMPTION OF CORRECTNESS TO THE 

STATE COURT JUDGE’S DETERMINATION OF THE 

CREDIBILITY OF A WITNESS THE JUDGE PERSONALLY 

HEARD AND OBSERVED TESTIFY. 

Carriger attempts to justify the majority’s refusal to apply the 28 
U.S.C. § 2254(d) presumption of correctness to the determination of 
witness “credibility” by utilizing the same methodology employed by the 
majority in its opinion—attempting to compartmentalize and explain away 
each individual piece of evidence establishing his guilt by drawing 
inferences most favorable to Carriger. (B.1.O. at 12-17) This methodology 
directly conflicts with well-established law. E.g., Parke v. Raley, 506 U.S. 
20, 35-36 (1992) (federal court must defer to state court on all inferences 
reasonably drawn from the record); Marshall v. Lonberger, 459 U.S. 422, 
432-35 (1983) (before rejecting a state court’s factual determination, a 
federal court “must conclude that the state court’s findings lacked even 
‘fair support’ in the record, including inferences fairly deducible” from the 
record). But it is not even necessary to engage in any sort of prolonged 
review of the record to realize the fallacy of the majority’s refusal to apply 
the 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d) presumption of correctness. The issue is simple— 
may a federal court disregard a state court judge’s determination of the 
“credibility” of a witness who testified before the state court judge and not 
the federal court. That is the nut of the issue, and the answer is obvious. 

Carriger also attempts to minimize the state trial judge’s credibility 
determination by stating that he “did not hear the trial testimony” and did 
not base his credibility determination on “Dunbar’s demeanor,” but on his 
“analysis of the physical evidence, and Dunbar’s motives to lie.” (B.1.O. 
at 12-13) However, Judge Ryan saw and heard Dunbar testify on three 
different occasions during the course of the post-conviction relief 
proceeding—when he confirmed his trial testimony, when he recanted his 
trial testimony, and when he recanted his recantation and confirmed his 
trial testimony. While Judge Ryan took the trouble to explain, in detail, 
how Dunbar’s recantation was “inconsistent with the physical evidence, 
testimony of other trial witnesses, and his prior testimony” (Appendix D 
at 3), and that he had a myriad of motives to recant (id.), this rejection of 
the short-lived recantation was based, in large part, on his evaluation of 
Dunbar’s demeanor on the witness stand. (See Jd. at 2-3, 5) 

Carriger, as did the majority below, makes much of the fact that 
Dunbar’s short-lived recantation of his trial testimony “was given without 
immunity of any kind.” (B.I.O. at 12 n 4; see also Appendix A at 30) But 


2 


this necessarily renders the recantation /ess reliable. At trial, and when he 
recanted his recantation at the post-conviction relief hearing, Dunbar was 
given testimonial immunity; he had admitted that he controlled stolen 
property (the jewelry). Dunbar did not request or obtain any sort of 
immunity when he recanted because he knew he was lying when he said 
that he and his ex-wife committed the robbery and murder. If, on the other 
hand, he had been telling the truth, he would have demanded immunity, or 
would not have so testified. 

Carriger, as did the majority, ignores the fact that Dunbar’s 
recantation is inherently incredible because it cannot be believed, unless 
one believes that Dunbar’s ex-wife (not Carriger) committed the robbery 
and murder with Dunbar. (See Appendix B at 10-11) And, everybody, 
Carriger included, agrees that this part of Dunbar’s recantation is not 
believable. (See Appendix B at 10) 

No matter how one views the state court record, it is patently clear 
that the majority below stripped away the presumption of correctness due 
the state court judge’s credibility determination in violation of 28 U.S.C. 
§ 2254(d) and this Court’s well-established caselaw. No federal circuit 
court, to Petitioners’ knowledge, has done so before. This Court should 
grant certiorari review to bring the Ninth Circuit back in line with the rest 
of the federal judiciary.

THE SCHLUP ACTUAL INNOCENCE STANDARD, AND 

IGNORED THE “RELIABILITY” REQUIREMENT. 

Carriger erroneously asserts that Petitioners have argued that the 
Schlup actual innocence standard “is the same as the Herrera standard.” 
Not so. In fact, Petitioners set forth in detail the Schlup standard, pointing 
out where and how the majority misapplied that standard. (Petition for Writ 
of Certiorari (P.W.C.) at 14-24) Petitioners did point out that the majority 
also misapplied the Herrera standard, effectively lowering the Herrera 
standard to the Schlup standard, and the Schlup standard to a Strickland'- 
type “confidence in the verdict” standard. (P.W.C. at 22 n.7) 

Carriger also claims that Petitioners, and the dissent below, are 
incorrect in maintaining that the majority “adopted Dunbar’s confession as 
the truth” for purposes of conducting a Schlup analysis. (B.I.O. at 11) 
Neither Petitioners nor the dissent asserted that the majority accepted the 
recantation as “the truth.” Rather, both criticized the majority for accepting

Carriger does not take issue with Petitioners’ assertion that the

Regarding the “new reliable evidence” that clearly establishes his guilt

Carriger states that one of the reasons the majority gave this new

he could not run away from the evidence fast enough when the State

State post-conviction relief proceeding, Carriger filed an improper ex parte 
motion to have the tape released for examination and the motion was 
denied because ex parte proceedings are not authorized under Arizona law. 
(Appendix J) Carriger then abandoned the request. Carriger did not request 
release of the tape again until after he filed his successive petition for writ 
of habeas corpus and Petitioners had answered the petition. (See Appendix 
C at 26) Petitioners argued, and the district court found, that Carriger had 
procedurally defaulted on any challenge to the reconstruction of the tape 
by failing to pursue the issue in state court. (/d. at 29.) 

Carriger asserts that “another reason” the majority gave the 
reconstruction of the tape evidence “little weight” was because of an 
alleged “inconsistency of the expert’s testimony as to the location of the 
latent to the end of the roll of tape.” (B.1.0. at 20) However, as pointed 
out in detail in the petition for writ of certiorari, the criminalist 
unequivocally testified that Carriger’s fingerprint was located 
“Approximately 21 and a half inches” from the “end of the roll of tape” 


(Appendix H at 30). (P.W.C. at 19) The majority's confusion does not 
change the testimony.° 

In short, the majority simply ignored the Schlup “reliability” 
requirement in accepting and relying upon Dunbar’s repudiated recantation, 
then turned its back on extremely probative reliable evidence that 
establishes Carriger’s guilt. This is contrary to the very basis upon which 
this Court decided Schlup. 

Finally, Carriger implicitly acknowledges that it is an open question 
whether a Schlup showing of “actual innocence” must be linked to a 
constitutional violation, but claims that “the state’s withholding of critical 
exculpatory and impeachment evidence is intricately tied to his miscarriage 
of justice evidence.” (B.1.0. at 21-22) This is not true. Dunbar’s 
recantation of his trial testimony, and his short-lived claim that he and his 
ex-wife committed the crimes, has nothing to do with the majority’s finding 
of a Brady violation relating to the alleged failure to obtain and disclose to 
the defense potential impeachment evidence. There has been no allegation, 
or finding, that the State knowingly presented perjured testimony. Clearly, 
the majority's (erroneous) Schlup finding is not linked to a federal 
constitutional claim. See O'Dell v. Netherland, 95 F.3d 1214, 1246 n.26 
(4th Cir. 1996).

BRADY DOES NOT REQUIRE THE PROSECUTION TO 

OBTAIN AND REVIEW POTENTIAL IMPEACHMENT 

MATERIAL IN THE POSSESSION OF GOVERNMENT 

AGENCIES NOT INVOLVED IN THE INVESTIGATION OR 

PROSECUTION OF THE CASE, ESPECIALLY WHERE THE 

DEFENSE HAS KNOWLEDGE OF THE MATERIAL AND 

FAILS TO REQUEST IT. 

Carriger takes the position that Brady and its progeny require that the 
prosecution obtain, review, and hand over to the defense any “state 
records” that might contain potentially exculpatory or impeachment 
material, even if the state records are in the possession of an agency not 
involved in the investigation or prosecution of the case and even though the 
defense has actual knowledge of the material but fails to request the 
material. (B.1.0. at 24-25) Carriger’s assertion conflicts with this Court's 
decision in Kyles v. Whitley, 514 U.S. 419, 437 (1995), limiting the 
prosecutor’s duty to Brady material “known to others acting on the 
government’s behalf in the case, including the police.” It also directly 
conflicts with the Seventh Circuit decision in United States v. Morris, 80 
F.3d 1151, 1169 (7th Cir. 1996), holding that Kyles does not impose “a 
duty on the prosecutor’s office to learn of information possessed by other 
government agencies that have no involvement in the investigation or 
prosecution at issue.” 

Carriger cites to four cases he claims support the absurd proposition 
that the prosecution must locate and scour through any government records 
or files that may contain Brady material, regardless of whether the 
government agency is involved in the investigation or prosecution of the 
case. (B.1.0. at 24-25) None of those cases even arguably support any 
such duty. United States v. Strifler, 851 F.2d 1197, 1201-02 (9th Cir. 
1988) deals with a trial judge's review of a witness’ probation file for 
potential Brady material; it has nothing to do with imposing upon the 
prosecution a duty to seek out potential Brady material from government 
agencies. In Martinez v. Wainwright, 621 F.2d 184, 186-88 (Sth Cir. 
1980), a witness’ “rap sheet” was in the possession of “the medical 
examiner’s office” and an “assistant medical examiner testified for the 
prosecution at trial.” Thus, the medical examiner was, most certainly, 
involved in the investigation and prosecution of the case. In { :.e’ States 
v. Deutsch, 475 F.2d 55, 57 (Sth Cir. 1973), the federal ,overnment 
prosecuted two defendants for bribing a postal office employee, but refused, 
upon defendants’ request, to produce the employee’s personnel file. The 


district court refused to order the federal government to obtain the 
personnel file from the post office, and the circuit court remanded with 
directions that the district court obtain and review the personnel file and 
determine whether it contained Brady material. /d. Finally, in United States 
v. Burnside, 824 F. Supp. 1215, 1252-54 (N.D. Ill. 1993), the “lead 
prosecutor,” other “personnel” in the United States Attorney’s Office, 
officers and agents involved in the investigation, and “the Warden and 
other personnel in [a prison]” all had actual knowledge that government 
inmate witnesses were “using illegal drugs” and did not disclose such 
information to the defense. The court noted, parenthetically, that, even if 
none of the prosecutors, federal agents, or police officers knew of the 
witness’ drug use, knowledge by the prison warden and other prison 
personnel would be “attributed to the government” because they worked 
closely with the United States Attorney’s Office on the case. /d. at 1254. 

Thus, neither Petitioners nor Carriger have located a single case 
supporting the unprecedented duty the majority has placed on the 
prosecution. Brady and Kyles do not place any sort of duty on a prosecutor 
to ferret out and scour through files or records of a “government agency”, 
not involved in the investigation or prosecution of the case, in search of 
potential exculpatory or impeachment material. But that is precisely whai 
the Ninth Circuit has done. 

Carriger claims that Petitioners have taken a “reversal of the position” 
taken in 1982 in state court where the State of Arizona argued that 
Carriger’s trial counsel were not ineffective in failing to obtain Dunbar’s 
DOC file because a state statute and state court of appeals opinion (a civil 
case) prohibited inmates or the public from obtaining an inmate’s DOC 
file. (B.1.0. at 25) However, the State was not successful in that argument, 
because the Arizona Supreme Court held that a defendant’s right to 
confront witnesses trumped the statute. State v. Morales, 630 P.2d 1015, 
1017-18 (1981). Moreover, even if the State had been correct, nothing 
prevented Carriger or his counsel from requesting that the prosecution 
attempt to obtain Dunbar’s DOC file, or requesting that the trial court 
order its production. 

Regarding Carriger’s assertion that his trial counsel did not know that 
Dunbar had been an informant, that is simply not true. In a letter to trial 
counsel, Carriger wrote, “Bobby Dunbar was the informant for the state 
against John Douglas Troutmon for a safe burglary of the National Cash 
Register Company (about 1968 I think).” (Appendix B at 22) Thus, 
Carriger and his counsel knew full well that there was a DOC file on 


Dunbar and that he had been an informant. They simply failed to obtain, 
or even request, Dunbar’s DOC file.’ 

As pointed out in the petition for writ of certiorari, every other circuit 
to address the issue has held that where the defense has knowledge of the 
existence of potential exculpatory or impeachment material and fails to take 
steps to obtain it, Brady is not violated. (P.W.C. at 26) Again, the Ninth 
Circuit stands as a minority of one in holding otherwise."

The court reviewed the State’s Motion for Order Prohibiting Ex Parte 
Proceedings. Petitioner’s response, and the State’s reply were received 
also. The pleadings were considered along with the files. 


Petitioner filed a number of ex parte motions requesting orders for 
release of exhibits, unsealing of documents, etc. Pursuant to the Arizona 
Supreme Court’s recent ruling in State v. Apelt, -Ariz.-, 861 P.2d 
634(1993), the State requests an order prohibiting such motions. The 
State’s request is well-taken. Apelt, supra, prohibits such procedures. The 
reasoning for this prohibition is sound and the federal and other authority

inappropriate in Rule 32 proceedings. Therefore,
