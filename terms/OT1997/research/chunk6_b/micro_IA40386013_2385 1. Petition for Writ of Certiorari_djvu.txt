CAPITAL CASE 
QUESTIONS PRESENTED FOR REVIEW 


The Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals en banc held that a state judge’s 
finding regarding the credibility of a witness’ repudiated recantation of his 
trial testimony was not entitled to the 28 U.S.C § 2254(d) presumption of 
correctness because, after purporting to review the state court record, the 
court concluded that “each piece of evidence is as consistent” with the 
witness’ recantation as it is with his trial testimony so that the state judge’s 
credibility finding was “not fairly supported by the record as a whole.” 
The en banc court then accepted the repudiated recantation and concluded 
that Carriger had met the Schlup v. Delo, 513 U.S. 298 (1995) 
“fundamental miscarriage of justice” (“actual innocence”) standard to allow 
the court to review otherwise abusive or procedurally defaulted 
constitutional claims. The en banc court then considered a constitutional 
claim unattached to the “actual innocence” showing, concluding that the 
State of Arizona committed a Brady violation by failing to obtain from a 
government agency, not involved in the investigation or prosecution of the 
case, a witness’ prison file and turn over to the defense information in the 
file that might have been used to impeach the witness’ trial testimony— 
even though the defense knew of the existence of the file but failed to 
request or obtain it. This case presents the following questions: 

1. Whether the en banc court’s methodology in stripping away the 28 
U.S.C. § 2254(d) presumption of correctness from the state judge's 
credibility finding violates the statute, is inconsistent with this Court’s 
caselaw, and will seriously undermine the deference given state court 
factual findings by federal courts within the Ninth Circuit? 

2. Whether the en banc court misconstrued and misapplied this Court's 
recent decision in Schlup by utilizing inherently unreliable evidence to. meet 
the Schlup “actual innocence” standard, refusing to consider new reliable 
evidence that establishes Carriger’s guilt, lowering the Schlup standard to 
a “confidence in the verdict” standard, then reviewing the merits of a 
constitutional claim unattached to the showing of “actual innocence”? 

3. Whether the en banc court erred, and created a circuit split, in 
holding that the prosecution must obtain from a government agency, not 
involved in the investigation or prosecution of the case, material that might 
be used to impeach a witness, and turn the material over to the defense, 
even if the defense has actual knowledge of the existence of the material?

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


On March 15, 1978, Paris Hoyt Carriger (hereafter Carriger) was 
charged with the offenses of robbery and first-degree murder. The facts 
giving rise to the charges and subsequent convictions are as follows. 
Around 1970 Carriger met Robert Dunbar while they were incarcerated in 
Arizona State Prison, and the two became friends. Dunbar was released 
from prison in 1976. Carriger was released in late 1977. 

On March 11, 1978, Carriger called Dunbar and asked if he could 
Stay at Dunbar’s home. Carriger told Dunbar that he would sleep outside 
in his van. Dunbar agreed and Carriger moved in on March 12, 1978. 

On the morning of March 13, 1978, Carriger called his employer and 
told him that he would not be coming to work that day. He spent the rest 
of the morning and early afternoon with Dunbar. They purchased a gun, 
then went to Shaw’s Jewelry Store, which was about 6 blocks from 
Dunbar’s home. The owner, Robert Shaw, buffed some scratches out of the 
crystal on Dunbar’s watch. After returning home, Dunbar took a nap 
around 4:00 p.m.. Carriger then left the Dunbar home in his van. 

When Mr. Shaw had not arrived home by his normal time, his wife 
called the store, but received no answer. She later drove to the store, 
arriving at about 6:40 p.m. The store was in disarray. Mrs. Shaw found 
her husband’s body lying on the floor at the rear of the store. Mr. Shaw 
had been brutally murdered. His skull had been crushed and his tie was 
knotted around his neck. A jeweler’s ring sizer and a broken cast iron 
skillet were found near his head. His hands were bound behind his back 
with adhesive tape. 

Carriger awoke Dunbar from his nap around 6:00 p.m. He and 
Dunbar went outside to Carriger’s van. Carriger said that he had robbed 
the jewelry store, and showed Dunbar jewelry, watches, and rings. Dunbar 
helped remove the price tags from the jewelry. Carriger gave Dunbar some 
of the loot. He put the rest in an attache case, and he and Dunbar took that 
case and a gun case to the apartment of Dunbar’s stepdaughter. Carriger 
handed the cases to Dunbar’s stepdaughter 

Dunbar then drove Carriger to various locations where he disposed of 
his clothes and other items. Carriger cut his name out of the pocket of the 
pants he had been wearing and threw them in the canal. Carriger told 
Dunbar he had pulled a gun on Mr. Shaw, tied him up, and then repeatedly 
beat him with a skillet and a “flat iron.” When Mr. Shaw’s heart would 
not stop beating, Carriger used the victim's tie to strangle him. 

The following morning, Dunbar called the police. Officer Dan Barker 
received the call from Dunbar, who identified himself as “Bob,” and asked 


l 


if the police would be interested in clearing up the jewelry store murder. 
Dunbar told Barker that he was an ex-con, that he had supplied the killer 
with a gun, and that he wanted immunity for a burglary he (and Carriger) 
had committed a few days earlier. Barker eventually set up a meeting 
between Dunbar and a detective. 

Dunbar met with officers shortly after the phone call. He told them 
about Carriger’s confession to the murder and helped arrange Carriger’s 
arrest. Dunbar retraced the route he had taken the night before and helped 
the police recover Carriger’s discarded clothing, as well as credit cards and 
personal papers that belonged to the victim. He also took the police to his 
stepdaughter’s apartment, where she turned over the attache case and the 
gun case. The police seized Carriger’s luggage, and retrieved Carriger’s 
pants from the canal. All of the clothing recovered fit Carriger. The shoes 
had blood on them. Pairs of pants found in Carriger’s luggage had 
Carriger’s name on the inside of the pockets. The pants retrieved form the 
canal were the same brand and size as the pairs found in Carriger’s 
luggage and the pocket was cut where Carriger’s name would have been. 

Carriger had shaved his head just before his arrest. He had two keys 
in his possession when arrested. One unlocked the Dunbar residence and 
the other unlocked the attache case that contained the stolen jewelry. 

An officer present at the autopsy cut the wad of tape from Mr. Shaw’s 
hands and, using forceps, placed it in a container. An identification 
technician dusted the outside of the tape wad with fingerprint powder and 
found one latent print. He removed the piece of tape bearing the print and 
put it on a lift card. The latent print was matched to Carriger’s right 
thumb. Carriger’s prints were also found on the gun case, and on a gun 
slick and a piece of paper inside the case. 

Following a jury trial, Carriger was convicted of robbery and first- 
degree murder. The trial court (Honorable Dorothy Carson) held an 
aggravation-mitigation hearing, found two aggravating circumstances 
(Carriger’s prior conviction for a crime of violence and the especially 
heinous, cruel or depraved nature of the murder), and no mitigating 
circumstances sufficiently substantial to call for leniency. It then sentenced 
Carriger to prison for not less than 99 years nor more than 100 years for 
the robbery and imposed a death sentence for the murder. The Arizona 
Supreme Court affirmed Carriger’s convictions and sentences on direct 
appeal. State v. Carriger (Carriger 1), 599 P.2d 788 (1979)

relief in the trial court, raising several claims, including the claim that trial 
counsel were ineffective in failing to obtain Robert Dunbar’s Arizona 
Department of Corrections (DOC) file to obtain impeachment material. The

On July 5, 1985, Carriger filed a petition for writ of habeas corpus

On September 25, 1986, Carriger filed his second petition for post- 
conviction relief in state court. Because Judge Carson had retired, the 
matter was assigned to the Honorable Michael Ryan. During the course of

Attorney's Office, and the Phoenix Police Department because none of 
these entities would help him out in prison. Several other witnesses 
testified, but their testimony was cumulative to testimony or evidence 
presented at trial or during the first post-conviction relief proceeding.

On November 4, 1991, a three-judge panel of the Ninth Circuit Court

On February 4, 1994, Carriger filed a third petition for post- 
conviction relief and subsequently obtained a stay of execution. On May 4,

successive petition for post-conviction relief and/or were precluded under

(Appendix F) The trial court denied Carriger’s motion for rehearing. 
(Appendix G) The Arizona Supreme Court denied his petition for review. 

On August 8, 1995, Carriger filed a successive petition for writ of 
habeas corpus in federal district court. On November 2, 1995, the district 
court issued a memorandum of decision and order, denying the petition, as 
well as several ancillary motions Carriger had filed with the district court. 
(Appendix C) The district court held that: (1) Carriger’s “actual innocence” 
claim failed to meet the Carrier/Schlup “gateway” standard; (2) Carriger’s 
claim that the state courts on collateral review failed to recognize “new” 
evidence allegedly implicating Dunbar related to a state law question and 
the state court did reconsider Carriger’s death sentence in light of the 
alleged “new” evidence; (3) Carriger’s Brady claims constituted an abuse 
of ihe writ and, alternatively, were meritless; (4) Carriger’s competency 
claims were abusive, procedurally defaulted, and alternatively, meritless; 
and (5) Carriger’s ineffective assistance of counsel at resentencing claim 
was abusive, procedurally defaulted, personally waived, and alternatively, 
meritless to the extent it alleged ineffectiveness related to the competency 
issue. (/d.) 

Carriger appealed and the district court issued a certificate of probable

and affirming the district court’s denial of the successive petition for writ

The Ninth Circuit granted rehearing en banc. 

On December 17, 1997, the sharply divided en banc court issued its 
opinion. The six judge majority refused to defer to the Arizona trial judge’s 
finding that Robert Dunbar was not credible when he recanted his trial 
testimony and claimed that he and his ex-wife had robbed and murdered 
the victim. (Appendix A at 24-30) The majority concluded that “each piece 
of evidence is as consistent with Dunbar’s confession’ as it is with his trial 
testimony” (id. at 26) and, therefore, in its view, “the state court’s 
credibility determination is not fairly supported by the record as a whole.” 
(Id. at 30) The majority accepted Dunbar’s recantation, and considered it 
in conducting its review of the district court’s rejection of Carriger’s 
Herrera (Herrera v. Collins, 506 U.S. 390 (1993)) and Schlup claims. (Jd. 
at 31-37) 

The majority concluded that Carriger failed to establish a Herrera 
“freestanding claim of innocence” because Dunbar’s short-lived recantation 
failed to establish “that Carriger more likely than not is innocent.” 
(Appendix A at 31-34) The majority then held that Dunbar’s recantation 
was sufficient to meet the Schlup “miscarriage of justice” standard, 
because, in the majority’s view, although Carriger failed to “affirmatively 
prove his actual innocence,” he had “more than shown sufficient doubt 
about the validity of his conviction to satisfy Schlup and permit 
consideration of his constitutional claims.” (/d. at 34, 36) Having passed 
through the Schlup “gateway” to otherwise abusive and procedurally barred 
claims, the majority held that the State of Arizona had committed a Brady 
violation by failing to obtain, review, and hand over to the defense, Robert 
Dunbar’s DOC file. (/d. at 37-45) The majority vacated the panel opinion, 
reversed the district court’s judgment, and ordered the matter remanded to 
the district court with instructions to grant the writ. (/d. at 45) 


1. Throughout its opinion, the majority refers to Dunbar’s short-lived recantation as 
a “contession.” 


Judge Kozinski, writing for the five dissenting judges, took issue with 
“the majority’s hopelessly one-sided review of the record” and its refusal 
to defer to the state court judge’s factual determination that Robert 
Dunbar’s short-lived recantation was “not credible,” stating that “[t}he 
majority’s methodology will seriously undermine the degree of deference 
federal courts in this circuit give to state court findings of fact in habeas 
proceedings.” (Appendix B at 14-16) 

The dissent also criticized the majority for holding that “[a] repudiated 
recantation” is the type of “reliable new evidence” sufficient to unlock the 
Schlup gateway (id. at 2) and that, if the majority decision were allowed 
to stand, the abuse of the writ doctrine “will be severely undermined,” and 
“the Schlup gateway becomes a freeway off death row.” (/d. at 6) The 
dissent called the majority to task for finding “unreliable” and giving “little 
weight” to new reliable evidence that “destroys Carriger’s theory and 
provides strong proof that he was present and participated in the murder.” 
(Id. at 18) And, the dissent pointed out that the majority “cuts the heart out 
of Schlup” by watering down the “actual innocence” standard articulated 
by this Court in Schlup, to a standard more akin to the Strickland v. 
Washington, 466 U.S. 668 (1984), prejudice standard (confidence in the 
verdict undermined). (/d. at 8) 

Finally, the dissent pointed out that the majority’s finding of a Brady 
violation was flawed because the defense knew of the existence of Dunbar’s 
DOC file and “[tJhe government need not give notice sua sponte where the 
accused has knowledge of the documents in question.” (/d. at 22-23) 


REASONS FOR GRANTING THE WRIT 


I 

THE EN BANC COURT’S REFUSAL TO DEFER TO 
THE STATE COURT JUDGE’S DETERMINATION OF 
WITNESS CREDIBILITY DIRECTLY CONFLICTS 
WITH WELL-ESTABLISHED PRECEDENT OF THIS 
COURT AND WILL SERIOUSLY UNDERMINE THE 
DEFERENCE GIVEN STATE COURT FACTUAL 
FINDINGS BY FEDERAL COURTS WITHIN THE 
NINTH CIRCUIT.

C. THE MAJORITY LOWERED THE SCHLUP “ACTUAL 

INNOCENCE” STANDARD. 

In addition to utilizing unreliable repudiated recantation testimony to 
unlock the Schlup gateway. and refusing to consider new reliable evidence 
which establishes Carriger’s guilt, the majority “cuts the heart out of 
Schlup” (Appendix B at 8) by lowering the Schlup “actual innocence” 
standard to a “confidence in the outcome of the trial” standard. If allowed 
to stand, the majority holding will transform the “extraordinary” and 
extremely rare case envisioned by Schlup into the type of case often 
presented to federal courts on the eve of execution. 

In Schlup, this Court made it clear that its adoption of the Carrier 
“actual innocence” standard required a habeas petitioner seeking federal 
review of otherwise abusive or procedurally barred claims “to show that 
he is ‘actually innocent.’” 513 U.S. at 327 Indeed, it said so several times. 
See Id. at 324, 326, 327, 329. The Court explained how a district court 
was to determine whether a habeas petitioner had proven his “actual 
innocence”: 

The meaning of actual innocence as formulated by Sawyer 

and Carrier does not merely require a showing that a reasonable 

doubt exists in the light of the new evidence, but rather that no 

reasonable juror would have found the defendant guilty. \t is not 

the district court’s independent judgment as to whether 

reasonable doubt exists that the standard addresses; rather the 

standard requires the district court to make a probabilistic 
determination about what reasonable, properly instructed jurors 
would do. Thus, a petitioner does not meet the threshold 
requirement unless he persuades the district court that, in light 

of the new evidence, no juror, acting reasonably, would have 

voted to find him guilty beyond a reasonable doubt. 
513 U.S. at 329 (emphasis added). 

Rather than acknowledge and apply the Carrier standard adopted by 
this Court in Schlup, the majority seizes upon part III of this Court’s 
opinion in which the Court distinguished between an hypotesized Herrera 
“freestanding” “actual innocence” claim, and the type of “gateway” 
“actual innocence” claim raised in Schlup. (Appendix A at 34-36). See 
Schlup, 513 U.S. at 313-17. It is from this discussion that the majority 
pulls (then applies) the phrase “a court cannot have confidence in the 
outcome of the trial unless the Court is also satisfied that the trial was free 
of nonharmless error.” (/d. at 35, quoting Schlup, 513 U.S. at 316, 
emphasis added by majority) The majority failed to acknowledge that the

In Brady v. Maryland, 373 U.S. 83, 87 (1963), this Court held that 
“the suppression by the prosecution of evidence favorable to an accused 
upon request violates due process where the evidence is material either to 
guilt or to punishment, irrespective of the good faith or bad faith of the 
prosecution.” In Giglio v. United States, 405 U.S. 150, 153-55 (1972), the 
Court held that Brady applied to impeachment evidence. See United States 
v. Bagley, 473 U.S. 667, 676 (1985) In Bagley, the Court reaffirmed that 
impeachment evidence as well as exculpatory evidence fell within Brady 
material, 473 U.S. at 676-77, and announced that the Strickland prejudice 
analysis applied, regardless of whether the defendant requested disclosure. 
473 U.S. at 682. Finally, in Kyles v. Whitley, 514 U.S. 419, 115 S. Ct. 
1555, 1567 (1995), the Court placed upon the prosecution the following 
burden regarding “undisclosed” evidence: “the individual prosecutor has 
a duty to learn of any favorable evidence known to the others acting on the 
government’s behalf in the case, including the police.” (emphasis added) 

This Court has never held that the prosecution has an obligation to 
seek out from government agencies, not involved in the investigation or 
prosecution, possible impeachment evidence known to the defense (but not 
the prosecution), place it on a silver platter, and present it to the defense. 
See United States v. Morris, 80 F.3d 1151, 1169 (7th Cir. 1996) (“neither 
Kyles nor [United States ex rel. Smith v.] Fairmain [769 F.2d 386 (7th Cir. 
(1995)] can be read as imposing a duty on the prosecutor’s office to learn 
of information possessed by other government agencies that have no 
involvement in the investigation or prosecution at issue”) That is precisely 
what the Ninth Circuit has held is required by Brady and Kyles. (Appendix 
A at 38-40)

The other federal circuits uniformly agree: Brady is not violated if the

120 F.3d 551, 558-59 (Sth Cir. 1997); United States v. Flores-Mireles,

defense could have obtained the information from sources external to the 
prosecution through the exercise of reasonable “diligence.” Rector, 120

CONCLUSION 
The en banc majority has clearly erred in refusing to grant deference 
to a state court’s credibility determination, in its interpretation and 
application of this Court’s decision in Schlup, and in finding a Brady 
violation where the defense had actual knowledge of the documents 
containing the impeachment material. The decision is contrary to this 
Court’s caselaw and conflicts with virtually every other federal circuit court 
of appeals on all three issues. If allowed to stand, the decision will create 
chaos throughout federal courts within the Ninth Circuit, and will likely 
adversely influence federal courts nationwide. 
Therefore, the petition for writ of certiorari should be granted.
