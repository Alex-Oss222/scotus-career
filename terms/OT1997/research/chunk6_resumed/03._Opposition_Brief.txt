A. Procedural Default: 


It is undisputed that Breard never raised his “Vienna Convention” claim in state court. His 


failure to raise the claim in state court bars federal collateral relief. See Gray v. Netherland. 116 


S.Ct. 2074, 2080-2081 (1996). Breard already has litigated a state habeas petition and his claim 
undoubtedly now would be barred if he attempted to raise it in state court. See Va. Code §§ 8.01- 
654(B)(2) (prohibiting claims that could have been raised in a prior petition); 8.01-654.1 (limiting 
death row prisoners to one habeas petition). The “independent and adequate state ground doctrine” 


precludes federal collateral relief under such circumstances. See Coleman v. Thompson, 501 U.S. 


722, 731-732 (1991), citing Engle v. Isaac, 456 U.S. 107, 125-126 n.28 (1982). 

Breard’s contention that the procedural default doctrine does not even apply to treaty- 
based claims (Ptn. 15-20) is precluded by Coleman which makes the doctrine applicable in “all 
cases in which a state prisoner has defaulted his federal claims ‘n state court.” See 501 U.S. at 
750 (emphasis added). It would make no sense, moreover, to hold, as this Court frequently has 


done, that claims based upon specific constitutional provisions may be defaulted, see, e.g., Gray 


v. Netherland, 116 S.Ct. at 2080-2081 (due process “Brady” claim barred by default), but that 
treaty-based claims may not. Indeed, the procedural default doctrine is now firmly established in 
our law, and Article 36(2) of the Vienna Convention expressly provides that the “rights” 


conferred by the treaty “shall be exercised in conformity with the laws and regulations of the 


semis eR 


receiving State.” (Resp. App. 2). Article 36(2)’s proviso that the law of the receiving state 
“must enable full effect to be given to the purpose [of the Article]” (emphasis added) is thus 
perfectly consistent with the default doctrine that generally “enables” a § 2254 prisoner to raise a 
claim if he raised it properly in state court. 

In order to avoid his procedural default, Breard must show both “cause” and “prejudice.” 
The Fourth Circuit applied these well-settled principles to the facts of Breard’s case and 
concluded that he had not shown the required “cause” for his default.' See Breard, 134 F.3d at 
620. The Court of Appeals’ opinion listed the cases that existed when Breard filed his state 
habeas petition in May of 1995 which would have provided support for a claim that he was 
entitled to relief due to the State’s alleged failure to notify him of his right to consular assistance 
pursuant to the Vienna Convention. Id. Because the “tools” to construct his claims were 
available at a time when the claims could have been raised in state court, Breard clearly cannot 
show “cause” for his default. See Engle, 456 U.S. at 133 (“we cannot say that respondents 
lacked the tools to construct their constitutional claim”).

311 (7th Cir.), cert. denied, 510 U.S. 899 (1993). The assertion of “novelty” also is belied by

The preamble to the Vienna Convention specifically states that the signing nations “have 
agreed” to the Convention “[rJealizing that the purpose of such privileges and immunities is not to 
benefit individuals but to ensure the efficient performance of functions by consular posts on behalf

rights, enforceable by a state prisoner in a federal habeas proceeding under § 2254. Instead, an 
alleged failure to comply with the treaty is an issue that properly may be addressed only by the 
executive branch of the federal government through negotiations with a signatory nation. Even 


then, however, such negotiations could not mandate relief for a state prisoner from a valid

that no “miscarriage of justice” would result from a failure to reach the merits of the claim. Id.

certiorari or a stay of execution. Breard argues that, in conducting its review on direct appeal, the 
Virginia Supreme Court failed to adhere to what Breard says are the requirements of Virginia Code 
§ 17-110.1. (Ptn. 26). The fact of the matter, however, is that on direct appeal the Virginia 
Supreme Court expressly stated that it had conducted the proportionality review required by the 


State statute and concluded “that Breard’s death sentence is not excessive or disproportionate.” See 


Breard, 248 Va. at 89, 445 S_E.2d at 682. 


°  Breard thus is wrong when he asserts that the Court of Appeals’ denial of his rehearing

denial of rehearing must be read as a rejection of Breard’s contention that the panel had ignored 
his claim.

The United States misses the mark when it cites this Court's application of its 
procedural default jurisprudence to habeas corpus claims based on alleged violations of the 
United States Constitution. Congress could, if it chose, enact legislation providing that federal 
constitutional claims cannot be procedurally barred in habeas proceedings. It has not done so. 
The Vienna Convention, however, contains an express shield against the operation of 
procedural default when that domestic law impairs full effect from being given to specific 
provisions within the treaty, including the right to consular notification. The shield was part 
of the treaty to which the Senate consented, that the President ratified, and that is ae the law 
of the land. 

3, Citing Smith vy. Murray, 477. U.S. 527 (1986), the United States suggests that 
the unavailability of a legal claim does not provide cause for a procedural default unless the 
legal basis for the claim was impossible to discover. The better interpretation of the 
"unavailability" exception to procedural default is contained in Reed v. Ross, 468 U.S. 1 
(1984), and Coleman v. Thompson, 501 U.S. 722. Those cases require only “reasonable 
unavailability," meaning that compliance with the state procedural rule was “impracticable.” 
Id. at 753. 

4. The United States’ “new rule” argument is the same as that raised by the habeas 
respondent. Even the lower court's most ardent scholars of the "new rule” jurisprudence did

law of the land for more than a quarter of a century, which has been routinely complied with 
by federal law enforcement officials, and which has mandated consular notification for all 


detained foreign nationals from signatory countries is simply not a "new rule.”
