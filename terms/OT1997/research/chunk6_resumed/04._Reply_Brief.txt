Respondent seeks to avoid dealing with the Vienna Convention by contending that the 
"rights" afforded by it are not individual rights enforceable by a state prisoner in a federal habeas 
proceeding. Respondent's position is contrary: (1) to the language of Article 36 of the Vienna 
Convention ("the said authorities shall inform the person concerned without delay of his 
rights... . ."); (2) to the position of the United States Department of State (Article 36 of the Vienna 
Consular Convention provides that "the host government must notify the arrestee without delay of 
the arrestee's nght to communicate with the American Consul," United States Department of State, 
7 Foreign Affairs Manual 411.1 (1984); App. 280), and (3) to case law (Breard v. Pruett, 134 F.3d 
615, 622 (4th Cir. 1998) (Butzner, J., concurring) (Vienna Convention "provides rights to individuals 
rather than merely setting out the obligations of signatories“-), and Faulder v. Johnson, 81 F.3d 515, 
520 (Sth Cir. 1996) (Vienna Convention requires arresting government to notify foreign national "of 
his right to contact his consul")). Rather than demonstrate that Breard's Petition lacks merit, 
Respondent has left no doubt that Breard has raised questions of federal law which have not been, 
but should be, settled by this Court. Rule 10, Rules of the Supreme Court of the United States. 

Respondent's citation to Reed v. Farley, 512 U.S. 339 (1994) is inapposite because Breard 


has not claimed merely a violation of a treaty or federal statute. Rather, Breard contended below that

The Language of Article 36 Creates a Specific 
Rule Overcoming the General Rule of Default. 


Breard does not dispute the general proposition that treaty rights, like constitutional rights, 
may be defaulted. However, Article 36(2) of the Vienna Convention, which precludes local law 
from interfering with the implementation of its terms, requires more precise analysis than 
Respondent would wish. One could imagine a situation where a foreign national knowingly refused 
to raise the failure of the authorities to comply with the Convention. But these circumstances do not 
exist here. The Commonwealth of Virginia, in its wilful failure to comply with its duty under the 


Convention, kept Breard ignorant of his rights. Although he eventually gained access to consular 


assistance, he was by then convicted, condemned and, according to Respondent, defaulted. Article 
36(2) was deliberately drafted to prevent such injustice from occurring in the name of "local law." 


The default doctrine and Coleman vy. Thompson, 501 U.S. 722, 750 (1991), relied upon by 


Respondent, therefore, simply do not apply. 


1 Pri 996 

Respondent's contention that Breard's Vienna Convention claim was not novel prior to 1996 
is premised upon Respondent's misunderstanding of the standard previously established by this Court 
for determining novelty as a cause for avoiding default. Respondent, like the Court of Appeals, 
implies that novelty is available only where discovery of a claim is impossible. This Court has made 
clear, however, that novelty, as cause for avoiding default, is available where the "basis for a claim 
was not reasonably available to counsel . . . ‘or some interference by officials’ . . . made compliance 
impracticable ...." Coleman vy. Thompson, 501 U.S. at 753 (citing Murray v. Carrier, 477 U.S. 478, 
488 (1986). See also Amadeo y. Zant, 486 U.S. 214, 221-22 (1988). Certainly, there is no basis 
for concluding, as the Court of Appeals did, that the legal basis for Breard's claim was reasonably 
available to counsel merely because of the existence of one unpublished habeas corpus opinion on 
Lexis and four published deportation case opinions, one of which does not mention the Convention, 
and three of which, while mentioning the Convention, do not have any criminal law head notes. See 
Footnotes 14 and 15 on page 21 of Breard's Petition for a Writ of Certiorari. Moreover, "Virginia's 
persistent refusal to abide by the Vienna Convention . . .", Breard v. Netherland, 949 F. Supp. 1255, 


1263 (E.D. Va. 1996), affd sub nom, Breard vy. Pruett, 134 F.3d 615 (4th Cir. 1998) is the type of

Jailing of 6 Americans By Serbia Condemned; U.S. Decries Growing 'Pattern' of 
Harassment 

Lee Hockstader 
Washington Post Foreign Service 


Six Americans were in jail here tonight in what outraged U.S. 
diplomats called a case of official anti-American harassment 
masquerading as a garden-variety technical offense. 


Most of the six, who were sentenced to 10-day jail terms for 
not reporting their presence to local police, are members of a San 
Francisco-based group called Peace Workers. They had spent at least 
several days in Kosovo, the volatile southern province of Serbia, 
Yugoslavia's dominant republic, instructing local ethnic Albanian 
activists on nonviolent conflict resolution before being arrested late 
Friday and today, according to U.S. sources. 


In another episode involving Americans in the region today, 
Yugoslav authorities refused to grant visas to 14 members of a 
bipartisan delegation, including Rep. Jim Moran, a Northern Virginia 
Democrat, two other congressmen and Ed Turner, CNN editor at large. 


The group was attempting to enter the country from neighboring 
Macedonia to call attention to what it called, in a press release, 
"the recent reign of terror inflicted on the people of Kosovo" by the 
regime of Yugoslav President Slobodan Milosevic. Its visit would have 
coincided with Sunday's elections for president and parliament of 
Kosovo, an event the Serbian government regards as illegal. 


Kosovo, a poor province of Serbia, has a population that is 90 
percent ethnic Albanian but is dominated politically by ethnic Serbs. 
The two communities live so separately -- and unequally -- that the 
arrangement has been compared to apartheid. 


U.S. diplomats in Belgrade were protesting both incidents and 
trying to arrange for an early release for the Peace Workers 
volunteers.

"There is a developing pattern of clear harassment of 
Americans," said Robert S. Gelbard, President Clinton's senior 
representative for U.S. policy on the Balkans. "What they're showing, 
of course, is gross stupidity. . . . It is likely to produce a harder 
U.S. attitude” not only within the administration but on Capitol Hill 
as well. 


The arrests and visa denials come amid what appears to be a 
rising tide of anti-Americanism here in recent days, following a 
bloody police crackdown in Kosovo in the last three weeks. 


More than 80 people died in the crackdown, and Washington vowed 
to impose further economic sanctions on Belgrade. 


The U.S. criticism has whipped up resentment and a mood of 
victimization among Serbs, who say they were justified in taking harsh 
measures against what they consider ethnic Albanian terrorists. Even 
opponents of the government are furious at Washington's threat of new 
sanctions, believing they will do nothing but deepen sympathy for 
Milosevic's authoritarian regime and further deplete Yugoslavia's 
already staggering economy. 


Several recent events in Kosovo have angered administration 
officials. They include a threat about 10 days ago that political 
analyst Richard Norman -- stationed at the U.S. Embassy in Belgrade -- 
may be harmed, and Serbian police officers at several checkpoints have 
threatened to kill American journalists they stopped. 


The six Americans who were arrested were staying in private homes 
in Pristina, capital of Kosovo. According to a Yugoslav law once 
commonplace throughout Eastern Europe, foreigners visiting the country 
are required to report their presence to local police wherever they go 
within three days of arrival. Hotels do this automatically for their 
guests. 


One American diplomat likened the law to ones in the United 
States proscribing tearing fire-safety tags off mattresses. 


The six peace activists apparently failed to register with the 
police. One, Peter Lippman -- variously described as the group's 
translator or a journalist, though it was not known for whom he worked 
-- was arrested and sentenced to 10 days in jail late Friday. The 
other five were picked up and received identical sentences in separate 
hearings attended by U.S. diplomats today. 


Peace Workers spokeswoman Sandra Schwartz in San Francisco said 
the group's director, David Hartsough, had taken college students 
Daniel Perez, Bruce Hemmer, Daniel Sevallos and Teresa Crawford to 
Kosovo to monitor a March 13 student protest, the Associated Press
