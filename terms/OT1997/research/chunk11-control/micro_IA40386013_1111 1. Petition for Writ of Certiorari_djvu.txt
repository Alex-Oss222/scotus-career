QUESTIONS PRESENTED 


1. Should this Court resolve a split among the 
circuits over whether a trivial and constitutionally 
insignificant everyday occurrence in prison becomes 
actionable simply because a prisoner claims the act was 
the result of retaliatory motive? 


y Does the "some evidence" standard 
established in Superintendent, Mass. Corr. Institution v. Hill, 
472 US. 445 (1985), apply to review conduct of an officer 
who issues a disciplinary charge that is allegedly false and 
retaliatory? 


STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


On January 19, 1992, Correctional Officer Pearson 
was escorting inmate Hines back to his cell after Hines 
took a shower. Pearson claimed Hines threatened him 
and violated a prison rule when he stopped walking during 
the escort. Hines denied it. Hines v. Gomez, 108 F.3d at 
267. 


Pearson filed disciplinary charges against Hines. A 
hearing officer found Hines guilty, ordered Hines confined 
to quarters for ten days, and required Hines to send his 
television outside the institution. Hines v. Gomez, 108 
F.3d at 269. 


Thereafter, Hines filed a prison grievance claiming 
that Pearson used excessive force and threatened him 
during the incident. Hines v. Gomez, 108 F.3d at 269. 


Hines later filed a civil rights action alleging that 
the disciplinary charges were falsified in retaliation for his 
use of the prison grievance process. 


A jury found that the charges filed by Officer 
Pearson were false and in retaliation for Hines’ use of the 
prison administrative appeal process. The jury also found 
that Lt. Szmarciaz, the disciplinary hearing officer, 
retaliated against Hines by finding him guilty. Hines, 108 
F.3d at 267. The jury awarded Hines $100.00 
compensatory damages and $1,000.00 punitive damages 
against Pearson, and $200.00 compensatory damages and 
$1,000.00 punitive damages against Szmarciaz. Hines, 108 
F.3d at 267, n.2. 


Defendants Pearson and Szmarciaz each sought 
judgment as a matter of law on the basis that there was 
no evidence Pearson was aware of Hines’ use of the 
prison administrative appeal process, and the jury was 
allowed to impermissibly re-weigh the evidence in support 
of the disciplinary violation contrary to the "some 
evidence" standard set forth in Superintendent v, Hill, 472 
U.S. 455. Hines, 108 F.3d at 267. 


Judgment as a matter of law was entered in favor 
of Szmarciaz because the disciplinary charges were 
supported by "some evidence." However, the district court 
denied Pearson’s motion, holding that Hill’s "some 
evidence" standard does not apply to the charging officer. 
Hines, 108 F.3d at 267-68. 


Pearson appealed, contending that his conduct as 
the charging officer should also have been reviewed under 
the "some evidence" standard. Pearson also argued that 
the loss of Hines’ television and his confinement to 
quarters for ten days was not a sufficiently significant 
deprivation to form the basis of a cognizable retaliation 
Claim. Hines, 108 F.3d at 267. 


The Ninth Circuit ruled that other defendants’ 
knowledge of Hines’ reputation as a "whiner," together 


with the fact that Hines filed previous unspecified 
grievances and told Pearson he was "going to grievance 
him," allowed the jury to infer that Pearson was aware of 
Hines’ use of the grievance process and acted with a 
retaliatory motive. The Ninth Circuit rejected Pearson’s 
proposed rule that an inmate be required to establish an 
objectively significant injury to state a cognizable 
retaliation claim. The court also refused to apply the 
"some evidence" standard to Pearson as the charging 
officer. Hines, 108 F.3d at 268. 


REASONS FOR GRANTING THE WRIT 


I. 


THE COURT SHOULD SET FORTH A RULE 
THAT ONLY SIGNIFICANT DEPRIVATIONS 
MAY FORM THE BASIS FOR A 
CONSTITUTIONAL RETALIATION CLAIM. 


In Mt. Healthy City School Dist. v. Doyle, 429 US. 
274, 285 (1977), this Court discussed the difficult questions 
of causation posed in retaliation cases, and recognized 
that a complaining party should not be put "in a better 
position as a result of the exercise of constitutionally 
protected conduct than he would have occupied had he 
done nothing." Jd. at 285. The current state of prisoner 
retaliation law flouts the rationale articulated in Mt. 
Healthy because prisoners can circumvent the accepted 
standards of review that govern prisoner constitutional 
claims by simply alleging that everyday hardships incident 
to prison life occur because of retaliation. 


In Hines v. Gomez, 108 F.3d 265 (9th Cir. 1997), 
the Ninth Circuit makes clear that in order to avoid the 
special considerations that apply to  constitutionai 
challenges brought by state prisoners, all a prisoner has to 


Ee EE re TT 


do is allege that prison action was taken against him 
because he intended to complain about prison conditions. 
The Hines decision stands as a roadmap for state 
prisoners who seek to avoid the standards and competing 
interests that apply in challenges to the prison disciplinary 
process. 


Although this Court established long ago that 
“challenges to prison restrictions that are asserted to 
inhibit First Amendment interests must be analyzed in 
terms of legitimate policies and goals of the corrections 
system," Pell v. Procunier, 417 U.S. 817, 822 (1974), now 
the Ninth Circuit and other courts say that, when it comes 
to retaliation claims against prison officers, institutional 
interests take a back seat, and the potential "chilling" 
effect of any retaliatory action, no matter how 
inconsequential, is a sufficient injury. DeGuiseppe v. 
Village of Bellwood, 68 F.3d 187, 192 (7th Cir. 1995). The 
constitutional balance struck by this Court in assessing 
prisoner First Amendment claims (Thornburgh v. Abbott, 
490 U.S. 401 (1989); Pell v. Procunier, 417 US. 817) and 
reviewing the imposition of prisoner discipline (Wolff v. 
McDonnell, 418 U.S. 539 (1974); Superintendent v. Hill, 
472 U.S. 445 (1985)) is skewed when the claim bears the 
label of "retaliation." 


The magic incantation of retaliation is sufficient to 
trigger constitutional scrutiny of trivial, insignificant and 
commonplace incidents of prison life. Federal courts 
throughout the country are holding actionable claims in 
which the most trivial deprivations receive constitutional 
scrutiny because retaliation is alleged. Kent v. Johnson, 
821 F.2d 1220, 1228 (6th Cir. 1987) [female prison guards’ 
observation of male inmate in shower]; Hall v. Sutton, 755 
F.2d 786, 788 (11th Cir. 1985) [confiscation of a pair of 
tennis shoes}; Wiideman v. Angelone, 848 F.Supp. 136, 140 
(D. Nev. 1994) [confiscation of typewriter]; Rodriguez v. 


Kincheloe, 763 F.Supp. 463, 472 (E.D. Wash. 1991), cert. 
denied, 507 U.S. 933 (1993) reh’g denied, 507 U.S. 142 
(1993)) [confiscation of an Ace bandage]. 


The procedural and substantive protections 
afforded state prisoners under the due process clause have 
been extended only to those serious disciplinary 
proceedings that involve significant penalties. See Wolff v. 
McDonnell, 418 U.S. 539, 557 (1974); Superintendent, 
Mass. Corr. Institution v. Hill, 472 U.S. at 454. A 
prisoner’s claimed denial of access to the courts is not 
actionable unless the prisoner has suffered "actual harm." 
See Lewis v. Casey, __ U.S. __, 116 S.Ct. 2174, 2180 
(1996). Prison practices and regulations challenged on 
First Amendment grounds will survive scrutiny if they are 
reasonably related to the advancement of valid 
penological interests. See Thornburgh v. Abbott, 490 U.S. 
401, 414 (1989); Turner v. Safley, 482 U.S. 78, 89-90 
(1987). Only deliberate indifference to a "serious medical 
need" is cognizable. See Estelle v. Gamble, 429 US. 97, 
105 (1975) emphasis added. An inmate must establish a 
significant, objective deprivation to state a cognizable 
claim for violation of the Eighth Amendment. See Wilson 
v. Seiter, 501 U.S. 294, 298 (1991). A de minimis use of 
force is insufficient to state an Eighth Amendment 
violation. See Hudson v. McMillian, 503 US. 1, 9-10 
(1992). Prison regulations do not give rise to a 
constitutionally protected liberty interest unless a prisoner 
has suffered an “atypical and significant hardship . . . in 
relation to the ordinary incidents of prison life." See 
Sandin v. Conner, __ US. __, 115 S.Ct. 2293, 2300 
(1995). However, all the watchwords employed by this 
Court to limit the justiciability of prisoner claims to 
significant events have been rendered meaningless to the 
shibboleth of "retaliation." 


‘area 


In Sandin v. Conner, _ US. ___, 115 S.Ct. 2293 
(1995) this Court expressly repudiated lower court cases 
in which inmates relied on prison regulations to claim 
protected liberty interests in participating in a boot camp 
program, Klos v. Haskell, 48 F.3d 81 (2nd Cir. 1995), in 
receiving a tray lunch rather than a sack lunch, Burgin v. 
Nix, 899 F.2d 733, 735 (8th Cir. 1990), in receiving a 
paperback dictionary, Spruytte v. Walters, 753 F.2d 498, 
506-508 (6th Cir. 1985), and in freedom from transfer to 
a smaller cell without outlets for televisions. Lyon v. 
Farner, 727 F.2d 766, 768-69 (8th Cir. 1984), cert. denied 
469 US. 839 (1984). Id. at 2300. 


The rationale expressed in Sandin reflects the 
common thread through twenty years of this Court’s 
jurisprudence in prison cases to limit prisoner lawsuits 
based upon insignificant deprivations. The Ninth Circuit’s 
decision in this case, and developing retaliation law in 
other circuits, conflicts with Sandin’s principles by 
permitting retaliation claims to proceed on the basis of 
even less significant deprivations than those found 
constitutionally insufficient in Sandin. 


Allowing any deprivation, no matter how 
inconsequential, to form the basis of a retaliation claim 
magnifies the potential for abuse and would "“[t]rivialize 
the First Amendment ... .’ [Citation omitted.]" Crawford- 
El v. Britton, 93 F.3d 813, 826, cert. granted, 138 L.Ed.2d 
210 (U.S. 1997). The Ninth Circuit’s decision in this case 
does just that. 


The Ninth Circuit’s holding conflicts with holdings 
of the Fourth, Eighth and District of Columbia Circuits. 
In the Fourth Circuit, even admittedly retaliatory conduct 
is not actionable absent a "showing of adversity." See, e.g., 
ACLU of Maryland v. Wicomico County Md., 999 F.2d 780, 
785 (4th Cir. 1993). In Crawford-El v. Britton, 93 F.3d 813 


(D.C. Cir. 1996), the Circuit of the District of Columbia 
held that a de minimis injury is insufficient to form the 
basis of a retaliation claim and that the injury asserted 
must be sufficiently adverse that it "would chill or silence 
a ‘person of ordinary firmness’ from future First 
Amendment activities." Jd. at 826. The Eighth Circuit 
does not permit a claim for retaliation "when the alleged 
retaliation arose from discipline imparted for acts that a 
prisoner was not entitled to perform. Any other rule 
would allow a prisoner to openly flout prison rules after 
filing a grievance." Orebaugh v. Caspari, 910 F.2d 526, 528 
(8th Cir. 1990). 


The result of the Ninth Circuit’s departure from 
this Court’s caveat in Mt. Healthy is that the potential 
“chilling effect" of retaliatory action is given paramount 
consideration in disregard of the incredibly complex job of 
managing America’s prisons. This Court’s recognition of 
the complexity of prison administration has led it to 
remark that courts should not be the "front line agencies 
for the consideration and resolution of the infinite variety 
of prisoner complaints." Procunier v. Martinez, 416 U.S. 
396, 405, n.9 (1974). Yet the Ninth Circuit’s ruling in 
Hines shows that is precisely what the federal courts have 
become. 


The concern expressed by the courts that align with 
the Ninth Circuit over the "chilling effect" on a prisoner’s 
First Amendment rights is not reflected in the experience 
of the states. For example, in 1994, the California state 
inmate grievance process resolved 36,047 grievances. 


1. Procunier’s First Amendment standard of review to outgoing 
correspondence, which required a “least restrictive alternative" method 
of analysis, was overruled by this Court in Thornburgh v. Abbott, 490 
U.S. 401 which requires only that a regulation be reasonably related 
to a legitimate penological interest. Jd. at 413-14. 


Seiad 


Washington state satisfactorily resolved 87% of the 20,767 
grievances received that same year. While in the Ninth 
Circuit in 1994, prisoner cases represented 25% of the 
civil filings in the district courts, and 21% of the civil cases 
filed in the court of appeals. See Task Force of Prisoner 
Remedy Procedures, Effective Prisoner Remedy Procedures, 
Report to the Judicial Council of the Ninth Circuit, August 
1995, pages 2-12. 


Given the enormous volume of prisoner lawsuits 
overwhelming the federal judiciary, it is time for this Court 
to announce a rule that "reflects a healthy sense of 
realism," and defines the limits of actionable prisoner 
retaliation claims. Procunier v. Martinez, 416 U.S. at 405. 
Retaliation claims should not be exalted above all 
constitutional claims brought by prison inmates, including 
those brought directly for an infringement of the First 
Amendment.

THE "SOME EVIDENCE" STANDARD SHOULD 
APPLY TO ALL PRISON OFFICIALS WHO 
TAKE PART IN THE PRISON DISCIPLINARY 
PROCESS. 


In affirming the verdict against Correctional Officer 
Pearson, the Ninth Circuit refused to apply the “some 
evidence" standard set forth in Superintendent v. Hill, 472 
U.S. 445 (1985) to the officer who files disciplinary 
charges against an inmate.” 


2. Based on the Ninth Circuit’s prior holding in Barnett v. 
Centoni, 31 F.3d 814 (9th Cir. 1985), in which the "some evidence" 
Standard was used to review a claim of retaliatory discipline, as 
opposed to a "false retaliatory accusation,” the panel in this case was 
constrained to apply the "some evidence" standard to the hearing 


10 


The Ninth Circuit’s ruling is flatly inconsistent with 
the rationale of Hill, which forbade courts from second- 
guessing and re-litigating prison disciplinary decisions and 
stated that "[r]equiring a modicum of evidence to support 

| a decision to revoke good time credits will help to prevent 
arbitrary deprivations without threatening institutional 
interests." Hill, 472 U.S. at 454-55. 


In declining to apply the "some evidence" standard 
to the officer who files the disciplinary charges, but 
conceding its application to the hearing officer who 
adjudicates the charges, the Ninth Circuit set up an 
entirely artificial construct in which the disciplinary 
charges are distinguished from, and subject to, a different 
analysis than the actual punishment imposed. 


Based on the Ninth Circuit’s analysis, the inmate 
has two separate interests: freedom from false accusations 
and freedom from arbitrary discipline. The effect of the 
court’s ruling, however, is to review the freedom from 
arbitrary discipline under the deferential "some evidence" 
standard, while the freedom from false accusation is more 
closely scrutinized. This is the equivalent of applying a 
more demanding standard of review to arrest charges than 
that applied to the conviction resulting from the arrest. 
Since the disciplinary charges are inextricably linked to the 
discipline imposed, there is no logical basis to apply 
different standards of review depending upon a prison 
official’s role in the disciplinary proceedings. 


Just as a defendant’s deniai of guilt in a criminal 
case would provide absolutely no basis for a reviewing 
court to conclude that the defendant’s conviction was not 


officer who adjudicated the disciplinary charges filed by Officer 
Pearson. Hines, 108 F.3d at 268.

supported by substantial evidence, an inmate’s denial of 
guilt in prison disciplinary proceedings should likewise 
provide no basis to re-weigh the evidence de novo. Viens 
v. Daniels, 817 F.2d 1328, 1335 (7th Cir. 1989); U.S. v. 
Manos, 848 F.2d 1427, 1436 (7th Cir. 1988), reh’g denied 
(1988). 


Review by this Court is necessary because the 
circuits are divided on the application of the "some 
evidence" standard. The Seventh and Eighth Circuits 
apply Hill’s "some evidence" standard to claims of false 
and retaliatory discipline. McKinney v. Meese, 831 F.2d 
728, 733 (7th Cir. 1987); Henderson v. Baird, 29 F.3d 464, 
469 (8th Cir. 1994), cert. denied, US. _, 115 S.Ct. 2584 


(1995). 


The Second and Ninth Circuits, however, have 
refused to apply the standard to retaliation claims 
challenging disciplinary charges. Graham v. Henderson, 89 
F.3d 75, 80 (2d Cir. 1996); Hines v. Pearson, 108 F.3d at 
268. 


Even the Seventh Circuit, which does not require 
a significant, objective deprivation in order to state a 
retaliation claim, DeGuiseppe v. Village of Bellwood, 68 
F.3d 187, 192 (7th Cir. 1995), does apply the "some 
evidence" standard when reviewing a claim of retaliatory 
discipline. McKinney v. Meese, 831 F.2d 728, 733 (7th Cir. 
1987). 


In refusing to require a significant, objective 
deprivation, or to apply the "some evidence" standard, the 
Ninth Circuit stands alone in its deference not to prison 
officials, but to inmates who may bring a claim in federal 
court for the most trivial deprivations based on a mere 
allegation of a retaliatory motive. The Ninth Circuit has 
flouted this Court’s repeated admonitions emphasizing the 


12 


importance of affording prison officials the deference and 
flexibility necessary to manage the volatile environment of 
a prison. Sandin, 115 S.Ct. at 2299; Lewis v. Casey, 116 
S.Ct. at 2185. Applying the "some evidence" standard to 
claims of retaliatory disciplinary charges and requiring 
inmates to establish a significant, objective deprivation 
would further legitimate penological interests in managing 
a prison that is not subjected to abusive litigation by 
inmates and unwarranted second-guessing by courts and 
juries.

In Crawford-El, this Court granted certiorari to 
review a retaiiation claim in light of a qualified immunity 
defense. Accepting review in this case would provide the 
Court with an opportunity to address the standards that 
should govern retaliation cases and furnish much-needed 
uniformity in this area of the law. The two cases are 
complementary. The Court’s determination of the issue 
in this case could furnish the rule to be applied in cases 
like Crawford-El when considering qualified immunity in 
the context of a retaliation claim. This Court’s decision in 
Crawford-El could warrant reconsideration of this case by 
the Ninth Circuit in light of the rule that may be 
announced in Crawford-El.

SUMMARY 


Individual Rights/Civil Rights 


The court of appeals affirmed a judgment of the 
district court. The court held that an inmate’s reputation 
as a complainer and his threat to file a grievance supports 
a jury finding that a prison guard filed a disciplinary 
report in retaliation for the inmate’s use of the grievance 
system. 


Appellant prison guard S. Pearson filed a report 
against appellee death row inmate Gary Hines alleging 
that Hines stopped while being escorted in violation of 
prison rules and that Hines threatened him. Hines filed 
a civil rights suit against Pearson, alleging that Pearson’s 
charge was false and in retaliation for his past use of the 
grievance system. 


At trial, other guards testified as to Hines’s 
reputation for "complaining" and "whining." There was 
evidence that on the morning of the incident Hines told 
Pearson he was going to grievance him. A jury returned 
a verdict for Hines, expressly rejecting Pearson’s proffered 
reason for the disciplinary charge. A magistrate judge 
denied Pearson’s motion for judgment as a matter of law, 
finding that although there was no direct evidence that 
Pearson harbored a retaliatory animus towards Hines 
based on his prolific use of the prison grievance system, 
there was sufficient circumstantial evidence from which a 
jury rationally could have found that Pearson’s conduct 
was driven by a retaliatory motive. 


Pearson appealed, arguing that there was not 
substantial evidence supporting the jury’s finding of 
retaliatory motive because there was no proof that he 
knew of Hines’s use of the grievance system. He also 
contended that the trial court erred by failing to apply the 


3a 


"some evidence" standard of review and that Hines’s 
alleged injuries were not sufficient bases to sustain his 
retaliation claim. 


[1] The evidence supported the inference that 
Pearson knew of Hines’s use of the grievance system. The 
jury’s rejection of Pearson’s reason for the disciplinary 
charge, along with the circumstantial evidence, warranted 
the jury’s finding that Pearson filed the report in 
retaliation for Hines’s use of the grievance system. 


[2] The Supreme Court has held that if there is 
even "some evidence" to support a prison’s disciplinary 
decision, the requirements of due process are satisfied. 
[3] The distinction between the institutional interests in 
a prison board’s disciplinary decision and a guard’s 
accusation of wrongdoing removes cases such as this from 
that authority. A prison board bases its disciplinary 
decision on its own credibility determinations and on 
independent evidence. The board generally affords the 
inmate notice of the charges, an opportunity to call 
witnesses, and a written statement outlining evidence 
relied on and reasons for the disciplinary action. In 
contrast, there are no procedural safeguards protecting a 
prisoner from false retaliatory accusations. A guard may 
file an accusation solely on his own word. 


[4] The correctional officer’s accusations were 
not entitled to the "some evidence" standard of review that 
is afforded disciplinary administrative decisions. 


[5] Prisoners may base retaliation claims on 
harms that would not raise due process concerns. [6] 
Hines alleged that Pearson’s false charge infringed his 
First Amendment right to file prison grievances. His 
failure to demonstrate a more substantial injury did not 
nullify his retaliation claim.

We affirm.

BACKGROUND 


On January 19, 1992, Officer Pearson was escorting 
Hines back to his cell after Hines had his morning shower. 
Hines wore only his boxer shorts with his hands cuffed 
behind his back. Pearson was holding the very short chain 
that connected the cuffs. Nonetheless, Pearson reported 
Hines stopped to receive some unidentified object from 
some other, unidentified inmate. Pearson also reported 
that Hines threatened him. At San Quentin, stopping 
while being escorted violates a prison rule. Hines denies 
he attempted to stop. Hines later filed a prison appeal 
alleging Pearson used excessive force and threatened him 
during the January 19 incident. 


Hines brought this action under § 1983 alleging 
that Pearson’s charge was false and in retaliation for his 
use of the grievance system. The jury returned a verdict 
for Hines against Pearson. The magistrate judge denied 
Pearson’s motion for judgment as a matter of law and 
motion for a new trial. The magistrate judge found that 
although "there was no direct evidence that Officer 
Pearson harbored a retaliatory animus towards plaintiff 
based on plaintiffs prolific use of the prison grievance 
system, there was sufficient circumstantial evidence from 
which a jury rationally could have found that Pearson’s 
conduct was driven by a retaliatory motive." Hines v. 
Gomez, No. CV 92-0120, slip op. at 12 (N.D. Cal. Aug. 18, 
1995) (order denying in part post-trial motions) (emphasis 
in original). 


DISCUSSION 


This court reviews civil jury verdicts to determine 
whether the verdict is supported by substantial evidence. 
George v. City of Long Beach, 973 F.2d 706, 709 (9th Cir. 
1992). 


6a 


I. Pearson Appeal 


Pearson’s appeal raises several issues. We must 
decide whether there was substantial evidence that 
Pearson charged Hines in retaliation for Hines’ use of the 
prison grievance process. Pearson also claims the trial 
court erred by failing to apply the "some evidence" 
standard of review to Pearson, and that Hines’ alleged 
injuries are not sufficient bases to sustain his retaliation 
claim. 


A. Substantial Evidence of Pearson’s Retaliation 


Pearson maintains there was no evidentiary basis 
supporting the jury’s finding that he filed the disciplinary 
report with a retaliatory motive. Hines’ retaliation claim 
must rest on proof that Pearson filed the disciplinary 
action against him in retaliation for Hines’ exercise of his 
constitutional rights and that the retaliatory action 
advanced no legitimate penological interest. See Barnett 
v. Centoni, 31 F.3d 813, 815-16 (1995) (citing Rizzo v. 
Dawson, 778 F.2d 527, 532 (9th Cir. 1985)). Pearson 
challenges whether the evidence satisfies the first prong, 
particularly contending there was no proof that he even 
knew of Hines’ use of the prison grievance system. 


[1] We agree with the magistrate judge’s 
appraisal that reasonable inferences from the evidence 
support the jury’s verdict. Officials and guards other than 
Pearson testified of Hines’ reputation for "complaining" or 
"whining." Hines had in fact filed many grievances. More 
specifically, there was evidence that on the morning of the 
incident giving rise to this suit Hines expressly informed 
Pearson he was "going to grievance him." This evidence 
amply supports the inference that Pearson knew, at least 
to some extent, of Hines’ use of the grievance system. 
Pearson does not contest the jury’s determination that he 
falsely accused Hines of attempting to receive an object 


Ta 


from another inmate. The express rejection of Pearson’s 
proffered reason for the disciplinary charge, the 
circumstantial evidence of Hines’ reputation within the 
prison as a complainer and a whiner, and evidence that on 
the same morning of the incident in question Hines 
threatened Pearson with a grievance, warrants the jury’s 
finding that Pearson filed the disciplinary report in 
retaliation for Hines’ use of the grievance system. 


B. "Some Evidence" Standard to Pearson 


Pearson argues the district court should have 
applied the deferential "some evidence" standard of review 
in analyzing his accusation that Hines violated a 
disciplinary rule. In Superintendent v. Hill, 472 U.S. 445 
(1985), the Supreme Court considered the deference 
courts must afford a prison disciplinary board’s finding 
that a prisoner committed an infraction where the 
punishment is the loss of good time credits. Hill held that 
if there is even "some evidence" to support the prison’s 
disciplinary decision, the requirements of due process are 
satisfied. 472 US. at 455. 


The question becomes whether the deferential Hill 
standard of review should apply not only to a prison 
disciplinary board’s finding of a rule violation, but also to 
a prison guard’s initial accusation of a rule violation where 
the guard’s accusation itself allegedly is false and 
retaliatory. We think not. 


The Hull Court sought to strike the proper balance 
between a prisoner’s right to rational decisions that affect 
his incarceration period and the prison’s interest in 
maintaining institutional safety through efficient discipline 
of prisoners who violate prison rules. In concluding that 
existence of "some evidence" sufficiently accommodates 
the competing interests, the Court reasoned that 
"[r]equiring a modicum of evidence to support a decision 


8a 


to revoke good time credits will help to prevent arbitrary 
deprivations without threatening institutional interests or 
imposing undue administrative burdens." 472 U.S. at 455. 
Because "[p]rison disciplinary proceedings take place in a 
highly charged atmosphere, and prison administrators 
must often act swiftly on the basis of evidence that might 
be insufficient in less exigent circumstances," the Due 
Process Clause does not mandate that courts abrogate 
prison administrators’ decisions "that have some basis in 
fact." Id. at 456. 


[3] The distinction between the institutional 
interests in a prison board’s disciplinary decision and a 
guard’s accusation of wrongdoing removes cases such as 
this from Hill’s authority. A prison board will base its 
disciplinary decision on its own credibility determinations 
and on independent evidence provided to the board. The 
board generally will afford the accused inmate advance 
written notice of the charges, an opportunity to call 
witnesses provided there is no conflict with institutional 
safety and correctional goals, and a written statement 
outlining the evidence relied on and the reasons for the 
disciplinary action. Wolff v. McDonnell, 418 U.S. 539, 
563-67 (1974). The Hill Court expressly considered these 
due process protections when it weighed the prisoner’s 
interests against those of the institution. See Hill, 472 U.S. 
at 454-55. In contrast, there are no procedural safeguards 
protecting a prisoner from false retaliatory accusations; a 
guard may, and often does, file an accusation solely on his 
own word, making his mere accusation the only "modicum 
of evidence." 


Pearson argues that this court’s decision in Barnett 
mandates that we apply the deferential Hill standard to 
his allegation against Hines. We disagree. Barnett does 
not extend the "some evidence" standard to immunize 
retaliatory accusations by prison guards. Barnett focused 


ee 


9a 


on a prison classification committee decision to discipline 
the prisoner. Barnett, 31 F.3d at 814. 


[4] We hold that where a prisoner alleges a 
correctional officer has falsely accused him of violating a 
prison rule in retaliation for the prisoner’s exercise of his 
constitutional rights, the correctional officer’s accusation 
is not entitled to the "some evidence" standard of review 
that we afford disciplinary administrative decisions. The 
magistrate court properly refused to apply the "some 
evidence" standard to Pearson’s allegedly false and 
retaliatory accusation. 


C. Sufficiency of Claimed Injuries 


Pearson argues that Hines’ punishment of ten-day 
confinement and television loss did not constitute an 
“atypical and significant hardship" as required in Sandin v. 
Conner, 115 S. Ct. 2293, 2300 (1995). In Sandin, the 
Supreme Court determined that the Due Process Clause 
afforded a prisoner no protected liberty interest where the 
prisoner claimed prison officials wrongly sentenced him to 
thirty days segregation in a holding cell. Sandin, 115 S. Ct. 
at 2296, 2302. The Court noted, however, that prisoners 


retain other protection from arbitrary state 
action even within the expected conditions 
of confinement. They may invoke the First 
and Eighth Amendments and the Equal 
Protection Clause of the Fourteenth 
Amendment where appropriate, and may 
draw upon internal prison grievance 
procedures and state judicial review where 
available. 


Id. at 2302 n.11. 


10a 


[5] | Since Sandin, this court has reaffirmed that 
prisoners may still base retaliation claims on harms that 
would not raise due process concerns. In Pratt v. 
Rowland, 65 F.3d 802 (9th Cir. 1995), a prisoner 
complained of being double-celled after a long stretch of 
being single-celled, and claimed that prison officials made 
the change to retaliate for his agreement to participate in 
a television interview. This court noted that the prisoner 
had no constitutionally protected liberty interest in being 
held in a given facility, but that the Rizzo line of 
retaliation cases survives Sandin. Pratt, 65 F.3d at 806-07. 
Pratt then concluded that "it would be illegal for [prison] 
officials to transfer and double-cell Pratt solely in 
retaliation for his exercise of protected First Amendment 
rights." Jd. at 807. 


[6] Hines has alleged Pearson’s false charge 
infringed his First Amendment right to file prison 
grievances. The allegation here falls squarely within 
Sandin’s eleventh footnote and Pratt’s reasoning; the injury 
asserted is the retaliatory accusation’s chilling effect on 
Hines’ First Amendment rights, not the additional 
confinement or the deprivation of the television. We hold 
that Hines’ failure to demonstrate a more substantial 
injury does not nullify his retaliation claim. Cf. Perry v. 
Sindermann, 408 U.S. 593, 597-98 (1972) (holding that 
although teacher had no entitlement to employment 
contract renewal, the college administration’s nonrenewal 
based on the teacher’s public criticism of the college is 
actionable as a violation of the teacher’s free speech 
rights).

Szmaciarz 


Hines’ cross-appeal requires us to determine 
whether the trial court erred by applying the "some 


— 


lla 


evidence" standard of review to Szmaciarz. Hines 
contends the standard does not apply where the 
disciplinary charge itself, not the punishment, is contested. 
Obviously, the deferential standard would never apply if 
prisoners could simply fashion their complaint to allege 
retaliation based on the disciplinary charge instead of the 
punishment. In any event, as discussed above, Hill regards 
the degree of deference to afford a prison factfinder’s 
guilt determination; because that determination necessarily 
considers the merits of the charge, we find Hines’ 
argument unpersuasive. In accord with our earlier 
decision in Barnett, 31 F.3d at 815-16, we apply the "some 
evidence" deferential standard of Hill to the findings of 
the disciplinary board in the present case.* 


CONCLUSION 


In sum, we find that substantial evidence supports 
the jury’s verdict that Pearson charged Hines in retaliation 
for Hines’ use of the prison grievance process. The trial 
court’s refusal to apply the "some evidence" standard of 
review to Pearson was not error. Nor was it error to 
apply the standard to Szmaciarz. We also determine that 
Hines’ retaliation claim requires no more substantial injury 
than alleged. 


Accordingly, we affirm the judgment. 


3. The Second Circuit in Graham v. Henderson, 89 F.3d 75 (2d 
Cir. 1996), found that the Hill "some evidence" test was inapplicable 
where the prisoner claimed that the Board’s decision was based on 
retaliation for the exercise of the prisoner’s First Amendment rights. 
Ninth Circuit precedent in Barnett, however, controls our decision 
and we accordingly adhere to it.
