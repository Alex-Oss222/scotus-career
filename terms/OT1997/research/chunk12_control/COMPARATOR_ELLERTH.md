# Burlington Industries v. Ellerth: verified historical comparator

Historical comparator only. Full official [524 U.S. 742-774](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-524/pdf/USREPORTS-524-742.pdf), all three writings and footnotes, read by control. No. 97-569; argued April 22, 1998; decided June 26, 1998. All nine participated.

Judgment: Seventh Circuit affirmed, 7-2, leaving reversal of summary judgment in place for remand. Kennedy wrote for Rehnquist, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter and Breyer. Ginsburg concurred in judgment but expressly adopted the stated employer-liability rule and rejection of dispositive harassment labels. Thomas dissented, joined by Scalia.

* Kennedy, 746-766: employer attribution follows federal general agency principles adapted to Title VII, not the labels quid pro quo/hostile environment or a particular state's law. Unfulfilled threats require sufficiently severe/pervasive conduct; the Court assumed that showing here and reserved whether one unfulfilled threat suffices. Sexual harassment ordinarily lies outside employment scope; employer negligence remains a minimum basis. Actual misuse of supervisory power is analyzed through aid from the agency relationship, not ordinarily apparent authority. An unusual reasonable mistaken belief about supervisory status raises a different apparent-authority question.
* His operative rule covers actionable hostile environments created by an immediate or successively higher supervisor. Employer liability is vicarious. If there is no tangible employment action, the employer may prove by a preponderance both reasonable care to prevent/correct promptly and the employee's unreasonable failure to use provided opportunities or otherwise avoid harm. A written policy is not mandatory in every case; unreasonable failure to use a complaint procedure normally satisfies the second element, but is not its only possible proof. No defense is available when harassment culminates in tangible action. A tangible action means a significant employment-status/benefit change through company authority; the rule is not limited to loss of pay.
* The actual remand allowed Ellerth an adequate opportunity to prove liability and Burlington to assert/prove the new defense. The district court could decide whether pleading amendments or discovery supplementation were appropriate; neither became categorically mandatory. The decision did not adjudicate ultimate employer liability.
* Ginsburg, 766, specifically endorsed the operative rule, substantively identical to Faragher's, and agreed the labels do not govern attribution. She did not join the remainder of Kennedy's reasoning. Therefore the operative rule has seven express supporters; the entire Court opinion has six joins.
* Thomas/Scalia, 766-774: would require proof of employer negligence for a hostile environment, preserving attribution without fault for actual company employment decisions. They rejected the agency-aid theory as a basis for all supervisory harassment, objected to unclear defenses and disparate treatment of sex/race harassment, and would restore summary judgment because Burlington lacked notice and Ellerth had not used its known policy. Their writing treats unfulfilled threats as hostile environment only and completed discriminatory job actions as disparate treatment.

Reconciliation caution: Preserve the difference between six full opinion joins, Ginsburg's narrower substantive endorsement, and seven judgment votes. The defense is available, not established on this record; the remand's permission to consider amendment/discovery is not an order that either must occur. A separately dismissed constructive-discharge claim is not resolved by assumptions about the general phrase reversal of summary judgment.
