# Clinton v. City of New York: factual source extraction

Objective factual and lower-court supplement only. Excludes the historical Supreme Court result, rationale and lineup. Source: official [524 U.S. 417-497](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-524/pdf/USREPORTS-524-417.pdf), fully read including every writing and footnote by control. Do not pass raw source to independent modeling. Fresh neutral validation required. Evidentiary cutoff: June 25, 1998.

* The Line Item Veto Act was enacted April 1996 and effective January 1, 1997. Its cancellation categories are discretionary budget-authority dollars, new direct spending, and limited tax benefits. Under section 691 the President must consider specified legislative-history/purpose information, determine deficit reduction/no impairment of essential government functions/no harm to national interest, and notify Congress by special message within five calendar days excluding Sundays. Cancellation takes effect upon receipt; an enacted disapproval bill makes it null and void. The Act denies cancellation authority over such disapproval bills. Neither challenged cancellation was disapproved by enacted legislation. Both items had been signed into law before cancellation, and compliance with the statutory cancellation procedures was undisputed. (436-437.) These are separate statutory conditions; do not turn a discretion to cancel into a duty to cancel.
* New York's dispute involves Balanced Budget Act section 4722(c), enacted August 5, 1997, and canceled August 11. A 1991 statute reduced federal Medicaid subsidies by specified state provider taxes. HHS notified New York in 1994 that fifteen taxes were covered and that $955 million was then repayable; it allowed waiver applications. New York submitted applications that remained formally undecided. The State estimated exposure through March 1997 as high as $2.6 billion. Section 4722(c) deemed identified pre-June 1, 1997 taxes permissible where the specified waiver had been requested or would otherwise be required and, for requests made, administrative/judicial action was incomplete on July 23, 1997. (422-423; complete statutory quotation in n.2.) Do not assume HHS denied the requests or that the full estimated sum was finally assessed.
* Plaintiffs in the New York action were the City, two hospital associations, one hospital and two employee unions. Under existing New York provisions, adverse HHS action would trigger retroactive provider assessments; NYCHHC's estimated assessment was approximately $4 million for each relevant year. The lower court treated the cancellation as removing statutory protection against that contingent liability. (425-426; 431; relevant New York statutes reproduced at n.10.) The source does not establish that the unions independently suffered a distinct injury.
* Taxpayer Relief Act section 968 amended section 1042 to permit deferral of specified sellers' capital gains on sales to eligible farmers' cooperatives. The described qualifying refiner/processor is a domestic corporation substantially engaged in refining/processing agricultural or horticultural products, purchasing more than half those products in the prior year from the purchasing cooperative or its member farmers. Other qualifying conditions cannot be inferred from this summary; it is not a substitute for the entire tax provision. The provision was expressly designated among limited tax benefits subject to cancellation and was canceled August 11, 1997. (423-425.)
* Snake River is an Idaho cooperative of approximately thirty potato growers formed in May 1997, with acquisition of processing capacity among its purposes. Mike Cranney, a member and officer, also sued. Cranney's declaration reported a May discussion with Idaho Potato Packers' owner about a tax-deferred acquisition if the proposed legislation passed, the owner's expressed interest, and an earlier discussion by Snake River's general manager. Cranney stated that cancellation caused him to terminate the discussions; the declaration also described consideration of other Idaho facilities. (426-427; declaration quoted at 458-459; App. 111-115.) No quoted portion establishes an agreed price, a binding sale, or the seller's refusal after cancellation. The factual extraction deliberately avoids converting discussions into a completed or certain bargain.
* The two actions were consolidated in the District of Columbia. Both sought declarations that the Act and particular cancellations were invalid; neither requested an injunction against the President. Their complaints invoked both the Act's expedited-review clause and federal-question/Declaratory Judgment Act jurisdiction. The district court found at least one plaintiff in each action had Article III standing; it ruled the cancellations inconsistent with bicameralism/presentment and alternatively with separation of powers, 985 F. Supp. 168 (D.D.C. 1998). (425 n.9; 426-428; 455.)
* The government filed both a direct Supreme Court appeal and a protective D.C. Circuit appeal. Section 692 authorizes specified persons to challenge constitutionality in district court, requires prompt service to both Houses with intervention rights, provides a ten-day direct appeal window, and directs expedition. The statutory question whether corporate/governmental appellees are individuals was first raised on appeal. (428-429; 455.) These litigation events do not resolve the statutory route or authorize importing a historical Supreme Court action.

# Eastern Enterprises: factual source extraction

Control-only extraction of objective record facts and lower-court posture. This document excludes the historical Supreme Court decision, its reasoning, voting, and writings. The underlying opinion source must not be handed to independent modeling. Source: official [U.S. Reports, 524 U.S. 498](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-524/pdf/USREPORTS-524-498.pdf), factual recitations at 504-519; the historical opinion set was read in full. A fresh neutral context must validate this extraction before using it. In-world evidentiary cutoff: June 25, 1998.

* Eastern was a Massachusetts business trust formed in 1929. It conducted coal mining until the end of 1965 and signed each NBCWA between 1947 and 1964. The opinion reports contributions exceeding $60 million to the 1947 and 1950 welfare funds, attributing that figure to Eastern's brief. Eastern remained in business through other holdings, including Boston Gas and a barge operator. (516.)
* Eastern transferred its coal operations to EACC, a subsidiary, by the end of 1965. The transfer was described in tax and SEC filings as including assumption of coal-operating liabilities. Eastern retained its interest through CPC until selling CPC to Peabody in 1987. Eastern reported receipt of more than $100 million in EACC dividends during the intervening period. The 1987 agreement assigned Peabody, CPC and EACC responsibility for certain benefit-plan payments. These corporate events do not, by themselves, establish the interpretation or enforceability of any contractual indemnity. (516-517.)
* The 1947 benefit agreement delegated eligibility and benefit levels to trustees. The 1950 agreement established a per-ton contribution, several rather than joint, for the agreement's duration. The 1950 fund's annual reports expressly reserved termination, revision, or amendment of benefits by trustees and disclaimed vested beneficiary interests. Benefits were in fact revised. The 1974 NBCWA expressly addressed retiree health cards and expanded widow coverage; it continued fixed contribution obligations limited to the agreement's duration. The 1978 arrangement added guarantee and evergreen terms. (505-511.) Whether earlier practices created an implied lifetime-benefit understanding is disputed; do not silently equate written contractual language with the entirety of the historical expectation evidence.
* The Coal Act merged the 1950 and 1974 benefit plans into the Combined Fund and prescribed substantially the same benefits. Annual premiums attach to signatory operators remaining in business, including business outside coal; certain related persons may be liable when the signatory no longer operates. The assignment sequence is: (1) the most recent 1978-or-later signatory employing the retiree for at least two years; (2), if none under (1), the most recent such signatory employing the retiree; (3), if none under (1) or (2), the pre-1978 signatory employing the retiree longer than any other signatory. (514-515, quoting 26 U.S.C. sections 9701, 9703, 9706(a).) Exact statutory source text is present in the downloaded PDF/text; no paraphrase here supersedes it.
* The Commissioner assigned Eastern over 1,000 retirees employed by Eastern before 1966 under tier three, section 9706(a)(3). Its annual premium exceeded $5 million. The claim does not concern assignment of strangers to Eastern's former workforce or the separate 1992 UMWA plan. Neither tiers one and two nor unassigned-beneficiary charges are the basis of this assessed liability. (514 n.2; 515 n.3; 517.) The parties estimated cumulative payments differently, approximately $51 million and $100 million; these are estimates rather than adjudicated lifetime damages. (529.)
* Eastern sued the Commissioner, Combined Fund, and trustees in the District of Massachusetts. It asserted facial and as-applied substantive-due-process and takings claims and challenged the Commissioner's statutory interpretation. It sought declaratory and injunctive relief against enforcement, not compensation from the federal Treasury. The district court entered summary judgment against it on all claims, 942 F. Supp. 684 (D. Mass. 1996). The First Circuit affirmed, 110 F.3d 150 (1997), including the statutory construction ruling. (517-520.)
* The lower courts treated the legislation as economic regulation subject to deferential review and accepted a rational relationship between prior participation, miners' expectations, and assigned benefit responsibility. Those are lower-court conclusions; they are not independent findings that Eastern made a legally enforceable lifetime promise. The First Circuit applied the economic-impact, expectations, and character factors to the takings claim. (517-519.)
* The federal respondent initially raised Court of Federal Claims/Tucker Act jurisdiction in its answer. The official recitation reports that the Commissioner no longer challenged district-court adjudication in its Supreme Court brief. Payments run to the privately operated Combined Fund rather than the Treasury. (519-521.) This describes the litigating position and payment destination; it does not determine jurisdiction.

Source limits: No petition, merits briefs, or appendix was independently recovered or read for this extraction. Authenticated published recitations support these facts under Engine section 14(2). Before resolving any fork that turns on disputed implied promises, corporate control, precise indemnity language, or preservation beyond the reported claims, inspect the relevant underlying record, starting with the Internet Archive collection's own docket/name search. No source absence establishes waiver or default.

# NEA v. Finley: factual source extraction

Objective facts and lower-court proceedings only; historical Supreme Court outcome, rationale and lineup excluded. Source: fully read official [524 U.S. 569-623](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-524/pdf/USREPORTS-524-569.pdf). Every writing/footnote read by control. Raw source is withheld from independent modeling. Fresh neutral validation required. Evidentiary cutoff June 25, 1998.

* Section 954(d) is reproduced exactly, by mechanical copying, in FACTS_STATUTE_FINLEY.md. Keep section 954(d)(1)'s decency/respect considerations distinct from section 954(d)(2)'s separate express prohibition on funding obscenity and consistency with statutory purposes. The factual record does not itself resolve what statutory consideration requires or whether panel diversity satisfies that duty.
* NEA applications first go to advisory panels, then to the National Council on the Arts, which advises the Chairperson. The Chairperson has ultimate grant authority but cannot approve an application the Council recommends against. The 1990 statute separately requires diverse artistic/cultural perspectives, broad geographic/ethnic/minority representation and knowledgeable lay members on panels. (573; sections 955(f), 959(c).)
* In December 1990, the Council adopted a resolution seeking implementation of section 954(d)(1) through diversity of panel composition; the Chairperson made a similar statement. There was no promulgated official interpretation of section 954(d)(1). (577.) This describes the agency's conduct, not the legal adequacy of that implementation.
* Finley, Fleck, Hughes and Miller applied before section 954(d)(1) existed. An expert panel recommended the projects, initially and after reconsidering three applications at the Chairperson's request. The Council later recommended disapproval; denial notices issued in June 1990. The artists sued asserting political denial, failure to use statutory procedures, and Privacy Act injury from disclosure of application excerpts; they sought grant restoration/reconsideration and privacy damages. NAAO joined when they amended the complaint to challenge the new provision. (577-578.) Allegations of political motivation or confidentiality breach are not findings.
* The statutory and as-applied claims were settled with payment of the denied grants, damages and fees. The surviving litigation concerns a facial First/Fifth Amendment challenge to section 954(d)(1), not an adjudication that the four original denials violated the later-enacted standard. Two artists, Hughes and Miller, received subsequent grants in September 1991. (578; 586-587.)
* The district court entered summary judgment for the challengers and enjoined enforcement, 795 F. Supp. 1457 (C.D. Cal. 1992). It held the standard insufficiently definite and facially incompatible with protected speech, rejecting diversity of panels as adequate implementation. No stay was sought, so the challenged clause had not been applied since June 1992. A divided Ninth Circuit affirmed, 100 F.3d 671 (1996), on vagueness and viewpoint discrimination; rehearing en banc was denied with dissent, 112 F.3d 1015 (1997). (578-580.)
* The 1989/1990 appropriations restriction and certification requirement were different enactments/practices. Bella Lewitzky Dance Foundation, 754 F. Supp. 774 (C.D. Cal. 1991), invalidated the earlier certification requirement; NEA did not appeal. Do not merge that ruling with the facial challenge to the permanent 1990 amendment. (574-576.)
* The official recitation records that 1998 appropriations restricted individual grants primarily in favor of eligible organizations and state agencies, with subgrant constraints. Exact exceptions and transition depend on section 329, 111 Stat. 1600, which is not reproduced completely in this opinion. (574.) NAAO asserted representative standing through member organizations; the published recitation identifies its director's declaration about potentially controversial member projects. (617; App. 21-25.)

## Bragdon v. Abbott — No. 97-156

Source: [official report, 524 U.S. 624](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-524/pdf/USREPORTS-524-624.pdf), factual and procedural account at 628–630; reproduced statutory terms at 629–632, 648–649; identified record material at 640–641, 650–654, 658–659. Raw PDF and complete extracted text are `USREPORTS-524-624.pdf` and `.txt` in this directory. The source itself is control-only; the neutral context receives this extraction.

- Sidney Abbott had been infected with HIV since 1986. On September 16, 1994, before the infection had manifested its most serious symptoms, she disclosed it on the registration form for a dental appointment at Randon Bragdon's Bangor, Maine office. Bragdon examined her, found a cavity, and declined to fill it in his office under his policy concerning HIV-positive patients. He offered treatment in a hospital without an additional professional fee, but Abbott would bear the hospital-facility charge. She declined. The offer did not include proof that Bragdon possessed hospital privileges or that an available hospital had the safety equipment he described. These are the published account's descriptions of the summary-judgment record, not findings from an evidentiary trial.
- Abbott brought ADA Title III and state-law discrimination claims. The United States and Maine Human Rights Commission intervened as plaintiffs. After discovery, both sides moved for summary judgment. The District of Maine entered judgment for plaintiffs, finding ADA disability coverage and no genuine material factual dispute on direct threat. 912 F. Supp. 580, 585–591 (1995). The First Circuit affirmed both determinations. 107 F.3d 934, 939–948 (1997). The state-law claims were outside the federal questions presented for review.
- The two lower courts used different evidence. The district court relied on affidavits from CDC Oral Health Director Donald Wayne Marianos asserting that treatment with universal precautions was safe. The First Circuit declined to rely on those affidavits because the record did not show that the stated conclusion had been published at the September 1994 treatment date. It instead relied on the CDC's 1993 dentistry guidelines and the American Dental Association's 1991 HIV policy. The guideline's text recommends universal precautions to reduce disease transmission. The association's policy states that HIV-positive patients can be safely treated in private dental offices when appropriate infection-control procedures are used. These are distinct documents from distinct institutions.
- The record included Abbott's unchallenged testimony that knowledge of her HIV infection controlled her decision not to have a child, App. 14. It also included her negative answer when asked whether the infection had impaired her ability to perform her life functions. The published report identifies no evidence of her pre-infection plans for childbearing. Preserve both parts of that record without resolving their legal significance.
- The published account reports record evidence that untreated perinatal transmission risk was about 25%, and that antiretroviral treatment could reduce it to about 8%; the account cites App. 53 for the latter. These are historical evidentiary figures, not present medical guidance. They should not be silently updated with later treatment developments.
- The risk evidence included seven dental workers listed in a June 1994 CDC report as possible occupational HIV transmissions, and 42 documented occupational transmissions to other health-care workers. The seven dental-worker cases were not confirmed occupational transmissions. A 1995 publication later explained the testing limitation preventing attribution; that later explanation must not be treated as information available to Bragdon in September 1994. The record also included a 1991 surgical-power-instrument study that called for further research and expert testimony acknowledging the absence of positive evidence that dental spray transmits HIV. Other expert submissions cited material published after the refusal date. Whether particular conclusions were available in September 1994 must be assessed item by item.
- The operative ADA definition, 42 U.S.C. §12102(2), supplies three alternatives: an impairment substantially limiting one or more major life activities of the individual, a record of such an impairment, or being regarded as having such an impairment. Section 12181(7)(F) includes a health-care provider's professional office among public accommodations. Section 12182(a) prohibits disability discrimination in full and equal enjoyment of covered goods, services, facilities, privileges, advantages, and accommodations by a person operating a public accommodation. Section 12182(b)(3) permits exclusion when an individual presents a direct threat to others' health or safety; its definition requires a significant risk that cannot be eliminated through modifications of policies, practices, or procedures, or auxiliary aids or services. Section 12201(a), except as otherwise provided by the ADA, forbids construing it to apply a lesser standard than Rehabilitation Act Title V or its implementing federal regulations. These statutory alternatives and qualifications must remain distinct.

Source limit: the complete appendix and original expert materials were not separately read. If a disposition depends on a specific exhibit's complete contents or what was established beyond the reproduced account, those materials require targeted record retrieval. The published account itself supports the limited facts above and does not establish the ultimate degree of dental-treatment risk.

## United States v. Balsys — No. 97-873

Source: [official report, 524 U.S. 666](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-524/pdf/USREPORTS-524-666.pdf), factual and procedural account at 669–672; institutional source descriptions at 699 and notes 18–19. Raw PDF and full text are `USREPORTS-524-666.pdf` and `.txt` here; control-only originals.

- Aloyzas Balsys was a resident alien living in Woodhaven, New York. He entered the United States in 1961 on an immigrant visa issued by the American Consulate in Liverpool. His application said he had served in the Lithuanian army from 1934 to 1940 and hidden in Plateliai, Lithuania, from 1940 to 1944. He signed a warning that materially false, misleading, or concealed information could expose him to criminal prosecution and deportation. The account does not establish that those application statements were false.
- The Justice Department's Office of Special Investigations was investigating whether Balsys participated in Nazi persecution and whether his immigration representations were false. Those were investigative subjects, not adjudicated findings of wartime criminal conduct. The identified immigration grounds were 8 U.S.C. §§1182(a)(3)(E), 1251(a)(4)(D), 1182(a)(6)(C)(i), and 1251(a)(1)(A).
- OSI issued an administrative deposition subpoena. Balsys appeared, identified himself and his address, and declined the substantive wartime and immigration questions on self-incrimination grounds. He asserted exposure to prosecution by Lithuania, Israel, and Germany. He asserted no exposure to domestic criminal prosecution. The Government represented that limitations barred prosecution for any immigration misrepresentation; that is a recorded Government representation, not independently reconstructed limitations analysis.
- OSI sought enforcement under §1225(a). The district court found a real and substantial danger of prosecution by Lithuania and Israel, but not Germany, and ordered testimony because it considered the privilege inapplicable to exclusively foreign exposure. 918 F. Supp. 588 (E.D.N.Y. 1996). The Second Circuit vacated that order, holding that real and substantial foreign prosecutorial risk could support the privilege without domestic criminal exposure. 119 F.3d 122 (1997). The Government did not dispute the reasonableness of Balsys's fear concerning Lithuania and Israel. The requested testimony arose in an investigation potentially leading to deportation, not a domestic criminal prosecution.
- Attorney General Order No. 851-79 assigned OSI duties including liaison with foreign prosecution, investigation, and intelligence offices and coordination with domestic agencies. An August 3, 1992 memorandum between DOJ and Lithuania's Procurator General addressed cooperation, mutual legal assistance, and locating or making witnesses available concerning alleged World War II war crimes. The published account identifies its reproduction at Second Circuit appendix 396–397. It does not establish that foreign officials directed this particular subpoena or that a joint prosecution was underway.
- Preserve the Israel source distinction: the district court had not been shown an Israel agreement requiring disclosure of war-crimes evidence. It found that OSI had shared such material with Israel before and that doing so again would accord with OSI's mandate. The existence of an Israel disclosure treaty must not be inferred from the separate, documented Lithuania memorandum.

Source limit: the immigration application, subpoena, OSI order, and Lithuania memorandum were not independently recovered. Their described contents are supported only to the extent recited above. A theory dependent on additional actual coordination, direction, assurances, foreign law, or a particular treaty clause needs targeted primary-record retrieval; no such additional premise is supplied here.

## Monge v. California — No. 97-6146

Source: [official report, 524 U.S. 721](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-524/pdf/USREPORTS-524-721.pdf), factual and lower-court account at 724–727; statutory sentencing ranges at 729. Raw PDF and full text are `USREPORTS-524-721.pdf` and `.txt` here; control-only originals.

- The jury convicted Monge of using a minor to sell marijuana, selling or transporting marijuana, and possessing marijuana for sale, under California Health and Safety Code §§11361(a), 11360(a), and 11359. The information also gave notice of two sentence-enhancement allegations: a prior assault conviction and a prison term served for it. The challenged prior conviction's serious-felony qualification and the separate prior-prison-term enhancement must not be collapsed.
- California's three-strikes provisions double the term for a current felony when there is one qualifying prior serious felony, and prescribe 25 years to life when there are two. An assault qualifies under the provisions at issue if the defendant personally used a dangerous or deadly weapon or personally inflicted great bodily injury. A generic assault-with-a-deadly-weapon conviction does not alone establish that personal-conduct requirement. California afforded a jury, confrontation, self-incrimination protection, rules of evidence, and proof beyond a reasonable doubt for the allegations.
- Monge waived a jury on the enhancement allegations and obtained bifurcation. At the subsequent bench proceeding, the prosecutor asserted personal use of a stick but introduced only a prison record showing conviction for assault with a deadly weapon and imprisonment. The personal-use assertion was not itself the missing evidentiary proof. The trial judge found both allegations true and imposed 11 years: a five-year sentence on count one doubled to ten, plus one year for the prior prison term. A three-year sentence on count two was stayed; a two-year sentence on count three ran concurrently.
- The Court of Appeal requested supplemental briefing on evidentiary sufficiency. The State conceded that the sentencing record failed to prove beyond a reasonable doubt personal use of a weapon or infliction of great bodily injury, and requested a new opportunity on remand. The appellate court found the evidence insufficient and held retrial barred by double jeopardy. The California Supreme Court reversed the double-jeopardy ruling. 16 Cal.4th 826, 941 P.2d 1121 (1997). Its judgment allowed another attempt to prove the prior-conviction allegation; it did not erase the conceded insufficiency of the first record.
- The published account gives the first-offender maximum for count one as seven years and the one-prior qualifying maximum as fourteen years. The actual sentence selected before doubling was five years. Keep the authorized maximum, selected term, and separate one-year enhancement distinct.

Source limit: the appendix prison record and complete California opinions were not separately recovered. The reproduced account establishes the State's concession, evidence introduced, and disposition below without requiring an invented new fact about the underlying assault. Any later attempt to identify the assault's actual personal conduct would require the underlying record; it is not supplied by the prosecutor's assertion.

# Burlington Industries v. Ellerth: factual source extraction

Objective facts and lower-court posture only. Historical Supreme Court reasoning, result and lineup are excluded. Source: fully read official [524 U.S. 742-774](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-524/pdf/USREPORTS-524-742.pdf), including every writing and footnote. Raw source must be withheld from independent modeling. Fresh neutral validation required. Evidentiary cutoff: June 26, 1998.

* Ellerth worked as a Chicago salesperson from March 1993 until May 1994. Slowik, a vice president within a business unit, was a midlevel manager above her immediate supervisor. He could make hiring/promotion decisions subject to his superior's approval and signature; the record described him as outside the upper policymaking hierarchy. (747.)
* Ellerth alleged repeated sexual comments/gestures, and three episodes involving threats associated with job benefits: a business-trip lounge conversation; remarks and touching during a promotion interview followed by an actual promotion; and sexual remarks while she sought permission to use a customer logo in a sample. She rejected his advances. The threats were not carried out through a denial of promotion or other tangible retaliation. Because the employer won summary judgment, the allegations were taken favorably to her for review; they are not trial findings. (747-749.)
* After her immediate supervisor criticized delayed customer callbacks, Ellerth resigned with a letter stating reasons unrelated to sexual harassment. About three weeks later she sent a letter attributing her resignation to Slowik. She knew Burlington had an antiharassment policy but did not report Slowik to an authority during employment. She once told Slowik a remark was inappropriate. (748-749.) Those reported facts do not by themselves resolve the legal reasonableness of her conduct or Burlington's prevention efforts.
* After an EEOC right-to-sue letter, she filed in the Northern District of Illinois in October 1994. The district court found the alleged conduct sufficiently severe/pervasive to create a hostile environment, but entered summary judgment for Burlington because there was no triable evidence that it knew or should have known of the harassment. It noted the unused complaint procedures and separately dismissed constructive discharge. 912 F. Supp. 1101 (N.D. Ill. 1996). (749.)
* The Seventh Circuit en banc reversed the summary judgment in Jansen v. Packing Corp. of America, 123 F.3d 490 (1997). Eight opinions disclosed disagreement on attribution of supervisor conduct to an employer; the dispute on appeal was vicarious liability rather than proof of independent employer negligence. Some judges treated unfulfilled threats as sufficient; others required a company act or employer negligence. (749-751.) These are lower-court approaches, not source-established statutory rules.
* The petition's quoted question asks whether Title VII permits the claim where the employee neither submitted to the sexual advances nor suffered tangible effects in compensation, terms, conditions or privileges as a consequence of refusing. (753, quoting petition i.) No party argued that Slowik's rank made him the employer's alter ego or that a nondelegable duty supplied liability. (758.)
