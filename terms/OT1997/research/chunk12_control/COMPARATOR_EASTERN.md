# Eastern Enterprises: verified historical comparator

Historical comparator only; not in-world law or an independent-modeling input. Full official opinion set, 524 U.S. 498-568, read including footnotes; [govinfo PDF](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-524/pdf/USREPORTS-524-498.pdf). Argument March 4, 1998; decision June 25, 1998; No. 97-42. Official local PDF and mechanically extracted UTF-8 text are preserved alongside this memo.

Historical judgment: First Circuit reversed and remanded, 5-4. O'Connor announced judgment, joined in her opinion by Rehnquist, Scalia and Thomas. Kennedy concurred in judgment and dissented in part. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer dissented. Every Justice participated.

* O'Connor's four-Justice plurality, 503-538, applied regulatory-takings analysis to tier-three liability as applied to Eastern. Economic impact ($50-100 million estimated), interference with settled expectations, and the unusual retroactive allocation made the burden unconstitutional. It treated the pre-1974 written funds and later guarantees as distinct, viewed post-1965 subsidiary ownership as unrelated to the statutory assignment formula, and rejected possible indemnity as curing Eastern's direct burden. It allowed equitable relief in district court because Treasury compensation was not meaningfully available for compelled payments to a private fund. It did not decide due process or tiers one and two; remedy was an injunction against section 9706(a)(3) as applied to Eastern and remand.
* Thomas, 538-539, joined the plurality fully, while expressing willingness in an appropriate case to reconsider Calder's criminal-only limitation on the Ex Post Facto Clause. That proposed reconsideration was not adopted.
* Kennedy, 539-550, rejected takings analysis because an undifferentiated obligation to pay money does not operate on a specific property interest. Whether a transfer is to government or private parties was not his decisive distinction. He instead found the severe retroactivity arbitrary under due process: Eastern did not generate the later promises and funding crisis. He did not decide the plurality's Tucker Act discussion. He emphasized that invalidation under deferential economic due process is rare.
* Stevens, joined by Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, 550-553, viewed the historical evidence as supporting an implicit pre-1974 understanding of continued benefits, and would defer to Congress and the Coal Commission rather than replace their fairness judgment. Eastern had not carried its burden under either asserted constitutional provision.
* Breyer, joined by Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg, 553-568, agreed with Kennedy that no specific property interest was taken and treated due process as the proper route. He would uphold: Eastern benefited from the miners' work, helped create expectations of continuing benefits, operated through a profitable subsidiary until 1987, and faced obligations limited to its former workers with indemnity rights preserved. He distinguished a contractual promise from industry understandings and reasonable expectations, while accepting that extreme retroactivity can violate due process.

Topology caution: Four plurality votes for takings are not five votes; Kennedy's due-process rationale is not the same legal rule, nor is either rationale automatically a logical subset of the other. Five Justices rejected using the Takings Clause here, but that agreement spans opposite judgments. Historical reasoning remains comparator material and cannot supply simulated joins.
