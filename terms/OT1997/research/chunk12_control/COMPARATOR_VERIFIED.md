# Clinton v. City of New York: verified historical comparator

Historical comparator only. Full official [524 U.S. 417-497](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-524/pdf/USREPORTS-524-417.pdf), all four writings and footnotes, read by control. No. 97-1374; argued April 27, 1998; decided June 25, 1998. All nine participated.

Historical judgment: District court affirmed. Stevens wrote the Court opinion, joined by Rehnquist, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg: six votes holding the cancellation procedures invalid under the Presentment Clause. Kennedy separately concurred. Scalia concurred in part/dissented in part, joined throughout by O'Connor and in Part III by Breyer. Breyer dissented, joined in Part III only by O'Connor and Scalia. Do not flatten this structure to a single disagreement: all nine accepted New York standing; seven accepted Snake River standing, Scalia and O'Connor rejecting it.

* Stevens, 420-449: construed expedited-review individuals broadly; found New York's revived contingent liability a present injury and Snake River's lost statutory bargaining advantage sufficiently concrete, traceable and redressable. He treated cancellation after enactment as a partial repeal lacking Article I procedures. Field's contingent, prescribed implementation of prior congressional policy differed from discretionary rejection of an already-enacted policy. Ordinary spending discretion did not authorize unilateral alteration of enacted text. The holding did not reach the district court's alternative separation-of-powers/delegation ground, discretionary-budget severability, or the disputed details of lockbox applicability. No union standing determination was needed.
* Kennedy, 449-453: fully joined. Structural limits protect individual liberty as well as institutional prerogatives; voluntary congressional surrender and a desirable deficit-reduction goal cannot cure departure from constitutional structure. Concentrated power to favor or burden identifiable groups makes the liberty interest concrete.
* Scalia, 453-469: Part I, joined by O'Connor, would give individuals its ordinary natural-person meaning, but reach the New York dispute through certiorari before judgment on the protective circuit appeal. Part II, O'Connor joining, rejected Snake River standing because discussions and potential tax benefits did not establish a sufficiently likely bargain or third-party response. Part III, joined by O'Connor and Breyer, accepted New York standing but upheld direct-spending cancellation: enactment had complied with presentment and the issue was permissible delegation, supported by longstanding discretionary appropriations practice. Part IV declined the tax-benefit merits because no proper plaintiff had standing in his view. Never attribute a tax-benefit merits vote to Scalia or O'Connor through this writing.
* Breyer, 469-497: accepted both sets of plaintiffs' standing; would uphold the Act. Part III, joined by O'Connor and Scalia, argued that exercising a congressionally granted power to prevent legal effect executes the law rather than amends its text, discussing conditional laws and lockbox effects. The rest of his opinion was unjoined: the delegation is executive in operation, Congress retains checks and exemption/disapproval powers, and the limited budgetary authority satisfies intelligible-principle review. He separately recognized the thinner history and increased arbitrariness concern for tax benefits but found those distinctions insufficient. Agency-style judicial review and subsidiary rules are missing, but presidential electoral responsibility and the bounded subject matter mattered to him.

Reconciliation cautions: A 6-3 shorthand for the constitutional judgment does not resolve the separate 7-2 Snake River standing divide or Scalia/O'Connor's refusal to decide the tax-benefit merits. Partial cross-joins are limited to the named Parts. Historical quotations of private declarations are factual evidence only after separate sanitization and neutral validation.

# Eastern Enterprises: verified historical comparator

Historical comparator only; not in-world law or an independent-modeling input. Full official opinion set, 524 U.S. 498-568, read including footnotes; [govinfo PDF](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-524/pdf/USREPORTS-524-498.pdf). Argument March 4, 1998; decision June 25, 1998; No. 97-42. Official local PDF and mechanically extracted UTF-8 text are preserved alongside this memo.

Historical judgment: First Circuit reversed and remanded, 5-4. O'Connor announced judgment, joined in her opinion by Rehnquist, Scalia and Thomas. Kennedy concurred in judgment and dissented in part. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer dissented. Every Justice participated.

* O'Connor's four-Justice plurality, 503-538, applied regulatory-takings analysis to tier-three liability as applied to Eastern. Economic impact ($50-100 million estimated), interference with settled expectations, and the unusual retroactive allocation made the burden unconstitutional. It treated the pre-1974 written funds and later guarantees as distinct, viewed post-1965 subsidiary ownership as unrelated to the statutory assignment formula, and rejected possible indemnity as curing Eastern's direct burden. It allowed equitable relief in district court because Treasury compensation was not meaningfully available for compelled payments to a private fund. It did not decide due process or tiers one and two; remedy was an injunction against section 9706(a)(3) as applied to Eastern and remand.
* Thomas, 538-539, joined the plurality fully, while expressing willingness in an appropriate case to reconsider Calder's criminal-only limitation on the Ex Post Facto Clause. That proposed reconsideration was not adopted.
* Kennedy, 539-550, rejected takings analysis because an undifferentiated obligation to pay money does not operate on a specific property interest. Whether a transfer is to government or private parties was not his decisive distinction. He instead found the severe retroactivity arbitrary under due process: Eastern did not generate the later promises and funding crisis. He did not decide the plurality's Tucker Act discussion. He emphasized that invalidation under deferential economic due process is rare.
* Stevens, joined by Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, 550-553, viewed the historical evidence as supporting an implicit pre-1974 understanding of continued benefits, and would defer to Congress and the Coal Commission rather than replace their fairness judgment. Eastern had not carried its burden under either asserted constitutional provision.
* Breyer, joined by Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg, 553-568, agreed with Kennedy that no specific property interest was taken and treated due process as the proper route. He would uphold: Eastern benefited from the miners' work, helped create expectations of continuing benefits, operated through a profitable subsidiary until 1987, and faced obligations limited to its former workers with indemnity rights preserved. He distinguished a contractual promise from industry understandings and reasonable expectations, while accepting that extreme retroactivity can violate due process.

Topology caution: Four plurality votes for takings are not five votes; Kennedy's due-process rationale is not the same legal rule, nor is either rationale automatically a logical subset of the other. Five Justices rejected using the Takings Clause here, but that agreement spans opposite judgments. Historical reasoning remains comparator material and cannot supply simulated joins.

# NEA v. Finley: verified historical comparator

Historical-only material, withheld from independent modeling. Full official [524 U.S. 569-623](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-524/pdf/USREPORTS-524-569.pdf), including three writings and all footnotes, read by control. No. 97-371; argued March 31, 1998; decided June 25, 1998. All nine participated.

Judgment: Ninth Circuit reversed/remanded, 8-1. O'Connor wrote for Rehnquist, Stevens, Kennedy and Breyer throughout, and Ginsburg except Part II-B. Scalia, joined by Thomas, concurred in judgment. Souter dissented. Ginsburg's refusal is the spending-priority discussion, not Part III vagueness. Souter expressly agreed against vagueness in n.17, despite his overall dissent.

* O'Connor, 572-590, held the provision facially valid. Part II-A (six votes) treated decency/respect as considerations, not categorical exclusions, and declined to decide whether panel diversity alone meets the statutory command. Subjective competitive excellence criteria differ from the generally available subsidy in Rosenberger. The opinion did not authorize invidious viewpoint penalties: particular denials, coercive use of subsidies, or burdens designed to drive disfavored ideas from the marketplace remained open. Part II-B (five votes, excluding Ginsburg) addressed Congress's broad latitude to choose spending priorities and referenced Rust/Maher. Part III (six joined votes) rejected facial vagueness in a selective, inherently subjective grant process, distinguishing criminal/regulatory consequences.
* Scalia, joined by Thomas, 590-600: the statutory command must be considered for every grant although need not always be dispositive; diverse panels alone do not satisfy it. He accepted, or assumed, viewpoint discrimination and upheld it because declining subsidy does not abridge speech. He viewed First Amendment limits and vagueness as inapplicable to funding as such, except that government cannot use a nominal subsidy coercively to abridge speech; he did not accept a requirement to subsidize all art unable to attract private support. He distinguished Rosenberger as a limited-public-forum case.
* Souter, 600-623: the proviso requires viewpoint discrimination; vague wording or nonconclusive weight does not cure the constitutional problem. Government as patron differs from government speaking or buying a work, and selective competition does not remove viewpoint neutrality. He would sustain facial invalidation under substantial overbreadth because of many impermissible applications and chilled applications, citing the association declaration and grant imprimatur/matching effects. He expressly rejected the separate vagueness challenge (622 n.17): unavoidable imprecision in artistic merit is tolerable; his objection was discrimination against ideas.

Limits: The facial judgment does not determine the settled artists' individual claims. Do not treat all eight judgment supporters as endorsing the Court's reasoning. Do not attribute II-B to Ginsburg or a vagueness dissent to Souter. The Court's express unresolved panel-diversity construction is not an adopted saving interpretation.

## Bragdon v. Abbott, 524 U.S. 624 (1998)

[Official full report](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-524/pdf/USREPORTS-524-624.pdf). No. 97-156; argued March 30, 1998; decided June 25, 1998. Historical disposition: First Circuit's disability determination affirmed; direct-threat judgment vacated and remanded for further proceedings consistent with the Court's opinion, with the same eventual lower-court conclusion left possible. All nine participated.

The Court's opinion was by Kennedy, joined completely by Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Its disability ruling was 5–4. The five also supplied the controlling direct-threat analysis and remand. Rehnquist, Scalia, Thomas, and O'Connor agreed that the risk judgment should be vacated but adopted a different view of the needed proceedings; do not flatten that agreement into a unanimous rationale or an undifferentiated merits vote.

- Kennedy, 628–655: HIV is a physical impairment from infection; reproduction is a major life activity; Abbott's infection substantially limited it, including through risk to partner and child. The holding was applied to her and did not reach per se disability or the alternative recorded/regarded-as definitions. It did not resolve whether mitigating measures are disregarded: even the cited treated-risk figure sufficed. Rehabilitation Act regulatory continuity and agency interpretations reinforced the reading. Direct threat depends on objective, then-available scientific evidence, with no personal deference to the treating dentist; public-health views carry special but nonconclusive weight and can be rebutted on a credible scientific basis. The CDC and dental-association materials did not conclusively establish the needed risk level on this record. Because briefing had not explored the whole sufficiency question, remand permitted fuller evaluation; it did not command a trial or decide Bragdon ultimately prevails.
- Stevens, joined by Breyer, 655–656: would have affirmed outright because Bragdon's data were insufficient to create a material factual dispute. Nevertheless joined Kennedy's opinion and remand to provide a judgment with majority support. This is an explicit remedial accommodation, not a withheld join.
- Ginsburg alone, 656–657: HIV pervasively limits life choices and meets actual or perceived disability concepts through family relations, employment potential, and self-care. Agreed with cautious remand for an informed risk determination.
- Rehnquist, joined by Scalia and Thomas throughout and O'Connor only in Part II, 657–664: Part I demanded an individual inquiry, rejected reproduction as the relevant statutory major activity, and found no established present substantial limitation in Abbott's own activities; he also resisted the alternative regarded-as route. Part II agreed with vacatur but found sufficient evidence for trial, including the possible dental transmissions and other occupational transmissions. Rejected special evidentiary weight merely because a public authority endorsed a scientific view; agreed the perspective is the decision date.
- O'Connor alone, 664–665, plus her Part II join above: disability required individual proof; childbirth was not generally comparable to the listed activities and Abbott had not shown a qualifying limitation. Did not decide other intimate/family activities or whether reproduction would be substantially limited if recognized as a major activity. Joined Rehnquist's direct-threat Part II and agreed remand was necessary.

## United States v. Balsys, 524 U.S. 666 (1998)

[Official full report](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-524/pdf/USREPORTS-524-666.pdf). No. 97-873; argued April 20, 1998; decided June 25, 1998. Historical disposition: Second Circuit reversed and remanded, 7–2. All nine participated. Souter authored; Rehnquist, Stevens, O'Connor, and Kennedy joined the entire opinion; Scalia and Thomas joined Parts I–III only. They supplied no separate explanation. Ginsburg and Breyer dissented. Parts IV and V therefore have five, not seven, joins.

- Souter, 669–700: fear solely of foreign prosecution falls outside the Self-Incrimination Clause on these facts. Parts I–III read the Clause in Fifth Amendment context and retained Murdock's same-sovereign understanding as altered by incorporation. Murphy's result followed Malloy's application of one constitutional privilege to state and federal governments; its alternative English-history rationale was rejected. Part IV rejected extending the privilege through generalized policy analysis, noting conditional testimonial privacy, immunity constraints, and uncertain comparative costs. Part V reserved—not adopted—a possible application where substantially similar international criminal prohibitions and deliberately shared evidence gathering/prosecution make the supposedly foreign case effectively American too. OSI support and evidence sharing here did not establish such cooperative prosecution. The foreign sovereign's mere receipt of evidence is not the reserved scenario's complete test.
- Stevens alone, 700–701: joined without reservation, emphasizing fairness in American courts and the danger that foreign criminal laws could disable American evidence gathering. Favored the clear domestic limitation over exceptions crafted around foreign obstruction laws.
- Ginsburg alone, 701–702: the privilege governs American officials' conduct and protects civilized treatment of an examinee reasonably fearing later prosecution, irrespective of prosecuting country; expressly joined Breyer's dissent.
- Breyer, joined by Ginsburg, 702–720: read Murphy as rejecting the same-sovereign principle rather than merely adjusting federalism; found its history at least plausible and controlling under stare decisis. Dignity, privacy, accusatorial justice, and prevention of coercive overreach applied when foreign risk was real and substantial. Increasing international cooperation strengthened the analogy. The privilege would be question-specific; adverse inferences in civil proceedings and other evidence remained possible. Government assurances concerning deportation, extradition, or foreign use could sometimes remove risk, with genuine practical difficulties acknowledged.

## Monge v. California, 524 U.S. 721 (1998)

[Official full report](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-524/pdf/USREPORTS-524-721.pdf). No. 97-6146; argued April 28, 1998; decided June 26, 1998. Historical disposition: California Supreme Court affirmed, 5–4. O'Connor authored, joined by Rehnquist, Kennedy, Thomas, and Breyer. Stevens dissented alone. Scalia dissented with Souter and Ginsburg. All nine participated; no concurrences or partial majority joins.

- O'Connor, 724–734: double jeopardy does not prevent retrial of a prior-conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing. Sentencing findings generally concern punishment rather than a separate offense and lack acquittal finality. Bullington's capital exception depends on both trial-like procedures and capital punishment's special reliability demands; state provision of trial protections for noncapital enhancements does not itself extend that exception. The Court treated the element theory as neither raised below nor discussed in the petitioner's brief and also foreclosed by historical Almendarez-Torres. The opinion acknowledged that some other circumstances might require treating a purported factor as an element; it did not purport to decide every conceivable sentencing factor.
- Stevens, 734–737: distinguished insufficiency from trial error under Burks. The State failed its first proof attempt and should receive no second opportunity; long-standing procedural safeguards reflected fundamental fairness rather than merely new legislative grace. Emphasized the actual automatic additional punishment and change in range.
- Scalia, joined by Souter and Ginsburg, 737–741: agreed that Bullington should not extend to true noncapital sentencing, but rejected classifying this maximum-increasing fact as merely sentencing. Legislative labels cannot defeat offense elements, jury rights, proof beyond reasonable doubt, and acquittal finality. Would treat the insufficiency ruling as acquittal of the additional offense. Acknowledged conflict with Almendarez-Torres and called that precedent constitutionally erroneous; noted its reservation of nonrecidivist enhancements. In footnote 2, considered the element theory fairly included in Monge's preserved double-jeopardy claim despite its not being expressly articulated. This preservation disagreement is part of the historical comparator, not proof of a new filing.

No missing historical writing was identified in these three complete official reports. Remaining source limits concern underlying records, as separately stated in FACTS_SUBSET.md, not the completeness of the historical opinion sets.

# Burlington Industries v. Ellerth: verified historical comparator

Historical comparator only. Full official [524 U.S. 742-774](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-524/pdf/USREPORTS-524-742.pdf), all three writings and footnotes, read by control. No. 97-569; argued April 22, 1998; decided June 26, 1998. All nine participated.

Judgment: Seventh Circuit affirmed, 7-2, leaving reversal of summary judgment in place for remand. Kennedy wrote for Rehnquist, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter and Breyer. Ginsburg concurred in judgment but expressly adopted the stated employer-liability rule and rejection of dispositive harassment labels. Thomas dissented, joined by Scalia.

* Kennedy, 746-766: employer attribution follows federal general agency principles adapted to Title VII, not the labels quid pro quo/hostile environment or a particular state's law. Unfulfilled threats require sufficiently severe/pervasive conduct; the Court assumed that showing here and reserved whether one unfulfilled threat suffices. Sexual harassment ordinarily lies outside employment scope; employer negligence remains a minimum basis. Actual misuse of supervisory power is analyzed through aid from the agency relationship, not ordinarily apparent authority. An unusual reasonable mistaken belief about supervisory status raises a different apparent-authority question.
* His operative rule covers actionable hostile environments created by an immediate or successively higher supervisor. Employer liability is vicarious. If there is no tangible employment action, the employer may prove by a preponderance both reasonable care to prevent/correct promptly and the employee's unreasonable failure to use provided opportunities or otherwise avoid harm. A written policy is not mandatory in every case; unreasonable failure to use a complaint procedure normally satisfies the second element, but is not its only possible proof. No defense is available when harassment culminates in tangible action. A tangible action means a significant employment-status/benefit change through company authority; the rule is not limited to loss of pay.
* The actual remand allowed Ellerth an adequate opportunity to prove liability and Burlington to assert/prove the new defense. The district court could decide whether pleading amendments or discovery supplementation were appropriate; neither became categorically mandatory. The decision did not adjudicate ultimate employer liability.
* Ginsburg, 766, specifically endorsed the operative rule, substantively identical to Faragher's, and agreed the labels do not govern attribution. She did not join the remainder of Kennedy's reasoning. Therefore the operative rule has seven express supporters; the entire Court opinion has six joins.
* Thomas/Scalia, 766-774: would require proof of employer negligence for a hostile environment, preserving attribution without fault for actual company employment decisions. They rejected the agency-aid theory as a basis for all supervisory harassment, objected to unclear defenses and disparate treatment of sex/race harassment, and would restore summary judgment because Burlington lacked notice and Ellerth had not used its known policy. Their writing treats unfulfilled threats as hostile environment only and completed discriminatory job actions as disparate treatment.

Reconciliation caution: Preserve the difference between six full opinion joins, Ginsburg's narrower substantive endorsement, and seven judgment votes. The defense is available, not established on this record; the remand's permission to consider amendment/discovery is not an order that either must occur. A separately dismissed constructive-discharge claim is not resolved by assumptions about the general phrase reversal of summary judgment.
