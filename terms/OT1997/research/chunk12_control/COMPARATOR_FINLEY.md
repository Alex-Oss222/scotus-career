# NEA v. Finley: verified historical comparator

Historical-only material, withheld from independent modeling. Full official [524 U.S. 569-623](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-524/pdf/USREPORTS-524-569.pdf), including three writings and all footnotes, read by control. No. 97-371; argued March 31, 1998; decided June 25, 1998. All nine participated.

Judgment: Ninth Circuit reversed/remanded, 8-1. O'Connor wrote for Rehnquist, Stevens, Kennedy and Breyer throughout, and Ginsburg except Part II-B. Scalia, joined by Thomas, concurred in judgment. Souter dissented. Ginsburg's refusal is the spending-priority discussion, not Part III vagueness. Souter expressly agreed against vagueness in n.17, despite his overall dissent.

* O'Connor, 572-590, held the provision facially valid. Part II-A (six votes) treated decency/respect as considerations, not categorical exclusions, and declined to decide whether panel diversity alone meets the statutory command. Subjective competitive excellence criteria differ from the generally available subsidy in Rosenberger. The opinion did not authorize invidious viewpoint penalties: particular denials, coercive use of subsidies, or burdens designed to drive disfavored ideas from the marketplace remained open. Part II-B (five votes, excluding Ginsburg) addressed Congress's broad latitude to choose spending priorities and referenced Rust/Maher. Part III (six joined votes) rejected facial vagueness in a selective, inherently subjective grant process, distinguishing criminal/regulatory consequences.
* Scalia, joined by Thomas, 590-600: the statutory command must be considered for every grant although need not always be dispositive; diverse panels alone do not satisfy it. He accepted, or assumed, viewpoint discrimination and upheld it because declining subsidy does not abridge speech. He viewed First Amendment limits and vagueness as inapplicable to funding as such, except that government cannot use a nominal subsidy coercively to abridge speech; he did not accept a requirement to subsidize all art unable to attract private support. He distinguished Rosenberger as a limited-public-forum case.
* Souter, 600-623: the proviso requires viewpoint discrimination; vague wording or nonconclusive weight does not cure the constitutional problem. Government as patron differs from government speaking or buying a work, and selective competition does not remove viewpoint neutrality. He would sustain facial invalidation under substantial overbreadth because of many impermissible applications and chilled applications, citing the association declaration and grant imprimatur/matching effects. He expressly rejected the separate vagueness challenge (622 n.17): unavoidable imprecision in artistic merit is tolerable; his objection was discrimination against ideas.

Limits: The facial judgment does not determine the settled artists' individual claims. Do not treat all eight judgment supporters as endorsing the Court's reasoning. Do not attribute II-B to Ginsburg or a vagueness dissent to Souter. The Court's express unresolved panel-diversity construction is not an adopted saving interpretation.
