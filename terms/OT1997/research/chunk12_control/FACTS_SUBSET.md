## Bragdon v. Abbott — No. 97-156

Source: [official report, 524 U.S. 624](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-524/pdf/USREPORTS-524-624.pdf), factual and procedural account at 628–630; reproduced statutory terms at 629–632, 648–649; identified record material at 640–641, 650–654, 658–659. Raw PDF and complete extracted text are `USREPORTS-524-624.pdf` and `.txt` in this directory. The source itself is control-only; the neutral context receives this extraction.

- Sidney Abbott had been infected with HIV since 1986. On September 16, 1994, before the infection had manifested its most serious symptoms, she disclosed it on the registration form for a dental appointment at Randon Bragdon's Bangor, Maine office. Bragdon examined her, found a cavity, and declined to fill it in his office under his policy concerning HIV-positive patients. He offered treatment in a hospital without an additional professional fee, but Abbott would bear the hospital-facility charge. She declined. The offer did not include proof that Bragdon possessed hospital privileges or that an available hospital had the safety equipment he described. These are the published account's descriptions of the summary-judgment record, not findings from an evidentiary trial.
- Abbott brought ADA Title III and state-law discrimination claims. The United States and Maine Human Rights Commission intervened as plaintiffs. After discovery, both sides moved for summary judgment. The District of Maine entered judgment for plaintiffs, finding ADA disability coverage and no genuine material factual dispute on direct threat. 912 F. Supp. 580, 585–591 (1995). The First Circuit affirmed both determinations. 107 F.3d 934, 939–948 (1997). The state-law claims were outside the federal questions presented for review.
- The two lower courts used different evidence. The district court relied on affidavits from CDC Oral Health Director Donald Wayne Marianos asserting that treatment with universal precautions was safe. The First Circuit declined to rely on those affidavits because the record did not show that the stated conclusion had been published at the September 1994 treatment date. It instead relied on the CDC's 1993 dentistry guidelines and the American Dental Association's 1991 HIV policy. The guideline's text recommends universal precautions to reduce disease transmission. The association's policy states that HIV-positive patients can be safely treated in private dental offices when appropriate infection-control procedures are used. These are distinct documents from distinct institutions.
- The record included Abbott's unchallenged testimony that knowledge of her HIV infection controlled her decision not to have a child, App. 14. It also included her negative answer when asked whether the infection had impaired her ability to perform her life functions. The published report identifies no evidence of her pre-infection plans for childbearing. Preserve both parts of that record without resolving their legal significance.
- The published account reports record evidence that untreated perinatal transmission risk was about 25%, and that antiretroviral treatment could reduce it to about 8%; the account cites App. 53 for the latter. These are historical evidentiary figures, not present medical guidance. They should not be silently updated with later treatment developments.
- The risk evidence included seven dental workers listed in a June 1994 CDC report as possible occupational HIV transmissions, and 42 documented occupational transmissions to other health-care workers. The seven dental-worker cases were not confirmed occupational transmissions. A 1995 publication later explained the testing limitation preventing attribution; that later explanation must not be treated as information available to Bragdon in September 1994. The record also included a 1991 surgical-power-instrument study that called for further research and expert testimony acknowledging the absence of positive evidence that dental spray transmits HIV. Other expert submissions cited material published after the refusal date. Whether particular conclusions were available in September 1994 must be assessed item by item.
- The operative ADA definition, 42 U.S.C. §12102(2), supplies three alternatives: an impairment substantially limiting one or more major life activities of the individual, a record of such an impairment, or being regarded as having such an impairment. Section 12181(7)(F) includes a health-care provider's professional office among public accommodations. Section 12182(a) prohibits disability discrimination in full and equal enjoyment of covered goods, services, facilities, privileges, advantages, and accommodations by a person operating a public accommodation. Section 12182(b)(3) permits exclusion when an individual presents a direct threat to others' health or safety; its definition requires a significant risk that cannot be eliminated through modifications of policies, practices, or procedures, or auxiliary aids or services. Section 12201(a), except as otherwise provided by the ADA, forbids construing it to apply a lesser standard than Rehabilitation Act Title V or its implementing federal regulations. These statutory alternatives and qualifications must remain distinct.

## United States v. Balsys — No. 97-873

Source: [official report, 524 U.S. 666](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-524/pdf/USREPORTS-524-666.pdf), factual and procedural account at 669–672; institutional source descriptions at 699 and notes 18–19. Raw PDF and full text are `USREPORTS-524-666.pdf` and `.txt` here; control-only originals.

- Aloyzas Balsys was a resident alien living in Woodhaven, New York. He entered the United States in 1961 on an immigrant visa issued by the American Consulate in Liverpool. His application said he had served in the Lithuanian army from 1934 to 1940 and hidden in Plateliai, Lithuania, from 1940 to 1944. He signed a warning that materially false, misleading, or concealed information could expose him to criminal prosecution and deportation. The account does not establish that those application statements were false.
- The Justice Department's Office of Special Investigations was investigating whether Balsys participated in Nazi persecution and whether his immigration representations were false. Those were investigative subjects, not adjudicated findings of wartime criminal conduct. The identified immigration grounds were 8 U.S.C. §§1182(a)(3)(E), 1251(a)(4)(D), 1182(a)(6)(C)(i), and 1251(a)(1)(A).
- OSI issued an administrative deposition subpoena. Balsys appeared, identified himself and his address, and declined the substantive wartime and immigration questions on self-incrimination grounds. He asserted exposure to prosecution by Lithuania, Israel, and Germany. He asserted no exposure to domestic criminal prosecution. The Government represented that limitations barred prosecution for any immigration misrepresentation; that is a recorded Government representation, not independently reconstructed limitations analysis.
- OSI sought enforcement under §1225(a). The district court found a real and substantial danger of prosecution by Lithuania and Israel, but not Germany, and ordered testimony because it considered the privilege inapplicable to exclusively foreign exposure. 918 F. Supp. 588 (E.D.N.Y. 1996). The Second Circuit vacated that order, holding that real and substantial foreign prosecutorial risk could support the privilege without domestic criminal exposure. 119 F.3d 122 (1997). The Government did not dispute the reasonableness of Balsys's fear concerning Lithuania and Israel. The requested testimony arose in an investigation potentially leading to deportation, not a domestic criminal prosecution.
- Attorney General Order No. 851-79 assigned OSI duties including liaison with foreign prosecution, investigation, and intelligence offices and coordination with domestic agencies. An August 3, 1992 memorandum between DOJ and Lithuania's Procurator General addressed cooperation, mutual legal assistance, and locating or making witnesses available concerning alleged World War II war crimes. The published account identifies its reproduction at Second Circuit appendix 396–397. It does not establish that foreign officials directed this particular subpoena or that a joint prosecution was underway.
- Preserve the Israel source distinction: the district court had not been shown an Israel agreement requiring disclosure of war-crimes evidence. It found that OSI had shared such material with Israel before and that doing so again would accord with OSI's mandate. The existence of an Israel disclosure treaty must not be inferred from the separate, documented Lithuania memorandum.

## Monge v. California — No. 97-6146

Source: [official report, 524 U.S. 721](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-524/pdf/USREPORTS-524-721.pdf), factual and lower-court account at 724–727; statutory sentencing ranges at 729. Raw PDF and full text are `USREPORTS-524-721.pdf` and `.txt` here; control-only originals.

- The jury convicted Monge of using a minor to sell marijuana, selling or transporting marijuana, and possessing marijuana for sale, under California Health and Safety Code §§11361(a), 11360(a), and 11359. The information also gave notice of two sentence-enhancement allegations: a prior assault conviction and a prison term served for it. The challenged prior conviction's serious-felony qualification and the separate prior-prison-term enhancement must not be collapsed.
- California's three-strikes provisions double the term for a current felony when there is one qualifying prior serious felony, and prescribe 25 years to life when there are two. An assault qualifies under the provisions at issue if the defendant personally used a dangerous or deadly weapon or personally inflicted great bodily injury. A generic assault-with-a-deadly-weapon conviction does not alone establish that personal-conduct requirement. California afforded a jury, confrontation, self-incrimination protection, rules of evidence, and proof beyond a reasonable doubt for the allegations.
- Monge waived a jury on the enhancement allegations and obtained bifurcation. At the subsequent bench proceeding, the prosecutor asserted personal use of a stick but introduced only a prison record showing conviction for assault with a deadly weapon and imprisonment. The personal-use assertion was not itself the missing evidentiary proof. The trial judge found both allegations true and imposed 11 years: a five-year sentence on count one doubled to ten, plus one year for the prior prison term. A three-year sentence on count two was stayed; a two-year sentence on count three ran concurrently.
- The Court of Appeal requested supplemental briefing on evidentiary sufficiency. The State conceded that the sentencing record failed to prove beyond a reasonable doubt personal use of a weapon or infliction of great bodily injury, and requested a new opportunity on remand. The appellate court found the evidence insufficient and held retrial barred by double jeopardy. The California Supreme Court reversed the double-jeopardy ruling. 16 Cal.4th 826, 941 P.2d 1121 (1997). Its judgment allowed another attempt to prove the prior-conviction allegation; it did not erase the conceded insufficiency of the first record.
- The published account gives the first-offender maximum for count one as seven years and the one-prior qualifying maximum as fourteen years. The actual sentence selected before doubling was five years. Keep the authorized maximum, selected term, and separate one-year enhancement distinct.
