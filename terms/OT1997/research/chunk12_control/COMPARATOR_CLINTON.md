# Clinton v. City of New York: verified historical comparator

Historical comparator only. Full official [524 U.S. 417-497](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-524/pdf/USREPORTS-524-417.pdf), all four writings and footnotes, read by control. No. 97-1374; argued April 27, 1998; decided June 25, 1998. All nine participated.

Historical judgment: District court affirmed. Stevens wrote the Court opinion, joined by Rehnquist, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg: six votes holding the cancellation procedures invalid under the Presentment Clause. Kennedy separately concurred. Scalia concurred in part/dissented in part, joined throughout by O'Connor and in Part III by Breyer. Breyer dissented, joined in Part III only by O'Connor and Scalia. Do not flatten this structure to a single disagreement: all nine accepted New York standing; seven accepted Snake River standing, Scalia and O'Connor rejecting it.

* Stevens, 420-449: construed expedited-review individuals broadly; found New York's revived contingent liability a present injury and Snake River's lost statutory bargaining advantage sufficiently concrete, traceable and redressable. He treated cancellation after enactment as a partial repeal lacking Article I procedures. Field's contingent, prescribed implementation of prior congressional policy differed from discretionary rejection of an already-enacted policy. Ordinary spending discretion did not authorize unilateral alteration of enacted text. The holding did not reach the district court's alternative separation-of-powers/delegation ground, discretionary-budget severability, or the disputed details of lockbox applicability. No union standing determination was needed.
* Kennedy, 449-453: fully joined. Structural limits protect individual liberty as well as institutional prerogatives; voluntary congressional surrender and a desirable deficit-reduction goal cannot cure departure from constitutional structure. Concentrated power to favor or burden identifiable groups makes the liberty interest concrete.
* Scalia, 453-469: Part I, joined by O'Connor, would give individuals its ordinary natural-person meaning, but reach the New York dispute through certiorari before judgment on the protective circuit appeal. Part II, O'Connor joining, rejected Snake River standing because discussions and potential tax benefits did not establish a sufficiently likely bargain or third-party response. Part III, joined by O'Connor and Breyer, accepted New York standing but upheld direct-spending cancellation: enactment had complied with presentment and the issue was permissible delegation, supported by longstanding discretionary appropriations practice. Part IV declined the tax-benefit merits because no proper plaintiff had standing in his view. Never attribute a tax-benefit merits vote to Scalia or O'Connor through this writing.
* Breyer, 469-497: accepted both sets of plaintiffs' standing; would uphold the Act. Part III, joined by O'Connor and Scalia, argued that exercising a congressionally granted power to prevent legal effect executes the law rather than amends its text, discussing conditional laws and lockbox effects. The rest of his opinion was unjoined: the delegation is executive in operation, Congress retains checks and exemption/disapproval powers, and the limited budgetary authority satisfies intelligible-principle review. He separately recognized the thinner history and increased arbitrariness concern for tax benefits but found those distinctions insufficient. Agency-style judicial review and subsidiary rules are missing, but presidential electoral responsibility and the bounded subject matter mattered to him.

Reconciliation cautions: A 6-3 shorthand for the constitutional judgment does not resolve the separate 7-2 Snake River standing divide or Scalia/O'Connor's refusal to decide the tax-benefit merits. Partial cross-joins are limited to the named Parts. Historical quotations of private declarations are factual evidence only after separate sanitization and neutral validation.
