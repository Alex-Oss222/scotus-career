Source: [Bauchman, 132 F.3d 542](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/132/132.F3d.542.95-4084.96-4101.html). The downloaded CAP copy is available for cross-check; this receipt does not independently certify a full CAP reading.

Murphy **agrees with dismissal of the original complaint**: religious repertoire and religious performance venues standing alone establish neither a per se violation nor, on his reading, the pleaded improper-purpose theory. He dissents from denying amendment. His alternative is not a merits victory, automatic trial, or exclusion of sacred music. He would allow the proposed amended claim and discovery not categorically confined to 1994–95, followed by the next proper merits test, which may be summary judgment (paragraphs 120–145).

His central reasoning distinguishes a teacher's religious identity or belief, which does not prove unconstitutional purpose, from concrete prior acts allegedly showing use of classroom authority to promote religion. Mergens concerns legislative collective purpose and does not, in his analysis, render the individual responsible teacher's intent irrelevant. Wallace, Edwards and O'Connor's Lynch concurrence supply his actual-purpose analysis. Prior conduct can support an inference about the current practice; it is not automatically irrelevant because it affected other students. He invokes Rule 404(b) and analogous discrimination cases for that limited evidentiary proposition, rather than liability for earlier nonparties' injuries.

The proposed amended complaint and supporting affidavits alleged a continuing course of advocacy/proselytizing, not merely the following historical acts in isolation:

- In 1977, compulsory attendance at LDS prayers and sacraments as part of regular graded choir work.
- In 1980, an application inquiry about religion allegedly used to limit choir admission to LDS members because the choir participated in LDS services and not other services.
- During 1992–93, approximately monthly choir performances at LDS worship; during that year and following years, use of devotional lyrics to advocate Torgerson's beliefs.
- In 1993–94, statements allegedly acknowledging and rejecting the legal prohibition on classroom religious advocacy; rehearsal with lights out and a direction to visualize Jesus dying for the students' sins.
- An Oregon fireside at which the choir allegedly was presented as an LDS religious choir, members bore religious testimony and proclaimed the LDS Church uniquely true, and the choir sang exclusively devotional music during worship.

These are alleged acts, not verified findings. Separate note 11 adds an April 1994 official reprimand for a prayer before a choir performance at Mormon worship. Because the same repertoire and religious venues allegedly continued in 1994–95, Murphy identifies the possible inference that the prior expressed purpose continued less openly after reprimand. The reprimand does not prove that inference, and unchanged songs/venues alone are not worship. These details materially strengthen the best amendment argument while preserving the defendants' rebuttal and ordinary proof requirements.

**Critical limits:** Separate note 4 says Bauchman did not argue on appeal that Torgerson's religious beliefs, alone or combined with past acts, sufficed. Note 10 expressly reserves ultimate admissibility: Rule 403 balancing of probative value and unfair prejudice still applies, and the district court had not performed it. Note 1 rejects respondeat superior for the district under Monell. Note 13 does not reach the parties' other alternative dispositive issues. No immunity, policymaker, supervisory-liability or damages conclusion can be inferred from this dissent.

**Amendment and discovery posture:** Murphy regards futility as the sufficiency of the proposed amendments supported by attached affidavits to cure the pleading deficiency, under then-applicable Rule 12(b)(6), Rule 15(a) and Foman. He says the trial court had erroneously treated purpose as exclusively objective; the majority accepted actual purpose in principle but restricted relevant proof. Discovery had been confined to 1994–95 on that theory. The dissent therefore disputes both the pleading futility conclusion and the adequacy of the record for a summary-judgment alternative. It is not a claim to unrestricted discovery.

**Timeliness remains an alternative below, not an appellate holding.** The district court relied on failure to amend before dismissal and failure to add previously unknown essential facts. The majority expressly declined to decide timeliness and questioned that rationale after court-authorized further discovery. Murphy would reject it, stressing the discovery limitation and absence of a prejudice, bad-faith or dilatory-purpose finding. The neutral packet must not transform that absence of findings into a universal rule excusing postdismissal amendment or a new finding of timely filing.

**Candidate wording to tighten:** The candidate states the majority “approved the district court's alternative ground that the amendment would not survive a motion for summary judgment.” Paragraph 70 recognizes summary-judgment futility as a legally permissible alternative and rejects the claim that the district order had converted itself into an actual summary-judgment judgment. It also affirms the insufficiency conclusion after considering the submitted materials. Murphy's note 12 characterizes the majority as not reaching the merits of the district court's alternative forecast. A precise neutral account should preserve both: the majority affirms futility and permits such a forecast as a ground, but no actual summary judgment was entered and no independent trial finding exists. The main validator should quote or tightly link paragraph 70 if the precise reach matters, rather than treating the record as a completed summary-judgment adjudication.

**Other note checks for the main packet:** Majority note 5 excludes the unappealed RFRA dismissal and apparently the high-school defendant dismissal. Note 6 refuses partial appellate vacatur of prospective rulings while leaving appropriate requests to the district court possible; it is not a universal rule that a surviving damages claim eliminates prospective mootness. Note 4 reports the prior injunction/contempt sequence, not a contempt violation found against defendants. Note 8 requires a worshipful context before treating singing as school prayer; the amendment's worship allegations therefore matter. Note 11 identifies Utah's statutory/regulatory waiver protection but does not establish that every practical participation consequence was harmless. Note 13 treats individual roles as unnecessary only because the majority found no sufficiently pleaded underlying establishment claim; it is not a holding that those roles automatically suffice if that conclusion changes.
