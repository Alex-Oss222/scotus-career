## Directly relevant earlier decisions

**Tinker v. Colwell, 193 U.S.473, 480–490 (1904).** Under the former Bankruptcy Act, liability for the then-recognized marital-rights tort was excepted from discharge. The Court treated the intentional invasion of the husband's legally recognized rights as willful and malicious without requiring personal hostility toward that husband. Its language about intentional wrongful acts and implied malice supports a broader construction, but its negligent-driving example expressly distinguishes negligent injury from intentionally driving over the victim. Its claim-specific holding and treatment of malice cannot silently become a rule that every intentional act causing harm is nondischargeable. [Official Reports](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-193/pdf/USREPORTS-193-473.pdf); [Cornell text](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/193/473).

**McIntyre v. Kavanaugh, 242 U.S.138, 139–142 (1916).** Deliberately selling entrusted securities without authority and appropriating their proceeds permanently deprived the owner of property and qualified under the former Act. The Court rejected a categorical exclusion of conversion and repeated Tinker's distinction between malicious wrong and a requirement of personal animus. The case involves deliberate appropriation, not deficient professional treatment; its old-Act partnership-imputation ruling is not assumed to decide the present Code's separate by-the-debtor language. [Official Reports](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-242/pdf/USREPORTS-242-138.pdf); [Cornell text](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/242/138).

**Davis v. Aetna Acceptance Co., 293 U.S.328, 331–335 (1934).** Conversion does not automatically establish willful and malicious injury. An honest but mistaken belief in authority, and an express finding excluding willful or malicious intent, prevented an ordinary conversion judgment from establishing the exception. McIntyre's unexcused appropriation was distinguished. The Court separately rejected the asserted fiduciary theory because the transaction was a secured loan rather than the requisite antecedent trust, and reserved any distinct conversion-of-proceeds theory outside the record. The lesson for this case is to separate tort liability from the additional discharge-exception requirements, not to decide a fiduciary claim not presented here. [Official Reports](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-293/pdf/USREPORTS-293-328.pdf); [Cornell text](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/293/328).

## Fair competing analytical paths

These are analytical arguments supported by the sources; they are not represented as recovered party filings or concessions.

**Broader injury exception:** The statute protects victims of willful and malicious injury, and historical cases did not demand an express wish to harm a named creditor or proof of personal animosity. Deliberate wrongful invasions can carry their injurious character with them. The creditors can argue that knowingly choosing inferior treatment, canceling transfer and stopping antibiotics should be assessed cumulatively as an intentional disregard of the patient's rights rather than insulated by the malpractice label. Tinker's broad language and McIntyre's treatment of intentional appropriation provide the strongest prior support. This path must explain why the conduct meets willfulness concerning injury rather than merely showing a consciously chosen treatment decision, and why Davis's distinction between tort and qualifying injury does not govern. It cannot convert the existing negligence/recklessness finding into an unsupplied desire or knowledge finding.

**Injury-intent requirement:** Grammatically the statute modifies injury, not merely the action that happens to cause it. Treating all knowingly undertaken wrongful acts as sufficient would encompass many negligent torts and ordinary intentional contractual breaches. Davis requires a separate assessment; Tinker's own negligent-driving example is incompatible with treating every voluntary act causing foreseeable harm as willful injury. McIntyre's intentional deprivation differs from an unintended medical injury. Field supplies the general method of reading established legal terminology in context while preserving federal statutory predicates separately from state tort liability. The criminal-scienter decisions in `01_geiger.md` illustrate context-sensitive interpretation only; their criminal burdens and lenity holdings do not automatically apply to civil discharge.

**Text/history fork:** The official Code's historical notes reproduce House Report 95-595 and Senate Report 95-989 discussions describing willful as deliberate or intentional and rejecting Tinker-derived reckless-disregard interpretations. Those reports reinforce a distinction from recklessness but do not replace the enacted words or themselves resolve every injury-versus-act question. The same notes contain a separate House discussion of temporary conversion and a legislative statement covering willful and malicious conversion; those passages should be reconciled in context, not suppressed or turned into a universal conversion rule. The reports are legislative materials, not holdings or enacted text; their original proposed paragraph numbering differs. The Court may resolve the presented negligence/recklessness question without deciding a complete malice definition, every intentional tort, subjective substantial certainty, or the current scope of old-Act partnership imputation.

**Remedy discipline:** The en banc judgment's statutory rule is under review. A broader construction does not automatically authorize a new ultimate intent finding or nondischargeability judgment on every item of damages; any necessary factual application requires its own supported posture. A narrower construction may resolve the liability described without announcing a universal medical-debt exemption. No policy objection to uninsured practice creates an extra statutory discharge exception.
