## Appeals, questions and exact dispositions

The litigation concerns Tier 2 and Tier 3 community notification for people whose offenses preceded the October 31, 1994 enactment. Registration and Tier 1 police notification had been sustained in Artway; that earlier community-notification challenge was unripe. The W.P. class includes people required to register whose offenses preceded enactment and who have been or will be placed in Tier 2 or 3. The question of applying the law to post-enactment offenses was outside the appeal. [¶¶3–4, 51–54.]

**E.B., No. 96-5132:** State officials appealed a federal preliminary injunction against notification and the refusal to stay that injunction. E.B. had already contested his Tier 3 classification and plan in state court, lost in the Appellate Division, and unsuccessfully sought New Jersey Supreme Court review. The Third Circuit reversed and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction under Rooker-Feldman: the requested federal relief would undo a completed state judgment. Existing adverse state precedent did not eliminate the opportunity to raise constitutional objections. This is a jurisdictional disposition, not an individual merits approval of E.B.'s classification or notification geography. [¶¶55–57, 78–86; majority nn.9–11.]

**W.P., No. 96-5416:** The class plaintiffs appealed summary judgment for defendants. Some representative plaintiffs had initiated no state judicial proceedings and faced no judicial order when they sued; their claims supplied class jurisdiction. The State sought federal merits affirmance, so the Third Circuit did not review the district court's rejection of Younger abstention. The Third Circuit rejected the Ex Post Facto and Double Jeopardy challenges but reversed the judgment and remanded with instructions to enjoin Tier 2/3 notification without a prior opportunity to challenge classification **and the notification plan** at a hearing where the prosecutor bears persuasion by clear and convincing evidence, and to deny further relief. This is not a blanket invalidation of the statute, a bare affirmance, or a general reopening/remand of every procedural question. [¶¶58–59, 87, 152, 181–192; majority nn.12–13.]

Becker joined the jurisdictional disposition and procedural-due-process ruling but dissented from the punishment ruling; he would treat retrospective notification as punishment. No additional separate opinion appears. [¶¶193, 197–200, 251, 270–282.]

## Operative scheme and individualization

The published account describes registration for persons completing relevant sentences after enactment; people who completed incarceration, probation and parole earlier register only if sentencing found repetitive and compulsive behavior. Address confirmation, moves and new-municipality registration have separate requirements. The obligation does not automatically expire after fifteen years: only after the statutory period may a registrant apply for termination, upon the specified non-danger showing. [¶¶7–11.]

Tier 1 reaches likely-encounter law enforcement; Tier 2 reaches qualifying schools, child-care facilities and organizations serving children or supporting women; Tier 3 reaches members of the public likely to encounter the person. The New Jersey Supreme Court added the likely-encounter restriction to Tier 2; the statute already supplied it for Tiers 1 and 3. Institutions are not included automatically because they registered for information. Geography, actual encounter opportunities, the offender's characteristics and relationship to prior victims matter. The Guidelines' applications include different risks to household members, adult women and children. [¶¶12–13, 35–40; majority n.4.]

The Scale scores thirteen factors in four categories, using different category weights and totals of 0–36 / 37–73 / 74–111 for low / moderate / high risk. Two overrides matter: a credible supported indication the offender will reoffend requires high-risk treatment irrespective of scoring; a physical condition minimizing risk, including advanced age or debilitating illness, requires low-risk treatment irrespective of scoring. The Scale is a tool rather than an automatic adjudication. The court permits individualized challenges to calculation/facts, cases outside its usual range, and excessive notification in the particular case, but not attacks on the Scale itself or its factor weights in that review. [¶¶29–34, 45–50; majority n.2.]

The disseminated package includes name, photograph, physical description, conviction, residence, employment or school and vehicle details. Tier 2 recipients receive a confidentiality instruction; all notifications warn against threats, vandalism and assault. Becker questions effective containment after disclosure; that is disagreement about operation, not a finding that statutory restrictions do not exist. [¶35; majority n.3; Becker ¶230 and n.10.]

## Punishment: strongest supported opposing paths

**Nonpunitive path:** Public safety and prevention explain registration plus tailored warnings. A reasonable connection to that end need not be a perfect prediction tool or individually edited message. The scheme grades risk and limits the audience; it directly imposes no residence, movement, employment, licensure or benefits prohibition. The majority analogizes the dissemination to public criminal records and hazard warnings. It acknowledges severe reputational, employment, housing and relationship consequences and some private violence, while emphasizing their indirect character, limited documented frequency, anti-vigilante warnings and ordinary police protection. [¶¶109–152.]

**Punitive path:** The State affirmatively broadcasts both past wrongdoing and an official assessment of future dangerousness, so ordinary passive public-record access is an incomplete analogy. Becker identifies historical shaming as a closer analogue, including punishments whose sting was publicity rather than bodily pain. Criminal-code placement, implementation by prosecutors and police, burdens on people outside vulnerable groups, disclosure of remote workplaces, foreseeable redistribution and the combined lasting effects support his argument. He disputes a rule under which effects matter only if a fundamental constitutional interest is deprived and urges cumulative examination. He ultimately rests his dissent on objective-purpose/history, not an independent finding that effects alone decide the case; alternatively he concludes the cumulative evidence can satisfy clearest proof. [¶¶212–251, 252–278; Becker nn.10–15.]

**Record limits:** The source reports evidence involving at least nineteen offenders, but only six had actually received state-compelled Megan's Law notification; other publicity had other sources. It separately reports 135 completed New Jersey notifications, police reports and out-of-state studies. These are different denominators and cannot be merged into one incidence calculation. They do not establish that all harms occurred under the challenged notification process. Becker expressly recognizes causation difficulty. [¶¶60–77, 138–151; majority nn.6–8; Becker n.13.]

## Due process is a separate question

The majority finds a protected state-created liberty interest in the New Jersey Constitution's substantive privacy restriction, as construed in Doe: disclosure requires a legitimate and substantial state interest and minimized invasion. It does not decide whether the Federal Constitution itself supplies the liberty interest. This is a substantive constraint, not a rule that state-prescribed procedures alone create protected liberty. [¶¶153–157; majority nn.26–27.]

The former state process required a prosecutorial prima facie presentation but put the ultimate burden on the registrant to disprove the determination by a preponderance. The Third Circuit separately changes **who bears persuasion** and **how convincing the showing must be**. Both classification and the scope of the plan require justification. [¶¶45–50, 159–192.]

The factual questions include accurate scoring and unadjudicated circumstances of offenses; past conviction need not have established all relevant facts. The individualized exceptions and notification scope require assessment of future risk. Reliance on reliable hearsay, restricted victim testimony, credibility disputes, predictive uncertainty and the 40–45-day schedule increase error risk. The State already must marshal a prima facie case, so shifting persuasion need not substantially increase administrative cost or defeat prompt action. On proof strength, the majority emphasizes immediate and irreversible disclosure from overclassification, while underclassification does not necessarily cause harm because registration and law-enforcement monitoring continue. Addington and Santosky support its greater-certainty requirement without demanding physical confinement; McMillan's criminal-sentencing setting differs. [¶¶160–191.]

The State's contrary arguments include public protection and prompt warning, the absence of physical confinement, the existing hearing and prima facie safeguards, the danger of underclassification, and the administrative effect of demanding more proof. These considerations require an actual Mathews comparison; nonpunishment does not dispose of the process question. Conversely, procedural defects do not automatically establish punishment. [¶¶168–191.]

**Open issue precisely identified:** The attack on dispensing with advance notice in exceptional circumstances was unripe. No representative alleged notification without notice, and the record showed no prosecutor had done or planned it. The opinion recounts a judicial-order mechanism where release information arrives only after a Tier 3 offender's release, or where a registrant evades service despite every good-faith effort; after three days without completed service the prosecutor may apply for an order. These are permissions to apply, not mandatory bypasses. Whether the practical-impossibility standard is constitutional in a concrete application remains unresolved; possible narrowing state construction also counseled Pullman abstention. The remedial paragraph must be read with this explicit reservation, not as an adjudication of the emergency issue. [¶¶41–44, 192; majority n.28; Becker ¶199.]

## Effective-law correction required in neutral modeling

The historical opinion's reasoning repeatedly depends on historical Hendricks as a decision sustaining the Kansas commitment and treating it as a nonpunitive benchmark. That premise is unavailable under the authorized entering-law selection: the existing Supreme Court decision invalidates Hendricks's original commitment as cumulatively punitive while preserving civil intent and clearest proof. Hudson likewise requires examination of supported actual combined operation and preserves Halper in its distinct personal-penalty field. The lower judgment and arguments remain source facts; neither its historical Hendricks outcome nor its characterization of confinement as a controlling nonpunitive fixed point may be imported as current law. Equally, the actual confinement outcome does not mechanically classify notification. [09_wp.md, Hendricks and Ursery entries; CURRENT_PUBLIC_LAW.md, Hudson Holdings 1–3 and Precedent Treatment.]
