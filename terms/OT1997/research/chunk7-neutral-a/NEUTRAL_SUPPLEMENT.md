## Women's Medical Professional Corp. v. Voinovich — April 20

### Record and scope validation

The complete Sixth Circuit opinion, 130 F.3d 187 (Nov. 18, 1997), was retrieved from the supplied Public.Resource.Org URL and read, including the dissent and footnotes. It describes H.B.135, enacted August 16, 1995 and intended to take effect November 14, 1995. The district court's preliminary injunction became permanent January 12, 1996 by the parties' stipulation consolidating the hearings with trial. The Sixth Circuit affirmed an injunction against the whole Act. Its principal reasons were the D&X definition's reach into common D&E practice; lack of culpability directed to emergency/necessity assessments; and exclusion of serious nontemporary mental-health needs. It treated postviability application of the method prohibition as inseverable under Ohio law. The lower court's legal conclusions remain reviewable.

The lower opinion supplies an actual distinction between purposeful insertion during D&E and incidental contact: physicians testified that their goal could be suctioning the skull contents, even if successful insertion was uncertain. The majority relied on that evidence; the dissent read the language as identifying a distinct method and emphasized the availability of other safe procedures and the absence of comparative peer-reviewed proof establishing D&X's superiority. Neither account establishes a Supreme Court finding. The lower opinion's record citation is adequate support for that dispute without pretending to have independently read the underlying transcript.

Keep the commands separate. Section 2919.15 prohibits knowingly performing or attempting the defined D&X procedure at any gestational stage, with explicit exclusions for suction curettage and suction aspiration. Its criminal health defense requires the physician first to establish a prima facie case that every available alternative is riskier; the prosecutor then must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that at least one available alternative is not riskier. The civil provision has its own affirmative defense and no copied criminal burden shift. Sections 2919.17(A) and 2919.16(F)/(J) separately govern postviability necessity and emergency delay. Section 2919.18 begins testing at the twenty-second week and contains an emergency exception; the postviability ban uses a rebuttable twenty-four-week presumption. The five postviability procedural duties and their emergency exemption remain distinct. Exact published statutory passages are appended mechanically.

The provider's postviability standing was upheld below because the twenty-four-week presumption and testing requirements could apply to his practice; the State did not appeal that ruling in the Sixth Circuit. ## Campbell v. Louisiana — April 21

### Record, law, and lawful alternatives

Campbell's timely pretrial motion alleged no Black grand-jury foreperson between January 1976 and August 1993 despite a registered-voter population more than twenty percent Black; the State did not contest that stated evidence. The Court of Appeal nevertheless regarded proof as inadequate and sought an evidentiary hearing. Standing does not establish purposeful discrimination. Louisiana selects the foreperson from the venire before choosing the other members by lot, and the foreperson votes as a full member. The lower courts' comparison with a ministerial federal foreperson must therefore be tested against the method of selecting a member, not just later clerical duties.

Equal-protection third-party standing and the defendant's own due-process injury are distinct. Powers, as retained and applied by McCollum and J.E.B., supplies concrete injury, relationship, and impediment principles for challenges to excluded jurors' rights; Hobby concerns selection of a federal foreperson from an already constituted grand jury and must be assessed within that posture. A path recognizing standing may permit adjudication of discrimination without deciding it or ordering an unconditional new trial. A contrary path must identify a legally material grand-jury distinction or a failed standing predicate, rather than infer absence of injury solely from the defendant's race. The due-process route must confront structural composition and cannot depend simply on disagreement with third-party standing. A narrow remand can preserve these separate routes and leave the evidentiary merits below.

The fair-cross-section claim was raised initially, but the supplied published recitation says neither Louisiana appellate court discussed it and describes an unestablished appellate presentation. This is a specific preservation limitation, not an inference from an unavailable file; no new Sixth Amendment merits holding should be silently attached to resolution of the preserved challenges.
