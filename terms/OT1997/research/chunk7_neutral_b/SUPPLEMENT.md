The spoken-prayer and silence components require separate treatment. Tanford's December 22 holding applies the updated Lemon test to an official university ceremony: actual worship organized as the government's program is forbidden despite maturity, brevity, nonsectarian wording and voluntary attendance. Its precise university-prayer holding and retained limits are copied into the entering-law slice. Agostini makes purpose, effect and institutional connection evidence within one inquiry, not three freestanding prongs. Tanford leaves moments of silence distinct and does not establish their categorical validity.

Lawful alternatives for the former prayers include application of Tanford to the established official selection and program incorporation, with relief confined to legally live claims; or antecedent resolution/remand of a genuinely unresolved jurisdictional or remedial predicate before constitutional merits. The counterargument to reaching damages merits is that the present neutral record does not identify a legally available damages defendant or cause of action. The counterargument to treating the entire dispute as moot is that voluntary cessation does not automatically eliminate recurrence or a lawful damages residue. Neither the lower majority's apparent prospective mootness nor Jones's view of possible recurrence supplies a new Supreme Court finding. A new prospective injunction would require an actual live controversy and appropriate relief, while immunity would turn on the governing law at the conduct date rather than retroactive use of Tanford.

For silence, one path treats the actual program as nondevotional civic reflection leaving thoughts to each participant; Sherman distinguishes civic function from worship, and the O'Connor Wallace concurrence quoted in lower paragraph 37 distinguishes silence from inherently religious conduct. The strongest contrary path examines the 1988 counsel letter, replacement of prayers and official presentation to determine whether the University actually organized a devotional exercise under the updated Lemon inquiry. The letter is relevant evidence but is not automatically President Hefner's operational purpose; the president stated dignity, formality and solemnity. The stipulated lack of evidence of University complicity in the audience recitations prevents treating those recitations as proof of official direction. Neither anticipated private prayer nor failure to suppress private expression alone establishes government worship. Practical employment pressure remains its own issue: the record contrasts Chaudhuri's university-service assertion with uncontradicted nonmonitoring/no-penalty representations and the dean's affidavit. A bare claim of pressure must not become a finding of a penalty.

## Perry v. Los Angeles Police Department — May 4, 1998

Review concerns Ninth Circuit No. 96-55545, decided August 25, 1997, after an appeal from judgment on partial findings under Rule 52(c) at the close of plaintiffs' evidence in a bench trial. The supplied May 4 review date is a scheduling premise; no separate Supreme Court docket or argument date is supplied. Ordinary appellate review under §1254(1) is available. The City seeks restoration of the district judgment. The full lower opinion, including its footnote, is copied in research/chunk7_neutral_b/perry_lower_opinion.md; no later-citation metadata is included.

The lower opinion supports a more precise record than the brief's general summary. Perry performed music with Sikh religious content and sold his recordings; Newman solicited and sold message-bearing materials for an unincorporated animal-rights group. Both were cited in 1995 for failing to document nonprofit status. The district court found their goods inextricably intertwined with protected messages and recognized the City's congestion and local-merchant interests. Those findings must not become findings that nonprofit participants cause less congestion or competition. The lower appellate court assumed content neutrality and held the nonprofit distinction insufficiently tailored; it did not need to decide the full content-classification question. The opinion's account of §42.15's prohibition and both exceptions is copied below mechanically.

The decisive comparison concerns the same expressive sales or donation activity performed by an individual and a qualifying nonprofit. One lawful path holds that the exemption's speaker classification fails ordinary content-neutral time, place and manner tailoring even assuming neutrality, because nonprofit membership is not shown to track either physical congestion or commercial character. Discovery Network supplies the need for a relation between the distinction and the asserted harm; it does not require all harms to be addressed at once. The City's strongest response is that the status requirement is an administrable way of identifying expressive nonprofit activity and reducing boardwalk commerce. The record's finding that these individuals' activity is fully expressive and the City's argument that Newman could obtain nonprofit status test that asserted proxy directly.

A second rationale would assess the message relevance condition and speaker classification as a content-based limitation under the traditional public-forum rule. Its counterargument is the lower court's assumed neutrality and the availability of a narrower tailoring resolution; a benign purpose alone does not establish neutrality under Discovery Network. A path sustaining neutral boardwalk restrictions remains legally available for a materially supported classification and adequate alternatives, but the City must defend the actual nonprofit line and may not substitute an unadopted blanket vending law. Heffron, Ward, Clark and Perry Education Association are received pre-divergence sources; ordinary regulation does not require the least restrictive means. The injunction-specific heightened fit in Madsen is not the test for this legislative ordinance. No universal right to sell ordinary goods, compelled nonprofit affiliation, new licensing program or suppression of nonprofit expression follows automatically from finding the present classification invalid.

The supplied second question mentions alternatives. Failure of tailoring can resolve the dispute without deciding every alternative channel. If a Justice relies independently on lack of alternatives, the actual record must support that conclusion; imagined free distribution, other sites or compelled nonprofit registration are not established adequate substitutes. Remedy must address the invalid restriction and leave further lawful municipal choices to ordinary proceedings; severability and an exact new decree are not established merely by the lower court's facial-invalidity description.

Justice-specific prior authorities are copied with their actual joins: Stevens authored Discovery Network, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter among the present Associates; Thomas did not join that ruling. Kennedy's Rosenberger opinion, joined by O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas, supplies a bounded prohibition on religious viewpoint exclusion within an opened private-expression program; its funding and establishment limits remain separate. Stevens's Ladue opinion was joined by every present Associate except Breyer, who was not yet on the Court, and addresses meaningful alternatives at its actual residential scope. Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer joined McIntyre's protection of individual issue advocacy; Thomas used his distinct historical anonymous-pamphlet ground and Scalia dissented. Madsen supplies O'Connor, Souter and Ginsburg's tighter injunction standard and Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas's different objections, but does not govern this ordinance as an injunction. ## Perry ordinance text - exact lower-opinion reproduction

Copied mechanically from the lower opinion, paragraphs 11-12. This is an authenticated judicial quotation of the operative text, not an assertion that a separately archived municipal code was retrieved.

¶11Section 42.15 provides, “No person shall hawk, peddle or vend any goods, wares or merchandise, or beg or solicit alms or donations upon” any sidewalk, boardwalk, or public way adjoining a specified length of the Pacific Ocean, including the area known as the Venice Beach Boardwalk (“Boardwalk”).
¶12There are two exceptions to this prohibition.
One is for the sale of newspapers, magazines, periodicals “or other printed matter commonly sold or disposed of by newsboys or news vendors.” The second is for “the solicitation of donations by a non-profit organization [and] the sale of merchandise by a non-profit organization constituting, carrying or making a religious, philosophical or ideological message or statement relevant to the purpose of said non-profit organization.” The exception for non-profit organizations was added in 1991 in response to this circuit’s decision in Gaudiya Vaishnava Soc.
v. City of San Francisco, 952 F.2d 1059 (9th Cir.1990).
