## Regions Hospital v. Shalala

**Record and regulatory detail.** The intermediary issued the Hospital's original 1984 notice on February 28, 1986. The original aggregate GME figure was $9,892,644; the later reaudit figure was $5,916,868. The reported average per-resident figures were $70,662 and $49,805 respectively; those figures should be carried as reported rather than recomputed by assuming an unchanged resident denominator. The reaudit began in late 1990. The Secretary sought changes only to still-open and future reimbursement years, not repayment of the closed 1984 reimbursement. The initial determination came from an intermediary's notice; no PRRB hearing had adjudicated the reasonableness of those base-year costs. Sources: 522 U.S. 448, 454-455, 463.

The final September 1989 regulation instructed intermediaries to verify base-year GME costs and full-time-equivalent residents, exclude nonallowable or misclassified costs including costs previously allowed, and, at a hospital's request, include GME costs previously misclassified as operating costs. The rule also permitted an upward adjustment of the hospital-specific prospective-payment rate for costs the reaudit showed belonged to operating costs instead of GME. For missing physician-time records, the Secretary allowed documentation from later reporting periods to support the allocation of physician compensation in the base period. Regions actually used 1989 and 1990 time studies because it had not retained base-year records of time spent teaching medical students. These are features of the administrative process and this record, not findings that every disputed expense was correctly disallowed. Sources: id. at 454, 463 & n.7; 42 CFR sections 413.86(e)(1)(ii)(A)-(C), (e)(1)(v), (j)(1)(i) (1996); 55 Fed. Reg. 36063 (1990).

**Actual arguments and lower proceedings.** The Hospital argued that the reaudit was impermissibly retroactive; that the base amount already recognized under the existing reimbursement process could not be replaced outside its reopening period; that Congress had specifically delegated rulemaking for resident counts but not a new composite-cost determination; that indefinite reaudits threatened finality; that lost records, faded memories, and changed personnel made renewed proof unfair; and that the final-and-binding regulations supplied issue preclusion. Sources: id. at 456, 458-460 & nn.2,4, 462-463. The Secretary's published explanation was that the change from annual determinations to an enduring base required accurate figures and should not permanently carry forward nonallowable or misclassified costs; the agency acknowledged competing Medicare changes had delayed scrutiny. Id. at 454, 458-459, 461.

The Hospital obtained expedited judicial review after the PRRB said it lacked authority to invalidate the regulation. On cross-motions, the District of Minnesota granted the Secretary summary judgment, following Administrators of the Tulane Educational Fund v. Shalala, 987 F.2d 790 (D.C. Cir. 1993), on ambiguity, reasonableness, and retroactivity. The Eighth Circuit followed Tulane in 91 F.3d 57 (1996). The Sixth Circuit's Toledo Hospital v. Shalala, 104 F.3d 791, 795, 797-801 (1997), rejected the Secretary's statutory interpretation and alternatively its reasonableness, but did not accept the hospital's retroactivity objection. Source: id. at 455-456. These are lower-court positions, subject to entering simulated law.

**Dates.** Argument December 1, 1997; supplied event anchor February 24, 1998. The Hospital changed its name from St. Paul-Ramsey Medical Center to Regions Hospital on September 15, 1997. Source: id. at 448, 454 n.1.

## Rivet v. Regions Bank of Louisiana

**Correction to posture.** The Fifth Circuit treated prior federal **claim preclusion**, through Carpenter v. Wichita Falls Independent School District, 44 F.3d 362, 370 (5th Cir. 1995), as a basis for removal. The packet's phrase that it treated the judgment as "completely preempting" the state action should be replaced by "completely precluding." The asserted prior-judgment bar and complete statutory preemption are distinct litigated theories. Sources: 522 U.S. 470, 473-474, 476.

**Additional record and grounds below.** The partnership granted the Bank's mortgage in 1983 and petitioners' second mortgage in 1984. Bankruptcy orders in June and August 1986 authorized sale free and clear of claims, approved the Bank's purchase as the sole auction bidder, and directed cancellation of the liens and mortgages. Petitioners' mortgage nevertheless remained inscribed on Orleans Parish's rolls. The Bank acquired the underlying land from the Browns and sold the property to Fountainbleau Storage Associates in 1993. Petitioners sued on December 29, 1994, requesting recognition and enforcement of their mortgage or, alternatively, damages. Sources: id. at 472-473.

All parties to the dispute were Louisiana parties; petitioners relied exclusively on Louisiana law. Respondents invoked federal-question removal on the ground that the 1986 bankruptcy orders had extinguished the mortgage rights. The district court denied remand and entered summary judgment for the Bank and FSA on preclusion grounds. It separately entered summary judgment for the Browns for failure to state a claim against them. The Fifth Circuit affirmed, reading Moitie's second footnote through Carpenter. Judge Jones's lower-court dissent understood Moitie to concern relabeled essentially federal claims after a federal loss, and considered petitioners' mortgage claim nonfederal. Sources: id. at 473-474, 476. These are distinct reasons below; the Browns' judgment should not be recast as another merits determination of federal claim preclusion.

**Dates.** Argument January 21, 1998; supplied event anchor February 24, 1998. Id. at 470.

## National Credit Union Administration v. First National Bank & Trust Co.

**Administrative and litigation facts.** Before 1982 the NCUA and predecessor administrators required the same occupational common bond across the credit union's membership. The 1982 policy instead allowed multiple unrelated employer groups, requiring a common bond within each group. The original additional geographic condition placed all groups within one defined area; the 1989 revision permitted each group to lie within an area around the credit union's home or a branch office that the NCUA determined could reasonably be served. Sources: 522 U.S. 479, 483-484; IRPS 82-1, 47 Fed. Reg. 16775; IRPS 82-3, 47 Fed. Reg. 26808; IRPS 89-1, 54 Fed. Reg. 31170.

The challenged charter amendments were approved in 1990. ATTF and the Credit Union National Association intervened as defendants. According to the NCUA's brief, ATTF had approximately 110,000 members nationwide and about 35% were employees of AT&T or affiliates; other groups included employees of Lee Apparel, Coca-Cola Bottling, Ciba-Geigy, Duke Power, and American Tobacco. The report expressly states that injury in fact was undisputed: the interpretation enabled persons who otherwise might be respondents' customers to become ATTF members and customers. Sources: id. at 484-485, 488 n.4. This is the supported status of injury in this action, not a legal prediction about every competitor suit.

**Additional lower-court grounds and relief.** The first district decision, 772 F.Supp. 609 (D.D.C. 1991), considered competitive injury insufficient under its zone-of-interests analysis and rejected the banks as suitable challengers. The first appellate decision, 988 F.2d 1272 (D.C. Cir. 1993), agreed that Congress had not intended to shield commercial banks but found the challengers' interests sufficiently congruent with statutory beneficiaries' interests. On remand, 863 F.Supp. 9, 12-14 (D.D.C. 1994), the district court found ambiguity at Chevron's first step; it said a second-step reasonableness decision was unnecessary because respondents had not seriously argued unreasonableness. The later appellate decision, 90 F.3d 525, 527-529 (D.C. Cir. 1996), found an unambiguous common-bond requirement, relying on avoiding surplusage and treating the parallel occupational and geographic clauses consistently. Following that decision, the district court issued a nationwide injunction against approval of additions of unrelated employer groups. The injunction fact is attributed in the report to ATTF's brief at 14 n.5; the injunction's full text has not been independently read. Sources: 522 U.S. at 485-487 & n.3.

**Actual competing arguments and concession.** Petitioners argued that Congress sought credit access for people of modest means rather than protection of commercial banks' competitive position. They relied on Air Courier and contended the banks fell outside the zone of interests. The banks' brief asserted that Congress did have concerns about competitive injury to banks. Sources: id. at 495-498, 508-509. On the merits, the banks relied on the singular article accompanying common bond; petitioners relied on the plural groups. The NCUA conceded that under the geographic clause all constituent groups must come from the same neighborhood, community, or rural district. It sought to distinguish the occupational clause's participial wording from the geographic clause's prepositional wording. Sources: id. at 500-502, with brief pages identified there. Those are attributed submissions and a specific recorded concession, not adopted constructions.

**Dockets and dates.** Nos. 96-843 and 96-847 are the paired NCUA and ATTF matters within the indexed event. Argument October 6, 1997; supplied event anchor February 25, 1998. Id. at 479.

## Alaska v. Native Village of Venetie Tribal Government

**Additional record and lower-court test.** The school construction was state financed. Under the joint-venture agreement, Alaska was responsible for the approximately $161,000 tax demanded in December 1986. The tribal collection proceeding named the State, school district, and contractor. Alaska then sought a federal injunction, and the district court denied the Tribe's motion to dismiss on August 2, 1995, reasoning that these lands were not Indian country and the Tribe could not tax the nonmembers. Sources: 522 U.S. 520, 525.

The Ninth Circuit, 101 F.3d 1286, 1294, 1300-1302 (1996), treated federal set-aside and superintendence as requirements to be construed broadly through six factors: the area's nature; inhabitants' relationship to tribes and the Federal Government; government agencies' established practices toward the area; the degree of federal ownership and control; inhabitants' cohesiveness; and the extent the area had been set aside for dependent Indian peoples' use, occupancy, and protection. It found its requirements met. A separate lower-court concurrence considered itself bound by Ninth Circuit precedent despite concern that ANCSA preserved some tribal sovereignty while severing territorial control. Sources: 522 U.S. at 525-526. These are the grounds below, not conclusions this Court has reached.

**Actual tribal arguments and statutory limits.** The Tribe invoked political dependence and federal recognition, citing Cherokee Nation and Worcester; argued that its election under section 1618(b) to retain former reservation land in exchange for other settlement benefits preserved a set-aside; relied on continuing federal health, social, welfare, and economic programs; and described ANCSA as advancing self-determination while renouncing agency paternalism. Sources: id. at 530-531 n.5, 532, 534, identifying respondent brief pages 23-24, 28, 40-41, 44. No assertion that Alaska conceded these arguments is supported.

The source identifies statutory protections under section 1636(d) against adverse possession, real-property taxes, and certain judgments while the land remains unsold, unleased, and undeveloped. These qualified protections must be distinguished from restrictions on conveyance or use. No Indian allotment is at issue here. Sources: id. at 527, 533. This extraction does not establish the status of every Alaska Native land category or a different parcel.

**Dates.** Argument December 10, 1997; supplied event anchor February 25, 1998. Id. at 520.

## Spencer v. Kemna

**Correction to posture.** The Eighth Circuit **affirmed** the district court's dismissal, concluding under Lane v. Williams that there were no collateral consequences; it did not itself dismiss the habeas appeal in lieu of affirmance. The district court had combined its mootness rationale with a statement that expiration meant Spencer was no longer in custody under section 2254(a). Source: 523 U.S. 1, 6. Those are the lower courts' reasons, not an adopted account of the jurisdictional rule.

**Underlying record with allegations distinguished.** The September 24, 1992 order sustained violations of parole conditions concerning obedience to law, controlled substances, and weapons, identifying the July 27 violation report as evidence without separately describing the conduct. That report summarized a woman's allegation that Spencer smoked crack cocaine with her and raped her while pressing a screwdriver to her side. According to the report, Spencer admitted cocaine use but asserted consensual intercourse and denied the screwdriver allegation. The first officer tentatively recommended continuing parole with drug treatment pending prosecutorial disposition; no criminal charge was filed. A September 14 follow-up report recommended revocation; the revocation order did not cite that follow-up. Sources: id. at 3-5. The respondents' brief characterized the condition-one violation as commission of rape, as reported at id. at 23 n.1. That attributed characterization should remain separate from what the written revocation order expressly said and from proof of criminal guilt.

**Habeas procedure and asserted injuries.** Spencer filed federal habeas on April 1, 1993 while imprisoned under the challenged revocation, after unsuccessful state applications. He alleged absence of a preliminary hearing on armed criminal action, absence of a hearing on cancellation of conditional release, inability to confront witnesses, lack of notice that the case rested entirely on out-of-court report statements, denial of his chosen representative, and a four-month delay in notice of the revocation and evidence despite a ten-working-day regulation. These are claims described from his brief, not findings that the procedures were unlawful. Sources: id. at 5-6 n.1.

The district court granted two extensions, moving the State's response deadline from June 2 to July 7, 1993. On July 14 Spencer sought expedited decision to prevent mootness. He was released on parole again on August 7, and the challenged sentence expired October 16, 1993. The court acknowledged the request February 3, 1994 and dismissed August 23, 1995. By the appeal he was again imprisoned on a separate seven-year attempted-felony-stealing sentence; the State's brief reported a scheduled January 24, 1999 parole release. Sources: id. at 6 & n.2. The later imprisonment was not imprisonment under the challenged 1992 revocation.

Spencer conceded the validity of the original burglary and stealing convictions and challenged the revocation alone. He asserted possible use of the revocation in future parole, sentencing, impeachment, and criminal-evidence decisions; argued that Heck could bar a damages action unless he first invalidated the revocation; invoked capable-of-repetition-yet-evading-review in reply; and complained of State and district-court delay. Sources: id. at 8, 14-18. The report does not identify a damages claim pleaded in this habeas action. The report states that Spencer himself did not assert a reputation-only injury in the Supreme Court submissions; this is a source limit on attribution, not a prohibition on independently evaluating the supported record under the Engine. Id. at 16-17 n.8.

**Dates.** Argument November 12, 1997; supplied event anchor March 3, 1998. Id. at 1.

## Lexecon Inc. v. Milberg Weiss Bershad Hynes & Lerach

**Additional preservation and judgment detail.** Lexecon's Illinois diversity suit asserted malicious prosecution, abuse of process, tortious interference, commercial disparagement, and defamation. Judge Bilby recused after issuing his own disputed characterization of the earlier settlement; the Panel assigned the June 1993 transfer to Judge Roll. Before disposition of the present claims, the related Ninth Circuit appeal was dismissed and the Arizona document depository closed. The remaining Lincoln Savings parties reached final settlement with judgment in March 1994. Sources: 523 U.S. 26, 29-30.

Lexecon requested a remand suggestion in August and November 1994. The firms opposed because discovery remained incomplete and requested self-transfer under section 1404(a). In April 1995 the district court resolved all remaining claims except defamation against Milberg by summary judgment and dismissed Milberg's counterclaims. Cotchett obtained Rule 54(b) judgment June 7, 1995. Lexecon opposed the exercise of Rule 54(b) discretion but did not deny the Arizona court's authority to enter that pretrial judgment. The court simultaneously denied remand-suggestion motions and granted self-transfer for trial. Lexecon pursued mandamus, then direct review after the defense judgment at trial. Sources: id. at 30-32.

On direct appeal Lexecon challenged denial of remand, dismissal of malicious prosecution and abuse of process, and the final judgment for Cotchett. It did not contest subject-matter jurisdiction, as distinct from venue, or claim error in how the trial itself was conducted. The Ninth Circuit relied on Panel Rule 14 and prior decisions, statutory compatibility, and efficiency; its lower-court dissent relied on the statutes and the plaintiff's forum choice. Id. at 32. These procedural distinctions matter to the reach of any remedy and should not be collapsed into one undifferentiated trial judgment.

**Actual respondent arguments.** Milberg relied on Panel Rule 14(b) and longstanding self-assignment practice; argued that section 1407(a) regulated the Panel's duties while section 1407(b) addressed the transferee court; characterized self-transfer as ending the case's section 1407 venue status under the previously-terminated exception; relied on the wording of section 1407(h)'s separate Clayton Act trial-transfer authority; and invoked legislative history recognizing continued use of section 1404. Sources: id. at 32-39. It also argued harmless error because Lexecon could originally have selected Arizona and claimed no independent substantial trial injury; invoked Caterpillar; requested prospective application; and contended the surviving claim should already have been dismissed before trial. Sources: id. at 41-42 & n.5. The prospectivity argument was not presented below or in the opposition to review, according to the report. That procedural history does not itself decide forfeiture or relief in this run.

**Dates.** Argument November 10, 1997; supplied event anchor March 3, 1998. Id. at 26.

## Bogan v. Scott-Harris

**Additional legislative and litigation record.** Scott-Harris administered Fall River DHHS from 1987 to 1991. She prepared termination charges after receiving complaints that temporary employee Dorothy Biltcliffe had repeatedly used racial and ethnic slurs. Biltcliffe contacted political officials including council vice president Roderick. The council accepted a sixty-day unpaid suspension settlement, subsequently reduced substantially by Mayor Bogan. While those charges were pending, Bogan prepared the fiscal-1992 budget, anticipating a 5-10% reduction in state aid and proposing salary freezes and elimination of 135 city jobs. The package included eliminating DHHS, whose sole employee was Scott-Harris. Roderick chaired the committee approving the abolition ordinance; the council adopted it 6-2, with Roderick voting yes, and Bogan signed it. Sources: 523 U.S. 44, 46-47. The council vote is a municipal event, not any Supreme Court tally.

The second district-court rejection of immunity occurred on a post-verdict motion for judgment, not on a second pretrial motion to dismiss. The court characterized the ordinance as an individually targeted administrative action. The jury rejected racial discrimination but found the city and both individual officials liable for First Amendment retaliation. Several other defendants had been dropped, and the city administrator received a directed verdict. Only Bogan and Roderick are petitioners here. The First Circuit set aside the city's judgment because only two council members could reasonably be inferred to have unlawful intent, but affirmed the individual judgments. Sources: id. at 47-48 & nn.1-2.

**Actual competing arguments.** The officials sought the same immunity for local legislative work as for other legislative officials and characterized introducing, voting for, and signing the ordinance as legislative functions. Scott-Harris relied on historical cases and treatises imposing municipal-officer liability, including Amy v. Supervisors, and asked that the conduct's substance, not only its formal legislative form, be examined. Sources: id. at 46, 51-52, 55. These identify litigated positions without deciding whether the particular acts qualify or whether immunity defeats the judgment.

**Dates.** Argument December 3, 1997; supplied event anchor March 3, 1998. Id. at 44.
