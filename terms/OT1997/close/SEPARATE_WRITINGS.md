## 1997-11-17 — Jeffries v. Harleston; Second Circuit No. 93-7876; no separate Supreme Court docket assigned.

Stone-Zsela would classify the address as citizen speech as a matter of law and expressly narrow the disruption rule: offense, controversy, audience hostility, institutional embarrassment, reputational pressure, and disruption consisting of reactions to the viewpoint may not themselves justify punishment of this private public-concern speech. ## 1997-11-24 — United States v. Rybar; Third Circuit No. 95-3185; no separate Supreme Court docket assigned.

Stone-Zsela would affirm both possession convictions through an independent constitutional application. Rybar holds the personal right without militia affiliation, and a substantial possession restriction cannot be justified merely by calling it commercial regulation. Fully automatic operation—repeated discharge through a single trigger function—is a relevant functional characteristic. Limiting additions to civilian stocks of these completed weapons directly addresses the distinctive risks of sustained automatic fire. Registration can identify and trace arms; licensing and recordkeeping do not themselves prevent additions to that stock. He finds that preventive fit sufficient for this substantial burden in these transactions.

He treats preservation of prior lawful stock as a transition limiting expansion, not evidence that older weapons are safer; age alone establishes no exception. Government channels do not make civilian military usefulness either disqualifying or immune. The continuing availability of ordinary firearms describes the restriction's reach, but substitutes alone would not justify prohibiting a protected arm. His conclusion rests on the identified weapons and mechanism, not invented casualty findings. It establishes no universal rule for dangerous or military arms, no equivalence between machineguns and ordinary semiautomatic firearms, and no result for receivers, conversion components or accessories. The dismissed transfer charges provide no alternative basis for conviction. This independent application is his ground for dissent from remand, not a holding of the Court.

## 1998-01-21 — Lunding v. New York Tax Appeals Tribunal, No. 96-1462.

Ginsburg, joined by Kennedy, would affirm on two independent grounds.

First, the payer deduction and recipient inclusion allocate taxable income between former spouses. When both are New York residents, the State taxes the recipient and permits the payer's deduction; when both are Connecticut residents, denying the deduction preserves taxation of New York-source income that would otherwise pass outside the State's resident tax base. In their view, that source-tax and income-attribution relationship supplies a substantial justification in this case. Article IV requires substantial rather than mechanically precise equality. Imperfect treatment of other, mixed-residence couples does not establish these taxpayers' entitlement to remove the income from New York taxation. This position does not depend on a finding of aggregate interstate-payment balance or on Connecticut's imposing a tax.

Second, even treating alimony as an expense, the Connecticut payer, recipient and decree locate this particular marital obligation in the home State; it is not an expense of producing New York professional income. Shaffer and Travis permit the corresponding source limitation, and Goodwin's home-State expense analysis supports leaving that personal expense to Connecticut's law and policy. Travis's general personal exemption is different from a deduction for a particular nonlocal obligation. They also object that correlation with a payer's income does not distinguish alimony from many other personal expenditures; the Court's disclaimer of a universal deduction rule does not, in their view, resolve that distinction.

Their objection to relying on other former couples is a merits objection, not a vote to dismiss for lack of standing. They accept Austin's rule against curing established discrimination through another State's tax choices but find this allocation justified in the first place. They join neither the invalidity holding nor the correction mandate; their grounds are noncontrolling.

## 1998-01-26 — Allentown Mack Sales & Service, Inc. v. NLRB, No. 96-795; D.C. Circuit judgment, 83 F.3d 1483.

**Stone-Zsela, joined by O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas, concurring in Parts I and III–IV and the judgment and dissenting from Part II.** The Act's protection of employee choice does not adequately justify demanding essentially the same stringent showing before a safeguarded inquiry as before recognition withdrawal or a binding Board election. Those acts have different consequences: inquiry can resolve uncertainty, while withdrawal immediately changes bargaining and an election invokes distinct statutory rules. Stability is legitimate, but cannot make the rebuttable presumption practically untestable. Compliance with safeguards, independent secret-ballot supervision and the absence of a finding that this poll itself coerced employees support the statutory objection. The writing would invalidate the existing unitary polling threshold while preserving neutral purpose, notice, anti-reprisal assurances, secrecy, advance notice and protection against coercive unfair labor practices. It leaves lawful conclusive periods, the withdrawal standard and a prospectively adopted, source-grounded polling policy intact, and decides no independent First Amendment question. The authors also fully join the Court's independent substantial-evidence ground and denial-of-enforcement mandate even though the facial standard survives.

**Breyer, joined by Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg, concurring in Parts I–II and V and dissenting from Parts III–IV and the judgment.** The granted rule challenge does not require an independent factual-sufficiency decision. ## 1998-01-26 — Brogan v. United States, No. 96-1579; review of United States v. Wiener, 96 F.3d 35.

**Thomas's Part III-B(2), joined by O'Connor, Scalia and Kennedy.** These four Justices add that a simple-denial exception would not reliably prevent the feared escalation: an interrogator could press for a more detailed false account outside the proposed exemption. This policy response has no majority and establishes no rule approving investigative tactics or rejecting an actual abuse claim. Stone-Zsela does not join this additional argument; Souter joins neither part of III-B.

**Ginsburg, joined by Souter, concurring in the judgment.** The unqualified false-statements language forecloses the judicial exemption, but permits a serious problem requiring congressional attention. Officials who already know the answer can elicit a predictable denial and generate a new felony when the underlying conduct cannot be proved, limitations have run, or the conduct is innocent. This is different from merely imposing an additional punishment for an established underlying crime. The reported interview's surprise, absent prior warning and existing records illustrate the concern without proving that this prosecution was unlawful or that Brogan's underlying conduct was innocent.

The statute began with fraudulent claims in 1863, expanded in 1918 under a financial-fraud limitation and broadened in 1934 to reach regulatory falsehoods. Its present reach into informal criminal questioning extends well beyond those original problems. That history supports legislative attention, though it does not overcome the enacted language. In Nunley, the Court vacated a conviction at the Government's request because prosecution lacked normally withheld internal approval; that disposition established no statutory exculpatory-no defense. The charging-era manual said the statute did not apply to exculpatory denials; the 1996 and September 1997 versions stated restrictive charging policies. They evidence restraint, not judicially enforceable immunity. The sentencing policy excluding ordinary nonperjurious denials from an obstruction enhancement likewise does not amend the substantive offense.

Affirmance does not require conviction whenever a suspect denies wrongdoing. Wiener left open whether willfulness requires knowledge that the answer is unlawful and recognized that surprise or lack of reflection can bear on actual intent. Those reservations supply no finding of error or new proceeding here. Materiality language added in 1996 governs its own temporal scope and cannot be substituted for the 1993 statute. Proposed oral-denial protections and warnings were never enacted; they illustrate possible legislative choices, not present defenses. Congressional silence does not ratify the lower-court exception. The concern about creating a criminal offense also differs from adverse employment consequences for a proved knowing false denial under LaChance; accepting that employment rule does not eliminate this concern.

**Souter, concurring in part and in the judgment.** He joins the principal opinion except its discrete response to prosecutorial-abuse concerns in III-B, and joins Ginsburg's concurrence calling for congressional attention. His statement identifies that limited withheld assent; it does not withdraw his support from the remaining statutory, privilege or interpretive analysis.

**Stevens, joined by Breyer, dissenting.** They would reverse only insofar as the judgment sustains the §1001 conviction. A bounded statutory exception should cover a bare reactive oral denial of the questioned person's own suspected wrongdoing during an unsolicited criminal inquiry. Volunteered deception, affirmative misdirection, benefit submissions, regulatory reports and embellished accounts remain outside that exception. The Second Circuit accepted that Brogan's answer fell within the doctrine's core, so resolving its disputed margins is unnecessary here.

The dissent relies on intended statutory coverage, purpose, ordinary criminal-law background and sustained concrete judicial and executive practice. Williams illustrates that a claimed false instrument must actually make the statutory factual assertion; Sorrells and Palmer illustrate generally applicable limits concerning entrapment and statutory territorial reach. Their different holdings do not themselves create this defense, but demonstrate that broad language does not exhaust interpretation. The history of claims and regulatory reporting, together with longstanding treatment of ordinary defensive denials, supports the limited construction here. The dissent does not make executive policy binding law, infer congressional ratification from silence, adopt an unenacted bill, or announce a constitutional right to lie. Its substantial agreement with Ginsburg's concern leads to a different statutory judgment. The proposed reversal would not disturb the separate payment conviction, command release or prescribe an unverified sentence.

## 1998-01-26 — Oubre v. Entergy Operations, Inc., No. 96-1291.

Breyer, joined by O'Connor, fully agrees with the Court but explains why the deficient arrangement should be treated as voidable at the worker's election rather than absolutely void. The statute protects the employee; absolute nullity could let the employer use its own omission to cancel reciprocal benefits even when the employee does not sue. Filing the ADEA action rejects the promise not to sue. An employer may then seek whatever restitution, relief from reciprocal obligations or damages credit the law and agreement independently permit. Their concurrence awards none of those remedies and preserves the Court's prohibition on prior tender. This additional explanation is noncontrolling.

Scalia would affirm for failure to tender back. In his view, restoration before repudiating an exchange is a surviving common-law condition distinct from original waiver validity. He nevertheless rejects ratification on this record: discovering that required statutory safeguards were omitted does not remove that impediment, unlike discovering a concealed fraud or mistake that can be knowingly accepted afterward. He therefore does not join Thomas's ratification ground, and makes no finding that Oubre could repay or that a monetary judgment is warranted.

His background-common-law analysis, reflected in Astoria, Isbrandtsen and Norfolk Redevelopment, requires a sufficient statutory basis for displacing those distinct doctrines; he finds none here. The reported knowing retention therefore supports affirmance in his view. He does not find the original agreement compliant, shift §626(f)(3)'s original-validity burden or award restitution. His ground and Scalia's uncured-impediment objection remain different.

## 1998-02-09 — United States v. Fields / Crawley; Second Circuit Nos. 96-1168 and 96-1257, 113 F.3d 313. No separate Supreme Court docket is supplied.

**O'Connor, joined by Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg, in Parts II-B and V.** These four find the panel's actual-vantage analysis insufficient, even though it expressly considered trespass and shared tenant access. The fenced approach beyond street or sidewalk view, domestic room, respected closed door and lowered blinds require more than equating cotenant visibility with public exposure. The substantial gap, shared use and absence of enhancement are strong contrary facts and could support a lawful observation under the proper inquiry. They therefore make no final curtilage or search finding. O'Connor's Riley qualification leaves the defendant's privacy burden intact; the remand shifts no new public-access burden and guarantees no survey or second evidentiary opportunity. Stevens's different earlier position on the empirical aerial-use burden is not resolved anew. If the observation's legal status changes, the lower courts must consider actual untainted information, contemporaneous urgency and supported remedial alternatives. The warning is a serious potential exigency; the tactical plan does not by itself establish Stone-Zsela's asserted illegality. These Justices would remand for lawful application without automatic suppression, jury-verdict erasure or immediate conditional-plea withdrawal.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in Parts I and II-A and in the judgment.** The officers conducted a search by deliberately crossing a fenced, nonpublic side yard to obtain a view into the substantially screened bedroom unavailable from the public way. Fields's repeated paid access and key, together with Crawley's permitted sharing, establish their bedroom privacy; limited cotenant visibility is not general publication, and plain view requires lawful presence and access. He would decide the search question now, without protecting every apartment common area or restricting observation from a public or lawfully accessible position.

He also finds the warrantless entry unjustified. Once officers had probable cause, they could not create their own entry justification by choosing a tactic designed to expose or flush the occupants instead of pursuing a warrant. The warning and movement foreseeably produced by that plan cannot furnish the emergency the plan was designed to create. His rule preserves independent safety emergencies, hot pursuit and destruction risks not created by police. He would require the lower courts to resolve suppression, consent, independent source, attenuation and the later warrant under those illegality determinations, while entering no final suppression result. Neither his actual-search application nor his police-created-exigency ground commands a majority.

**Scalia, joined by Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer in Part I, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** The actual readily visible gap exposed the well-lit activity to ordinary unaided observation from a yard shared with other tenants. Defendants retained a protected relationship to the room, but not an expectation against this particular shared view. Pinson and Myers protect concealed information; they do not require officers to disregard what this opening exposed. Possible trespass does not alone answer the personal privacy inquiry. These Justices assume Crawley's protected interest rather than exclude commercial guests categorically, and they classify neither every shared yard as public nor this yard as an open field. Their disagreement concerns the sufficiency of the established view, not a denial that the occupants desired privacy.

On that lawful-observation premise, the warning before force, the identified suspect's known violence and the observed readily disposable cocaine establish actual entry urgency and particular danger/destruction grounds for omitting advance announcement. ## 1998-04-22 — Miller v. Albright, No. 96-1060.

### Stevens's opinion announcing the judgment

Justice Stevens would sustain the recognition requirement even under demanding intermediate scrutiny. Reliable parentage identification and an opportunity for a relationship with the citizen parent and this country during childhood are important objectives. Birth ordinarily establishes maternity and an initial opportunity for connection in a way conception alone does not establish paternity or knowledge of the child's existence. Lehr permits attention to a timely legal step rather than biology alone. The amended alternatives include acknowledgment and adjudication, not only marriage or the father's voluntary assent; the available former route likewise required legitimation before twenty-one. Neither deadline was met.

He regards the distinction as substantially fitted to those objectives, rather than justified by assumed paternal irresponsibility. Virginia, Kelley and Klinger allow relevant differences without requiring the least restrictive means. Accepted biology and later reliable proof do not supply the childhood recognition Congress selected. The deadline concerns the qualifying act's occurrence, not a prohibition on later proving an act that was timely. He neither assumes every mother creates enduring American ties nor upholds every father-only condition. He does not decide Fiallo's maximum deference or deny all constitutional remedial power.

### O'Connor's concurrence, joined by Kennedy

These Justices recognize Miller's concrete injury but regard the heightened sex-equality claim as her father's. Powers, Singleton and Campbell require a sufficiently close relationship and a genuine impediment in addition to injury. The father brought his own claim and did not appeal dismissal; ordinary expense, inconvenience and transfer do not establish the obstacle they require. Craig and the excluded-juror cases do not, in their view, eliminate that requirement.

Assuming consideration of Miller's own objection, they treat its classification as parentage rather than her own sex and apply rational review. Recognition during minority reasonably connects the citizenship route with the citizen parent. A separate legitimacy-versus-illegitimacy challenge falls outside the granted comparison. They do not join Stevens's heightened-scrutiny result. Under heightened scrutiny they would reject the stereotyped justification, but their standing conclusion prevents that assessment from becoming their operative adjudication of the father's claim. They also do not join Scalia's citizenship-remedy rule or establish general immunity of nationality legislation from review.

### Scalia's concurrence, joined by Thomas

These Justices accept Miller's permission to assert her father's claim under existing Craig precedent, notwithstanding their earlier misgivings about third-party standing. They consider her the appropriate affected challenger and decline O'Connor's demanded degree of hindrance. Their disagreement concerns the requested remedy.

Wong Kim Ark and Bellei distinguish Fourteenth Amendment birthright from foreign-birth citizenship governed by Congress's enactments. Pangilinan forbids conferring citizenship contrary to statutory conditions. Sections 1401 and 1409 form an integrated grant: the latter's unmet condition is not a later confiscation of citizenship already conferred unconditionally by the former. In their view, Congress's naturalization power and §1421(d)'s prescribed manner and conditions prevent judicial enlargement of that grant; that specific limit defeats reliance on the INA's general §406 severability clause. Mathews permits either extension or withdrawal to cure unequal treatment and does not make extension inevitable. Interlocking paternal and maternal conditions prevent assuming Congress would select the particular enlargement requested, and Westcott's ordinary benefit remedy does not resolve the citizenship-specific issue.

Their ground remains legal after accepting paternal citizenship, sufficient presence, undisputed biology and the former route's lack of a support promise. They do not decide the sex classification, the Government's separate extraterritorial own-right objection, withdrawal of anyone else's citizenship or a universal bar to constitutional review.

### Ginsburg's dissent, joined by Souter and Breyer

These Justices regard Miller herself as denied a statutory opportunity according to her citizen parent's sex. That classification does not cease to be sex-based because the child may be female or male. Virginia, Kelley and Klinger require scrutiny of the actual distinction and its substantial fit. Independently, Craig and Powers permit assertion of the father's right: the Government obtained his dismissal and transfer while the daughter's identical controversy continued, and the resulting practical obstacles suffice under their assessment. They do not claim that an appeal was legally impossible. Calling Miller an alien assumes the answer to her claim of citizenship at birth; Fiallo's admission preferences for conceded aliens do not settle that claim.

Reliable biological proof is important and can justify suitable evidence requirements reflecting actual differences between maternity and paternity. It does not justify the additional categorical age restriction here. Later reliable evidence can establish parentage, while a timely formal act does not itself ensure durable family or national ties. The maternal route requires no continuing custody, support or transmission of American culture. Actual knowledge, care and connection can be examined without sex as an overbroad substitute. This is a substantial-fit objection, not a least-restrictive-means requirement or a claim that biology alone confers citizenship.

The history of citizenship legislation reinforces that objection. Earlier rules subordinated mothers' independent citizenship, while later rules privileged maternity for children born out of wedlock. That change undermines an appeal to a timeless mother-child bond as sufficient justification. An apparent preference for women can enforce expectations of obligatory maternal care and absent paternal responsibility. The history supports functional scrutiny; it creates no conclusive presumption of invalid historical purpose and decides no unpresented condition.

They would remove the unconstitutional age restriction from Miller's available former-law route and direct declaratory recognition, retaining legitimation and the independent transmission prerequisites already accepted. Mathews requires attention to both equality remedies; Westcott, the benefit-conferring structure and the INA's general severability provision favor correcting the unequal barrier over withdrawing a separate maternal protection. Section 1421(d), in their view, does not bar recognizing citizenship conferred by the remaining statute after constitutional correction. That differs from equitable naturalization. They would permit ordinary implementation of the available election if needed, without a new support promise, invented election formality or hearing to relitigate accepted predicates. Their remedy imports neither the maternal one-year presence period nor an unpresented passport-processing order, and it does not declare every other §1409 condition invalid.

### Stone-Zsela's dissent

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela reaches Miller's own equality claim. He applies intermediate scrutiny to the sex classification and equivalent elevated review to the parentage rule. Government may require reliable biological proof and a timely voluntary legal parent-child relationship, but must make equivalent objective procedures available to fathers and mothers. Biological maternity may often be documented at birth; that does not justify a broader regime presuming maternal commitment and paternal absence. Records, acknowledgment, adjudication and support evidence can address legitimate proof and connection interests without that categorical asymmetry. The father's acknowledgment makes the distinction consequential here.

He would reverse and remand for a lawful equality remedy. The lower court could apply an equal acknowledgment and proof regime, allow Congress a reasonable opportunity to enact one, or recognize citizenship if no narrower lawful cure is available and the statutory prerequisites apart from the discrimination are met. His proposed remedy does not direct immediate recognition or reopen the accepted predicates. Neutral proof deadlines, safeguards against fraud and genuine legal relationships remain permissible. He defines no citizenship rule for every foreign birth and does not join the three-Justice dissent's directed former-route remedy.

A claim concerning compulsory use of that principal remains available for lawful consideration even under the dissenters’ alternative rejection of the asserted interest entitlement. The four also join Breyer’s alternative analysis if the Court decides ownership alone.

**Justice Breyer, separately dissenting, joined by Justices Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg.** He joins Souter’s proposed integrated remand. If ownership is reached in isolation, he would reject the claimed entitlement for funds properly placed under the no-net-return program rule. The operative arrangement makes a charitable return possible where a client otherwise has no legally and economically realizable net benefit. Webb’s involved an independently productive fund; its protection against public relabeling does not settle ownership of this otherwise unavailable return. Lucas protects established entitlements without deciding how this claimed benefit must be identified.

This argument does not assume that every individual is legally barred from a NOW account. Individual eligibility, restrictions on corporate and partnership funds, administrative and reporting costs, and the distinction between gross and net yield must remain separate. The Federal Reserve interpretation is part of the banking setting, not a holding that its validity has been adjudicated. The reported Texas concession allows assessment of net client earnings through feasible attorney pooling; it establishes no positive alternative yield for these particular funds. ## 1998-06-22 — Gebser v. Lago Vista Independent School District, No. 96-1866.

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's opinion announcing the judgment, Part III.** The complaint to the principal, the internal warning and the evidence about the absence of a functioning grievance route create, in his view, a factual dispute about the recipient's own fault. He would require the lower courts to examine whether school-conferred authority materially enabled the discrimination and whether actual recipient knowledge or willfully blind, deliberately indifferent reporting failure caused its continuation. A district cannot defeat that inquiry through its own failure to transmit substantial warning information. He does not treat the complaint about classroom remarks as an established report of a sexual relationship, resolve the conflicting policy evidence, or find liability. Employment, ordinary negligence and a technical regulatory violation alone remain insufficient. This record-specific application has no other joins; the attribution component of Part II has only the limited Marks force stated above.

**Justice Breyer, concurring in part and in the judgment, joined by Justices Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg.** They join Part I's rejection of exclusivity and support reversal and ordinary remand, but do not join Parts II–III. They renew the two independent attribution routes previously stated in their Kinman position. First, actual delegated educational power materially aiding intentional harassment can attribute the wrong to the recipient without a separate higher-management knowledge requirement. Second, negligent failure to correct after responsible officials knew or reasonably should have known, causing injury or continuation, supplies an independent ground. Teacher title or opportunity alone does not establish actual aid, and a policy breach alone establishes neither negligence nor causation.

Franklin permits compensation for intentional discrimination. These Justices distinguish the teacher's intentional wrong from the agency principles attributing that wrong; their negligence alternative is not compensation for every unintended discriminatory effect. Cannon supplies the established private action, while Title IX's administrative notice and correction process does not require a second intentional wrong by recipient management in every damages case. They acknowledge that Kinman had rejected their broader grounds and would reconsider that exclusivity. Title VII's agency decisions supply a limited analogy, not an automatic transfer of every employment rule.

The actual delegation, material aid, information received, correction, reporting arrangements and causal injury require ordinary further consideration. The affidavit/deposition tension over policy and uncertainty about dissemination are evidence, not conclusive findings. These Justices do not establish liability or compel a trial. Their judgment agreement does not endorse Stone-Zsela's record-specific awareness reasoning. Their broader attribution theories do not become controlling merely because they support the same judgment; only the narrower component identified in Holding II has the stated Marks force.

**Justice Ginsburg, separately concurring, joined by Justices Souter and Breyer.** Within their broader attribution framework, these three would permit a limited avoidable-consequences defense. The district would bear the burden of proving that internal remedies were adequately publicized and likely to provide redress without undue risk, effort or expense. Recovery would be barred only to the extent the plaintiff unreasonably failed to use those measures and consequently suffered avoidable harm. A paper policy, nonreporting alone, an unsafe or ineffective route or eventual correction would not establish the defense. The uncertain policy and dissemination record does not conclusively establish it here. This is a causal limit on avoidable recovery, not automatic complete immunity or an additional Court-adopted attribution condition. Stevens reserves the defense and does not join its terms; Stone-Zsela leaves defenses unresolved.

**Justice O'Connor, dissenting, joined by Justices Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.** They would affirm under Kinman's actual-notice and deliberate-disregard rule. Franklin's availability of compensation does not answer recipient responsibility. Title IX's recipient structure, its administrative correction mechanism and Pennhurst's notice principle support attention to the recipient's own culpable nonresponse rather than ordinary agency attribution or knowledge imputed from the teacher's conduct. They would preserve Kinman's exclusivity and reject the additional attribution route.

Even assuming that the principal had corrective authority, the reported information and response do not, in their view, establish actual knowledge of the relationship or a substantial risk followed by deliberate and unreasonable disregard. The parents complained about classroom comments. The principal met with Waldrop, received an apology and assurance, cautioned him and informed the guidance counselor; termination followed police discovery. These responses are not declared perfect, and a warning does not invariably suffice. But this record does not establish the required culpable refusal to protect.

The possible absence of a functioning complaint procedure is not itself institutional actual notice. The principal's information cannot be pooled with Waldrop's knowledge, and the policy conflict cannot be resolved by assuming that every procedure was absent. Their rule requires neither a written complaint, a victim's personal report, a confession nor proof that a completed sexual relationship was already known. Actual notice of a substantial danger can suffice. Administrative and injunctive enforcement remain distinct, and the district receives no personal qualified immunity. These four oppose both reversal and the limitation of Kinman.

## 1998-08-17 — Brewster v. Board of Education; Ninth Circuit No. 97-55203; 149 F.3d 971.
