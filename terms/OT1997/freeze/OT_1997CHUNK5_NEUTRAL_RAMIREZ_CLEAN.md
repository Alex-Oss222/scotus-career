## Direct pre-event statutory authorities

**Miller v. United States, 357 U.S.301, 305–314 (1958).** The Court treated the District of Columbia entry rule as materially identical to §3109 and held the forced entry unlawful without adequate notice of authority and arrest purpose. Closing the door was ambiguous and did not establish that the occupant knew the purpose; evidence found inside could not justify the entry retrospectively. Crucially, the Court expressly **reserved** a general exigency exception because the Government did not claim one. Harlan concurred in the result. Clark, joined by Burton, dissented on the local-law, outward-conduct and immediate-necessity grounds; those grounds are not the Court's holding. [Official Reports](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-357/pdf/USREPORTS-357-301.pdf); [Cornell](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/357/301).

**Sabbath v. United States, 391 U.S.585, 588–591 & n.8 (1968).** Section3109's breaking requirement includes opening a closed but unlocked door; physical damage is not necessary to activate its protection. The Court rejected the asserted danger excuse on that record because there was no substantial basis to believe the suspect armed, resistant or threatening the informant. It again left the general exigency question formally unresolved. Footnote8 reasons that the limited common-law exceptions may apply to the statute because it codifies that common law; it is strong interpretive support, not an already-entered holding establishing every exception or a particular suspicion quantum. Black dissented without a written opinion. [Official Reports](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-391/pdf/USREPORTS-391-585.pdf); [Cornell](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/391/585).

## Statutory competing paths

The full statutory text is in `CLEAN_18_USC_3109.md`, mechanically copied from the saved official 1994 Code and incorporated in the scoped clean freeze. It permits breaking doors, windows or other parts of a house to execute a search warrant after notice of authority and purpose and refused admission, or when necessary to liberate the officer or an assisting person. That liberation alternative is not a free-standing authorization to prevent any prospective danger, and the present facts establish no trapped officer or assistant.

**Government's analytical path:** Sabbath's understanding of a codified common-law protection supports its common-law limits as well as its breadth; current Wilson and Richards supply the constitutional treatment of particularized danger, futility and frustrated investigation. Because Sabbath treats undamaged entry through an unlocked door as breaking, the statute does not itself make damage a separate tier of announcement justification. The reported dangerous escapee and garage weapons supply concrete facts on which to argue an excuse here, unlike Sabbath's unsupported danger allegation. Any common-law construction and the quantum applied must be explained, not attributed to a holding Miller or Sabbath did not make.

**Ramirez's analytical path:** Congress specified notice, refused admission and a distinct liberation alternative. Prior cases protect against unannounced intrusion regardless of whether the door is locked; they do not erase the command or make a judge's no-knock authorization dispositive. A constitutional exception cannot simply be assumed to rewrite the statute. Even if the statute incorporates a narrow danger exception, the Government must connect the actual pre-entry information to the need to bypass the ordinary process and justify this destructive act. The stated police objective is evidence of purpose, not a judicial finding of necessity. Miller also explains the safety interest in avoiding a householder's mistaken resistance; Ramirez's reaction illustrates the interest but is not a retrospective veto over a justified entry.

# 18 U.S.C. §3109

Mechanical copy of the full section from the saved 1994 Code source; original line wrapping retained.

§ 3109. Breaking doors or windows for entry or  
exit 
The officer may break open any outer or inner  
door or window of a house, or any part of a  
house, or anything therein, to execute a search  
warrant, if, after notice of his authority and  
purpose, he is refused admittance or when nec -
essary to liberate himself or a person aiding him 
in the execution of the warrant. 
(June 25, 1948, ch. 645, 62 Stat. 820.)
