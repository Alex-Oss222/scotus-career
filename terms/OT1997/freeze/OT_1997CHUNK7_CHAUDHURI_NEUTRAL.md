* **Question Presented:** 1. Whether officially arranged, nonsectarian invocations at public-university functions violate the Establishment Clause when attendees are adults and attendance is not formally compulsory, including where a university employee alleges practical employment pressure to attend.
  2. Whether an officially designated moment of silence adopted as an alternative to spoken prayer violates the Establishment Clause. [Source formulation; not a verbatim petition quotation.]

* **Question Granted:** 1. Whether officially arranged, nonsectarian invocations at public-university functions violate the Establishment Clause when attendees are adults and attendance is not formally compulsory, including where a university employee alleges practical employment pressure to attend.
  2. Whether an officially designated moment of silence adopted as an alternative to spoken prayer violates the Establishment Clause.

* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Sixth Circuit rejected Chaudhuri’s claims concerning prayers and moments of silence at Tennessee State University events.
* **Relief Requested:** Chaudhuri seeks reversal, declaratory relief, damages, and an injunction against university-sponsored devotional exercises.
* **Material Record:** The record facts below are taken from the Sixth Circuit's opinion; bracketed references are to its paragraph numbering in the primary-source text cited in Section I.E.
  1. Tennessee State University (TSU), a state university in Nashville, formerly included invocations and benedictions at certain university events. Chaudhuri objected to prayers at functions such as graduation exercises, faculty meetings, dedication ceremonies and guest lectures. [130 F.3d 232, paras. 2, 4 (6th Cir. 1997)]
  2. Chaudhuri, born in India and a naturalized citizen, is a tenured professor of mechanical engineering at TSU and an adherent of Hinduism. [para. 4]
  3. Responding to Chaudhuri's complaint, the general counsel of the State University and Community College System issued an opinion letter in May 1988. The letter said prayers are not unconstitutional if they do not appear to favor or endorse a particular religious view, and it suggested that a moment of silence would be an "appropriate vehicle for allowing the tradition of prayer without running afoul of the Constitution." [para. 5]
  4. TSU officials then decided that the prayers should be generic and nonsectarian. Many were delivered by Dr. James Haney, a clergyman and TSU associate professor of history; others were delivered by local religious leaders at TSU's invitation. TSU did not review the prayers in advance and, apart from requesting that they be nonsectarian and omit references to Jesus Christ, gave no content guidelines. [paras. 6, 39]
  5. The opinion sets out Haney's May 1991 commencement prayer as typical. The prayer opens "Let us pray" and repeatedly addresses the "Heavenly Father." [paras. 7-12]
  6. In January 1991 Chaudhuri sued the State of Tennessee, TSU and TSU administrators Annie Neal, Decatur Rogers, James A. Hefner and Arthur C. Washington. He alleged Title VII failure-to-promote claims and Establishment and Free Exercise Clause claims, and he sought compensatory damages and declaratory and injunctive relief. [caption; para. 13]
  7. Chaudhuri asserted that faculty were required to attend university functions at which prayers were offered, and that his evaluations included a "university service" component. Defendants responded that attendance was encouraged but not required or monitored, and that no employee had suffered adverse consequences for non-attendance. [paras. 14-15] The engineering dean attested that Chaudhuri's attendance or non-attendance never affected his evaluations, and the Sixth Circuit described TSU's non-monitoring and no-penalty representations as made "without contradiction." [para. 47]
  8. In April 1993 Chaudhuri moved to enjoin prayer at the May commencement. At about the same time, President Hefner announced that there would be no verbal prayers at those exercises and that a moment of silence would be acceptable "to solemnize the occasion." The district court denied the injunction. [para. 16]
  9. At the May 1993 commencement, when the speaker asked everyone to rise and remain silent, someone or a group began reciting the Lord's Prayer aloud. Many audience members joined in, spontaneously by all accounts, and applause followed. TSU officials said they had no advance knowledge. [para. 17]
  10. Chaudhuri filed a second injunction motion, noting that prayers continued at non-graduation functions and asking that moments of silence also be enjoined. Hefner responded by affidavit that moments of silence would replace prayers at all university functions where prayers had been offered, and that he had told staff to clarify that faculty attendance was encouraged but not mandatory. The district court denied the injunction. [para. 18] Judge Jones's separate opinion states that the president ordered the prayer practice ceased on June 8, 1993. [para. 59 (Jones, J.)]
  11. At the August 1993 summer commencement, the keynote speaker was Dr. David Jones, Jr., a Nashville public-school educator identified in the program as a local pastor. When he asked the audience to stand for a moment of silence, part of the audience again recited the Lord's Prayer. TSU officials denied complicity. [para. 19] Chaudhuri conceded that there was no evidence of university complicity in either recitation. [para. 34]
  12. In January 1995 the district court granted summary judgment for defendants on the Establishment and Free Exercise claims, 886 F. Supp. 1374 (M.D. Tenn. 1995). The Title VII claims were resolved for defendants, by summary judgment on one and by judgment after a bench trial on the other. Chaudhuri appealed only the First Amendment rulings. [para. 20 and n.1]
  13. The Sixth Circuit decided the appeal on November 18, 1997, before this Court's December 22, 1997 decision in Tanford v. Brand. It stated that the prayer challenge remained live insofar as Chaudhuri sought money damages, and that his declaratory and injunctive claims regarding the prayers appeared to have become moot. [para. 22, at 234]
  14. Applying Lemon and distinguishing Lee v. Weisman, the Sixth Circuit held that the former nonsectarian prayers and the moments of silence did not violate the Establishment Clause. It rejected Chaudhuri's contention, based on the 1988 letter, that the moments of silence were a ruse to foster prayer. [para. 33] It held that the audience recitations were not attributable to TSU and that the Free Exercise claim lacked merit, and it affirmed the judgment for all defendants. [paras. 27-55]
  15. The Sixth Circuit's opinion cites the Seventh Circuit's decision in Tanford v. Brand, 104 F.3d 982, as supporting authority on the legitimacy of the prayers' secular purposes [para. 27], the de minimis character of the religious gesture [para. 36] and of any entanglement [para. 40], the absence of coercion to participate [para. 47], and Chaudhuri's freedom, even if obliged to attend, not to participate in the prayers [para. 48]. It does not describe Tanford's facts, and its separate discussion of the audience's age cites Lee v. Weisman, Marsh v. Chambers and other decisions, not Tanford. [paras. 50-51]
  16. The opinion does not address immunity, the capacity in which the individual defendants were sued, or the statutory basis of the damages claim. [entire opinion]
  17. Judge Jones concurred as to the moments of silence. He dissented as to the prayers: he would have held the prayer challenge not moot and found an Establishment Clause violation warranting declaratory and injunctive relief. [paras. 57-64, at 240-41]
