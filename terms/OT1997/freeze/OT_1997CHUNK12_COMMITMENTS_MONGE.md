## Reach, components, and fixed premises

All eight Associates reach the preserved federal double-jeopardy question on direct review of the California Supreme Court judgment permitting renewed proof. The claimed right is protection from the renewed proceeding itself. A remaining sentencing proceeding does not require postponing review of that conclusive rejection of the federal bar; Bullington, 451 U.S. 430, 437 n.8, supplies the analogous finality treatment. No Teague bar applies to this direct-review decision. No recusal, new procedural obstacle, independent challenge to the drug convictions, or separate challenge to the prior-prison-term enhancement is established.

The separable legal determinations are: (1) whether this serious-prior-felony adjudication receives acquittal-like double-jeopardy protection; (2) what follows from the conceded actual insufficiency if that protection attaches; and (3) the resulting sentencing mandate. A general constitutional classification of all maximum-increasing facts is a possible broader rationale, not a prerequisite established by entering law. There is no separate insufficiency dispute to send back for another search of the original record.

The prison record proves the assault conviction and imprisonment, but does not prove personal use of a dangerous or deadly weapon or personal infliction of great bodily injury. Those are the alternative qualifications at issue. The prosecutor's reference to a stick proves neither. No Justice's commitment assumes that the old assault actually had or lacked the required personal conduct. California required proof beyond reasonable doubt, offered trial safeguards, and obtained Monge's jury waiver. A bench proceeding is not less protected solely because the jury was waived.

The first selected term was five years; the first-offender maximum on count one was seven years; the qualifying-prior maximum was fourteen years. The imposed ten-year doubled term and independent additional one year are distinct. The eight rows do not invalidate the prior conviction or turn insufficiency of its serious-felony qualification into factual innocence of that conviction.

## Governing law and two available paths

**Path R — reverse the permission to retry.** The required personal-conduct finding authorizes a greater punishment ceiling and was submitted in a distinct, bounded adjudication with proof beyond reasonable doubt. Its function and trial character support treating failure of proof as an acquittal of the allegation necessary for that enhanced punishment. Bullington protects the finality of an acquittal-like sentencing adjudication; Rumsey confirms that a judicial factfinder does not defeat that protection. Burks and the April 6 Hinds holding then forbid another opportunity to supply missing prosecution proof. The strongest objection is that Bullington and Rumsey concerned capital eligibility, while recidivist status traditionally informs punishment for a present conviction without constituting a separate criminal charge. Trial safeguards alone do not establish constitutional offense status, and Almendarez-Torres did not settle the general constitutional question.

**Path A — affirm the permission to retry.** The proceeding determines the qualifying character of a prior conviction for noncapital recidivist sentencing; it does not prosecute the old assault anew or reconsider guilt of the drug offenses. DiFrancesco's sentencing/acquittal distinction and the distinct capital setting of Bullington and Rumsey permit retaining that characterization even with added safeguards. The strongest objection is concrete: the required personal conduct was not established by the prior conviction, the finding changes the lawful maximum, and this fully adversarial adjudication has failed on proof. Calling the proceeding sentencing cannot answer why its failed factual predicate lacks acquittal finality. Witte and Watts alone do not answer that objection because their holdings operate within conviction-authorized punishment.

**Shared limits.** Caspari reserved the direct-review merits. Its new-rule conclusion at 1986 finality supplies no merits vote here. Almendarez-Torres holds that section 1326(b)(2) creates an aggravated-offense element, with charging and proof consequences; it establishes neither a majority general constitutional maximum-increase rule nor a constitutional recidivism exception. Its four-Justice avoidance reasoning and four-Justice recidivism dissent are evidence of the respective Justices' views, not law that decides Monge. Witte and Watts remain bounded; neither validates every arrangement with a sentencing label. Dixon's retained Grady rule and Hudson's distinct sanctions framework remain in force, but neither independently resolves attachment here. All eight Associates joined Hinds's prohibition on new proof after actual insufficiency; the disagreement here concerns the constitutional character of this proceeding, not an exception to Burks once that character is established.

The June 25 screening receipt identifies no new attachment or recidivism rule. Balsys's independent-foreign-risk limit and Diamondstone's particular civil-insurance holding do not classify this domestic adjudication. The other appended June 25 public decisions likewise supply no retrial authorization or prohibition.

## Enhanced comparison, qualifications, and join barriers

### Stevens

Path R follows his own Caspari merits position: formal factual allegations, adversarial adjudication and actual failure of the State's proof can warrant finality even in noncapital recidivist sentencing. Here the maximum actually increases, making the punitive consequence more direct than the unchanged range in Caspari. His McMillan, Witte and Watts writings likewise refuse to make statutory labels or an abstract ceiling conclusive of the constitutional significance of additional punishment.

Path A's strongest case is the enduring distinction between sentencing and guilt, reinforced by the capital facts of Bullington. Stevens rejects that proposed limitation because the first proceeding resolved a prescribed factual predicate under the criminal burden, and the failure is genuine insufficiency rather than selection of a lower discretionary sentence. The State's historical use of prior convictions does not prove the unestablished personal qualification. Conversely, the strongest objection to his path is that voluntary state safeguards cannot create federal rights; his sufficient answer combines the adjudication's function with those safeguards and actual failure, rather than relying on procedural resemblance alone.

He can join the confined no-retrial rule for this completed allegation and its remedy without requiring a comprehensive rule for every sentencing fact. His broader objection to mandatory punitive increments within an unchanged ceiling remains his own; it is unnecessary here. He refuses any rationale that treats actual insufficiency as correctable trial error, infers waiver from the jury election, or promises the same punishment through an unproved alternative.

### O'Connor

Her Rumsey opinion makes the acquittal's substantive character decisive, protects a bench acquittal, and does not permit legal error to reopen it. Path R therefore has substantial support in her own jurisprudence. Its strongest application here is the binary personal-conduct qualification under a reasonable-doubt burden, coupled with a new maximum. Her sufficient ground for Path A is that this still determines recidivist punishment for the established present offenses rather than guilt or the distinct capital entitlement protected in Rumsey. Her Almendarez-Torres dissent supports that classification but is not treated as prevailing constitutional law.

The objection that the old conviction did not establish personal conduct is accepted as a proof objection, not answered by presuming the fact. Her position permits the State to prove the qualifying character anew under the existing safeguards; it does not declare conviction status conclusive of that character. Witte supplies the distinction between sentencing use and prosecution, while its ceiling limitation prevents it from doing the entire constitutional work here. Her McMillan join likewise supplies no already-decided maximum-increase rule.

She can join a bounded noncapital-recidivism rationale and preserve Bullington/Rumsey in full. She will not join a statement that no sentencing determination can ever be an acquittal, that state labeling controls, that the prior conviction conclusively established the missing conduct, or that every maximum-increasing fact may be removed from offense protections. If attachment were established, she accepts Burks's bar; no special forgiveness of prosecutorial insufficiency is proposed.

### Scalia

His Witte concurrence distinguishes a later actual prosecution from a sentencing use of information; his Watts join similarly leaves conviction-authorized limits intact. Path A can therefore rely on a real distinction in his own positions. Its strongest argument is that a prior adjudication's qualifying character remains status, not present offense conduct. His present answer is the statutory legal consequence: the present drug conviction without the additional personal-conduct qualification does not authorize the higher ceiling. This is not the previously permitted use of information within that authority. His Almendarez-Torres avoidance reasoning identified the same constitutional difficulty; the direct double-jeopardy issue now requires a conclusion about this actually tried allegation rather than another federal statutory construction.

Path R's strongest objection is that serious constitutional doubt in Almendarez-Torres did not itself decide the question. He does not treat it as having done so. His independent present commitment is to recognize the required qualification's element function for this successive adjudication, and then apply Burks. The State already provided notice, a criminal burden and a waived jury; there is no need to manufacture a separate trial-right defect.

He can join the confined rule barring another trial of this necessary maximum-increasing personal-conduct allegation. He does not make the result turn solely on the State's choice to provide trial safeguards. He need not join a general extension to every mandatory minimum, within-range fact, evidentiary use, or prior-conviction administrative determination. He refuses a rationale treating retained Grady as overruled, although no Grady expansion is needed to prohibit a second attempt at the same qualifying allegation.

### Kennedy

His Watts dissent is not an answer against that path: it sought full consideration and adopted no ultimate merits rule. That procedural objection is satisfied by this plenary posture. His Witte join and Almendarez-Torres recidivism-dissent join instead support his sufficient substantive ground for Path A: the inquiry remains the qualifying status of a previous conviction in sentencing the current offenses.

The strongest objection to Path A is that calling this status may conceal a renewed trial of criminal conduct. Kennedy acknowledges the missing personal-use or personal-infliction finding; he does not infer it from the assault conviction. The inquiry establishes eligibility for recidivist treatment without imposing another assault conviction, and he declines to equate this bounded noncapital status inquiry with Bullington's capital adjudication. The strongest objection to Path R is its extension beyond the capital holdings; for him the safeguards and changed ceiling do not sufficiently overcome the nature of the predicate being adjudicated.

He accepts narrow affirmance allowing another attempt under state law, not a general license for repeated prosecutions. He reserves unrelated maximum-increase arrangements, independent due-process questions, and any claim not supplied here. He rejects any argument that Hinds permits repair after a protected acquittal; his position depends on nonattachment.

### Souter

### Thomas

His Witte concurrence places primary weight on whether an offense was previously prosecuted, rather than treating every punitive increment as another punishment. His Almendarez-Torres dissent join supports treating recidivism as sentencing status; his Watts join accepts bounded factfinding without automatically creating another prosecution. Path R's strongest argument is that a personal-conduct fact necessary to double the lawful ceiling is functionally an element and has already been tried. Thomas does not dispute the failure of proof or collapse the qualification into the conviction. He concludes that determining the prior conviction's serious character for noncapital recidivist sentencing is not a second offense prosecution. The strongest challenge to Path A is the formalism of that distinction; his answer rests on what is adjudicated and punished—the current convictions with recidivist consequences—rather than merely where California placed a statutory provision.

He may join a bounded recidivism/noncapital rationale, reserving any general constitutional rule about other facts. He need not join a separate theory that all multiple punishment is outside the Clause, and none is adopted here. Bullington remains binding in its established setting. His Hinds join means he accepts finality after actual insufficiency in a protected prosecution; he will not characterize the existing proof as sufficient or permit retrial on the drug convictions.

### Ginsburg

Her Witte and Watts joins provide the strongest argument for Path A: sentencing use and a new prosecution are distinct, and even mandatory consequences do not universally establish element status. Her Almendarez-Torres joins supply the limiting distinction. This enhancement requires an additional personal-conduct qualification not established by the valid conviction; its successful adjudication authorizes punishment beyond the otherwise applicable ceiling.

Path R is confined to that completed adjudication and its conceded insufficiency. The strongest objection is that extension from capital acquittals may make federal protection depend on state-selected procedures. Ginsburg's commitment rests on both the substantive authorization function and the completed trial-like inquiry; it is not a rule that every generous procedure creates another federal prosecution. Her Caspari join imposed a habeas limit while leaving the direct-review constitutional issue open, so it supplies no contrary merits commitment.

She can join the same confined finality rule as Souter and its sentencing-only remedy. She leaves a universal jury-allocation or maximum-fact doctrine unresolved, preserves ordinary within-authority sentencing, and rejects any finding about the actual assault conduct. She will not join relief erasing the drug convictions, treating an unproved qualification as an invalid prior conviction, or using a remand to obtain a better prosecution record after recognizing acquittal protection.

### Breyer

His Almendarez-Torres recidivism-dissent join directly supports Path A's treatment of a qualifying prior conviction as a sentencing matter. His Witte and Watts joins distinguish sentencing use from prosecution; his Watts concurrence also treats the policy significance of an acquittal as potentially greater than the minimum constitutional requirement. These sources do not themselves resolve this changed ceiling, but support his present choice to retain the noncapital recidivist classification.

The strongest argument for Path R is a completed reasonable-doubt trial of an indispensable personal-conduct predicate: the State is asking for another opportunity after failing, not merely correcting discretionary sentence selection. Breyer accepts that description and the insufficiency. He nevertheless distinguishes the predicate's role in identifying the serious character of prior conviction from an adjudication of a new present offense or capital eligibility. The strongest objection to Path A is that this characterization permits punishment unauthorized by the bare drug conviction; his answer is limited statutory recidivist authority after lawful proof of the condition, not a conclusion that the condition has already been met or that Witte supplies a general ceiling exception.

He can join the narrow affirmance and existing-proof-burden requirements. He leaves open broader element-allocation rules and other sentencing schemes, and does not announce that every maximum increase is constitutionally immaterial. He accepts Hinds/Burks if protection attaches; the vote to permit retrial turns entirely on his contrary characterization here.
