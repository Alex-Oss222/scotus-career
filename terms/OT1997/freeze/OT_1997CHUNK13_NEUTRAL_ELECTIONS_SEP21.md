## 151 — corrected facts, present law and boundaries

The three injunctions must remain distinct. The November 1989 Richmond order expired after the election or compliance; that action was eventually dismissed in October 1994 for failure to prosecute. The 1989 Fairfax order became permanent following a hearing and was dissolved as to VLC and Jordahl by stipulation on September 30, 1994; it survived as to other persons until March 21, 1996. The 1993 Fairfax injunction against LaRock and others was dissolved by the Virginia Supreme Court on November 1, 1993 without an opinion. That court's act establishes dissolution, not its constitutional rationale or a finding of tort liability. Earlier federal Younger proceedings do not supply a present merits judgment on the disclosure law.

The packet's description of all the literature as nonendorsing is overinclusive. The Fourth Circuit's footnote 2 recites that one 1989 pamphlet expressly asked readers to vote against Linda Toddy Puller if they disagreed with her positions. Other voter guides were described as nonendorsing. The old §24.1-277 authorship provision, the 1993 replacement and the July 1, 1996 amendments are not interchangeable. No present narrowing construction is a retrospective adjudication of every earlier publication or injunction.

Count One requests prospective restraint of DPV's use of §8.01-620 injunctions to enforce §§24.2-908, -910 and -1014. The published courts characterize the pleaded system-of-prior-restraint claim as operating through injunctions against speech, rather than a separately substantiated general facial challenge. All orders against these plaintiffs had ended; later statutory amendments materially changed the asserted recurrence premise. No identified intended activity and realistic enforcement threat under the current scheme is established by this record. A litigant need not wait to be prosecuted: Pic-A-State and American Booksellers preserve credible pre-enforcement suits. DPV's mere failure to disavow every future suit is not enough here. Neither a universal rule that the Commonwealth itself is the only possible defendant nor an automatic prohibition on suits against private enforcers is adopted.

**Intervening authoritative state construction:** Caldwell, June 5, 1998, construes the current phrase concerning influencing an election in §§24.2-901, -910 and -1014 to reach express advocacy of election or defeat of an identified candidate, excluding solely issue advocacy. That construction carries through the §901 political-committee definition into §908. Section 908's organization statement concerns a political committee anticipating more than $200 in contributions or expenditures during a calendar year; that trigger must not be confused with other definitions' thresholds. Section 910(B) addresses independent expenditure reporting by persons or groups not political committees; §1014's authorship requirement is a separate provision. Section 901(B)'s express treatment of qualifying §501(c)(3) organizations does not imply that §501(c)(4) issue-only organizations are covered. Caldwell took VSHL's issue-only activity as the certified premise; it made no such finding about every Jordahl plaintiff or every pamphlet.

Caldwell's state-law construction is admitted as controlling Virginia law. Its discussion of federal McIntyre cannot enlarge this Court's narrower individual-leaflet holding. Nor does Maine's federal regulatory construction supply the meaning of these distinct state provisions. Caldwell independently weakens a present threat based solely on issue advocacy; it does not establish that planned express advocacy is immune, settle prior orders' validity or erase accrued damages claims. The absence of a supported current threat already permits Count One dismissal, so no invented post-Caldwell litigant response or mootness concession is necessary.

Counts Two and Three concern VLC/Jordahl's 1989 injunction injuries; Counts Seven and Eight concern LaRock's 1993 injunction injuries. The district and appellate opinions characterize them as damages claims requiring a declaration that those injunctions were erroneously issued. The record supplies no identified fraud or deception in procurement, no corrupt judicial conspiracy and no independent wrong severable from the complained-of restraints. There is nevertheless a serious legal disagreement about jurisdiction over past constitutional damages, especially after dissolution. ## 152 — corrected facts and review vehicle

Section 99.103(1) (1993) distributes approximately half the filing fees of candidates of qualifying parties to those parties' executive committees for party expenses. Eligibility requires at least five percent of Florida's registered voters. The parties concede the underlying candidate filing fees were lawfully collected; Wilson had already qualified for the ballot. Do not claim that minor-party fees were specially exacted for, or directly transferred to, rival parties. The published source supports the approximate allocation, not an invented exact dollar formula.

This is an objective, potentially changing support criterion, not a statutory naming of the Democratic and Republican Parties. It nevertheless creates a real comparative benefit and requires constitutional analysis. A funding label does not immunize discrimination, and an unequal classification does not automatically establish the severe burden required for strict scrutiny. Buckley's public-financing rule allows eligibility based on demonstrated support but also preserves political fluidity; its vote-based criteria, post-election funding and recipient spending limits are not facts of Florida's different scheme. The lawful paths are affirmance confined to this limited, objective subsidy on the demonstrated record, or reversal because its selective financial advantage lacks an adequate relationship to a legitimate interest once candidates have qualified. ## 153 — corrected facts and qualifications

Illinois distinguishes the electorates in which a party has demonstrated support. More than five percent in the last gubernatorial election establishes the party generally; more than five percent in certain other statewide races establishes statewide status; more than five percent in a district or political subdivision establishes status there. LPI's combined 1994 trustee vote was 5.5 percent, but its gubernatorial vote was 1.68 percent and it had no qualifying district result. Statewide status in the trustee contest does not itself establish support in each congressional electorate.

Established-party primary petitions require 0.5 percent of the relevant qualified primary electorate. New-party general-election petitions require, for statewide office, the lesser of 25,000 signatures or one percent of voters in the last statewide general election; for district office, the lesser of five percent of voters in the last regular district election or the statewide cap. A separate postdecennial-redistricting exception uses 5,000 for the district petition, with a different 600-signature established-party exception not at issue. The general rules' denominators and election stages differ; the comparison is not simply a tenfold threshold. The statute's complete-slate requirement operates within the relevant subdivision. Do not turn it into an asserted requirement that a congressional candidate run a slate for every statewide office.

The district average for the new-party route was about 7,610 signatures; the seven plaintiff districts averaged about 9,337, compared with established-party primary averages of approximately 658 Democratic and 532 Republican signatures. Those numbers matter to actual burden, but arithmetic alone does not decide severity. Two third-party congressional candidates qualified in 1994. The 1996 count of fourteen, including ten Libertarians, is disputed as proof of meeting the full threshold: several petitions were accepted without objection despite apparent signature insufficiency. Six 1992 qualifiers used the 5,000-signature redistricting exception. None is treated as conclusive proof that every normal requirement was actually satisfied or always reasonable.

The rejected December 1995 submissions sought access to the 1996 primary. Their apparent insufficiency even for established-party candidates is not an independently adjudicated standing ground; the continuing party access claim remains reviewable. The completed primary does not moot that recurring controversy. The record does not establish a genuine dispute requiring a trial on an impossible combined signature, slate and deadline burden. This conclusion does not forbid a supported future challenge. The normal alternative general-ballot route matters; no constitutional entitlement to an automatic publicly administered party primary is assumed.

The claim is First/Fourteenth Amendment association under Anderson, Burdick, Norman and Timmons. The lower court's footnote 9 says a separate equal-protection claim was not pleaded; do not invent one. Article I's Qualifications Clause separately distinguishes substantive personal eligibility rules from genuine ballot procedures requiring public support. Thornton's exclusivity holding remains binding with its actual six-Justice support, not a fictional unanimous join by its dissenters. No existing otherwise qualified component is disqualified merely because a different component fails, as occurred in Norman. No primary rerun, automatic established status, replacement threshold or new statewide qualification is ordered by the affirmance path.

## A. Case, record, and posture

- **Case:** *Jordahl v. Democratic Party of Virginia*, 122 F.3d 192 (4th Cir. 1997).
- **Supreme Court petition:** No. 97-859. **Term:** October Term 1997. **Simulation entry:** petition for certiorari, proposed for grant. The simulation's merits date has not been fixed.
- **Question presented, reconstructed from the appellate opinion:** Whether federal district courts can entertain a § 1983 suit asserting that a political party's resort to state-court injunctions unconstitutionally suppressed nonendorsing voter guides, and whether a live pre-enforcement claim survives changes to the state election-disclosure statute.
- **Judgment below:** Fourth Circuit affirmed dismissal of the federal claims for want of jurisdiction or justiciability and the decision not to retain supplemental state claims. It did not adjudicate the asserted First Amendment violation.
- **Record:** The Virginia Leadership Council, Vern Jordahl, and Mary-Beth LaRock distributed or planned nonendorsing voter guides in 1989 and 1993. The Democratic Party of Virginia obtained three state-court orders restricting distribution unless disclosure or registration requirements were met. The orders expired or were dissolved. Virginia repealed the original Fair Elections Practices Act in 1993 and amended its replacement in 1996. In federal court, Count One attacked the claimed system of prior restraint; other § 1983 counts sought relief for injuries attributed to the three injunctions. Additional state-law abuse-of-process, malicious-prosecution, and trespass claims remained.
- **Relief sought:** Damages, declarations, and injunctive relief on federal claims, with related state-law relief.
- **Threshold:** Article III live controversy and ripeness, *Rooker v. Fidelity Trust Co.* / *District of Columbia Court of Appeals v. Feldman* limits on collateral federal review of state judgments, private-party state action under 42 U.S.C. § 1983, supplemental jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1367.
- **Procedural anchors:** Fourth Circuit decided July 28, 1997; the challenged state orders had ended by the time of federal review.
- **Participation:** No recusal or other participation fact established in the consulted record.

## B. Law entering the case

The controlling rules are the First and Fourteenth Amendments, Article III, the civil-rights statutes, and the Court's decisions in force when the petition is considered. In the existing Court record, *McIntyre v. Ohio Elections Commission* (1995) protects an individual's truthful, independently produced political leaflets against categorical identification requirements, and *Johnson v. De Grandy* (1994) preserves the distinction between an independent federal claim and an impermissible appeal of a state judgment.

### Issue 1: Live challenge to election speech restrictions

- **Governing law:** U.S. Const. art. III; *Renne v. Geary*, 501 U.S. 312 (1991), on uncertain future enforcement; *Virginia v. American Booksellers Ass'n*, 484 U.S. 383 (1988), on credible pre-enforcement threats; *McIntyre v. Ohio Elections Commission* (1995), on core political leaflets and exacting review of compelled identification. The Court's pre-enforcement decision in *Pic-A-State Pennsylvania, Inc. v. Reno* (April 28, 1997) likewise requires a concrete present constraint or realistic enforcement threat, not an already completed controversy.
- **Open point:** Whether the claimed continuing threat under the amended Virginia law was concrete enough to make Count One facial or pre-enforcement litigation justiciable, without equating a former injunction with future enforcement.

### Issue 2: Review of state-court injunctions and damages

- **Governing law:** *Rooker*, 263 U.S. 413 (1923), and *Feldman*, 460 U.S. 462 (1983), reserve appellate review of state judgments to the proper appellate channel. The Court's *Johnson v. De Grandy* (1994) decision permits a genuinely independent federal enforcement claim without implying unrestricted collateral review. *Lugar v. Edmondson Oil Co.*, 457 U.S. 922 (1982), governs when joint use of state process becomes attributable state action under § 1983; ordinary private litigation alone does not establish that element. Section 1367 governs supplemental claims.
- **Open point:** Whether allegations of independently wrongful resort to judicial process can be resolved without determining the injunctions legally erroneous, and whether the political party can satisfy the separate state-action element.

## C. Decision-material litigation positions

- **Below:** Count One was moot as applied to repealed or amended statutes and premature as to the new law; the damages counts were effectively efforts to reverse state injunctions through federal district court. The court declined supplemental state-law jurisdiction.
- **Petitioners' strongest case:** Voter guides were core political speech, earlier injunctions interrupted distribution at election time, the ongoing threat was credible, and damage claims against those obtaining process should not be treated as appeals from judgments that no longer operated. An independent facial challenge may be different from a request to invalidate a particular order.
- **Respondents' strongest case:** No present application or concrete enforcement threat under materially amended statutes; the damages theory depended on declaring each state injunction unlawful; the party's use of ordinary litigation may not itself constitute state action. State-law tort issues belong in state court after federal dismissal.
- **Concession/limit:** Political speech's importance does not itself establish jurisdiction, state action, or a viable defendant.

## D. Lawful paths and uncertainty

1. Affirm for no live Count One controversy and lack of district-court appellate authority for judgment-dependent counts, without discussing speech merits.
2. Vacate in part where a federal damages claim identifies coercive misuse of state process to silence independently protected voter-guide speech, separable from a request to overturn the state injunctions; remand for the pleading, attribution and injury inquiries.
3. If federal claims remain dismissed, leave related state claims to the authorized forum. The pleadings' separation of independent injuries from judgment-caused injuries is material.

## A. Case, record, and posture

- **Case:** *Libertarian Party of Florida v. Smith*, 687 So. 2d 1292 (Fla. 1996).
- **Supreme Court petition:** No. 96-1867. **Term:** October Term 1997. **Simulation:** petition proposed for grant; merits date unassigned.
- **Question presented, reconstructed:** Whether Florida may rebate part of candidates' filing fees to executive committees of political parties with at least five percent of the State's registered voters, while denying the rebate to smaller recognized parties, consistently with First and Fourteenth Amendment political association and equal protection.
- **Judgment below:** Florida Supreme Court approved the First District Court of Appeal decision sustaining Fla. Stat. § 99.103(1) (1993).
- **Record:** Libertarian Party, its executive committee, and a House candidate sought declaratory and injunctive relief. The statute returned a portion of filing fees paid by candidates of major parties to those parties for expenses. Parties under the five-percent voter-registration threshold received no rebate. The challengers conceded the initial candidate fees were lawfully collected. A Libertarian candidate had satisfied the separate requirements to appear on the ballot.
- **Relief sought:** Declaration that the rebate distinction is unconstitutional and an injunction against its discriminatory operation.
- **Threshold:** Federal question is reviewable from a state court of last resort. Independent state free-association claims were considered coextensive in this case; distinguish entitlement to government funds from access to the ballot.
- **Dates:** Florida Supreme Court decision December 5, 1996; rehearing denied February 17, 1997.
- **Participation:** None established.

## B. Law entering the case

The controlling First and Fourteenth Amendment inquiry includes *Anderson*, the Court's *Burdick v. Takushi* (1992) decision measuring actual burdens against precise state interests, and *Norman v. Reed* (1992), which protects otherwise qualified minor-party candidates against unjustified cross-component disqualification. Neither decision requires states to give every political organization identical financial support.

### Issue 1: Equal treatment in election financing

- **Governing law:** First and Fourteenth Amendments; *Anderson v. Celebrezze*, 460 U.S. 780 (1983), and *Burdick v. Takushi*, 504 U.S. 428 (1992), use burden-sensitive scrutiny for voting and election administration. *Buckley v. Valeo*, 424 U.S. 1 (1976), permits public election financing schemes with objective demonstrated-support distinctions, subject to constitutional restrictions.
- **Other law:** Florida statute distinguished qualifying parties based on registered-voter share, rather than candidates' individual ballot access.
- **Open point:** Whether using major-party registration to distribute a percentage of qualified candidates' compulsory filing fees is legitimate electoral finance or state-maintained institutional advantage after the ballot-access gate has already been passed. *Buckley*'s approval of specific demonstrated-support public financing does not answer that different mechanism automatically.

## C. Decision-material litigation positions

- **Below:** The Florida court treated the rebate as financial support, not a ballot-access prerequisite, and found a sufficiently reasonable relationship to fostering established parties and preventing factionalism.
- **Petitioners:** A tax- or fee-funded political benefit to two dominant parties converts an already-qualified smaller party's candidates' fees into an institutional advantage denied to that smaller party. Ballot qualification has already established a nonfrivolous candidacy.
- **Respondents:** Subsidizing stable parties through a neutral registration threshold is permissible, no candidate is removed from a ballot, and the First Amendment supplies no right to equal public assistance for every party.
- **Limit:** A neutral threshold need not distribute money equally; a State may not use an ostensibly minor subsidy as a disguised ballot exclusion or viewpoint sanction.

## D. Lawful paths and uncertainty

1. Affirm: rebate is a limited public financing allocation, not a fee on voting or ballot eligibility, with no record of severe burden.
2. Reverse the categorical rebate preference if it treats already-qualified minor-party participation as an insufficiently valuable form of political association and serves no support-related interest other than maintaining major parties' fiscal advantages.
3. Remand for impact findings if the comparative value of rebates is indispensable. The record quoted by the state high court does not quantify a party-specific constitutional injury sufficient for such findings to be assumed.

- Fla. Stat. §§ 99.103(1), 97.021(15) (versions described in opinion); *Buckley*, 424 U.S. 1; *Anderson*, 460 U.S. 780; *Burdick*, 504 U.S. 428.

- **Case:** *Libertarian Party of Illinois v. Rednour*, 108 F.3d 768 (7th Cir. 1997).
- **Supreme Court petition:** No. 97-25. **Term:** October Term 1997. **Simulation entry:** petition proposed for grant; merits calendar not set.
- **Question presented, reconstructed:** Whether Illinois constitutionally may require a new party to obtain a congressional-district petition with signatures of up to five percent of the prior electorate to nominate a general-election candidate, although candidates of an established party use a substantially lower primary-election signature threshold.
- **Judgment below:** Seventh Circuit affirmed summary judgment for state election officials; rejected the First and Fourteenth Amendment challenge.
- **Record:** Illinois deemed a party established statewide for all races based on sufficient gubernatorial votes; votes in other statewide contests could establish the party only for statewide races. For a congressional district where the Libertarian Party had not demonstrated the required local support, a new-party petition was necessary for the general-election ballot. Established-party candidates petitioned for the primary at 0.5 percent of their party's qualified primary electors; new-party slates petitioned for the general election using the lesser of five percent of district voters in the last election or the statewide new-party cap. For the 1996 primary, the opinion reported average Democratic congressional primary petition requirements of 658 signatures and Republican requirements of 532. The different procedures were challenged by Libertarian candidates and supporting voters.
- **Relief:** Declaration and injunction allowing Libertarian congressional candidates into the legally authorized nomination and election process.
- **Threshold:** Equitable election timing and Article III continuity after the election; distinction between access to a primary and access to the general ballot; no voter-qualification power over federal office.
- **Date:** Seventh Circuit decided March 7, 1997.

## B. Law entering the case

The controlling Court decisions include *Norman v. Reed* (1992), protecting authorized party growth and independently qualified candidates while permitting support requirements for separate electorates; *Burdick v. Takushi* (1992), weighing the actual cumulative burden against the State's precise interests; and *Timmons v. Twin Cities Area New Party* (April 28, 1997), permitting the specific anti-fusion nomination restriction only after examining available candidacy and advocacy routes and rejecting incumbent protection as an independent justification.

### Issue 1: Petition thresholds and new-party ballot access

- **Governing law:** *Jenness v. Fortson*, 403 U.S. 431 (1971), upheld a five-percent nominating petition in its operating context; *Norman v. Reed*, 502 U.S. 279 (1992), permitted local support but forbade disqualifying independently qualified slate components; *Anderson v. Celebrezze*, 460 U.S. 780 (1983), and *Burdick v. Takushi*, 504 U.S. 428 (1992), calibrate scrutiny to actual electoral burden; *Munro v. Socialist Workers Party*, 479 U.S. 189 (1986), allowed preventive administration; *Timmons v. Twin Cities Area New Party* (1997) sustained only the particular anti-fusion restriction in its overall system, with no incumbent-protection rationale.
- **Open point:** Whether the combined complete-slate, petition-count, district-by-district, and nomination-route requirements are severe as operated, despite earlier approval of comparable signature percentages.

### Issue 2: Equal protection and congressional ballot structure

- **Governing law:** Federal election law permits States to organize ballot access but not add substantive qualifications for congressional office under *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton*, 514 U.S. 779 (1995). Party-primary and new-party general-election qualification are not automatically equivalent procedures.
- **Open point:** Whether the State uses a legitimate local-support measure or artificially declares a successful statewide party new in every district to suppress competition.

## C. Decision-material litigation positions

- **Below:** New parties can nominate by convention and access general ballots without primary participation; two minor-party congressional candidates qualified in 1994, undermining the claim of an impossible pathway. District-specific support is a legitimate state interest.
- **Petitioners:** Tenfold petition disparities and the failure to translate statewide support into congressional party status impose needless barriers on voters with nonmajor-party preferences.
- **Respondents:** The denominators and ballot stages differ, and the State reasonably demands support where the particular office is contested. Earlier Supreme Court holdings allow five-percent measures and district-specific verification.
- **Limit:** Compliance cannot be defended by a numerical label alone if practical timing or geography renders the alternative inaccessible.

## D. Lawful paths and uncertainty

1. Affirm under *Jenness*, *Norman*, and *Anderson-Burdick*, if the full route leaves realistic general-election access.
2. Reverse the challenged unequal congressional nomination structure if its linked recognition, compulsory-slate and petition routes impose a disproportionately exclusionary burden without proof of the necessity of those additional features. Proof that an alternative party once qualified does not eliminate a serious unequal burden.
3. Remand only if a necessary cumulative-burden fact is genuinely disputed; summary-judgment posture prevents inventing such a dispute.
