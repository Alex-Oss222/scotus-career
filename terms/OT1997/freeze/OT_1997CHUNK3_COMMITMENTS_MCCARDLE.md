## Governing propositions and supported alternative paths

**Principal path — enforce the actually omitted ground.** The 1997 Rule 50(a)(2) requires the motion to specify the judgment sought and supporting law and facts. Rule 50(b) permits renewal of the legal questions raised in a motion made at the close of all evidence, within ten days after entry of judgment; it supplies no general entitlement to add a new ground after the verdict. The answer expressly pleaded immunity. The relevant defect is the later failure to present search immunity in the trial motion, not failure to plead, a requirement to use magic words, a supposed discovery default, or a new categorical jury-instruction prerequisite.

The published quotation is claim-specific: counsel requested judgment “on the issue of illegal stop” on the merits “or on the issue of qualified immunity.” Counsel separately argued the search lawful but did not invoke its immunity ground. The legal distinction matters: reasonable conduct under the Fourth Amendment and protection for a reasonable legal mistake are not interchangeable propositions. The motion's title, “judgment of acquittal,” is immaterial; its substance controls. The court and opposing party need notice of the actual ground before submission, when it can be addressed in the ordinary trial process.

**Strongest alternative — functional presentation or an established exception.** Rule 50 should not defeat a ground that the full motion actually conveyed merely because counsel omitted a label. A legal defense may overlap with the merits, and the pleaded immunity defense supplies relevant context. A properly supported exceptional-injustice claim could also require consideration under the lawful exception applicable to that record. Those possibilities are not categorically barred. But the actual separation of stop immunity from search legality, with no other timely claim-specific invocation identified in the published record, defeats functional preservation here. The importance of immunity does not supply notice of an unasserted ground. No supported injustice requires overlooking the omission. This conclusion rests on affirmative reported motion history, not on the operator's lack of original papers.

**Why the exception analysis does not decide immunity.** Michigan v. Long, 463 U.S. 1032, 1049–53 and notes 14 and 16 (1983), permits a protective vehicle search on specific articulable grounds reasonably supporting danger and possible access to weapons, within a protective scope; it does not permit a search during every valid stop. On the plaintiff-favorable search record, Haddad denied a search, arrest, custody of the car or a weapons/safety purpose, and the panel found no supported probable-cause, inventory, consent, arrest or protective-search justification. The separate stop verdict does not supply those omitted facts. These circumstances defeat the claim that failure to reach immunity obviously inflicts manifest injustice. They do not entail an independent holding that every officer in those circumstances necessarily lacked qualified immunity. A subjective denial of fear alone is not made the constitutional test; the complete supported objective circumstances matter. Later Smith, Clanton, Crawford-El and Lanier explain present review but cannot establish notice retrospectively for March 24, 1992.

**Exact entering-law limits.** Elder's requirement to consider all relevant precedent concerns a preserved immunity issue; it imports neither new claims nor facts. Harlow and Anderson retain objective, particularized conduct-date protection. Crawford-El preserves ordinary procedure and independent defenses while rejecting special motive proof barriers; it does not excuse Rule 50 or add motive to this search claim. Fletcher distinguishes liability from a personal defense without making every pleaded defense perpetually available. Johnson and Behrens distinguish legal questions from factual selection in authorized interlocutory appeals; neither enlarges a renewed posttrial motion. No mandatory merits-first sequence follows from any of them.

**Fee boundary under the different Farrar law.** The actual governing Farrar recognizes nominal prevailing-party eligibility and an explained Hensley assessment of actual success and unrelated unsuccessful work; zero remains possible, mechanical proportionality is impermissible, and substantial fees are not presumed. It does not categorically prohibit alternative-ground affirmance. The panel's reliance on historical Farrar therefore is not adopted. That issue cannot expand the authorized immunity review on its own. If a clean later scope determination adds fee review, the fee component must be independently modeled and frozen on that expanded question before assembly; this freeze supplies no contingent fee vote or entitlement to $5,330.
