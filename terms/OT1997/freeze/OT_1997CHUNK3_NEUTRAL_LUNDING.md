## Actual taxpayers and assessment

- Christopher and Barbara Lunding lived in Connecticut in 1990. Christopher earned substantial income practicing law in New York and paid alimony arising from a previous marriage. The former spouse also lived in Connecticut, and the divorce decree was issued by a Connecticut court. Support: 522 U.S. at 293, 317, 326. Residence and decree origin are extracted only as record facts, without the judicial evaluations appearing alongside them.
- Their 1990 New York nonresident return deducted a pro rata portion of alimony in computing New York-source income, based on their determination that approximately 48% of Christopher's business income was attributable to New York. The Audit Division disallowed that deduction under § 631(b)(6), producing $3,724 additional New York income tax plus interest. The return's complete income and alimony amounts are not reproduced in the source recitation relied on here. Support: 522 U.S. at 293.
- Connecticut did not impose income tax on the couple's earned income in the tax year at issue. This is the narrower earned-income fact reported at 522 U.S. 314, citing the petitioners' reply brief at 4 n.1; it is not a finding that Connecticut had no taxes of any kind.

## Complete challenged provision and tax computation

N.Y. Tax Law § 631(b)(6), as applicable to the challenge, provided:

> The deduction allowed by section two hundred fifteen of the internal revenue code, relating to alimony, shall not constitute a deduction derived from New York sources.

Support: 522 U.S. at 292 n.1. This is the entire challenged paragraph, not a quotation of all § 631.

The statutory computation described at 522 U.S. 291–292 operates as follows:

1. Sections 631(a) and (b) tax nonresidents' net income from New York real property or tangible personal property and from employment, business, trade, or professional operations within New York.
2. Under § 601(e)(1), first calculate the taxpayer's hypothetical liability as though a New York resident. Federal adjusted gross income is the starting point; it includes the § 215 deduction for qualifying alimony. After New York adjustments, apply resident rates to the resulting taxable income.
3. Compute the apportionment percentage: New York-source income divided by federal adjusted gross income. The denominator includes the alimony deduction through federal law and § 612(a). The numerator excludes an alimony deduction by § 631(b)(6).
4. Multiply the hypothetical resident tax by that percentage to obtain actual New York tax. The applicable procedure did not cap the percentage at 100%. The 1990 Form IT-203 instructions expressly contemplated a percentage above 100% if the New York amount exceeded the federal amount. Support: 522 U.S. at 291–292 & n.2. The original return instructions were not separately read; their cited text is reproduced in the official source.

Federal law at the relevant time included alimony in the recipient's gross income under 26 U.S.C. § 71(a), with a corresponding payer deduction under §§ 215(a) and 62(a)(10) for qualifying payments taxable to the recipient. New York used that federal structure for resident taxpayers. A payer's New York residency, rather than the recipient's residency, controlled availability of the challenged payer deduction; New York residents receiving alimony included it in taxable income. Support: 522 U.S. at 291, 312–313, 316–317. The qualification of these particular payments as alimony is not reported as disputed; this receipt does not reproduce the entire federal alimony-definition statute or hypothesize a disqualification.

Section 631(b)(6) was enacted in the Tax Reform and Reduction Act of 1987. Previously, the Court of Appeals had construed the applicable New York statutes to permit nonresidents a proportionate alimony deduction: *Friedsam v. State Tax Commission*, 64 N.Y.2d 76, 473 N.E.2d 1181 (1984). The provision removed that statutory allowance. Older statutory arrangements had differed: the reported 1944/1948 provisions linked a nonresident's deduction to the recipient's liability for New York tax, and the 1961 arrangement generally allowed proportionate itemized deductions. Support: 522 U.S. at 292–293, 304–305, 309 n.4. These historical provisions are background, not the tax law governing the challenged assessment.

## Administrative and judicial proceedings below

The couple contested the assessment administratively, asserting Article IV Privileges and Immunities, Equal Protection, and Commerce Clause claims. The administrative appeals failed without adjudicating the constitutional claims. They then sought review in the Appellate Division under N.Y. Tax Law § 2016. Support: 522 U.S. at 293.

The Appellate Division invalidated § 631(b)(6) under Privileges and Immunities, concluding that the differential treatment lacked a substantial reason beyond nonresidence and relying on its prior *Friedsam* analysis: 218 A.D.2d 268, 639 N.Y.S.2d 519 (3 d Dep't 1996). Support: 522 U.S. at 293–294.

The New York Court of Appeals reversed and sustained the challenged provision: 89 N.Y.2d 283, 675 N.E.2d 816 (1996). Its actual grounds, as quoted and described in the official report, were:

- A State's limited taxation of nonresidents' in-state income could justify limiting corresponding deductions to expenses producing that income; the court invoked *Shaffer* and *Travis* and assessed practical operation.
- Resident taxation of worldwide income supported full deductions, whereas personal expenses of nonresidents were appropriately allocated to their State of residence. The court relied on *Goodwin*, involving disallowed deductions for a New Jersey resident's New Jersey property taxes, mortgage interest, medical expenses, and insurance premiums.
- *Golden v. Tully* was distinguished as a case in which only nonresidence had been offered as justification; *Friedsam* was distinguished as a statutory construction superseded by § 631(b)(6).
- The court considered that nonresidents still received a benefit from subtracting alimony when calculating the hypothetical resident tax. It found sufficient reasons on the face of the tax scheme despite the absence of legislative history explaining the paragraph.
- It also rejected the Equal Protection and Commerce Clause claims. Those two claims were not included in the reported Supreme Court merits issue; the reviewed issue was Article IV Privileges and Immunities.

Support: 522 U.S. at 294–296, with specific quotations and citations to 89 N.Y.2d 288–291. These are the lower court's grounds, not conclusions adopted by this receipt.

## Issue-specific law entering the event

**Text and governing constitutional inquiry:** Article IV,§ 2, cl.1: “The Citizens of each State shall be entitled to all Privileges and Immunities of Citizens in the several States.” Under Toomer,334 U.S.385,395–399 (1948), doing business in another State receives substantial equality; the Clause bars discrimination without a substantial reason beyond noncitizenship, and the degree of discrimination must bear a close relation to the independent reason. States retain considerable leeway addressing local problems, but an asserted legitimate objective does not validate every discriminatory means. The inquiry asks about the actual reason and relationship, not ordinary rational-basis review alone. A residence classification covering all nonresidents includes other-state citizens; the label does not evade the Clause.

**Source-based income and deductions:** Shaffer,252 U.S.37,52–57 (1920), permits nondiscriminatory taxation of a nonresident's local property/business/occupation income, with a ratable contribution rather than immunity or preferential treatment. Its local-income base permits corresponding limits on losses incurred elsewhere; the opinion also records that the plaintiff showed no such losses and could not independently raise that particular objection. Travis,252 U.S.60,75–82 (1920), expressly applies the source-related deduction principle but invalidates unequal personal exemptions without an adequate corresponding justification. Thus neither “personal deduction” nor “nonlocal expense” is a complete constitutional answer independent of what the deduction does and how this tax operates. The proper characterization of alimony under that distinction is a live legal issue, not a source fact already settled by either precedent.

**Substantial equality and offsets:** Travis evaluates the concrete tax incidence, refusing to presume unrelated untaxed income offsets the denied exemption for nonresidents as a class. Austin,420 U.S.656,662–668 (1975), preserves substantial equality, examines the practical distribution of burdens, and rejects reliance on another State's tax credit to cure the taxing State's discrimination. A reasonably fair equivalent burden may matter, but general labels or unsupported assertions do not establish it. The constitutionality of one State's tax cannot depend on another State's present statute, although mutually favorable coordination is not forbidden. Connecticut's actual absence of an earned-income tax in 1990 remains a case fact; it does not supply a universal rule about other residents or States.

**Simulated Commerce Clause authorities:** Fulton(February 21, 1996), Thomas for all nine, treats an issuer-based deduction as discrimination in its actual interstate operation and requires an identified local burden, roughly equivalent/nonexcessive charge, and substantially equivalent taxable events for a compensatory-tax defense. Oregon Waste(April 4, 1994), Thomas's eight-Justice opinion, supplies the express-origin discrimination and compensatory predicates; unrelated general income taxation does not compensate a disposal surcharge. These decisions are relevant analogies against conclusory cross-tax offsets and formal deduction labels, but their Commerce Clause burdens and distinct field are not automatically substituted for Article IV's substantial-reason/close-relationship inquiry. Fulton overruled only Darnell's contrary Commerce Clause permission; its other determinations survive. No independent Commerce Clause judgment is added to this review.

**Relief:** Reich(December 6, 1994), unanimous O'Connor opinion, forbids withdrawal after payment of a clearly held-out postpayment remedy, while permitting ordinary proof/amount conditions and adequately announced prospective procedures. Fulton preserves lawful state severability and constitutionally adequate correction: McKesson's backward-looking requirement is triggered by payment compelled before a meaningful opportunity to challenge; lawful refunds, nondiscriminatory retroactive equalization, or a combination may be available. Reich's separate clear-remedy protection can apply even after voluntary payment. No record here establishes withdrawal of a remedy; these rules constrain a remedy if their predicates occur, without converting assessment into proof of payment or choosing a mandatory refund.

**Formula and burden precision:** The hypothetical-resident tax already recognizes qualifying alimony. Section 631(b)(6) separately excludes the deduction from the source numerator, while adjusted gross income in the denominator reflects it. That operation must be assessed as a whole; the hypothetical-resident benefit neither proves complete equality nor proves a constitutionally material excess by itself. The actual challenged additional assessment is$3,724 plus interest. No percentage ceiling may be invented: the reported 1990 instructions allowed ratios above 100%. ## Strongest supported positions and lawful alternatives

The following are analytical formulations grounded in the supplied dispute, not new party quotations or concessions.

- **Sustain the provision under Article IV:** Residents bear New York tax on worldwide income, while these nonresidents owe tax only on New York sources. Shaffer/Travis permit corresponding source limits, and these alimony payments arose from a Connecticut divorce between Connecticut residents rather than an identified expense producing New York income. The hypothetical-resident calculation also preserves part of the federal deduction's effect. The strongest objection is that a personal deduction can reduce ability to pay across all earnings, as a personal exemption does, without being geographically located or dependent on production of income; mere residence cannot be repackaged as the required substantial independent reason. The actual formula and relation of the denial to New York's asserted tax allocation must do the legal work.
- **Find the categorical denial insufficiently justified:** Travis/Austin protect substantial equality in local earnings taxation and require more than broad assertions of unrelated resident burdens. The legislature's payer-residence line and the differing roles of numerator, denominator, and hypothetical tax must be tested against the asserted source allocation and family-income rationale. The strongest State response is that those cases involved inadequately justified exemption or exclusive-tax differences, while this statute limits a personal payment associated with an out-of-state marital relationship and a narrower tax base. A contrary conclusion must explain why those source differences fail to justify this denial rather than impose universal identical deductions.
- **Bounded remand and lawful correction:** If the stated justifications fail as a matter of law, reverse/vacate the sustaining judgment and leave state-law severability, actual assessment correction, payment/refund predicates, and appropriate relief to the proper state proceedings. The taxpayers requested a proportionate deduction; that request does not require this Court to legislate a formula or preclude every lawful alternative. If decisive facts or a constitutionally adequate justification are truly unresolved, an appropriate remand must identify the legal defect and real remaining issue, without ordering evidence just to remedy missing research originals. A supported affirmance need not decide distinct constitutional claims outside the question.
