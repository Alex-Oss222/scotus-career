## Actual remedies and state-law limits

The reported N.J.S.A. 2A:31-1 permits recovery when the death of a person results from a wrongful act, neglect or default that would have supported that person's damages action had death not occurred. Wrongful death compensates survivors' pecuniary loss, including the pecuniary value of qualifying support, services and advice. Emotional loss alone is not that recovery. Section 2A:31-5 additionally permits specified medical, hospital and funeral expenses.

The quoted N.J.S.A. 2A:15-3 permits executors and administrators to sue for trespass to the deceased's person or property and recover the deceased's own damages if living; its second paragraph permits reasonable funeral and burial expenses in qualifying death cases. Survival damages concern the deceased's predeath injury, including conscious pain and suffering and the relevant state's loss-of-enjoyment rules. Wrongful death and survival have different beneficiaries and measures; a reason sufficient for one must not automatically be treated as sufficient for the other.

*Giardina v. Bennett*, 111 N.J. 412, 545 A.2d 139 (1988), authoritatively excludes stillbirth from New Jersey wrongful death and recognizes the parents' own tort injuries from malpractice causing stillbirth. **Giardina did not decide survival.** The District Court inferred a parallel survival restriction and neither party disputed that construction on appeal; the circuit expressly assumed it. That is the reported litigation premise, not a newly discovered state-high-court holding. The Court can decide the federal challenge on that premise while identifying its status; it cannot announce an authoritative new New Jersey construction.

The parents' remedy is limited. As footnote 17 quotes *Carey v. Lovett*, 132 N.J. 44, 622 A.2d 1279, 1288 (1993), emotional distress must produce physical manifestations or destroy basic emotional security; ordinary worry, stress or upset is insufficient. The availability of that cause of action is not equivalent to an award or full compensation for every loss. Footnote 15 expressly disclaims holding that the Fourteenth Amendment requires a prenatal-injury tort remedy. Conversely, the absence of a general duty to create a remedy does not itself answer whether an existing remedial classification is constitutional.
