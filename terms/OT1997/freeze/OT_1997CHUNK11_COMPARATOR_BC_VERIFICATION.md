## Caron v. United States — 1998-06-22

Historical result: affirmed, 6–3. Kennedy wrote for Rehnquist, Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Thomas dissented, joined by Scalia and Souter. No other writing or nonparticipation.

The Court treated restoration as a conviction-status inquiry and the federal unless clause as all-or-nothing. Restoration by operation of law sufficed, but Massachusetts's retained handgun restriction activated the federal consequence for all firearms. Its lenity analysis rejected a merely grammatically possible reading inconsistent with the statutory purpose. The dissent read the express prohibition language to require more than a restriction on carrying handguns outside home/business; it relied independently on lenity and Congress's reliance on state law. Neither writing rests on a complete state handgun-possession ban.

## Gebser v. Lago Vista Independent School District — 1998-06-22

Historical result: affirmed, 5–4. O'Connor wrote for Rehnquist, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas. Stevens dissented with Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Ginsburg separately dissented with Souter and Breyer. No Scalia separate writing.

The Court required actual knowledge by an official with corrective authority and deliberate indifference for private damages in cases not involving recipient official policy. It distinguished administrative enforcement and rejected automatic attribution based on ordinary agency or constructive notice. The parents' complaint concerned classroom comments; the Court did not equate it with notice of a sexual relationship. Regulatory noncompliance alone did not establish discrimination or the required notice and indifference.

Stevens relied on Cannon, Franklin, congressional validation of the private remedy, and agency principles where delegated teacher authority enabled abuse. He rejected judicial reduction of an established remedy and the analogy between administrative fund termination and private damages. He reserved possible preventive-policy defenses. Ginsburg accepted that dissent but proposed an affirmative defense: the district must prove adequately publicized internal remedies likely to provide redress without undue risk, effort, or expense; unreasonable failure to use those measures would bar relief only for consequently avoidable harm. This is not a rule that any written policy automatically defeats liability.

Sources: [syllabus](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/96-1866.ZS.html), [Court](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/96-1866.ZO.html), [Stevens](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/96-1866.ZD.html), [Ginsburg](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/96-1866.ZD1.html), [U.S. Reports](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-524/pdf/USREPORTS-524-274.pdf). ## United States v. Bajakajian — 1998-06-22

Historical result: affirmed, 5–4. Thomas wrote for Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Kennedy dissented with Rehnquist, O'Connor, and Scalia. No other writing.

The Court held this criminal forfeiture punitive and applied gross disproportionality. Its instrumentality discussion does not exempt all modern in-rem forfeitures: punishment in part suffices for coverage, as notes 4 and 6 expressly state. Proportionality receives de novo appellate review; underlying facts receive clear-error review. Note 11 reserves the validity of the $15,000 forfeiture and judicial authority to substitute it; absence of cross-appeal prevents treating this as approval of $15,000 or a $15,001 ceiling. Note 14 distinguishes the $250,000/five-year statutory maxima from the $5,000/six-month applicable Guidelines range; note 15 reserves wealth/livelihood issues not argued or found.

Kennedy accepted gross disproportionality and legislative deference as the proper standards but disputed their application. He treated the reporting offense as independently serious, emphasized the statutory maximum and mandatory forfeiture guideline, disputed the majority's division between remedial and punitive historical forfeitures, and warned against excluding in-rem penalties from scrutiny. His reading that affirmance necessarily endorsed $15,000 is an objection explicitly rejected by the majority, not a description of its holding.

## Pennsylvania Board of Probation and Parole v. Scott — 1998-06-22

Historical result: reversed and remanded, 5–4. Thomas wrote for Rehnquist, O'Connor, Scalia, and Kennedy. Stevens dissented separately. Souter dissented with Ginsburg and Breyer. Stevens agreed with Souter's deterrence explanation but did not formally join his opinion.

The Court refused a federal exclusionary remedy in parole revocation, including parole-officer searches. It relied on revocation's costs and administrative character, criminal-trial exclusion, and its view of parole supervision. It expressly did not decide search constitutionality or resolve the state/federal source of the consent ruling; note 3 records the invited consent/reasonable-suspicion question and jurisdictional dispute.

Stevens added that exclusion is constitutionally required to make Fourth Amendment prohibitions effective. Souter used the deterrence framework but rejected the asserted marginal benefit: revocation often replaces prosecution, police and parole officers cooperate, and parole officers can act as investigators/prosecutors. He disputed unsupported reliance on alternative discipline and damages. He would affirm, treating the consent ruling as unchallenged and applying reasonable suspicion to the search; he did not decide the general validity of suspicionless-search waiver conditions. Distinguish this reasoning from the Court's express reservation.

## Wisconsin Department of Corrections v. Schacht — 1998-06-22

Historical result: vacated and remanded, unanimous. Breyer wrote for all nine; Kennedy also concurred.

The Court distinguished a waivable claim-specific sovereign-immunity defense from incomplete diversity, and read §1447(c) as not requiring remand of an entire case merely because one claim is barred. It did not decide whether Eleventh Amendment immunity is subject-matter jurisdiction. The actual complaint alleged federal claims; supplemental state-law claims were an analogy, not an additional actual disposition. Schacht did not appeal dismissal of the official-capacity claims.

Kennedy expressly joined the Court and reserved removal waiver because it was not briefed or decided below. He considered voluntary invocation of federal jurisdiction, unfair tactical late assertion, the analogy to personal jurisdiction, attorney authority, and unequivocal consent. These are proposed future changes or questions; he did not adopt removal waiver or change existing late-assertion doctrine in this case.

## Swidler & Berlin v. United States — 1998-06-25

Historical result: reversed, 6–3. Rehnquist wrote for Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer. O'Connor dissented with Scalia and Thomas.

The Court applied Rule 501, the established privilege, and predictable confidentiality to reject an investigative-importance balancing exception after death. The testamentary exception promotes the client's intent and is not a general license to balance later investigative need. Note 1 leaves work product undecided. Note 3 leaves exceptional circumstances implicating a criminal defendant's constitutional rights undecided; it does not establish absolute survival against every constitutional claim.

O'Connor agreed with ordinary posthumous survival, but would permit a narrow override for necessary information unavailable elsewhere when exculpation or compelling law-enforcement need outweighs confidentiality. She emphasized diminished posthumous risks, truth seeking, and existing exceptions. She would affirm the remand for case-specific balancing. Separately, she rejected denial of work product merely because notes arose in an initial interview, but thought the doctrine inapplicable where the client was no longer a potential party to adversarial litigation. That distinct ground must not be omitted if describing the full dissent.
