**Question and scope:** Whether looking through the five-to-six-inch gap under window blinds from this common side yard was a Fourth Amendment search. The lower opinion also decides personal privacy interests, the later warrantless entry, announcement, and suppression, so those consequences and alternative grounds must be considered as far as necessary to dispose of the reviewed suppression question. ## Full lower record, including route and qualifications

Cardamone wrote for himself and Oakes. Judge Mahoney died after argument; the remaining two judges agreed and decided under their local rule, as the sole reporter footnote explains. That lower-panel participation says nothing about Supreme Court participation. No separate writing appears. The circuit affirmed both conviction judgments. Crawley pleaded guilty to one count with a reservation to appeal denial of suppression; Fields was convicted by a jury of conspiracy and possession with intent to distribute cocaine base. Their distinct conviction routes and remedies must be preserved.

Carlyn Warner rented the ground-floor apartment of a three-family building, lived there with her children, and allowed Fields to use it 40–50 times over about six months. Fields paid $125 weekly from fall 1994, held a key, could enter in Warner's absence, and could bring guests. Neither defendant lived there or kept belongings there. Their primary use was preparing cocaine, with some beer drinking and television. Crawley usually accompanied Fields, paid no rent, held no key, and had stayed overnight once but had no demonstrated overnight plan on the relevant night. Both had Warner's express permission that evening; when they closed the child's bedroom door, she would not disturb them.

At 7:45 p.m. on December 15, 1994, a known informant with a reliable arrest-producing history said Fields and “Chris” were bagging cocaine at the rear of the first floor, visible through the window, and could leave at any time. The informant supplied vehicle details, later corroborated. Police knew Fields as a violent drug-gang leader with prior drug convictions and knew Crawley's association with him. Officers arrived around 8:25 p.m. They could not see into the windows from street or sidewalk. They entered the fenced rear yard and then the fenced side yard, which was accessible to other tenants. The broader account describes a partly enclosing chain-link fence; neither gates, permission to enter, exact measurements, nor exclusive occupant control is established.

The officers saw into the well-lit bedroom through blinds raised five or six inches. They did not stand on tiptoe, lean against the building, or use technology. By 9 p.m., after observation, they had not sought a warrant. The district court found obtaining one would take over an hour. Police planned to have uniformed officers knock at the front while others waited at the rear to intercept a flight with the drugs or obtain entry. As the rear team positioned, a man on the porch called “5-0,” entered the building and closed the door. Officers then believed the alerted suspects might arm themselves or destroy evidence. They used a battering ram on the locked common-building rear door, entered Warner's apartment through its open door while shouting “police,” swept and secured it, and caught defendants trying to flee toward the front bedroom. A loaded weapon under a mattress and cocaine on the back-bedroom table were observed. The weapon's eventual discovery is not contemporaneous justification for earlier entry.

Two officers then obtained a warrant, returning about ninety minutes later. While the apartment was secured, Warner received an explanation of rights and signed consent. The later warrant search produced 453.76 grams of crack cocaine. The complete affidavit, warrant, consent form and suppression transcript are not reproduced. Neither independent-source predicates, consent attenuation, nor the warrant's untainted sufficiency is conclusively established merely by their existence.

The district court initially denied both defendants personal standing. The circuit rejected that reasoning: Fields's paid access and key and Crawley's extended permitted visit with a host willing to share privacy sufficed. It rejected illegality of activity as an automatic defeat of privacy. It nevertheless upheld the observation because neither defendant closed the visible gap and other tenants could use the side yard. The panel viewed that exposure as defeating subjective privacy in the observed activity and distinguished the more intrusive viewing in the then-vacated-for-rehearing Blount panel decision. No independently controlling rule is imported from that procedural status.

The circuit also sustained warrantless entry and lack of advance announcement on the specific danger/destruction circumstances. It referred to serious drug crime, Fields's reputation, probable cause after viewing, presence, possible escape/destruction, and the partly nonpeaceful entry. Its description of entry exigency as wholly clear-error review must be distinguished from the governing simulated Ornelas/Richards allocation. The general statements and postentry weapon reference cannot replace independent examination of the facts known before each intrusion.

The full opinion’s other holdings affirmed nondisclosure after an in camera informant interview with defense-suggested topics, cocaine-base penalties, Fields’s fine based on specific financial evidence, and the jury-selection ruling. Those are contextual completion facts only; they are not grants in this review.

## Governing law and supported alternatives

Read [the selected exact-law slice](../entering-law/OT_1997CHUNK3_FIELDS.md): Pinson, Myers, Wilson, Richards and Ornelas, corresponding Standards and their effective authority, plus surviving pre-divergence personal-privacy, vantage, curtilage, home-entry and remedy authorities. Irrelevant name-matched financial, domestic-relations, preclusion and private-prison-immunity decisions have been removed. No historical later Carter, Jones, Jardines, King or Hudson rule enters the case.

**Personal interest before exposure.** Rakas, Jones and Olson require each defendant's own legitimate privacy interest. Fields's repeated paid control/key provides a substantially stronger premise than mere presence. Crawley's permission, extended visit and shared private room provide a supported claim but Olson's actual overnight holding does not mechanically decide every commercial visit. The court may sustain both interests, distinguish them on legally relevant facts, or decide the window question on an assumed protected interest if that method resolves the merits without evading Article III. “Fourth Amendment standing” here concerns personal substantive protection; it is not a license to adjudicate another person's rights. Criminal activity alone does not defeat an otherwise legitimate interest.

**Window route A — ordinary exposure.** The Government's strongest argument is that a large obvious opening exposed the room to anyone using the tenants' common yard, and officers used unaided vision without manipulating the building. Ciraolo/Riley recognize that actual exposure can defeat otherwise legitimate residential privacy. The objection is that a nonpublic fenced yard occupied through an uninvited investigative approach may differ materially from a public street or ordinarily used airspace; shared tenant use is not necessarily public invitation. The rule must identify why this actual vantage was one from which observation reasonably could be anticipated, rather than equate any opening or potential trespasser with public exposure.

**Window route B — protected observation from an unauthorized vantage.** Fences, a private rear bedroom, closed interior door, blinds, and absence of street visibility support a privacy claim notwithstanding imperfect screening. A reasonable observer might expect tenants or invited users, without accepting deliberate police entry into the fenced rear/side area to peer into the home. The objection is that defendants exercised no established exclusive control of the common yard and left the substantial opening visible to lawful co-users. The property relationship and Dunn factors must be assessed on actual evidence; no unproved curtilage finding, universal common-yard immunity, or modern trespass doctrine may be supplied. Pinson/Myers protect concealed home information but distinguish ordinary viewing through actual openings; neither automatically resolves this vantage.

**Entry and announcement, only if reached.** A sound observation may provide probable cause without itself authorizing home entry. Payton and Hayden require a valid exception; the specific “5-0” alert, known violence and readily disposable evidence support the Government. Defendants can argue police had time and deliberately planned a confrontation, so their conduct and the causal sequence matter. The record supports the plan and failure to seek a warrant, not an invented purpose to evade law or a settled categorical police-created-exigency rule. Richards rejects drug-category automatic exceptions, requires the actual announcement-delay danger/futility/destruction connection, and separates execution from warrantless-entry authority. A lawful or unlawful announcement ruling does not itself decide the prior observation or later suppression.

**Review and remedies remain independent.** Historical facts and credibility receive clear-error protection; Richards’s ultimate announcement sufficiency is independently reviewed. Ornelas does not by name decide every mixed question but prevents treating ultimate law as factual merely by label. If the search premise is corrected, Pinson/Myers favor lawful disposition of the preserved suppression claim without automatic exclusion, trial, release or acquittal. Murray requires genuine independence of a later warrant; original information and issuance are not assumed untainted. Warner’s consent after seizure must satisfy its own lawful scope/voluntariness and any applicable taint requirements. Leon’s actual limits, preserved independent-source or inevitable-discovery arguments, and ordinary forfeiture rules remain available only if supported and procedurally open. Crawley’s reserved plea route under Rule 11(a)(2) is distinct from Fields’s jury conviction; a limited legal correction is not automatic success entitling withdrawal.

## Date-eligible Justice-specific support

Scalia authored Pinson and Myers; Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg joined their concealed-information and warrant analyses, while Kennedy and Breyer rejected the search premise. Their precise subjects are technological acquisition, not every window view. Scalia and Kennedy joined Riley's plurality; O’Connor's separate concurrence insisted on regular public use rather than legality alone; Stevens joined Brennan’s dissent. Stevens and O’Connor had joined Ciraolo's public-airspace result. Those distinctions provide actual support for examining the vantage without predetermined outcomes.

Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia and Kennedy joined Olson; Kennedy separately qualified its exigency application. O’Connor authored Ornelas; Scalia dissented from its review allocation. Scalia authored Murray's independent-source rule, while Stevens and O’Connor joined the contrary dissent.
