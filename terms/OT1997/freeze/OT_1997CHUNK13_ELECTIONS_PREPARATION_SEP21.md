## 151 — corrected facts, present law and boundaries

The three injunctions must remain distinct. The November 1989 Richmond order expired after the election or compliance; that action was eventually dismissed in October 1994 for failure to prosecute. The 1989 Fairfax order became permanent following a hearing and was dissolved as to VLC and Jordahl by stipulation on September 30, 1994; it survived as to other persons until March 21, 1996. The 1993 Fairfax injunction against LaRock and others was dissolved by the Virginia Supreme Court on November 1, 1993 without an opinion. That court's act establishes dissolution, not its constitutional rationale or a finding of tort liability. Earlier federal Younger proceedings do not supply a present merits judgment on the disclosure law.

The packet's description of all the literature as nonendorsing is overinclusive. The Fourth Circuit's footnote 2 recites that one 1989 pamphlet expressly asked readers to vote against Linda Toddy Puller if they disagreed with her positions. Other voter guides were described as nonendorsing. The old §24.1-277 authorship provision, the 1993 replacement and the July 1, 1996 amendments are not interchangeable. No present narrowing construction is a retrospective adjudication of every earlier publication or injunction.

Count One requests prospective restraint of DPV's use of §8.01-620 injunctions to enforce §§24.2-908, -910 and -1014. The published courts characterize the pleaded system-of-prior-restraint claim as operating through injunctions against speech, rather than a separately substantiated general facial challenge. All orders against these plaintiffs had ended; later statutory amendments materially changed the asserted recurrence premise. No identified intended activity and realistic enforcement threat under the current scheme is established by this record. A litigant need not wait to be prosecuted: Pic-A-State and American Booksellers preserve credible pre-enforcement suits. DPV's mere failure to disavow every future suit is not enough here. Neither a universal rule that the Commonwealth itself is the only possible defendant nor an automatic prohibition on suits against private enforcers is adopted.

**Intervening authoritative state construction:** Caldwell, June 5, 1998, construes the current phrase concerning influencing an election in §§24.2-901, -910 and -1014 to reach express advocacy of election or defeat of an identified candidate, excluding solely issue advocacy. That construction carries through the §901 political-committee definition into §908. Section 908's organization statement concerns a political committee anticipating more than $200 in contributions or expenditures during a calendar year; that trigger must not be confused with other definitions' thresholds. Section 910(B) addresses independent expenditure reporting by persons or groups not political committees; §1014's authorship requirement is a separate provision. Section 901(B)'s express treatment of qualifying §501(c)(3) organizations does not imply that §501(c)(4) issue-only organizations are covered. Caldwell took VSHL's issue-only activity as the certified premise; it made no such finding about every Jordahl plaintiff or every pamphlet.

Caldwell's state-law construction is admitted as controlling Virginia law. Its discussion of federal McIntyre cannot enlarge this Court's narrower individual-leaflet holding. Nor does Maine's federal regulatory construction supply the meaning of these distinct state provisions. Caldwell independently weakens a present threat based solely on issue advocacy; it does not establish that planned express advocacy is immune, settle prior orders' validity or erase accrued damages claims. The absence of a supported current threat already permits Count One dismissal, so no invented post-Caldwell litigant response or mootness concession is necessary.

Counts Two and Three concern VLC/Jordahl's 1989 injunction injuries; Counts Seven and Eight concern LaRock's 1993 injunction injuries. The district and appellate opinions characterize them as damages claims requiring a declaration that those injunctions were erroneously issued. The record supplies no identified fraud or deception in procurement, no corrupt judicial conspiracy and no independent wrong severable from the complained-of restraints. Hearings occurred, and the 1993 order noted constitutional objections. This is not a record of an exclusively ex parte procedure with no opportunity to object.

There is nevertheless a serious legal disagreement about jurisdiction over past constitutional damages, especially after dissolution. Feldman's general-rule versus individual-adjudication distinction, Dennis's independent corrupt-procurement claim, De Grandy's independent enforcement suit and Stevens's express Feldman collateral-attack objection must all be addressed. Do not simply adopt the lower court's statement that every but-for consequence of a judicial order is outside federal jurisdiction. Do not describe Heck as a Rooker-Feldman decision: the simulated Heck expressly concerns cognizability of particular in-custody conviction-negating damages claims. State action under Lugar, merits, preclusion, immunity, personal responsibility and limitations remain separate questions. No damages are awarded by either proposed jurisdictional path.

State Counts Four and Five allege abuse of process, Six malicious prosecution and Nine trespass to chattels. The district court's actual ground is §1367(a): without original federal jurisdiction there is no supplemental-jurisdiction foundation. The Fourth Circuit's discretionary §1367(c)(3) description is not the same ground. A jurisdictional affirmance leaves state claims without prejudice and decides no tort merits. A partial federal remand requires reconsidering the associated state claims, without directing their retention.

## 152 — corrected facts and review vehicle

The Florida Supreme Court decided the federal equality and association challenge and expressly treated the relevant Florida constitutional protection as no greater in this case. Its state-law discussion therefore supplies no adequate independent ground insulating the federal judgment from §1257 review. This Court may decide the federal question without announcing a general construction of Florida's Constitution.

Section 99.103(1) (1993) distributes approximately half the filing fees of candidates of qualifying parties to those parties' executive committees for party expenses. Eligibility requires at least five percent of Florida's registered voters. The parties concede the underlying candidate filing fees were lawfully collected; Wilson had already qualified for the ballot. Do not claim that minor-party fees were specially exacted for, or directly transferred to, rival parties. The published source supports the approximate allocation, not an invented exact dollar formula.

This is an objective, potentially changing support criterion, not a statutory naming of the Democratic and Republican Parties. It nevertheless creates a real comparative benefit and requires constitutional analysis. A funding label does not immunize discrimination, and an unequal classification does not automatically establish the severe burden required for strict scrutiny. Buckley's public-financing rule allows eligibility based on demonstrated support but also preserves political fluidity; its vote-based criteria, post-election funding and recipient spending limits are not facts of Florida's different scheme. The State's actual asserted interest is stable parties and avoidance of public incentives to splinter groups. Naked preservation of current incumbents or suppression of minority viewpoints cannot serve as an independent justification. Ballot qualification for a candidate does not automatically establish party-wide support sufficient for a party-expense subsidy.

The lawful paths are affirmance confined to this limited, objective subsidy on the demonstrated record, or reversal because its selective financial advantage lacks an adequate relationship to a legitimate interest once candidates have qualified. No path automatically awards a rebate: invalidation would leave the State's lawful allocation choice and remedy for consideration. No ballot exclusion, expenditure ceiling, state-law refund entitlement or general party-funding immunity is adjudicated.

## 153 — corrected facts and qualifications

Illinois distinguishes the electorates in which a party has demonstrated support. More than five percent in the last gubernatorial election establishes the party generally; more than five percent in certain other statewide races establishes statewide status; more than five percent in a district or political subdivision establishes status there. LPI's combined 1994 trustee vote was 5.5 percent, but its gubernatorial vote was 1.68 percent and it had no qualifying district result. Statewide status in the trustee contest does not itself establish support in each congressional electorate.

Established-party primary petitions require 0.5 percent of the relevant qualified primary electorate. New-party general-election petitions require, for statewide office, the lesser of 25,000 signatures or one percent of voters in the last statewide general election; for district office, the lesser of five percent of voters in the last regular district election or the statewide cap. A separate postdecennial-redistricting exception uses 5,000 for the district petition, with a different 600-signature established-party exception not at issue. The general rules' denominators and election stages differ; the comparison is not simply a tenfold threshold. The statute's complete-slate requirement operates within the relevant subdivision. Do not turn it into an asserted requirement that a congressional candidate run a slate for every statewide office.

The district average for the new-party route was about 7,610 signatures; the seven plaintiff districts averaged about 9,337, compared with established-party primary averages of approximately 658 Democratic and 532 Republican signatures. Those numbers matter to actual burden, but arithmetic alone does not decide severity. Two third-party congressional candidates qualified in 1994. The 1996 count of fourteen, including ten Libertarians, is disputed as proof of meeting the full threshold: several petitions were accepted without objection despite apparent signature insufficiency. Six 1992 qualifiers used the 5,000-signature redistricting exception. None is treated as conclusive proof that every normal requirement was actually satisfied or always reasonable.

The rejected December 1995 submissions sought access to the 1996 primary. Their apparent insufficiency even for established-party candidates is not an independently adjudicated standing ground; the continuing party access claim remains reviewable. The completed primary does not moot that recurring controversy. The record does not establish a genuine dispute requiring a trial on an impossible combined signature, slate and deadline burden. This conclusion does not forbid a supported future challenge. The normal alternative general-ballot route matters; no constitutional entitlement to an automatic publicly administered party primary is assumed.

The claim is First/Fourteenth Amendment association under Anderson, Burdick, Norman and Timmons. The lower court's footnote 9 says a separate equal-protection claim was not pleaded; do not invent one. Article I's Qualifications Clause separately distinguishes substantive personal eligibility rules from genuine ballot procedures requiring public support. Thornton's exclusivity holding remains binding with its actual six-Justice support, not a fictional unanimous join by its dissenters. No existing otherwise qualified component is disqualified merely because a different component fails, as occurred in Norman. No primary rerun, automatic established status, replacement threshold or new statewide qualification is ordered by the affirmance path.
