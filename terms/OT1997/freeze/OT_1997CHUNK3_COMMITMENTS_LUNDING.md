## Judgment components and sufficient operative ground

**C1 — Article IV treatment of the alimony deduction:** Each Associate would reverse the judgment sustaining §631(b)(6)'s categorical refusal to recognize any qualifying alimony deduction in nonresidents' New York-source income, and remand for lawful correction of the challenged assessment. The section denies nonresident taxpayers substantial equality in taxation of their New York earnings without a sufficiently related independent justification on the reasons and statutory operation supplied. This does not require identical worldwide tax bases or identical treatment of every personal deduction.

**C2 — whole-formula/source and family-income justifications:** Each rejects the State's asserted justifications as sufficient to sustain this categorical paragraph. Shaffer and Travis allow genuine source-related limits on deductions and losses; their distinction does not make every payment labeled personal geographically exclusive to the State of residence. Qualifying alimony transfers income from the payer to a recipient whom federal law separately taxes on that receipt, and the deduction adjusts the payer's income across its sources. The liability's origin in a Connecticut divorce and the residence of both former spouses do not establish a relation between this payer-income adjustment and the production of a particular subset of New York earnings. They identify these parties and their marital obligation, not an independent source-allocation reason sufficient for a complete denial of proportionate recognition.

The State's worldwide taxation of residents and limited taxation of nonresidents is a real distinction. The deduction at issue must nevertheless be considered with the particular income it adjusts and the disparity the section creates. Travis's personal-exemption holding shows why the existence of a smaller nonresident base does not by itself validate unequal personal-income allowances; unrelated excluded income cannot be presumed to compensate for the denied allowance.

The actual calculation recognizes qualifying alimony when setting hypothetical resident liability and in the federal-adjusted-gross-income denominator, but denies it in the New York-source numerator. The hypothetical-resident benefit therefore does not establish equality in the ultimate apportioned burden. The same deduction that lowers the hypothetical base also reduces the denominator against a numerator that receives no corresponding recognition. This is an analysis of the supplied statutory formula, not a reconstruction of the Lundings' full return. It proves no unreported rate, income amount, total effective burden, or payment. The actual $3,724 additional assessment identifies the concrete consequence of this paragraph's denial in the reported dispute.

A family-income justification also fails to establish the needed close relation. Payer residence controls the challenged deduction; the paragraph does not condition it on the recipient's liability to New York tax. As a matter of statutory classification, a resident payer's deduction is allowed without such a recipient-residence condition, and a nonresident payer's deduction is categorically denied without one. These are implications of the supplied legal rule, not findings about the number or actual tax payments of hypothetical families. The asserted symmetry between deductible payments and receipts taxed by New York is not the statutory line enacted. The Court need not decide the validity of an unpresented replacement recipient-based rule.

**C3 — other States, other constitutional grounds and preserved tax authority:** Austin excludes reliance on another State's credit, deduction or lack of earned-income tax as a cure for the taxing State's discriminatory burden. Connecticut's 1990 earned-income tax fact neither establishes that Connecticut had no taxes nor makes this New York constitutional duty depend on Connecticut's choice. States retain source taxation, genuine corresponding source limits and permissible cooperative arrangements. Fulton and Oregon Waste support attention to actual operation and the inadequacy of unsupported offset labels only by analogy; their Commerce Clause discrimination burdens and three compensatory-tax predicates are not converted into the governing Article IV test. No independent Commerce Clause holding or strict requirement of mechanically identical deductions follows.

**C4 — remedial disposition:** Reverse and remand for state-law severability, lawful treatment of the assessment and constitutionally adequate relief under the facts and procedures actually established. Do not automatically reinstate every aspect of the Appellate Division remedy or prescribe the taxpayers' proposed formula as the sole constitutional remedy. The record establishes an assessment, not payment; no automatic $3,724 refund, interest award, new deduction amount, tax cancellation on unrelated income or mandatory recovery from other taxpayers is ordered. McKesson/Harper/Fulton require backward-looking correction when payment was compelled before meaningful challenge; Reich independently protects a clearly held-out postpayment remedy from withdrawal after payment. Their predicates are not found here merely because those rules could govern further proceedings.

## Enhanced adversarial comparison of the material issues

**Path A — sustain as a source-based allocation.** The strongest State case is Shaffer/Travis's permission to align deductions with a State's tax base. These taxpayers are Connecticut residents, their divorce and alimony recipient are in Connecticut, and the payments are not identified as costs of producing New York professional income. New York taxes its residents' worldwide income and gives the deduction a real role in calculating nonresident hypothetical resident liability. A State may reasonably avoid apportioning every personal expenditure across income sources, and Article IV leaves considerable latitude rather than demanding identical treatment.

**Strongest counterargument and selected resolution:** The source limitation is legitimate for expenses or losses actually associated with another source, but this statutory income transfer is not assigned to a source merely by the payer's residence or the divorce court's location. Travis independently prohibits unequal personal allowances without an adequate corresponding reason. Here the categorical rule, its payer-residence line, the asymmetric numerator/denominator operation, and the failure of the recipient-tax rationale to match that line leave the discrimination inadequately justified. The Court does not declare alimony universally indistinguishable from every exemption or personal expense; it addresses what this particular deduction does and how this paragraph removes it.

**Path B — invalidate the denial.** Toomer supplies the substantial independent reason and close-relationship requirements; Travis and Austin require examination of the actual incidence and refuse presumed offsets. The deduction distinction burdens a citizen earning income across state lines. It cannot be insulated by being called a sourcing rule, by a lower hypothetical resident tax viewed apart from the multiplier, or by another State's fiscal choices.

**Strongest counterargument and limit on the selected path:** Not every difference in resident/nonresident deductions is discrimination without justification. The actual Connecticut marital connection gives the State more than the bare word “nonresident,” and a narrower resident tax-base distinction can be constitutionally real. The answer is limited to the absence of a sufficiently close relation between those facts and the complete denial of a proportionate income adjustment under this statute. No universal rule that all domestic expenses must be apportioned, no least-restrictive-means test, no empirical exact-equivalence requirement borrowed from Commerce Clause cases, and no automatic tax-code equality formula is adopted.

**Path C — remand without deciding whether the reasons suffice.** A narrower remand could require the lower court to apply the correct Article IV standard to the complete formula, leaving constitutional sufficiency undecided. It is strongest if the formula, classification or offered reasons are uncertain. Those matters are established by the authorized recitations here, and the asserted defects are legal mismatches, not missing numerical findings. The Associates choose merits reversal rather than direct a further factual search for hypothetical offsets or require new evidence simply because originals are unreproduced. Genuine remaining severability, assessment, payment and remedial facts remain for the actual state proceedings.

**Remedy fork.** A direct award of the additional assessment plus interest could be defended as matching the taxpayers' requested correction. It would presume payment, fix unprovided amounts and foreclose lawful state remedial choices. Mere prospective invalidation could be insufficient if the actual payment/meaningful-review or held-out-remedy predicates require backward-looking relief. The selected conditional remedy preserves both governing duties without inventing either their predicates or the resulting award.

## Scope of common joins and operative remedy

All eight independently support the same sufficient level of generality: Article IV permits real source-based allocation but does not sustain this complete nonresident alimony-deduction exclusion on the particular source, hypothetical-resident-benefit, family-income and interstate-credit reasons supplied. Toomer's substantial-independent-reason/close-relationship standard controls. Travis supplies the distinction between genuine source-expense limits and an unjustified personal-allowance disparity, and Austin excludes a cure based on another State's taxing choices. Shaffer remains in force for nondiscriminatory local-income taxation and appropriately connected deduction limits; it is not overruled or reduced to a universal alimony rule. Friedsam's earlier statutory construction was superseded by the challenged enactment and is not revived as the constitutional holding. The recited Goodwin reasoning is addressed as the lower court's source/personal-expense analogy; its unreproduced full opinion supplies no additional Supreme Court rule.

The controlling question is not whether every resident and nonresident must pay the same total tax. The challenged paragraph and complete computation are examined at their actual statutory scope; no empirical finding about all taxpayers or an undisclosed hypothetical calculation is made. The reported 1990 procedure permits apportionment above 100%; neither an invented cap nor an inference that these taxpayers actually exceeded 100% enters the analysis.

The judgment reverses the Court of Appeals' sustaining judgment and returns for severability and lawful relief from the unconstitutional treatment of the challenged assessment. It leaves genuinely available state remedial choices and the actual payment, preservation, refund, interest and procedural predicates unresolved. It orders no reopening of unrelated final tax years, no automatic refund or assessment against others, no reinstatement of all aspects of the Appellate Division order, and no new evidence or hearing solely to cure a research-source limitation. Equal Protection, Commerce Clause merits, federal alimony qualification and other source rules remain outside the judgment.
