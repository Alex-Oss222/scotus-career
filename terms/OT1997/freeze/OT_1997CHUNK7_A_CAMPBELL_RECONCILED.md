## Proposition-level scope and controlling explanations

**P1 — Six Associates.** A white defendant may assert excluded Black citizens' equal-protection rights against purposeful racial discrimination in selecting a voting member of the grand jury indicting him. Powers supplies three requirements: concrete injury to the accused, a sufficiently close relationship with excluded jurors, and obstacles to their asserting their own rights. Discriminatory composition undermines the integrity of the accusatory body and ensuing process; selecting only one member and the absence of a publicly visible series of strikes do not eliminate that injury. The defendant and eligible jurors share an interest in lawful selection, and the defendant has a direct incentive to litigate. The practical costs and limited individual recovery hinder jurors' separate enforcement. Evidence of prior exclusions supports the alleged discrimination in this defendant's selection; it does not change the claim into an unconnected lawsuit for earlier venires. Powers, reinforced within its respective scope by McCollum and J.E.B., supplies individual equality, not racial proportionality or an entitlement to group loyalties. Purposeful exclusion remains to be proved.

**P2 — Eight Associates.** Campbell has standing to litigate whether the selection of a member of his indicting grand jury violated his own due-process rights. Under Louisiana's Article 413(B) procedure, the judge selects the foreperson from the venire before the remaining members are drawn by lot; the foreperson is a full voting member. Hobby's federal office was filled from an already constituted grand jury and had ministerial duties. That decision therefore does not foreclose Campbell's own-process claim concerning the selection of a member. This is standing, not an adjudication of the nature, extent or violation of his due-process entitlement; it creates no automatic new-trial rule and decides no harmless-error issue.

**P3 — Eight Associates.** Reverse the judgment denying standing and remand to permit the discrimination claims to be considered under applicable proof and remedial rules. The recited absence of a Black foreperson from January 1976 through August 1993 despite more than twenty percent Black registered voters is the uncontested evidence described in the source, not a Supreme Court finding of purposeful discrimination. The intermediate appellate court considered the evidence inadequate and contemplated an evidentiary hearing. Necessary evidentiary development and legal consideration remain below. The Court neither presently quashes the indictment nor orders an unconditional new trial, and it invents no new evidentiary burden.

**P4 — Eight Associates.** Do not reach the Sixth Amendment fair-cross-section claim. Neither Louisiana appellate court discussed it, and Campbell has not shown proper appellate presentation despite the State's specific objection. Initial assertion alone does not cure that gap. Adams v. Robertson supports requiring the showing; absence of archived papers is not the basis for this limitation. No fair-cross-section merits holding follows.

## Separate-writing function preserved after full-opinion review

Scalia and Thomas join P2–P4 while dissenting from P1. Their separate position rejects Powers' use of perceived institutional unfairness as injury in fact and regards the excluded juror's interest in service as distinct from the defendant's interest in overturning a conviction. Even assuming Powers, selection of a single grand-jury foreperson outside defendant-conducted voir dire supplies neither its asserted relationship nor its visible exclusion rationale. A claim of systemic discrimination also presents a potential class of prospective jurors able to seek declaratory or injunctive relief; ordinary litigation expense does not suffice for third-party standing. They would not use another person's equal-protection claim to undo a conviction without the required standing injury. Their argument about the intervening petit-jury conviction supports that standing objection and their expressed doubt about eventual due-process success, not a Court holding that the conviction cured all violations. Campbell's assertion of his own right independently supports standing and the remand. Their continued criticism of Powers does not itself overrule governing law. No author of this shared position is selected here.
