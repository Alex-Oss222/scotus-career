## Adversarial alternatives

**Actual perfection acts within the federal period — selected.** The federal statute gives a purchase-money lender a specified interval after the debtor receives possession to complete protection against the defined judicial-lien creditor. State law identifies what must be done. The federal provision determines how soon it must be done to secure this exception. A later retrospective priority date does not change the fact that the condition could still depend on further acts when the federal interval ended.

The strongest contrary argument is textual: §547(e)(1)(B) itself invokes priority against a judicial creditor, and state relation back may ultimately defeat a judicial lien obtained in the intervening period. On that reading, Fidelity was perfected from creation, so no extension of the federal deadline is formally needed. The difficulty is that this treats the later performance and its retrospective consequence as though the statutory protection had already become unconditional. The statute asks when the protection against acquisition of the superior judicial lien was achieved, not what priority a later act may eventually assign to an earlier date. Before the required act, the creditor still must do something to obtain the relation-back protection. Reading the later event as performance at creation would allow each State's longer period to replace the 20-day condition Congress specified.

This construction gives real work to both the state-law reference and the federal deadline. It does not choose a nationally uniform filing method or create a new lien. Nor does general uniformity alone decide the case: Congress could adopt state grace periods, and §546 shows that state perfection law has expressly protected roles elsewhere. The particular wording and structure of §547 decide this exception.

**Retrospective state priority satisfies the federal deadline — rejected.** Fidelity's path takes the state-defined priority relationship seriously and invokes Butner's general respect for property interests. If accepted, it would require reversal of the present timing rationale, with any necessary remaining state-law and exception issues considered without inventing delivery. Its strongest answer to the federal-period objection is that Congress used a state-dependent term and accepted differences among state perfection systems.

The decisive distinction is between different state-required acts, which the federal rule accommodates, and a longer state interval for doing them, which would deprive the express 20-day condition of its own operation. Barnhill permits state property law to identify the interest without allowing state characterizations to control every federal timing consequence. BFP's controlling rule likewise rejects the premise that state compliance necessarily satisfies a distinct federal avoidance condition. Neither precedent alone compels F1, but neither supports incorporating Missouri's additional ten days without addressing the federal language. The limited §546 enumeration reinforces that Congress did not simply make all avoidance powers subject to every state relation-back rule.

**Decision on mailing sufficiency or a fact-development remand — not selected.** On the supported account the mailing occurred on day 21. The federal question can be decided even giving Fidelity the benefit of that date for the argument. It is unnecessary to determine delivery, establish a later date, or hold that mailing failed Missouri requirements. An evidentiary remand on an expressly unraised issue would add a dispute the selected legal ground does not need. The model does not infer that missing originals conceal an earlier timely act; the decision uses the reported transaction, not an inference from the research archive.
