# OT1997 chunk 8 — Montana independent commitments

**Matter and event:** Montana v. Crow Tribe of Indians, No. 96-1829; May 18, 1998.
**Boundary:** Eight Associate Justices only; no Stone position, comparator reconciliation, assignment or assembled Court judgment. These affected commitments use the additional neutral objective supplement and the same pre–May 18 law. The original B handoff remains preserved.

## Corrected common premises

The State taxes' invalidity is established and is not relitigated. The United States intervened as trustee in June 1983. The 1988 district court considered Interior's ceded-strip refusal legally mistaken because the reservation-tax approval covered the minerals; that conclusion cannot be described as a finding of no lawful tribal taxing authority. Its payment ruling nevertheless concerned registry funds governed by the 1982 agreement and did not establish a pre-agreement tax debt owed by Westmoreland.

The actual refusals and the company's inability to obtain the contemplated customer pass-through remain historical and commercial facts. The finding that Westmoreland would not have paid earlier tribal taxes is not equivalent to a holding that none was legally authorized. The agreement's treatment of Westmoreland's earlier obligation, the distribution of registry funds, the lessee's separate refund procedures, and the absence of quantified market-loss findings remain distinct. No assumed compensatory claim or allowed 1993 amendment enters the case.

## Competing sufficient grounds

### A. Reverse the mandatory full-disgorgement direction

The Tribe's independent federal interest is real, but invalidity of the State levy and lawful tribal tax coverage do not alone establish a right to every dollar paid by Westmoreland. Equitable restitution still requires a connection between this claimant's protected interest, the defendant's enrichment and the measure ordered. The 1988 ruling supplies no binding adjudication of the earlier debt or the present entire-collection claim. The actual commercial arrangements, the agreement, the taxpayer's refund position and the distribution already made remain relevant to the trial court's equitable assessment.

United States v. California distinguishes a claimant's economic connection from its own or derivative refund entitlement; Fulton separates invalidity from claimant-specific, procedurally lawful relief. The Indian-tax cases preserve exact legal incidence and authorization rather than an automatic monetary consequence. These authorities do not foreclose a distinct tribal restitution claim, but they prevent converting invalidity into a compulsory transfer without its separate equitable predicates.

The strongest contrary argument is that the State's benefit was obtained by burdening this Tribe's federally protected trust mineral estate, and that benefit-based restitution need not match proved loss. That argument is stronger once the erroneous approval refusal is acknowledged. The response is not that Interior conclusively disabled the Tribe. It is that lawful authority to tax and a direct interest in the minerals still do not conclusively make all money paid by the lessee the Tribe's equitable entitlement, especially under the actual agreement and commercial circumstances. A bounded reversal leaves any properly supported independent relief to its own predicates.

### B. Affirm this particular benefit-based restitution disposition

The Tribe and its trustee assert their own federal interest in exploitation of the trust minerals, not merely an assignment of the company's refund. The State extracted the challenged benefit by imposing taxes already adjudged incompatible with that protected interest. The 1988 coverage ruling removes the supposed absence of lawful tribal taxation as a substantial reason to let the State retain the benefit. Equity can require surrender of the benefit obtained through that particular interference without requiring proof that each dollar would otherwise have been collected by the Tribe.

The contemporaneous commercial obstacle and later release of Westmoreland concern what the company would pay or owe; they do not themselves settle what the State may retain from its own unlawful exaction. The trustee's June 1983 participation also defeats an account of belated federal involvement after the gross-proceeds deposit arrangement. The present restitution theory does not require a new damages claim or a finding of lost royalties equal to the State receipts.

The strongest contrary argument is that this transfers a different taxpayer's money to the Tribe despite no adjudicated earlier debt, limited registry ruling, and possible differences between gain and tribal loss. This path answers by treating the trust-resource and established preemption relationship as the independent equitable link. It does not treat all economically affected claimants as owners of invalid taxes. The full amount is the identified unlawful benefit in this specific relationship, not a presumed compensatory measure or a penalty for general governmental misconduct. Detailed accounting and any lawful interest determination remain distinct from entitlement.

## Proposition-level alignment and remedy

O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Ginsburg adopt Path A at the same operative level: no automatic right to the entire State collection has been established merely by the fixed invalidity and clarified tribal tax coverage. They would reverse the Ninth Circuit's compulsory direction and remand for disposition consistent with that distinction. They do not revive the State taxes, declare the actual Interior refusal lawful, or categorically forbid every properly available independent tribal remedy. The existing registry distribution remains undisturbed.

Stevens, Souter and Breyer adopt Path B's particular equitable entitlement, not a binding earlier debt or a derivative refund theory. They would affirm the restitution direction for the identified unlawful preregistry benefit, with the ordinary implementation of amounts and lawful interest rather than a newly invented award. Their disagreement is substantive: the corrected record supplies, in their view, the direct equitable connection that the five reversing Justices still find insufficient for compulsory whole-benefit recovery.

The three changed commitments do not arise from a different characterization of the 1988 judgment's binding scope. All eight accept its limited distribution ruling and the absence of an adjudicated earlier Westmoreland debt. They differ on what the corrected legal-coverage premise means for the Tribe's independent equitable claim. The three need a substantive separate writing explaining that benefit-based ground and its response to the commercial arrangements; the five need to answer it without relying on the superseded unqualified no-approval premise.

No Justice directs allowance of the unverified 1993 amendment, awards compensatory damages, finds an amount of market injury, revives a lapsed taxpayer claim, or infers that the Tribe released its independent claim against the State. No historical alignment or authorship is used. These are independent affected commitments ready for separate reconciliation.
