#### Day v. Holahan

**Docket or dockets:** No. 94-672.
**Decided:** May 15, 1995.

##### Authority of the responsive-financing judgment

**Authority of the judgment:** The section 10A.25(13) responsive-financing invalidation is affirmed 5–4: Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas support the judgment. Kennedy's plurality, joined by O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas, treats the adverse proportional legal trigger as an expressive burden; Stone-Zsela's judgment concurrence rests on insufficient programmatic connection on this record and rejects categorical invalidity. Neither framework controls, and no controlling rationale is established under Marks. The judgment binds the parties; the three independently majority-supported holdings below do not supply a unified financing rule.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The invalidated section 10A.25(13) mechanism increases candidate limits by independent expenditures opposing the candidate plus expenditures for the candidate's major-party opponents, excluding an association's communications solely to its dues-paying members. The provision itself has no $100 threshold; that figure describes the lower court's operational reporting scenario. The Board must notify every candidate in the race within 48 hours after receipt of the report or notice under section 10A.20(3), (6) or (6b), and within three days after notice must pay half the expenditure only to a subsidy-eligible candidate who has raised twice the minimum match, from an appropriation from the general fund. The lower court's electoral-result-eligibility concern describes implementation; it is neither a statutory payment exception nor a finding that every subsidy is immediately payable.

##### The special immediate-notice obligation falls with this trigger

**Holding:** Section 10A.20, subdivision 6b's additional immediate affidavit and service obligation cannot be enforced independently of the invalid responsive-financing mechanism on this scheme's demonstrated function and legislative relationship. Under Minnesota section 645.20, the special mechanism is essentially and inseparably connected with the invalid provision such that the legislature cannot be presumed to have enacted it alone; ordinary campaign reports remain enforceable within their separate terms.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, Part II, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas: five direct votes. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer dissent on this component.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The additional obligation is a twenty-four-hour affidavit and service on the candidates and campaign treasurers in the affected race. The holding rests on essential legislative connection under section 645.20, not physical impossibility of reporting. The independent vagueness and overbreadth theories are not reached.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Buckley, 424 U.S. 1: preserves distinct disclosure interests and associational protections; no categorical disclosure invalidation follows.
- Gibson, 372 U.S. 539: applies the need for a substantial relation between information demanded and the legitimate subject; does not eliminate ordinary reports.
- Brown v. Socialist Workers, 459 U.S. 87: recognizes genuine disclosure interests with protection for demonstrated associational harms; no new general exemption is created.

##### This $100 intermediary-contribution ceiling lacks sufficient fit

**Holding:** Minnesota's $100 annual ceiling on contributions received by the covered political committees and political funds is unconstitutional on the established burden and insufficiently fitted corruption/circumvention justification. Contributions remain distinct from independent expenditures: realistic routes around valid candidate limits may be regulated, but a broadly restrictive ceiling must preserve meaningful collective political association and fit an identified corruption or circumvention mechanism; no permanent dollar minimum follows.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, Part III, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: nine direct votes, at this stated functional application.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Section 10A.27(12) covers aggregate annual contributions from individuals, political committees and political funds, but excludes a candidate's principal campaign committee and a political party unit defined in section 10A.275 from its covered recipients. Higher limits, coordination controls, candidate transfers and demonstrated conduit restrictions remain open. The record's substantial pooling burden does not establish a permanent constitutional dollar floor or equate contributions with independent expenditures.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Buckley: applies contribution/expenditure distinction, corruption fit and effective collective advocacy; its historical dollar figure is no minimum.
- California Medical, 453 U.S. 182: preserves candidate-transfer anticircumvention regulation; its plurality is not a categorical answer for every committee.
- Citizens Against Rent Control, 454 U.S. 290: applies protection of pooling for collective political expression; does not immunize a candidate-contribution conduit.
- NCPAC, 470 U.S. 480: preserves independent advocacy at its scope; contributions are not silently equated with expenditures.

##### MCCL receives protection on its actual nonprofit characteristics

**Holding:** Minnesota may not prohibit MCCL's independent expenditures solely because its incidental business activity and absence of a formal policy against significant corporate contributions fall outside section 211B.15, subdivision 15's literal safe harbor. On the undisputed minimal-business-income and insignificant-corporate-funding record, its relevant political-association characteristics support as-applied MCFL protection; material commercial accumulation, economic claimants or corporate/union conduit funding requires a different assessment.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, Part IV, joined by all eight Associates: nine direct votes on this as-applied holding. No general overruling or endorsement beyond Austin's surviving scope is adopted.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The section 211B.15(15) safe harbor requires no business activity, no economic shareholders or claimants, no establishment by a business corporation or union, and a policy against significant contributions from them. Protection rests on MCCL's actual minimal business income and undisputed insignificant corporate funding. That checklist cannot foreclose this as-applied protection; materially changed operations or corporate or union financing may require reassessment. Direct corporate candidate contributions and ordinary business-corporation treasury restrictions are not decided.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- MCFL, 479 U.S. 238: applies protection to actual political-resource, structure and funding characteristics; descriptive facts do not foreclose this narrow as-applied claim.
- Austin, 494 U.S. 652: distinguishes materially different commercially accumulated corporate wealth; its surviving corporate-treasury rule is not overruled.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm the Eighth Circuit on all four components. Maintain the permanent restraints on section 10A.25(13)'s responsive financing, section 10A.20(6b)'s linked special notice and section 10A.27(12)'s $100 annual ceiling, and MCCL's bounded as-applied protection under section 211B.15(15). Ordinary reports and otherwise lawful corporate and contribution regulation remain; no damages, general public-financing prohibition or facial excision of corporate restrictions follows.

### Official confidential access and obstructive speech



#### Colorado Republican Federal Campaign Committee v. Federal Election Commission, 518 U.S. 604 (1996)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 95-489.
**Decided:** June 26, 1996.

##### Whether the Senate party-expenditure ceiling may reach this independent advertisement

**Holding:** A political party's own campaign advertisement that is genuinely independent of a candidate may not be subjected to the Senate-election expenditure ceiling in 2 U.S.C. §441a(d)(3) merely because its speaker is an official political party. Party identity and a continuing electoral relationship do not, without more, justify applying that ceiling to this independent expression; the adverse enforcement judgment must therefore be vacated.

**Proposition-level authority:** O’Connor’s Part I, joined by Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer; six direct votes.

**Material application:** The party’s $15,000 pre-nomination advertisement was genuinely independent. Its prior assignment of the whole $103,248 adjusted election allowance explains statutory excess, not constitutional validity. Legitimate corruption and circumvention concerns do not establish an actual candidate connection from party identity alone.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No majority adopts the complete narrower expenditure framework or its prudential reservation rationale (O’Connor, Souter, Breyer), or Kennedy/Scalia/Thomas’s broader coordinated-party-expression protection. No Marks combination is used. Coordinated expression, direct candidate cash, earmarks, republication, sham independence and actual conduits are neither generally immunized nor upheld by implication. No universal bribery-proof, express-advocacy formula, constitutional dollar floor, vagueness or administrative-deference rule.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Adverse enforcement/penalty mandate vacated and remanded, 6–3. The broader Senate-election counterclaim receives no majority constitutional determination; no fixed penalty, damages, refund or compelled new factual trial.

##### Whether party identity supplies the connection required by the alternative contribution theory

**Holding:** For the government's theory that this advertisement is a contribution under §441a(a)(7)(B)(i), party status alone cannot substitute for expenditure made in cooperation, consultation or concert with, or at the request or suggestion of, a candidate, the candidate's authorized political committees or their agents. On the accepted independence premise, that theory cannot preserve the adverse enforcement judgment under §441a(a)(2).

**Proposition-level authority:** The same six-Justice Part I coalition.

**Material application:** A categorical agency characterization is not a factual finding of consultation; competing assertions about novelty and noncontest establish neither forfeiture nor concession. Transfers among national, State, district, local and subordinate same-party committees are exempt from §441a(a)(1) and (2). Candidate-material dissemination, distribution or republication under (a)(7)(B)(ii), and original-source attribution for earmarked/intermediary contributions under (a)(8), have distinct predicates absent here.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The constitutionality of a transaction with an actual connection, all interactions with (d)(1)’s notwithstanding clause, presidential allowances and other House limits are not decided.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- **Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1:** Its distinction between independent expenditures and candidate contributions supplies the present independent-advertisement rule. Its treatment of the party provision's Fifth Amendment challenge did not decide this First Amendment question. The general distinction remains in force.
- **FEC v. National Conservative Political Action Committee, 470 U.S. 480:** Its protection of genuinely independent political advocacy supports the result; it supplies no categorical immunity for actual contribution channels.
- **Day v. Holahan, May 15, 1995:** Its controlling protection of meaningful intermediary association and preservation of realistic conduit regulation inform the required distinction. Its recipient category excluded political-party units, and its responsive-financing judgment had no unified controlling rationale. Neither is converted into an earlier decision of this party-spending question.
- **FEC v. Massachusetts Citizens for Life, Inc., 479 U.S. 238:** Its protection of political expression and its nonprofit setting remain distinct; the Court does not rest today's judgment on a universal express-advocacy formula or extend the nonprofit exception to every entity.

### Student speech and curricular control



#### Hurley v. Irish-American Gay, Lesbian and Bisexual Group of Boston

**Docket or dockets:** No. 94-749.
**Decided:** June 19, 1995.

##### Private parade composition and compelled inclusion

**Holding:** Applying a public-accommodations law to compel private parade organizers to include an identified contingent conveying a message they do not choose violates the First Amendment. A parade remains expression despite broad admissions, multiple themes and the absence of a single precise message; the rule does not exempt ordinary nonexpressive goods or services from nondiscrimination law.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter’s Opinion of the Court, joined by all eight other Justices; nine Justices adopt the as-applied private-expression holding.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Public-street use under a neutral permit does not convert this private parade into a governmental forum. The dismissed and unappealed state-action/public-forum theory against the City is not revived. Independent review of intertwined expression characterizations retains credibility deference. Message misattribution in other settings remains open; no general antidiscrimination exemption or premise that ordinary customer identity is itself a message is adopted.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette: applies protection against compelled expression.
- Miami Herald Publishing Co. v. Tornillo: applies editorial selection protection.
- Turner Broadcasting System, Inc. v. FCC: applies existing medium-specific editorial protection, without importing unrelated cable results.
- Roberts v. United States Jaycees; PruneYard Shopping Center v. Robins: distinguish access not materially altering the private expressive composition.
- Bose Corp. v. Consumers Union; Fiske v. Kansas: apply independent review of intertwined First Amendment characterizations while retaining credibility deference.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Reverse and remand with the injunction compelling GLIB's inclusion dissolved. The public-accommodations law remains enforceable where its application does not compel private expression; no fee amount, fine or sanction is decided.

### Private religious displays in public forums



#### McCarthy v. Madigan, 503 U.S. 140 (1992)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 90-6861.
**Decided:** March 4, 1992.

##### Whether Congress requires exhaustion of the general BOP grievance procedure for this federal medical-care damages action

**Holding:** An express congressional exhaustion requirement controls according to its terms. Where Congress has not required exhaustion, courts must exercise sound discretion consistent with the statutory scheme, weighing prompt access to court against the actual interests in agency authority and judicial efficiency; neither the general BOP delegation nor the then-current §1997e imposes a mandatory gate for this federal prisoner’s *Bivens* medical-care claim.

**Proposition-level authority:** Blackmun’s opinion, joined by Stone, White, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy and Souter; seven Justices control the governing inquiry and statutory application.

**Current-force modification:** The statutory-primacy rule and the construction of former §1997e remain controlling. PLRA §803(d) replaces that text: a prisoner confined in a jail, prison or other correctional facility may not bring a prison-conditions action under §1983 or any other federal law until available administrative remedies are exhausted. For this provision, prisoner includes a person incarcerated or detained for an accusation, conviction, sentence or delinquency adjudication under criminal law or the terms of parole, probation, pretrial release or diversion. The former absence of a congressional mandate does not establish an exemption from this new command. The admission decides neither the amended terms' disputed scope nor their temporal application to a particular pending action; it does not reopen McCarthy's judgment.

**Material application:** General prison-administration authority under 18 U.S.C. §4042 contains no exhaustion command. The then-current 42 U.S.C. §1997e addresses specified state-prisoner §1983 actions through limited discretionary stays, not this federal action. The Bureau’s general grievance procedure is not a comprehensive congressional replacement for the existing medical-care damages channel.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No exception to an express congressional requirement or new *Bivens* action is created. Liability, immunity, causation and damages are not determined.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Patsy v. Board of Regents of Florida*, 457 U.S. 496, makes congressional design central. *McKart v. United States*, 395 U.S. 185, supplies the agency-authority, expertise, error-correction and efficiency purposes. *McGee v. United States*, 402 U.S. 479, supports sound judicial discretion absent congressional mandate. *Coit Independence Joint Venture v. FSLIC*, 489 U.S. 561, distinguishes statutory exhaustion and the adequacy of the administrative route. *Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14, remains the established medical-care damages channel without extension. *Bush v. Lucas*, 462 U.S. 367, and *Schweiker v. Chilicky*, 487 U.S. 412, are distinguished because there is no comparable comprehensive congressional remedial replacement here.

**Later-authority backlinks:** Prison Litigation Reform Act of 1995, Pub. L. 104-134, Title VIII, enacted April 26, 1996: §803(d), replacement §1997e(a), (h), and the former absence of a mandatory exhaustion command. This is a noncase-law change, not an overruling or new judicial holding.

##### Whether the actual BOP procedure justifies requiring exhaustion of this damages-only claim absent a statutory mandate

**Holding:** Absent a statutory mandate, a federal prisoner seeking only damages for the completed medical-care wrong alleged here need not exhaust the described BOP process: it cannot award compensation, its short filing periods risk forfeiture, and its limited adjudicative benefit does not outweigh access to court. The result does not decide a request for prospective relief, a procedure able to grant the sought compensation, or a materially different administrative adjudication.

**Proposition-level authority:** Blackmun’s opinion, joined by Stone, White, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy and Souter; seven Justices join this particular balancing and application. Scalia and Thomas concur in the judgment solely because monetary relief is unavailable; their votes do not support the deadline or broader balancing rationale.

**Current-force modification:** The balancing and damages-only result remain controlling where no statute requires exhaustion. For an action governed by amended §1997e(a), the former-law absence of a mandate cannot itself excuse compliance with the new available-remedies requirement described in the preceding question. No construction of availability, compensation-incapable procedures or pending-case application under the amended text is established by source admission.

**Material application:** The process provides investigation and written responses but no hearing, formal evidentiary findings or monetary award. It requires an initial complaint within 15 days and appeals within 20 and 30 days, with extensions for valid reasons. The rejected regional appeal illustrates forfeiture risk without establishing official misconduct. Speculative FTCA conversion or settlement does not establish an effective damages remedy under these regulations.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The deadline analysis does not invalidate short filing periods categorically. No finding of bias, deliberate indifference, damages entitlement or loss of a defense is made. The holding does not convert negligence into a constitutional violation or expand the established damages action.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Reverse the exhaustion dismissal and remand for ordinary proceedings on the damages complaint without completion of this grievance process as a prerequisite. Liability, defenses and damages remain for adjudication.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *McKart*, 395 U.S. 185, supports comparing actual individual and institutional interests. *Coit*, 489 U.S. 561, informs practical adequacy without categorically invalidating deadlines. *Turner v. Safley*, 482 U.S. 78, preserves judicial cognizance of valid prisoner claims without deciding this claim’s validity. *Hillsborough Township v. Cromwell*, 326 U.S. 620, and *Union Pacific Railroad Co. v. Board of County Commissioners*, 247 U.S. 282, address uncertainty in an administrative route to the requested relief.

**Later-authority backlinks:** Prison Litigation Reform Act of 1995, Pub. L. 104-134, Title VIII, enacted April 26, 1996: §803(d), the independent statutory exhaustion condition rather than former discretionary balancing. This is a noncase-law change, not an overruling or new judicial holding.

### Appellate jurisdiction and notices of appeal



#### Akins v. FEC

**Docket or dockets:** D.C. Circuit No. 94-5088; no separate Supreme Court docket supplied.

**Decided:** 1997-06-25.

For political-committee coverage and the separate membership and transaction provisions, see Akins v. FEC, D.C. Circuit No. 94-5088, decided 1997-06-25, Election Law — Political-committee coverage and membership communications.

##### I. The continuing denial of particular electoral information is a concrete injury

**Holding:** Voters denied particular campaign-financing information allegedly required by FECA, which they seek for their own electoral participation, have a concrete informational injury even when many voters share it. Here the Commission's legal ground blocks a statutory process capable of supplying information still sought; the possibility of a later lawful discretionary choice does not defeat traceability or redressability.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part I of Souter's Court opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer: seven direct votes.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Statutory complainant status, opposition to an organization's policies or a generalized wish for legal compliance alone is insufficient. The plaintiffs must support their own injury at the litigation stage; standing determines neither entitlement to every requested item nor ultimate regulatory classification.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Public Citizen supports a concrete widely shared informational injury. Lujan retains personal injury and stage-appropriate proof. Bennett requires examination of the real remedial chain despite lawful discretion. Steel Co.'s completed deprivation and already-current reports are distinguished from information still withheld.

##### II. FECA permits review of this dismissal's legal premise

**Holding:** Section 437g(a)(8) permits an aggrieved complainant to obtain judicial review of a complaint dismissal within sixty days and permits correction of a legally erroneous coverage premise. A court may declare the dismissal contrary to law and may direct conformity within thirty days; if the Commission fails to conform, the complainant may bring an action to remedy the violation involved in the original complaint.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part II has the same seven-Justice coalition as Part I.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The separate 120-day inaction route is not invoked. Judicial correction does not compel prosecution, disclosure or penalties, remove lawful discretion, or permit substitution of the agency's separate corporate-prohibition membership rationale for the actual political-committee dismissal ground. Genuine ambiguity and remaining discretion remain for lawful agency consideration.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Standing and legal-error review are affirmed, 7–2; the case returns through the enacted review channel. The separate corporate-prohibition nonenforcement choice is not disturbed merely by correcting this dismissal.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Brown Group separates statutory enforcement authority from Article III. Bennett requires an actual authorized channel, and Legi-Tech preserves lawful agency reconsideration and statutory prerequisites. The agency's interpretation of constitutional decisions is not conclusive because it administers FECA.
