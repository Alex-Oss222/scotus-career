## South Dakota v. Yankton Sioux Tribe, 522 U.S.329

**Identity:** No.96-1581; argued1997-12-08; reviewed99 F.3d1439 (8th Cir.1996). Official pages329–358,30 pages. One opinion, by O'Connor, for all nine: Rehnquist, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer. No separate writing, partial join, or nonparticipation. Judgment9–0: reversed and remanded.

**Historical components and exact limits:**

- The 1894 Act diminished the reservation by removing unallotted lands ceded under the agreement from reservation status; state primary jurisdiction extends to the waste site and the ceded lands. That common judgment and reasoning received all nine votes.
- The most probative evidence is statutory language; cession plus a sum certain yields the nearly conclusive/almost insurmountable presumption discussed in prior decisions. Congress's intent must be clear and plain; ambiguities favor Indians; these principles were applied rather than discarded.
- Article XVIII was construed to secure continued treaty annuities, not preserve the whole1858 boundary. The opinion considered the negotiating threats and annuity concerns, the incompatibility of literal treaty preservation with the agreed sale, and the specificity of ArticlesI–II.
- School sections and the liquor provision supported diminishment; the agency/school-land reservation in ArticleVIII cautioned against treating the entire reservation as terminated. Historical context reinforced but did not independently compel the result. Later administrative/congressional treatment was mixed; demographics were the least compelling consideration and did not substitute for statutory intent.
- **Express reservation:** The Court did not decide whether Congress disestablished the reservation altogether. Its holding addressed the ceded, unallotted lands. Status of all allotments, reserved agency tracts, or subsequently reacquired tribal/trust land cannot be resolved by broadening that holding. [343–358.]

**Comparator check:** The supplied comparator's date, unanimous O'Connor authorship, disposition, principal analysis, and express limitation are accurate. It relies on a syllabus, but no omitted separate writing exists. The full opinion adds material procedural facts omitted from the short neutral material: the Tribe did not appeal denial of its injunction, the Waste District did not appeal but supported the State, and EPA had permitted the clay liner. These objective facts were separately released; they do not create a new historical jurisdictional ruling beyond the source.

**Footnotes read:** All numbered notes1–6 and the counsel/amicus note. Note1 reproduces agreement ArticlesI,II,VII,VIII,XV,XVII,XVIII; notes2–3 address compensation and the landfill/EPA posture; notes5–6 preserve contrary later descriptions.

## Allentown Mack Sales & Service, Inc. v. NLRB, 522 U.S.359

**Identity:** No.96-795; argued1997-10-15; reviewed83 F.3d1483 (D.C.Cir.1996). Official pages359–397,39 pages. Three writings: Scalia's Court opinion, Rehnquist's concurrence/dissent, Breyer's concurrence/dissent.

| Component | Historical supporting Justices | Opposing Justices / scope |
|---|---|---|
| PartI factual/procedural account | All nine | None |
| PartII: facial rationality and consistency with NLRA of good-faith reasonable-doubt threshold | Scalia, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer (5) | Rehnquist, O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas (4) |
| PartsIII–IV: substantial-evidence application and rule actually applied must match rule announced | Scalia, Rehnquist, O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas (5) | Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer (4) |
| Judgment: reverse and remand **with instructions to deny enforcement** | Scalia, Rehnquist, O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas (5) | Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer (4) |

**Scalia's PartII:** A unitary threshold for polling, withdrawal, and Board elections is puzzling but not arbitrary/capricious. Polls may confirm actual support, improve employee relations, avoid needless bargaining disruption, or reduce practical risk of a charge. Competing reasons for a higher/lower threshold do not make equality irrational. This is a separate five-Justice majority from the application majority.

**Scalia's PartsIII–IV:** “Doubt” means genuine reasonable uncertainty, not certainty of opposition or disbelief defined as belief in the opposite. The Board failed to credit probative evidence, particularly Marsh's ambiguous dissatisfaction and Bloch/Mohr's reports. A union-successorship presumption cannot be used to deny any relationship between old/new workers' sentiments when contrary evidence is offered. Substantial-evidence review tests the standard the agency announces; consistent hidden use of a higher threshold does not amend the announced standard. Express, lawful evidentiary rules and presumptions remain possible, subject to ordinary review. The Court did **not** need to find that the Board systematically manipulated its threshold in all cases. [366–380.]

**Rehnquist, joined O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas:** JoinedI,III,IV and judgment; dissentedII. Found the polling restriction unsupported by §§8(a)(1)/(5) absent coercion or restraint and irrationally equating actions with different effects. Struksnes safeguards, employee free choice, employer interests, and Gissel/Thomas/free-expression principles supported a less restrictive approach. Applied constitutional avoidance and withholding deference where serious speech concerns arise. **Did not decide a freestanding First Amendment violation**; Board initially should decide how/when noncoercive polling is allowed within rational statutory bounds. The full writing extends well beyond the truncated comparator excerpt. [380–388.]

**Breyer, joined Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg:** JoinedI–II, dissentedIII–IV and judgment. Objected that substantial-evidence review was outside the petition's question; invoked Universal Camera's exceptional-intervention formulation; defended agency expertise in interpreting objective reasonable doubt and evidentiary rules of thumb developed through adjudication. Emphasized interview pressure, dissatisfaction versus rejection, missing substantiation for other employees' views, Mohr's limited departmental knowledge, changed workforce, unspecified majority size, and the ALJ's aggregate assessment. His argument was not simply that any agency decision deserves deference: it defended this specialized standard and the actual reasons given. [388–397.]

**Scope record:** The exact petition question is quoted at366n.1 and supplied unchanged in the sanitized facts. Scalia's explanation treats application and facial validity as within that question; Breyer disagrees. Neither the baseline neutral statement that there is no threshold issue nor a categorical assertion that application was unpresented should substitute for the actual question and published litigation record. Original petition possession is unnecessary to establish an authenticated exact quotation at this scope.

PartIII distinguishes general background interpretive doctrines from ad hoc exceptions and rejects treating lower-court consensus as authority to override the text. Souter's excluded passage is **the405–406 prosecutorial-abuse response**, not all of PartII orIII. Ginsburg is a judgment-only concurrence, not a join in Scalia's whole rationale. [400–408.]

**Souter's complete statement:** Agrees with the Court except its answer to the potential for prosecutorial abuse; joins Ginsburg's call for congressional attention to broad statutory authority. It ends at that statement. None of Ginsburg's following discussion is part of Souter's authored statement.

**Ginsburg/Souter grounds and limits:** Accept the text and affirmance while documenting agents' ability to generate a separate crime by eliciting a denial when they already know the facts, especially where underlying crimes cannot be proved or limitations have run. Discuss statutory origins, the difference from original fraudulent claims/reporting concerns, the Department's changing but consistently restrictive manuals, prosecutorial restraint, possible scienter and surprise issues left open below, and possible legislative reforms. They do not create an enforceable exculpatory-no defense or treat unenacted proposals as law; Ginsburg expressly rejects inferring legislative ratification of the defense from silence. Distinguishes criminal charges from adverse employment action (414n.5). [408–418.]

**Stevens/Breyer grounds:** Literal statutory coverage is insufficient to reject the longstanding limited exception; purpose, background principles, prior narrower readings of criminal laws, and sustained bench/bar/DOJ practice support it. Agrees with nearly all of Ginsburg's concerns but reaches a different judgment. [418–421.]

## Oubre v. Entergy Operations, Inc., 522 U.S.422

**Identity:** No.96-1291; argued1997-11-12; reviewed112 F.3d787 (5th Cir., per curiam). Official pages422–441,20 pages. Four writings and the Court's complete statutory appendix.

| Component/writing | Historical alignment |
|---|---|
| Judgment reverse/remand; noncompliant release and mere retention cannot bar ADEA suit; no tender-back precondition on this record | Kennedy, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer (6) |
| Judgment opposition | Rehnquist, Scalia, Thomas (3), on distinct grounds |
| Kennedy opinion of Court | The six judgment supporters above |
| Breyer concurrence,430–433 | Breyer and O'Connor, also full Court-opinion joiners |
| Scalia dissent,434 | Scalia alone |
| Thomas dissent,434–441 | Thomas and Rehnquist |

**Court grounds and reserved matters:** OWBPA imposes precise minimum conditions on waiving an ADEA claim; the original release and mere retention do not meet them. General common-law ratification/estoppel/tender principles cannot excuse statutory noncompliance or bar the action in this manner. The Court does not decide whether Entergy's asserted common-law rules are universally correct. It reserves restitution, recoupment, and setoff and recognizes complexity when a release may remain effective for non-ADEA claims. No decision on actual age discrimination, entitlement to damages, repayment liability, or an absolute immunity from restitution. Complete§626(f)(1)–(4) appears at428–430 and is transcribed in the sanitized facts. [425–430.]

**Breyer/O'Connor:** Agree no tender-back precondition and no ratification by mere retention. Characterize the procedurally defective release as voidable rather than absolutely void, preserving worker choice and preventing an employer from using its own statutory default to cancel promised benefits. After the employee sues, employer restitution or relief from reciprocal obligations can be possible without barring the action; amounts may be credited against damages. They read the Court as consistent with that distinction. Breyer's observation that “five or more” Justices appear to agree on voidability is not a Court-opinion adoption of a broad voidable-contract rule and must not be converted into a merged majority rationale. [430–433.]

**Scalia:** Would affirm for lack of tender back; regards the statute as not displacing common-law tender-back/ratification doctrines generally. **Rejects ratification as a second basis here:** it cannot occur until the impediment is removed, and later knowledge of noncompliance does not cure missing statutory waiver conditions. Thus his judgment agreement with Thomas/Rehnquist does not include their ratification application. [434.]

**Thomas/Rehnquist:** Would affirm because knowing retention after discovery of the defect can ratify the voidable release. The statute explicitly changes requirements for a knowing/voluntary original waiver but does not clearly abrogate ratification or tender back; those doctrines can coexist with its specific purpose. Ordinary common law governs a later ratification, and statutory waiting/revocation provisions do not naturally transpose to months of retention. Tender back is separately a condition precedent, not an act making the original waiver knowing/voluntary; common law can administer it flexibly. Agree with voidability rather than absolute voidness (436n.1), while rejecting the majority's statutory displacement analysis. [434–441.]

**Comparator defects:** Date, disposition, six-Justice Court lineup, and the three separate writing identities are correct. The Scalia excerpt improperly runs into Thomas's separate opinion. Scalia's actual dissent ends after the no-cure explanation at434, before Thomas's heading. Breyer's snippet omits the full void/voidable and restitution explanation; Thomas's snippet omits the distinction between ratification and tender back. Never report the three dissenters as all accepting ratification here, or characterize the six-Justice Court as foreclosing every employer recovery.

**Footnotes read:** Thomas1–3 and counsel/amicus note; the Court's statutory appendix in full; Breyer/Scalia have no numbered footnotes.
