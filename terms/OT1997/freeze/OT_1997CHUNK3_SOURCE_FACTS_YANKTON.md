## Record and lower-court posture

1. The Yankton Sioux Tribe's 1858 treaty reserved land in what is now southeastern Charles Mix County, South Dakota. The treaty described 400,000 acres; a later survey measured 430,405 acres. Under allotment legislation, individual tribal members received 160-acre parcels held in trust for 25 years. By 1890 the allotting agent had apportioned 167,325 acres; approximately 95,000 further acres were allotted under the February 28, 1891 Act, and some land was reserved for governmental/religious uses. Approximately 168,000 unallotted acres remained. [334–336.]
2. Three federal commissioners negotiated with the Tribe beginning in October 1892. Tribal objections and discussions included the sale price, the continuation of treaty annuities, and outstanding federal obligations. The published account reports no discussion of the future reservation boundaries in those negotiations. By March 1893, 255 of 458 eligible male tribal voters had signed. Congressional consideration was delayed by investigation of alleged fraud in obtaining signatures. Congress ratified the agreement on August 15, 1894, incorporating its full text, appropriating $600,000, and providing for scouts' claims, commemorative payments, liquor penalties, and school sections. The price was about $3.60 per acre. In 1980 the Court of Claims found the consideration unconscionably inadequate and awarded the difference between $600,000 and the value it determined, $1,337,381.50. [336–339 & n.2.]
3. President Cleveland's proclamation opened the ceded lands to settlement on May 21, 1895. Approximately 90 percent of unallotted tracts had been settled by the turn of the century. Most individual allotments were also eventually conveyed in fee to non-Indians. The published contemporary account identifies approximately 30,000 acres of allotted Indian land and 6,000 acres of tribal land. [339.]
4. Several South Dakota counties formed the Southern Missouri Recycling and Waste Management District in February 1992 to construct a municipal solid-waste facility. The District acquired the site in fee from a non-Indian. The site is within the 1858 boundaries. Its identity as unallotted land ceded under the 1894 Act is the factual predicate of the parties' claims; a 1904 Homestead Act deed to Lars K. Langeland corresponds to the complaint's parcel description. The published account notes that the allotments remained subject to the 25-year trust restriction in 1904 and that the Burke Act's authorization of early fee patents came in 1906. [340–341.]
5. The Tribe intervened in state permitting proceedings, asserting that the proposed compacted-clay liner would not adequately prevent leakage and seeking a synthetic composite liner. Following a December 1993 hearing, the State Board of Minerals and the Environment granted the permit and found state regulations did not require the requested composite liner. The Sixth Judicial Circuit affirmed; no appeal was taken to the state supreme court. [341.]
6. The Tribe filed this federal action in September 1994 to enjoin construction and to obtain a declaration that the permit failed federal EPA composite-liner requirements, 40 C.F.R. §258.40(b) (1997). The Waste District joined South Dakota as a third party to defend the State's permitting jurisdiction. [341.]
7. The District Court concluded that the Tribe lacked regulatory jurisdiction over this non-Indian activity on fee land and had not established a threat to tribal political integrity, economic security, or health or welfare sufficient for the asserted inherent-sovereignty exception. It declined to enjoin construction. **The Tribe did not appeal that denial.** The court separately declared that the 1894 Act had not altered the treaty reservation boundaries and that the site therefore lay in an Indian reservation where federal environmental regulations applied. [341–342.]
8. South Dakota appealed the declaratory disposition. A divided Eighth Circuit panel affirmed, 99 F.3d 1439 (1996), relying chiefly on the saving clause's unusually expansive language and construing other provisions narrowly to avoid conflict with the 1858 treaty. It also concluded that the historical and demographic material did not establish boundary change. This is the lower court's position, not an independently stipulated historical fact. [342.]
9. The Waste District did not appeal because it could proceed with construction, but supported South Dakota in the reviewing proceeding, stating that continuing assertions of tribal jurisdiction could affect it in this or similar settings. The EPA had meanwhile waived the composite-liner requirement and permitted construction with compacted clay; see Yankton Sioux Tribe v. EPA, 950 F.Supp.1471,1482 (D.S.D.1996). The continued controversy is not accurately described as a live request to compel this landfill's composite liner. [342 n.3.]
10. South Dakota maintains primary state jurisdiction over the ceded land. The Tribe and United States contend that it remains within the reservation; their stated textual position principally invokes Article XVIII and a construction modifying the earlier treaty only as needed to permit sales. The United States participated as amicus supporting the Tribe. The Waste District supported the State. These positions are attributed published recitations; no unexamined brief is represented as read. [332,345.]

## Treaty and agreement text quoted in the published source

The following complete quoted articles are the statutory/treaty words, not an adopted interpretation. Neutral preflight is separately checking original treaty/statutory sources; this handoff does not claim that every provision of the entire enactment is reproduced here.

**1858 treaty, Article I, quoted cession and reservation description (11 Stat.744):** The Tribe ceded “all the lands now owned, possessed, or claimed by them, wherever situated, except four hundred thousand acres thereof, situated and described as follows, to wit—Beginning at the mouth of the Naw-izi-wa-koo-pah or Chouteau River and extending up the Missouri River thirty miles; thence due north to a point; thence easterly to a point on the said Chouteau River; thence down said river to the place of beginning, so as to include the said quantity of four hundred thousand acres.” The treaty separately promised quiet and peaceable possession, restricted white settlement, and promised $1.6 million in payments or expenditure over 50 years plus $50,000 in initial assistance. [334.]

**1892 agreement as ratified, Article I:** “The Yankton tribe of Dakota or Sioux Indians hereby cede, sell, relinquish, and convey to the United States all their claim, right, title, and interest in and to all the unallotted lands within the limits of the reservation set apart to said Indians as aforesaid.”

**Article II:** “In consideration for the lands ceded, sold, relinquished, and conveyed to the United States as aforesaid, the United States stipulates and agrees to pay to the said Yankton tribe of Sioux Indians the sum of six hundred thousand dollars ($600,000), as hereinbefore provided for.”

**Article VIII:** “Such part of the surplus lands hereby ceded and sold to the United States as may now be occupied by the United States for agency, schools, and other purposes, shall be reserved from sale to settlers until they are no longer required for such purposes. But all other lands included in this sale shall, immediately after the ratification of this agreement by Congress, be offered for sale through the proper land office, to be disposed of under the existing land laws of the United States, to actual bona fide settlers only.”

**Article XVII:** “No intoxicating liquors nor other intoxicants shall ever be sold or given away upon any of the lands by this agreement ceded and sold to the United States, nor upon any other lands within or comprising the reservations of the Yankton Sioux or Dakota Indians as described in the treaty between the said Indians and the United States, dated April 19th, 1858, and as afterwards surveyed and set off to the said Indians. The penalty for the violation of this provision shall be such as Congress may prescribe in the act ratifying this agreement.”

**Article XVIII:** “Nothing in this agreement shall be construed to abrogate the treaty of April 19th, 1858, between the Yankton tribe of Sioux Indians and the United States. And after the signing of this agreement, and its ratification by Congress, all provisions of the said treaty of April 19th, 1858, shall be in full force and effect, the same as though this agreement had not been made, and the said Yankton Indians shall continue to receive their annuities under the said treaty of April 19th, 1858.” [337–338 n.1; 28 Stat.314–318.]

**School-section provision, quoted portion:** “the sixteenth and thirty-sixth sections in each Congressional township ... shall be reserved for common-school purposes and be subject to the laws of the State of South Dakota.” 28 Stat.319. The 1889 state enabling act had excepted reservation land from its school-section grant “until the reservation shall have been extinguished and such lands restored to ... the public domain.” [349.]

## Other historical material and evidentiary limits

- The 1892 negotiating record includes Commissioner Cole's warning that withdrawal of federal rations, clothes, supplies, and school funds would leave fewer than a quarter of the Tribe alive through the winter. The tribal historian testified that loss of rations would have been disastrous. These are recorded statements, not a premise that every signatory shared any particular interpretation of the agreement. [346–347.]
- The commissioners' report referred to sale of the last common-property bond and tribal interests as a thing of the past. A March 1894 letter by several chiefs/members urged ratification and recognition of state and federal law. Legislative and administrative references also described opening lands to the public domain. [352–354.]
- Later records contain inconsistent terminology. The 1896 statute referred to homestead settlers “upon the Yankton Indian Reservation,” while a contemporary commissioner's report called it the “former” reservation. Executive trust-extension orders used “Yankton Sioux Reservation”; other agency correspondence said “former” reservation or lands previously constituting part of it. A 1941 Interior opinion letter concluded the boundaries remained because allotments were scattered throughout; a 1969 agency memorandum described the 1894 Act as diminishing tribal territorial authority. The 1941 letter had not been disavowed. [354–355 & nn.5–6.]
- Fewer than ten percent of the 1858 lands were then in Indian hands, and non-Indians comprised more than two-thirds of inhabitants; state-incorporated municipalities existed within the original boundaries. Since the casino opened in 1991, tribal presence had increased, and acreage had returned to tribal or trust ownership. [356.]
- The Eighth Circuit acknowledged the State's consistent exercise of governmental authority over opened lands and stated that the Tribe presented no evidence of attempting civil, regulatory, or criminal jurisdiction over nontrust land until recently. That statement is bounded to the published evidentiary account; it is not a forfeiture, estoppel, or independent default finding. The tribal constitution, drafted in 1932 and amended in 1962, defined territory by reference to tribal land within the 1858 boundaries “now owned” by the Tribe. [357.]
