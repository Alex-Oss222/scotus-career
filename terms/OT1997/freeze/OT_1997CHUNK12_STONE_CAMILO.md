### Issue 1: Known dangerousness and personal supervisory responsibility

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Resolve the actual threshold and reviewable legal question before deciding factual matters outside the record.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** The supplied case-specific position and proposed contextual defense control this reconstruction of Stone. His proposed official-burden framework and rejection of a duplicative excuse for deliberate violations must therefore be identified openly as proposed doctrinal changes.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Delegation and official title do not automatically excuse or establish liability; the actual warning, authority, preventive opportunity, and causal responsibility govern.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Stone would affirm denial of summary-judgment immunity where repeated serious warnings, authority to act, and disregard support a causal connection to the abuse. The warning record is not merely proof that a subordinate eventually did something wrong: it can show a known, recurring danger that these officials had a concrete role in addressing. The psychiatrists’ functions and information differ from command decisions and must not be collapsed together. A reasonable response to uncertain evidence may remain defensible, but ignoring demonstrated violence cannot be sanitized as uncertainty solely because no prior case involves the same parking dispute. The trial must still prove each individual’s knowledge, culpability, and causal role.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Failure of immunity does not itself prove the constitutional claim, causation, damages, supervisory liability, or a right to trial.

**Proposed Stone disposition:** AFFIRM denial of qualified-immunity summary judgment and permit appropriate further proceedings.

**Reasoning:** Supervisory and professional officials may have constitutional responsibilities when serious, identifiable risks are known and lawful protective measures are available. The record contains evidence of repeated warnings concerning the officer's conduct and fitness. A court must assess each defendant's knowledge, authority, available preventive measures, and causal responsibility. Supervisory status alone does not establish liability, but neither does delegation of responsibility automatically excuse deliberate indifference.

**Doctrinal contribution:** Contextual supervisory liability; actual or constructive knowledge of danger; individualized responsibility; failure to take available preventive measures.
