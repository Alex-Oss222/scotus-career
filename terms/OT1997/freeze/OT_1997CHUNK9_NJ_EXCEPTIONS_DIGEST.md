## What New Jersey actually excepts to

The State accepts the Master's general landfill-sovereignty and no-prescription recommendations but excepts to three things: low water instead of mean high water; practical redesign instead of following the historical boundary; and inclusion of the pier as filled land (exceptions sheet; brief pp. 3–4, 27–28, 49). It requests the mean-high-water contour on the 1857 Coast Survey, excluding the pier, approximately 2.74 acres for New York. The Master's calculation starts with 4.69 acres at mean low water, adds approximately 0.2 acres of purported filled pier to reach 4.89, and then implements a practical boundary allocating 5.1 acres. These are different quantities, not interchangeable estimates of one contour (pp. 25–27, 48 n.21, 49 continuation).

The brief reports a 1996 trial, roughly 4,000 transcript pages and nearly 2,000 exhibits; final report March 31, 1997; New Jersey's two alternative surveys April 22; Master conference May 14; supplemented report May 30, selecting modified Survey A (pp. 5–6). These are antecedent procedural source recitations; use the operator-authorized reconstructed procedure, not automatic import of an unrecorded historical Supreme Court action.

## Compact and avulsion: agreement versus disputed rationale

New Jersey says it is **undisputed between the parties** that the post-1890 additions were artificial landfill and avulsive rather than accretive (p. 11, citing Report 97). That admission does not decide the antecedent question of whether Article II itself assigns an enlarged island or the later prescription issue.

New Jersey and the Master do not give precisely the same construction of Article II. New Jersey reads the present-tense preservation of jurisdiction as itself limiting New York to the 1834 island; the Master reads that wording in light of the federal military enclave and treats the Compact as not expressly deciding subsequent landfill, requiring the common-law avulsion rule (pp. 9–11, especially p. 10 n.5, Report 60–63). Preserve this as a real interpretive fork.

New Jersey invokes Article I's middle-water sovereign boundary and Article III(1)'s reserved submerged property, relying on Central Railroad, 209 U.S. 473, 478–79; it treats Article III jurisdiction as limited extraterritorial police power rather than transfer of sovereignty (pp. 6–10). Its assertions about the controlling force of any postdivergence cases cited in the brief are only research leads; the fresh validator must use actual simulated law.

## Prescription, notice, and contrary acts

The brief splits the history into 1834–1890 (no disputed fill), 1890–1934 (predominantly federal immigration control), 1934–1955 (immigration and some military use), and post-1955 (initial abandonment, eventual museum and overt interstate dispute), citing Report 103–106 (pp. 12–13). It contends neither continuous prescription nor New Jersey acquiescence is proved. Federal occupation reduced both States' opportunities for ordinary administration.

**Federal enclave issue:** New Jersey goes further than the Master. It argues New York ceded too much governmental authority to acquire prescription at all, citing Unzeuta and Murray; the Master treated cession as a complication affecting the quality of acts, not an absolute legal bar (p. 14 n.8, Report 110–11). The fresh packet should not merge those two positions.

**New Jersey's claimed sovereign acts and their precise sources:**

- Early objections to filling, followed by the 1904 deed. New Jersey says its demands began in 1892 (p. 35: P383(a), P405, P1 p.11). It treats the deed as a sovereign act and a rejection of New York's purported 1880 submerged-land conveyance (pp. 14–15; Report 112–13, 124–26; T886, T953–54, T4039–40). The deed was recorded in Hudson County December 23, 1904. **See the primary-appendix qualifications below; the brief's characterization is stronger than the letter's actual conditional wording.**
- Jersey City tax rolls listing the Island as federally exempt realty since at least 1940, plus water/gas utility taxes metered and paid in New Jersey (p. 16, Report 123, 131). An exempt listing differs from actual real-property tax collection.
- A New Jersey waterfront-development permit in 1933, sought by Immigration Commissioner Edward Corsi; Treasury and Army Corps references placed the work in New Jersey (pp. 16, 19; Report 134–35; P487 ¶28, P10–11; T1288–90, T2549–53, T1367–68; P374–380).
- New Jersey employment demands during 1930s projects, including Representative Mary Norton and unions; federal division of work between the States. New Jersey says these demands were expressly grounded in sovereignty, citing Report 132–33 (pp. 16, 19–20), rather than merely requests for economic favors.
- Federal Davis–Bacon locality decisions in 1947–1949 identifying Hudson County and rejecting New York construction wage rates (p. 20, P61–63; Report 135; P76–85; P490 ¶79). **Adverse qualification:** June 16, 1949 changed the locality determination to New York without a documented explanation (p. 20 n.12; P487 ¶¶45–47; P60–92, P428–434, P436–445; T2641-22 to T2645-17). Do not omit that reversal.
- 1963 Senate sale hearings with overt New Jersey sovereignty claims; New York Senator Keating recognized both States' interests, Mayor Wagner said jurisdiction could be resolved by agreement, and Representative Lindsay in 1965 described the fill as New Jersey jurisdiction (pp. 16–17, n.10; Report 137–39; P143, P154; P487 ¶¶69–78). These are relevant to New York's awareness by then, not proof of notice a century earlier.
- A 1986 Governors' Memorandum of Understanding to seek legislation sharing Ellis/Liberty tax revenues for programs for homeless persons; New Jersey enacted implementing law in 1987, New York did not (p. 17; Report 143–44 and 17a–23a; N.J. Stat. Ann. 32:32-1 et seq.). New Jersey characterizes this as recognition of taxing power. Do not convert the contemplated arrangement into a completed bilateral statutory settlement or agreed boundary.

**Federal recognition asserted as evidence, not adjudication:** Pierhead/bulkhead maps from 1890 through 1915 carry an Ellis Island, New Jersey designation (pp. 18–19; Report 118–22; appendices F–G). New Jersey says these enabled filling, not merely navigation, and notes Secretary Elihu Root's approval. It also cites the 1963 GSA legal opinion (Report 140–42; P144 p.70), 1968/1980 Park Service planning documents (P166; P484 p.9), 1984 historic-register nomination (D74 vol. III pp.1343–50; D952), and the federal New Jersey workers' compensation position in Collins, 956 F.2d 383 (pp. 20–21). These are attributed federal positions; the Park Service planning appendix estimates an original 3.5-acre area, so it is not a precise survey or a judicial determination of shoreline acreage.

## New Jersey's response to New York's acts

The response here largely adopts the Master's insufficiency conclusions. **It does not individually refute every fill-located hospital birth/death or residence item that New York may detail in its own brief.** That difference matters: the other source should not be displaced by a generic statement from this one.

- **Vital records:** New Jersey says many birth, death and marriage records are inconclusive as to fill rather than the original island (p. 22; Report 114). It gives no new certificate-by-certificate analysis in this brief.
- **Taxes:** It asserts no more than a scintilla before 1955, later evidence not tied to fill, and actual ARA Leisure sales/corporation tax payments only since 1990 (p. 22; Report 130). A Park Service employee's distribution of New York nonresident forms does not prove actual tax payments (p. 22 n.14; Report 115). The State emphasizes that the dispute had become fixed by 1984 and the tax sharing MOU followed in 1986, preceding those recent receipts (p. 23; Report 131–32).
- **Police:** New Jersey disputes credible proof of New York criminal enforcement or ordinary patrol on the island, distinguishing Harbor Police work in surrounding waters (p. 23; T2595-4 to T2614-1; T3950-23 to T3961-10). It cites countervailing New Jersey police activity (Report 114; T3636–37). This is advocacy about evidentiary weight; not a conceded absence of every New York police act.
- **Voting:** New Jersey distinguishes voter registration from proof someone voted. It says registers cover only eight years, no person claiming Ellis residence was registered after 1953 (pp. 23–24; Report 112 n.43; D957–965). It argues nine years of voting maps show the original almond-shaped island and Oyster Island, dredged away before 1900, rather than filled Ellis (p. 24 n.15; D932 p.9). This is a map-specific rebuttal, not proof that no registered resident ever lived on fill. The fresh validator should compare New York's person/building evidence.
- **Letterhead/address:** Forms using New York or New York Harbor do not necessarily state sovereign jurisdiction; immigration/postal districts did not follow State lines (p. 24; Report 116–17; T3944, T1494, P490 ¶¶129–134). INS historian Marian Smith says northern New Jersey was in the New York immigration district from 1891 to 1956, and the administrative office/post office were on the original island (p. 24 n.16; T3942-21 to T3950-22).

**Laches:** The brief reports New York's eve-of-trial amendment request, the Master's denial and allowance of relevant evidence nevertheless (pp. 5, 12 n.6; Interim Opinion 51; Report 27–29). New Jersey says laches cannot substitute for prescription/acquiescence. This brief does not furnish a separate developed record of prejudice or a complete independent laches analysis. Do not infer that the issue vanished from review or that late pleading establishes litigant default.

## Mean high water versus low water

New Jersey's substantive grounds, pp. 28–38:

1. The Master's low-water negotiation evidence was one **rejected 1827 offer**, preceding further litigation and later negotiations. Article II omits low-water language while Article III expressly uses it (pp. 28–30; Report 72; P280–292). New Jersey argues deletion/omission and overall structure outweigh that offer; the inference is disputed, not a textual express high-water designation.
2. The State uses the ordinary meaning of island and submerged/tidally flowed lands to favor high water; it invokes Article III(1) and the mid-water sovereign boundary. Handly's Lessee concerned a State bounded by a river, whereas this island is an exception within the other State's middle-water boundary (pp. 30–32, 38).
3. Its 1829 complaint described New York's adverse possession as limited to fast land (pp. 32–34; quoted in Devoe, 108 U.S. 401, 407). New Jersey denies the Master correctly called this an admission that sovereignty had already passed (p. 33 n.17). A party's older pleading is evidence of its position, not necessarily mutual agreement.
4. The 1808 New York conveyance expressly reaches ordinary high water; the 1904 New Jersey deed includes land between high and low water (pp. 34–36; T326-1 to T327-3; T2943-19 to -23; T695-15 to T706-41; P4–5, P7, P339–341, P351). New Jersey argues silent New York acquiescence in the later deed as practical construction; the primary letter/deed preserve important qualifications below.
5. An island surrounded by New Jersey waters was already an enclave, federal jurisdiction prevailed, and Congress reserved federal rights when consenting to the Compact; a modern access concern does not prove the States intended low water in 1834 (pp. 36–38). This is a direct reply to the Master's historical-convenience inference.

## Historical template versus practical redesign

The brief accepts the 1857 Coast Survey as the best evidence of the 1834 island and explains why (pp. 39–41): agency tidal measurement and forty-step method (T812-16 to T814-21), general agreement on best available methods (T3430-24 to T3431-20), scale focused on three islands, Liberty Island seawall control point and two Fort Gibson wall points matching 1980 photomap/1995 survey (Report 157). It says no proven intervening fill between 1834 and 1857, and the 1837 map lacked comparable detail/control confirmation (Report 158).

New Jersey challenges acreage estimates prepared by non-testifying Sander Prisloe: experts did not know equipment accuracy, methods or verification; Swanson was absent for part, and some calculations discarded unrecorded (pp. 40–41 n.18; T2940, T3041–42). This attacks numerical precision, not necessarily the contour's usefulness.

New Jersey acknowledges the template line would intersect a few buildings and put a strip between the Main Building and ferry landing (p. 42). It disputes **evidence of resulting administrative harm**: no instance of obstruction crossing New Jersey waters since 1834, no witness/document suggesting strip access would be blocked, no proof a fixed irregular line would be harder to identify, federal preservation consultation already involves both States, and the Park Service has supported split jurisdiction without expressing operational concern (pp. 42–44). These are responses to practical risk, not denials of the geometric facts.

The legal objection is that convenience cannot rewrite a congressionally approved compact; any new bargain belongs to States/Congress (pp. 44–46, citing Ohio v. Kentucky, Illinois v. Kentucky, Vermont v. New York and Wyoming v. Colorado). Georgia v. South Carolina's actual parts and joins must be independently assessed; this brief does not erase that distinction. Paragraphs 1–2 of the proposed decree identify fill as New Jersey and the Compact boundary, while paragraph 3's practical survey grants New York some fill; New Jersey calls that internally inconsistent (p. 46 n.19; Report 169–70; Supplement 16–18).

## Pier: concrete evidentiary objections and numerical caution

At pp. 46–48 New Jersey identifies two asserted supports for the Master: apparent accretion around the pier on an old map, and Squires's inference about an ammunition rail pier requiring fill. It supplies these specific objections:

- **Map-date problem:** The report's reference to an 1839 chart apparently means the 1819 map; no 1839 chart is in the record, and the 1819 map is the only pre-1834 map with possible accretion near the pier (p. 47 n.20; Report 158–59; T282-11 to -25). Do not silently invent another chart.
- **Pilings also trap sediment:** T282-11 to -25; T299-4 to -7; T3020-20 to T3021-4; T3061-12 to T3062-5; P382(e), P382(g). Squires conceded at least part could be dense pilings rather than fill, accounting for apparent accretion (T2928-19 to T2929-20; T3023-20 to -24; T3061-12 to T3062-5; P382(e)). This supplies a competing inference, not affirmative certainty that every part was piles.
- **Changing pier geometry, 1819–1857:** New Jersey says changes support a piling structure (T297-3 to T298-19; T313-3 to -8; T338-23 to T339-16; T3046-7 to -20; T3053-24 to T3054-3; T3059-24 to T3060-2; P382 series). The OCR of the exhibit letters is imperfect; consult the actual source before naming an exact subexhibit.
- **Engineering/use premise:** New Jersey says no convincing evidence of 1834 ammunition/rail use, cargo weight or necessary support method. Squires admitted he was not a licensed engineer and would not calculate the necessary support (p. 48; T2831-18 to T2832-25). This is a targeted foundation objection to the inference, not exclusion of all his historical testimony.
- **Alternative double count:** Even if fill existed, New Jersey says the 4.69-acre mean-low-water estimate already included the claimed filled pier, so the additional 0.2 acres was duplicative (p. 48 n.21). The printed/OCR transcript cite reads `T3061-6 to 3052-8`, internally inconsistent; do not repair it by guessing. The validator can assess the estimate against the Master and source evidence without importing false precision. The ultimate practical 5.1 acres is yet another alteration.

## Appendix qualifications that the advocacy summary can obscure

- **Appendix A, p.1a:** The 1800 New York federal cession expressly reserves execution of State civil and criminal process. Any claim that absolutely no State authority survived needs to confront that proviso.
- **Appendix B, pp.2a–5a:** The June 30, 1808 conveyance describes the Island to ordinary high water and an estimated two acres, three rood, thirty-five perches. It conveys the State's title for the specified federal purposes. This does not by itself establish an interstate sovereign boundary at high water.
- **Appendix C, pp.6a–8a, Moody July 15, 1904:** The Attorney General asks for **such title as New Jersey may have**, recognizes unquestioned New York power over the island proper, and says it would seem submerged ownership is in New Jersey under the Compact. He also asserts Congress's commerce power permits use without State consent or compensation and seeks a grant to avoid dispute and promote comity. Thus the brief's assertion of absolute federal rejection of New York's 1880 transfer overstates the letter's level of certainty. The request is strong evidence of recognition but qualified in the document itself.
- **Appendix D, pp.9a–12a:** The November 30, 1904 deed recites $1,000 consideration, conveys all New Jersey right/title/claim/interest in formerly or presently tidally flowed land, and records the grant in Hudson County December 23. It **expressly preserves all United States rights and privileges as though no grant had been accepted**. Do not omit that saving clause or infer a federal waiver; private title and sovereign jurisdiction remain distinct questions. The metes-and-bounds OCR has possible directional anomalies; this digest performs no boundary survey.
- **Appendix E, pp.13a–14a:** The 1980 Park Service environmental assessment identifies fill/submerged lands as New Jersey and the original portion as New York, but uses a 3.5-acre original-area estimate. It supports an attributed jurisdictional understanding, not precise acreages for decree drafting.
- **Appendices F–G, pp.15a–24a:** Reproduced harbor-line maps, badly OCR'd. The text of the brief identifies their dates/titles. No independent graphical verification or measurement here.
- **Appendix H, pp.25a–26a:** The 1880 New York act purports to cede its right/title and jurisdiction to specified submerged parcels for federal docks, wharves, sea walls, batteries and related structures. Its actual existence supplies the contrary claimed governmental act; its validity is contested, not erased by New Jersey's later deed.
