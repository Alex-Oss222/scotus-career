## What this adds to entering law

**Camps's controlling merits:** Breyer's Parts I–II, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Souter, plus the Chief Justice, invalidated a discriminatory residence condition on otherwise comparable private camp services. Tracy requires attention to the actual offering and supported service obligations; recipient residence alone did not create a materially different offering there. Forgone taxes were not a government purchase, and a public-benefit label did not remove private-clientele taxation from scrutiny. The opinion preserved actual public purchases, direct resident benefits, ordinary general-revenue assistance, and **genuinely different public systems** under Carbone. It did not require utility-style regulation as the only possible demonstration of a material service difference.

**Actual Tracy, February 18, 1997:** Souter's Part II, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, found no established comparable offering on that record because the regulated suppliers bore compulsory rate, reliability and service obligations and the evidence did not establish a distinct detached industrial-sales category. Neither a utility label nor equal exclusion of local outsiders alone was enough. There was no compensatory-tax or Pike holding, and no finding that all alternative taxation would endanger service. Stevens did not join this merits holding.

**Actual Fulton, February 21, 1996, Thomas, unanimous:** A local-taxpayer or deduction form does not neutralize geographic discrimination. Compensation requires an identified actual intrastate burden, a roughly equivalent nonexcessive charge, and substantially equivalent taxable events; economic linkage or aggregate contributions alone do not suffice. Camps preserved that rule, including in the Scalia/Thomas/Ginsburg dissent described below.

These holdings supplement, rather than replace, the actual Carbone, Oregon Waste and SSC rules already supplied. Waste Management differs materially from Camps: Metro **actually owns** the facility, and the dispute concerns compulsory routing and an outside-system charge, not a tax exemption based on the residence of a private charity's clients. That difference deserves adjudication; Camps does not decide it automatically.

## Material published noncontrolling positions

**Scalia's Camps dissent, joined by Thomas and Ginsburg:** The relevant comparison, in their view, was eligibility for Maine's resident-charitable assistance, rather than only the physical similarity of private camp services. Resident-serving charity performs the social-welfare function the State supports. Their primary proposed ground distinguished that limited assistance from commercial protectionism; their alternative ground accepted resident welfare as justification. They answered the suggested cash-payment alternative by observing that resident-directed cash payments would also select residents, so changing fiscal form did not establish an adequate substitute for the asserted resident-only objective.

The dissent did not deny the interstate transaction, say nonprofits never engage in commerce, discover an actual SSC purchase, find quantified governmental savings, or uphold a compensatory-tax defense. It preserved Fulton and Oregon Waste's ordinary commercial-tax rules and West Lynn's distinction between a dedicated commercial assessment and ordinary aid. It acknowledged that Tracy supplied no categorical utility exception. **Ginsburg joined this assistance/comparison ground within the existing constitutional framework.**

**Thomas's additional Camps dissent, joined by Scalia only:** They proposed abandoning the judicial negative Commerce Clause doctrine on textual and structural grounds, criticizing exclusivity, congressional silence and policy balancing. Thomas also proposed reconsidering Woodruff's interstate/foreign distinction when assessing the Import-Export Clause as a possible textual constraint on certain interstate taxes. That alternative was expressly tentative and not specifically presented by the parties; no final Import-Export Clause rule was adopted. His observation that a value-based real-property tax appeared unlike an impost or duty concerned the Camps tax, not Metro's disposal fee or routing law. **Ginsburg did not join this writing. Neither dissent changed controlling law.**

The original Waste packet's evidentiary caution against merely assuming Scalia's or Thomas's wholesale doctrinal rejection must now be read with this additional published evidence: Camps supplies an actual, later, date-eligible statement by both. Their earlier Carbone/Fulton/SSC joins remain real and relevant; they do not erase their subsequent published proposal. Conversely, the proposal does not silently erase those controlling precedents.

## Application to Waste's separate components and available paths

The lower opinion supplies public ownership, NTTC operation, compulsory routing of residential waste with a written-authorization exception, conditional pro rata routing of nonresidential waste, a separate charge for disposal outside Metro's system, environmental and fuel-supply purposes, and the recorded alternatives. It does not supply a complete operating contract, operator economics, an established Metro purchase of every covered transaction, quantified equivalent tax burdens, or additional compulsory service obligations. This supplement adds none.

1. **Public-service comparison within existing doctrine.** Camps's preserved public-system category, Tracy's actual-offering inquiry, and the three-Justice assistance dissent support examining whether Metro supplies a materially different municipal service, including the environmental and fuel obligations actually shown. Genuine public ownership distinguishes the record from the privately owned facilities in Carbone and SSC. A Justice may conclude that the routing rule should receive evenhanded-burden analysis rather than discrimination treatment, or that a sufficiently defined public-service objective supports a distinct application. The strongest counterargument is that this sovereign command still reserves independent private disposal demand to a local facility and burdens interstate processors; ownership and public purpose alone cannot establish purchase or remove that regulatory incidence. The record supports examining the distinction, not presuming every public system qualifies.
2. **Discrimination and justification within existing doctrine.** Camps's controlling refusal to let public-benefit labels cleanse private commercial restrictions supports extending scrutiny to this compulsory reservation if the services are relevantly comparable. The extension must confront the expressly reserved genuinely public case. Once discrimination is found, the actual legitimate-purpose/adequate-nondiscriminatory-alternatives rule governs; Camps and the waste cases impose no duty to disprove every imaginable substitute. The Scalia dissent's response to changing fiscal form requires testing whether the offered substitute serves the particular legitimate objective, not merely renaming it. Unlike resident-only charity, however, reliable disposal, environmental reduction and adequate fuel can potentially be served by the competitive pricing, actual municipal collection/contracts and generator/container tax alternatives reported below. Their adequacy and burdens remain legal questions on this record; no new feasibility finding is supplied.
3. **A separate fee determination.** Even a valid public routing distinction would not automatically validate the $8 outside-system fee, and invalidating the route would not decide every possible neutral charge. Examine actual incidence and any supported service difference. Camps's assistance dissent **did not** establish compensation or excuse Fulton/Oregon Waste's cumulative requirements. The lower record's absence of an identified equivalent intrastate tax burden therefore remains a material obstacle to that asserted defense. Public ownership, foregone revenue, generalized resident contributions or overall program cost are not substitute findings. Do not convert a conditional public-service rationale into a successful compensatory tax by implication.
4. **Express proposal to abandon negative Commerce Clause doctrine.** Thomas and Scalia's own Camps writing provides a source-supported broader doctrinal path that a fresh model must fairly consider. It would require an express present choice and treatment of contrary controlling authority; it is not the law entering this case and cannot be presumed from the mere identity of those Justices. A Justice may retain that objection while evaluating this case under precedent, or maintain a separate broader objection. Any Import-Export alternative must remain distinct: the Camps proposal gives no final rule for interstate service routing or this per-ton disposal fee. Its application would require statutory/constitutional analysis and an adequate issue basis; neither import/export status nor a party's presentation is invented here.

The same two injunction components and existing remedy limits remain. Nothing in Camps's state-tax refund discussion creates a refund claim, damages, substitute fee, new procurement agreement, or review of the ungranted takings component in Waste.

### Camps Newfound/Owatonna, Inc. v. Town of Harrison, No. 94-1988

Merits decision, May 19, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

On review of the Supreme Judicial Court of Maine, 655 A.2d 876, which on April 21, 1995 reversed summary judgment for the camp and sustained the challenged property tax. Argued October 9, 1996. The question is whether the nonresident-beneficiary condition in Maine's charitable-property exemption discriminates against interstate commerce and whether a subsidy, market-participant or resident-benefit justification sustains it. The Maine nonprofit's 180-acre Christian Science camp recruits nationally; about 95 percent of campers come from outside Maine. Its reported charge is roughly $400 per week and annual property taxes exceed $20,000. It timely pursued state refund and prospective-exemption procedures.

Section 652(1)(A) generally covers real and personal property owned and occupied or used solely for the purposes of benevolent and charitable institutions incorporated by Maine, without loss of exemption merely because of the source of funds or limitations in beneficiary classes, subject to the express nonresident subparagraph. An institution operated principally for nonresidents may exempt no more than $50,000 of current just property value only if the statutory average weekly charge per person does not exceed $30; above that rate it receives no exemption under that subparagraph. The computation divides all tax-year stipends or charges for services, benefits or advantages by all persons receiving them during that year, then divides by the weeks during that tax year when the institution is actually operated principally for nonresidents; §502 defines the tax year. The reported weekly price is not substituted for that statutory computation.

The nonresident subparagraph does not apply to a nonprofit corporation incorporated solely to conduct medical research; that exception does not waive the general exemption's other requirements and is not found applicable to this camp. The paragraph also includes nonprofit nursing, boarding and boarding-care facilities licensed under Title 22, chapter 1665 or its successor; nonprofit community mental-health facilities licensed under Title 34-B, chapter 3; and nonprofit child-care centers incorporated by Maine as benevolent and charitable institutions. Nonprofit means exempt under Internal Revenue Code §501(c)(3). No independent challenge to Maine incorporation, neutral qualification, religious liberty, equal protection or travel rights is decided.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. Paid interstate camp services remain protected against discriminatory taxation

**Holding and operative rule:** A charitable camp that recruits nationally and sells services to visitors crossing state lines participates in interstate commerce, although its property and performance are local and it is nonprofit. Where the same otherwise qualifying private camp offering receives worse tax treatment because its principal beneficiaries are nonresidents, that express geographic condition discriminates; a claimed resident-service distinction must rest on the actual relationship or offering, not beneficiary residence alone.

**Authority:** Breyer's Part I, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Souter, controls by six direct votes.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court examines the transaction and the comparison. *Heart of Atlanta* establishes that interstate travel to obtain locally consumed services is commerce. *Fulton* establishes that a local taxpayer and local property do not remove a tax condition keyed to an interstate relationship. Nonprofit organization does not eliminate the paid camp service and national clientele, though the Court decides no rule for every charitable act or public benefit.

*Tracy* requires attention to actual service obligations. Its regulated rate, reliability and service duties materially distinguished the offering there; it created no categorical public-utility or welfare exception. Here the asserted difference is that resident-serving charity benefits Maine's own community. That is a legitimate objective, but the present condition identifies recipients rather than a materially different private offering or supported public-service obligation. No actual service-segment uncertainty comparable to *Tracy* requires a new evidentiary hearing. The Court neither makes utility-style regulation indispensable in every case nor presumes all charitable services identical. It rejects this particular attempt to make the challenged residence criterion establish its own immunity. The same camp's tax treatment depends on changing the geographic mix of its clients. The low-charge property-value cap and medical-research exception do not make that applied condition neutral; the Court decides no hypothetical claimant's qualification under either provision.

##### II. Resident benefit, subsidy and public-capacity labels do not justify this tax condition

**Holding and operative rule:** This discriminatory private-clientele tax condition can survive only upon a legitimate local purpose that reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives cannot adequately serve; support for resident charity does not establish that showing here. Forgone taxes do not make Maine a purchaser of the camp's services or remove the discriminatory tax condition from review, and a compensatory-tax defense requires an identified intrastate burden, a roughly equivalent nonexcessive charge and substantially equivalent taxable events, none established by general resident contributions or asserted charitable savings.

**Authority:** Breyer's Part II has the same six direct joins as Part I.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court applies *Oregon Waste*'s discrimination-and-alternatives inquiry and *Fulton*'s refusal to let a deduction label cleanse geographic favoritism. Maine may support local welfare through direct resident programs, ordinary general-revenue assistance or neutral charitable-property criteria; it has not shown that resident support requires worsening a private provider's tax treatment because outsiders buy its services. The contrary argument treats the exemption itself as resident-directed aid and stresses that direct payments can also select residents. But permissible public assistance does not confer immunity on taxation controlling independent private clientele. This decision neither invalidates direct resident benefits nor requires charitable support everywhere.

*SSC* protects actual purchased performance, while no purchase, contract for camp services or government operation is established here. *West Lynn* examines practical fiscal operation and preserves ordinary assistance; Maine need not replicate its dedicated commercial assessment for a different discriminatory tax condition to fail. The tax/subsidy comparison is therefore not merely an economic equivalence inquiry. Compensation is independently governed by *Fulton* and *Oregon Waste*: no matching burden, equivalence or equivalent taxable event follows from generalized contribution or expenditure-saving claims. The Court finds no invented numerical disproportion, no litigant default and no duty to disprove every imaginable alternative. It applies the existing rule to the actual grounds supporting this condition.

##### III. Reversal requires lawful state relief, not an automatic exemption or fixed refund

**Holding and operative rule:** The judgment sustaining this discriminatory condition must be reversed and the case remanded for state-law severability, remaining neutral exemption conditions and constitutionally adequate relief. If payment was compelled before meaningful review, adequate backward-looking correction is required; independently, a clearly held-out postpayment remedy may not be withdrawn after payment and replaced by an unpursued prepayment route, even where payment was voluntary, subject to reasonable preannounced conditions and fair prospective changes.

**Authority:** Breyer's Part III, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Souter, has five direct votes. Stone-Zsela would require the exemption under the presented scheme and does not join this complete remedial choice; Scalia, Thomas and Ginsburg would affirm.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court applies *Fulton*'s separation of constitutional invalidity from the State's lawful remedial choices. *McKesson* supplies the conditional backward-looking duty when payment precedes meaningful review; *Reich* separately prevents withdrawal of the promised postpayment route. Where correction is required, lawful refunds, constitutionally permissible retrospective equalization or a lawful combination may suffice. The camp's timely pursuit of state procedures preserves its claim but does not establish the amount due or every neutral exemption qualification. Reversal therefore does not automatically reinstate every feature of the trial-level judgment. The state courts must address their law and the actual procedural predicates without enforcing the invalid residence preference. The Court orders no particular refund, interest, assessment against absent charities, unconditional enlarged exemption or substitute subsidy, and calculates no statutory weekly average.

#### Precedent Treatment

- *Heart of Atlanta Motel, Inc. v. United States*: Applies the interstate-travel and local-service principle without making all charity commerce.
- *General Motors Corp. v. Tracy*: Applies the actual-service comparison and distinguishes the supported regulated obligations there; its record-specific holding remains, with no public-utility immunity or reduced Commerce Clause scrutiny.
- *Fulton Corp. v. Faulkner*: Applies geographic-tax scrutiny despite a local taxpayer or exemption form, its cumulative compensatory conditions, and its conditional state-remedy framework.
- *Oregon Waste Systems v. Department of Environmental Quality*: Applies demanding justification after discrimination and the distinct compensation requirements; ordinary incidental-burden balancing is insufficient.
- *C & A Carbone, Inc. v. Town of Clarkstown*: Applies the private-service comparison and public/regulatory distinction while preserving genuinely different public systems.
- *West Lynn Creamery, Inc. v. Healy*: Applies attention to practical fiscal operation without requiring its dedicated-fund facts or condemning ordinary general-revenue assistance.
- *SSC Corp. v. Town of Smithtown*: Preserves protection for actual government-purchased performance; no purchase arises merely from a tax exemption.
- *McKesson Corp. v. Division of Alcoholic Beverages and Tobacco* and *Reich v. Collins*: Preserve their distinct backward-looking and promised-postpayment-remedy rules; neither supplies an automatic refund amount here.

#### Law After Decision

Effective May 19, 1997, the beneficiary-residence condition cannot sustain the unequal charitable-property treatment of these paid interstate camp services. Nonprofit status and local property do not remove the actual interstate transaction. Actual service differences, genuine public purchases and lawful ordinary assistance remain distinct; no new categorical utility or charity immunity is recognized. The statute's neutral qualifications, state severability and lawful remedial choices remain for the state courts, subject to the controlling constitutional limits. No independent holding invalidates the medical-research exception or determines a hypothetical low-charge claimant's capped exemption.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part:** The Chief Justice joins the nondiscrimination holding but would direct the Maine courts to provide the exemption under this presented statutory scheme and any refund or prospective relief authorized by state remedial law. His exemption direction is a selected remedy for this case, not a universal federal requirement to extend every discriminatory benefit. Refund remains subject to proper state-law and due-process analysis, with no amount fixed. Maine remains free to replace the scheme with a nondiscriminatory charitable policy.

**Scalia, joined by Thomas and Ginsburg, dissenting:** They regard the relevant comparison as eligibility for the State's resident-charitable assistance, rather than physical similarity of camp services alone. Property restricted to charitable work serving Maine residents contributes to the social-welfare function the State supports. They do not deny the interstate travel or declare that nonprofits never conduct commerce. Their primary ground distinguishes this limited assistance from commercial protectionism; alternatively, resident welfare justifies the geographic limitation here. They answer the proposed alternatives by observing that resident-targeted cash payments still select residents: changing the fiscal form does not establish an adequate nondiscriminatory substitute for resident-only assistance.

They preserve the ordinary commercial tax rules in *Fulton* and *Oregon Waste*, and *West Lynn*'s distinction between dedicated commercial assessments and ordinary aid. They find no successful compensatory tax, quantified state savings, compulsory utility duties or actual *SSC* purchase. *Tracy* supplies no categorical utility exception to extend; their charity-assistance distinction is their independent proposed ground. They would affirm and award no exemption, refund or new hearing. Ginsburg joins this ground alone, retaining the existing constitutional framework.

**Thomas, joined by Scalia, dissenting:** In addition to the charity ground, they would abandon the judicial negative Commerce Clause doctrine. The affirmative commerce power supplies no textual prohibition of this kind; exclusivity and congressional-silence theories do not, in their view, justify indeterminate judicial policy balancing. Thomas would reconsider *Woodruff*'s interstate/foreign distinction when examining the textual Import-Export Clause as a possible check on certain interstate taxes. That alternative is tentative: the parties have not specifically presented it, and a final Clause ruling is reserved. A value-based real-property tax appears unlike an impost at entry or a duty on imported goods, so he would likely sustain it under that alternative as well. This is no Court overruling, definitive Import-Export holding or join by Ginsburg.

#### General Motors Corp. v. Tracy

**Docket or dockets:** No. 95-1232.
**Decided:** February 18, 1997.

For the separate questions in General Motors Corp. v. Tracy, No. 95-1232 decided February 18, 1997, see Federal Courts — Standing and personal injury (The assessed purchaser may bring this challenge); Civil Rights — Rational basis for economic classifications (The economic classification has a rational basis).

##### This record does not establish the asserted discrimination

**Holding:** Ohio's exemption for sales by regulated local distribution companies does not violate the Commerce Clause on this record, because their compulsory rate, reliability and service obligations materially distinguish their regulated supply business from independent marketers' commodity sales. Commodity overlap alone does not establish equivalence of those offerings where the record does not establish a distinct industrial-sales category separated from the obligations relied upon; the holding confers no general immunity on utilities or all of their competitive transactions.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part II of Souter's opinion, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; seven Justices. Stone-Zsela and Stevens do not join.

**Material application:** The industrial-market record does not establish a distinct negotiated, unregulated sales category detached from the local distribution companies' compulsory service obligations during the 1986–1990 assessment period. Later legislation supplies no finding about those purchases.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Neither utility labeling nor equal taxation of local outsiders alone is sufficient. No finding attributes each sale to a particular public duty or presumes alternative taxation would endanger service. No compensatory-tax defense, incidental-burden claim or congressional authorization is decided.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The assessment judgment is affirmed, 7–2, without a refund, replacement rate, payment finding or new tax against utilities. Independent state classification and penalty-remission issues are unreviewed.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** West Lynn requires examination of integrated fiscal operation and Carbone of the actual service market. #### Fulton Corp. v. Faulkner, 516 U.S. 325

**Docket or dockets:** No. 94-1239.

**Decided:** February 21, 1996.

##### The issuer-based deduction discriminates against interstate investment

**Holding:** A State discriminates against interstate commerce when the tax on a resident's otherwise comparable corporate shares increases as the issuer's local income-tax exposure decreases. Neither the shareholder's local residence nor drafting the geographic advantage as a deduction removes that discrimination, and proof that a particular issuer already lost an investment is unnecessary.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Thomas), unanimous.

**Material application:** The tax is twenty-five cents per $100 of share value, reduced according to the issuer's North Carolina-apportioned income. For corporate shareholders the linked dividend-deduction proportion applies without the ordinary $15,000 dividend-deduction cap.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Oregon Waste's express-geographic-discrimination rule and West Lynn's examination of linked operation apply; a deduction label or shareholder residence does not remove discrimination.

##### Corporate income and share ownership are not shown to be equivalent taxable events

**Holding:** A compensatory-tax defense requires the State to identify the actual intrastate burden being offset, show a roughly equivalent and nonexcessive interstate charge, and establish substantially equivalent taxable events reasonably treated as mutually exclusive proxies. North Carolina's corporate-income and shareholder-property taxes do not satisfy those requirements merely because corporate profits may influence share value; the failure to establish equivalent events independently defeats the defense.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Thomas), unanimous.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Different nominal taxpayers do not invariably prevent compensation, and income and property taxes may coexist. This State failed to establish equivalent events.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Oregon Waste supplies the cumulative compensatory conditions, Maryland v. Louisiana the actual-local-burden requirement and Armco the independently fatal equivalent-events defect. Henneford preserves genuinely complementary taxation.

**Independent alternative holding:** A State does not establish the compensatory defense's actual-burden and approximate-equivalence requirements merely by invoking typical price/earnings ratios or aggregate corporate tax contributions. North Carolina's comparison fails to demonstrate the required matching burden on this record, an additional ground for rejecting its defense.

**Proposition-level authority for alternative holding:** Opinion of the Court (Thomas), unanimous.

**Limits, remedy, transition, or precedent treatment:** The 1990 claim is not governed by a new universal calculation using the later 7.75% rate; the state discussion also identifies the earlier 7% rate. Its general price/earnings reasoning is neither a statutory command nor an adopted equality finding for every taxpayer. Associated Industries requires the relevant actual matching burden, rather than aggregate or other-transaction offsets.

##### Darnell's contrary Commerce Clause permission is overruled

**Holding:** Darnell v. Indiana is overruled to the extent it permits discriminatory taxation of residents' shares in out-of-state corporations under the Commerce Clause merely by reference to a different corporate-level local tax and an asserted substantial equality. Its distinct equal-protection and standing determinations are not erased.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Thomas), unanimous.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Darnell v. Indiana, 226 U.S. 390, is expressly overruled only on its incompatible Commerce Clause permission. Oregon Waste and Associated Industries require the cumulative inquiry; Darnell's separate standing and equal-protection determinations remain.

##### Invalidity requires lawful remedial proceedings, not a predetermined refund

**Holding:** Reversal of the tax's constitutional justification requires remand for state-law severability and constitutionally adequate relief. When a State compelled payment before a meaningful opportunity to contest the tax, McKesson requires adequate backward-looking correction; independently, Reich forbids withdrawing a clearly held-out postpayment remedy after payment and substituting an unpursued prepayment route.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Thomas), unanimous.

**Limits and questions not reached:** McKesson permits lawful refunds, constitutionally permissible equalizing retrospective treatment or a lawful combination where backward-looking relief is required. Reich's protection applies to a clearly held-out postpayment remedy even after voluntary payment; reasonable previously announced conditions and prospective changes with fair notice remain permissible.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Reverse and remand for severability and constitutionally adequate relief according to actual payments, preservation, procedures and lawful state choices. No fixed refund, fee award, interest, assessment against others, blanket stock exemption, deduction change or automatic reinstatement of the intermediate appellate remedy follows.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** McKesson's conditional backward-looking duty and Reich's independent no-withdrawal protection govern relief without selecting a universal refund formula.
