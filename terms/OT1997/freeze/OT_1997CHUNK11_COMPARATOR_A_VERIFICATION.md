## Forney v. Apfel, 97-5737

Historical date: June 15, 1998; argument April 22, 1998. Ninth Circuit judgment, 108 F.3d 228, reversed and remanded unanimously; Breyer wrote; no separate writing. All eight non-Rehnquist historical Justices supported the decision. The sentence-four remand is final under section 405(g) and section 1291, and a claimant seeking outright reversal remains aggrieved when receiving only her alternative request for remand. The decision does not award benefits or decide the merits of the appeal. Finkelstein's government-appellant posture does not change the finality class. Schaefer supplies sentence-four finality; Jose supplies partial-relief appealability only to its extent adopted in this timeline. Courts cannot narrow a statutory class of appealable decisions based on docket burdens or potential success after remand. Roper's general bar addresses parties who received all requested relief; Parr concerned a nonfinal dismissal. Both claimant and Government urged appealability; appointed amicus defended the judgment.

## Phillips v. Washington Legal Foundation, 96-1578

Historical date: June 15, 1998; argument January 13, 1998. Fifth Circuit judgment, 94 F.3d 996, affirmed, 5–4. Rehnquist wrote for O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. Souter dissented with Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer; Breyer separately dissented with Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg. Those are two fully joined four-Justice dissents, with distinct preferred formulations rather than a single generic disagreement.

The historical majority decided ownership of actually generated interest only. Texas's background interest-follows-principal rule survives the absence of net realizable income. Traditional trust and marital-property exceptions do not allow the State to confiscate interest through a new declaration of ownership. Webb's supplies protection against such relabeling; Roth supplies the state-law property source. Anticipated earnings and regulation of opportunities to earn them are different from ownership of earnings already realized. Lack of positive net value does not itself negate possession, control and disposition rights. The Court did not decide whether a taking occurred, compensation owed, or the reasonableness of the Federal Reserve interpretation. Reasonable service fees remain possible; this scheme did not rest on fees for services rendered by the State. The majority's footnote 4 confines review to the property question actually presented.

Souter would vacate and remand for integrated consideration of property, taking and compensation. The client's inability to receive net interest outside IOLTA may make the isolated property ruling practically empty and distort the other questions. He identifies Penn Central's fact-specific analysis and compensation measured by the owner's loss, but does not adjudicate those unresolved questions in the Supreme Court. If the property question is reached alone, he adopts Breyer's answer. Breyer joins the remand approach and separately rejects a client property right to interest that the assumed legal framework makes impossible to earn absent IOLTA. He distinguishes Webb's because its funds independently earned interest, and uses valuation precedents to distinguish private loss from value produced through lawful public intervention. He treats principal as indisputably private property and does not hold that the State may freely appropriate it.

Material fact qualification: the published majority recites the State's concession that the attorney may assess alternative pooling across clients and must obtain interest for clients where feasible. If a proposed departure or rule depends on the scope of that concession or whether these particular funds could earn net interest independently, conduct the record inquiry required by Engine section 14 rather than turning the historical majority's characterization into an unqualified factual finding. No such new finding is supplied here.

## Pennsylvania Department of Corrections v. Yeskey, 97-634

Historical date: June 15, 1998; argument April 28, 1998. Third Circuit judgment, 118 F.3d 168, affirmed unanimously; Scalia wrote; no separate writing. State prisons fall within Title II's public-entity definition without an exception raising doubt. Gregory's plain-statement condition, assuming applicable, is satisfied by that text. Programs may benefit prisoners; qualification and participation do not imply that incarceration itself must be voluntary. The boot camp was also voluntary under the cited state statute. Breadth beyond anticipated applications is not ambiguity. Constitutional avoidance and a statutory title cannot defeat unambiguous text. Constitutional legislative-power questions were not decided below and were not reached; no determination that Yeskey otherwise satisfies every liability element or is entitled to damages follows from statutory coverage. Earlier historical Printz/Boerne references supply no operative proposition beyond the simulation's adopted law.

## Hohn v. United States, 96-8986

Historical date: June 15, 1998; argument March 3, 1998. Eighth Circuit judgment, 99 F.3d 892, vacated and remanded, 5–4. Kennedy wrote for Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Souter concurred separately while joining the entire opinion. Scalia dissented for Rehnquist, O'Connor and Thomas. The comparator's excerpt under Souter accidentally runs into Scalia's opening; do not attribute that material to Souter. Individual Cornell pages establish the boundary.

The majority held a denied certificate-of-appealability application to be a judicial case in the court of appeals, reviewable under section 1254(1), including denial by a circuit judge. Threshold rejection does not itself place a proceeding outside review. The explicit successive-application review bar in section 2244(b)(3)(E) is distinct from the absence of such a bar for certificates. House v. Mayo was overruled only to the extent it denied statutory certiorari over certificate denials; unaddressed jurisdictional assumptions did not themselves overrule it, and lower courts must follow directly controlling precedent until the Supreme Court acts. The specific jurisdictional holding's weak explanation, procedural operation and inconsistent application supported express reconsideration. The judgment returned to the Eighth Circuit for consideration in light of the Solicitor General's position; no certificate, release, automatic section 2255 relief, or general resolution of substantive procedural defaults was ordered.

Souter preferred common-law certiorari initially but joined express overruling because House was weakly reasoned, frequently disregarded, and a clear jurisdictional rule had value. This was full doctrinal joinder, not judgment-only agreement. Scalia read the certificate as the prerequisite to entry of the underlying case into the court of appeals and the statutory reference to a justice or judge as deliberately distinct from court action. A certificate request was not its own separate adversarial case. His statutory-stare-decisis argument emphasized congressional reliance on House and rejected unexamined practice as sufficient to overcome it. He also rejected extraordinary certiorari here: another circuit-justice application remained possible, an ordinary claim of certificate error was not exceptional, and the All Writs Act could not bypass the specific statutory scheme. He did not decide whether the additional requirement that a writ aid the Court's jurisdiction independently failed.
