## Business transition and administrative posture

Mack Trucks announced in May 1990 that it would sell its Allentown branch. Several managers formed Allentown Mack Sales & Service, Inc., purchased the branch assets on December 20, 1990, and operated an independent dealership. The old bargaining unit had 45 employees. All were dismissed in the transition, then 32 were interviewed and rehired from December 21 through January 1. Their new work was similar to their old jobs. Local Lodge 724 of the International Association of Machinists and Aerospace Workers, AFL-CIO, had represented service and parts employees for 17 years. [361–363,390.]

The union requested recognition and bargaining on January 2, 1991. Allentown rejected the request in its January 25 letter asserting a good-faith doubt of employee support and announcing an independent secret-ballot poll. A Roman Catholic priest supervised the February 8 poll. The vote was 19 against and 13 for the union. The union filed an unfair-labor-practice charge shortly thereafter. The poll result is a later event, distinct from the information Allentown possessed when it decided to poll. [362–363.]

The ALJ found Allentown a successor employer inheriting Mack's bargaining obligation and a rebuttable presumption of continued majority support. The Board adopted that finding; it was not challenged in the court of appeals. The ALJ also found compliance with the procedural polling safeguards of Struksnes Construction Co., 165 N.L.R.B.1062 (1967). The adverse administrative decision turned on the asserted absence of an objective reasonable doubt of continued union majority support. The Board adopted the findings and ordered recognition and bargaining. The D.C. Circuit enforced the order over a dissent, 83 F.3d1483 (1996). [363,390–391.]

**Charge-description limit:** The published introductory account describes findings under both NLRA §§8(a)(1) and 8(a)(5). A separate published source passage states that an §8(a)(5) violation was not alleged as the alternative basis for regulating the poll. This may distinguish the poll itself from refusal to bargain. The agency decision is reported at 316 N.L.R.B.1199 (1995), including the ALJ account through 1208. [363;382 n.1.]

## Employee evidence and the reasons actually given below

The employer presented managers' testimony reporting statements by 14 employees. The ALJ set aside five statements for reasons including speakers not being among the rehired workers, equivocal wording, or remoteness in time. Those five statements are not individually reproduced in this source. The ALJ accepted six employees' statements as supporting an objective doubt and was willing to assume a seventh did so, yielding seven of 32 employees. The three further statements of Marsh, Bloch, and Mohr were central disputed items. These categories should not be mechanically added: some reports concern overlapping groups, and exact employee sentiments remain distinct from the employer's asserted uncertainty. [368–371,391.]

- Rusty Hoffman said he did not want to work in a union shop and would seek another job if he had to work with the union. One other credited or assumed-credited statement said the speaker did not feel comfortable with the union and considered it a waste of $35 per month. [368.]
- Dennis Marsh told a manager, during his reemployment interview, that he was not being represented for the $35 he paid. The ALJ treated this as a desire for better representation rather than necessarily no representation. The ALJ also stated that interview remarks were somewhat tainted because applicants might say what a prospective employer wanted to hear. [369,392.]
- Night-shift mechanic Kermit Bloch told a manager during a job interview that the entire night shift, then five or six workers, did not want the union. Bloch did not testify, so he did not explain the basis of his account. The ALJ also noted that the other workers did not independently express their sentiments to Allentown and did not testify. No names or further supporting detail are supplied in this reported statement. [369–370,393–394.]
- Ron Mohr was a service-department shop steward and bargaining-committee member. He told a manager that he felt employees did not want a union and that, with a new company, the union would lose a vote. He did not express personal dissatisfaction; he said he could work with or without the union and was there to do his job. [362,370–371.]
- The ALJ acknowledged that Mohr was arguably situated to know service employees' views but said the source of his knowledge was not shown, nor whether he meant a large or small majority. The ALJ noted that Mohr referred to Mack's existing service group and did not speak for the parts employees; the composition of the rehired group could matter. The ALJ characterized the statement as almost off the cuff, referenced the Board's treatment of unverified reports of others' sentiments, and concluded that it was insufficient **even combined with the other statements**, rather than saying it carried no evidentiary significance. The Board's own explanation, in turn, said Allentown could not legitimately rely on Mohr's statement because it concerned Mack's existing employees rather than those later hired. Preserve the distinction between the ALJ's collective assessment and the Board's explanation. [370;395–397.]

The ALJ's quoted department figures were 32 old service employees/23 rehired and 11 old parts employees/seven rehired. Those quoted departmental numbers do not sum to the overall 45/32 figures. This handoff preserves the published quotation without silently reconciling its arithmetic. No proposed adjudication presently requires a finding about the missing departmental allocation; retrieve the administrative text if one does. [395.]

## Exact published petition quotation and attributable positions

The petition's question is reproduced verbatim in the source (Pet. for Cert. i):

> Whether the National Labor Relations Board erred in holding that a successor employer cannot conduct a poll to determine whether a majority of its employees support a union unless it already has obtained so much evidence of no majority support as to render the poll meaningless.

[366 n.1.]

Allentown challenged both the facial rationality of the polling threshold and its application in the court of appeals. Its reported reviewing-court submissions asserted (1) that the same threshold for polling, withdrawing recognition, and seeking a Board election makes polling useless; (2) that the evidence showed a good-faith reasonable doubt; and (3) that the Board had in practice abandoned reasonable doubt for a strict count of employee repudiations. Its brief at page 10 specifically claimed that practical abandonment. These recitations establish litigated content, not the legal scope of the petition as a decided issue. Do not replace the actual question with an unsupported certification that every possible substantial-evidence question was unquestionably granted. [363–364,372.]

The Board defended a unitary threshold; maintained that polls can disrupt established bargaining relationships and unsettle employees; stated that an employer could poll to confirm lack of majority support before withdrawing recognition; and pointed to the practical/legal consequences of elections and polls. It denied that it required a strict head count and cited cases accepting circumstantial evidence (Brief for Respondent 31–32 n.8). It acknowledged that the standard is sufficiently rigorous and fact specific that an employer often cannot know in advance whether its evidence will satisfy it (id.38). At argument the Board characterized “doubt” in its test as disbelief rather than uncertainty. [364–367,372 n.4,381–382.]

## Existing administrative rules and quoted statutory text

The agency's stated prerequisite was a “good-faith reasonable doubt,” based on objective considerations, about an incumbent union's majority support. A union's post-certification conclusive presumption and later rebuttable presumption are distinct. In this case the administrative successor finding supplied a **rebuttable** presumption. [363,390–391.]

Struksnes's procedural conditions, quoted from 165 N.L.R.B.1063, are: “(1) the purpose of the poll is to determine the truth of the union's claim to majority, (2) this purpose is communicated to employees, (3) assurances against reprisal are given, (4) the employees are polled by secret ballot, and (5) the employer has not engaged in unfair labor practices or otherwise created a coercive atmosphere.” Texas Petrochemicals added advance notice of the time and place. These are agency rule quotations; statutory authority and the legality of any additional threshold remain for independent analysis. [383.]

The source quotes 29 U.S.C.§160(e): “The findings of the Board with respect to questions of fact if supported by substantial evidence on the record considered as a whole shall be conclusive.” It separately identifies APA §706(2)(A), arbitrary/capricious review, and §706(2)(E), substantial-evidence review, and §8(a)(1)'s prohibition on interference, restraint, or coercion of protected employee rights. Neutral preflight is obtaining the complete relevant statutory texts. These excerpts are not presented as the full NLRA or APA provisions. [377,382.]

A losing union in a formal Board election is subject to the one-year election bar, 29 U.S.C.§159(c)(3); a union losing an employer poll can request a Board election. The reported Board position and rules regarding an employer losing its own poll include recognition and a reasonable bargaining period. [366;385 & n.3.]
