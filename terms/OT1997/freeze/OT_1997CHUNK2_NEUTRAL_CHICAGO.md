## 4. City of Chicago v. International College of Surgeons — December 15, 1997

### Event, record, and question

Argument was October 14, 1997. ICS owned two North Lake Shore Drive mansions. The Commission preliminarily designated a landmark district in July 1988; the City enacted the designation ordinance in June 1989. A February 1989 sale/redevelopment contract contemplated retaining the facades and building a condominium tower. ICS applied for demolition permits in October 1990. The Commission denied them on historical/architectural grounds and denied renewed applications under the economic-hardship exception.

Two Cook County complaints under the Illinois Administrative Review Law asserted facial and as-applied federal due-process, equal-protection, and takings claims, unconstitutional administrative procedure, state constitutional claims, and statutory review grounds. After removal, the District Court consolidated the cases, dismissed some constitutional claims, retained state claims, and granted the City summary judgment, upholding the ordinances/procedure and finding the administrative decisions supported and not arbitrary. A third Lakefront Protection Ordinance action is outside this review.

The Seventh Circuit, 91 F.3d 981 (1996), directed remand for lack of jurisdiction. It distinguished de novo review from appellate-style review confined to an agency record, treating the latter as inconsistent with §1441(a)'s original-jurisdiction requirement even alongside independently removable facial federal claims. Illinois §5/3–110 bars new supporting or opposing evidence and treats agency factual findings as prima facie true and correct; legal and factual review extends to the administrative record. The circuit classified as-applied and state-review claims as record-bound and facial claims as independent of that record.

The City seeks reversal of this jurisdictional judgment. ICS argues that its chosen statutory administrative-review proceeding, rather than a plenary §1983 action, does not acquire original jurisdiction merely by presenting federal grounds. ICS separately urges abstention and discretionary refusal under §1367(c); the District Court considered economy, convenience, fairness and comity, while the Seventh Circuit did not decide those alternative retention arguments. ICS's assertion that Illinois appellate courts had not construed the ordinance or decided the state constitutional questions remains its contention, not a verified exhaustive precedent search. No reversal of the jurisdictional ground automatically resolves these retention questions or the constitutional merits.

### Law entering the event

Sections 1331 and 1441(a) supply original federal-question jurisdiction and removal of a qualifying civil action; federal claims must arise from the well-pleaded complaint and be substantial. Section 1367(a), subject to express exceptions, supplies supplemental jurisdiction over other claims so related to claims within original jurisdiction that they form the same Article III case or controversy, including claims involving additional parties. Section 1367(c) separately authorizes declining a claim for novel/complex state law, substantial predominance, dismissal of all original-jurisdiction claims, or exceptional circumstances presenting other compelling reasons. Statutory power and its discretionary exercise are different questions. Gibbs, 383 U.S. 715 (1966), supplies common-nucleus background and the values of economy, convenience, fairness, and comity; it is not permission to disregard the enacted conditions.

**Peacock (February 21, 1996)** denies new full-debt liability against a new judgment debtor without an independent jurisdictional basis. It distinguishes ordinary execution from a later controversy involving new facts and duties. Its original action predates §1367's effective boundary and the later case supplies no original claim; it does not decide same-case supplemental review of administrative decisions. Thomas's ancillary holding has eight votes; Stevens's restorative-enforcement position is noncontrolling.

**Quackenbush (June 3, 1996)** unanimously separates statutory jurisdiction from abstention and the appellate route. Section 1447(d) covers remands actually resting on covered §1447(c) grounds; a separate abstention remand is not converted into a jurisdictional defect. Conclusive surrender of the federal forum supports the specified §1291 routes. Burford permits outright dismissal/remand only when relief is equitable or otherwise discretionary; an independent legal-damages claim may be stayed on a proper showing but cannot simply be surrendered. Neither the existence of abstention predicates nor a stay was decided universally. These limits must be matched to the actual relief here.

Exact modules: **Covered removal grounds and the bar on appellate remand review** (Things Remembered, December 5, 1995; Quackenbush clarification June 3, 1996); **Appeal of an abstention order surrendering the federal forum** and **Burford abstention and independent legal-damages claims** (Quackenbush, June 3, 1996); **Ancillary enforcement and a new judgment debtor** (Peacock, February 21, 1996). **Article III power before disputed statutory merits** (Steel Co., June 2, 1997) prevents merits-first avoidance of a genuine jurisdictional issue, but does not itself decide supplemental power here.

### Supported competing paths

The City can rely on substantial pleaded federal claims, common facts, and §1367(a)'s broad coverage of related claims; a state procedural label does not by itself alter federal statutory jurisdiction. ICS can argue that the demanded appellate-type review of a local adjudication falls outside the original civil action Congress authorized for district courts, and that federalism and state administrative structure matter to the statute's scope. The City must answer that structural argument; ICS must answer the enacted same-case language and distinguish actual state-court judgments from administrative action.

The Court may recognize power and return for properly preserved discretionary or abstention questions; it may conclude that the particular review claim lies outside the statutory grant and direct the corresponding remand; or it may resolve a supported narrower claim-classification or procedural ground. A decision recognizing supplemental power need not mandate its exercise or reinstate the District Court's entire merits judgment. A decision declining jurisdiction must identify whether it denies power or exercises lawful discretion; the remedy and appellate consequences differ. No categorical rule concerning every administrative appeal, diversity action, §1447 remand, or state judgment is assumed.
