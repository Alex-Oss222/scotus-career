**Event:** No. 120, Original; exceptions to the Master's Final Report of March 31, 1997 and Supplement of May 30, 1997; argued January 12, 1998; decision event May 26, 1998.

**Status and boundary:** Frozen independent modeling handoff, not a completed Court action or opinion assignment. ## Channel and component definitions

This Court exercises its original jurisdiction over an actual interstate sovereignty dispute under Article III and §1251(a). There is no appellate judgment to affirm or reverse and no appellate equal-division rule to substitute for judgment. The Master's supported technical findings receive weight, but Louisiana requires independent decision, not Rule 52 appellate deference. A lower private dispute's boundary interpretation cannot bind these States on sovereignty under Mississippi v. Louisiana.

The six components below are distinct:

1. **Compact:** original-island reservation versus enlargement of New York's territory by artificial fill.
2. **Prescription:** actual possession/dominion over the fill and New Jersey's sufficiently long acquiescence.
3. **Laches:** the independently asserted delay-and-prejudice defense, rather than an alternative name for prescription.
4. **Shore:** the 1834 mean-high-water versus mean-low-water extent of the original island.
5. **Pier:** proof that the disputed projecting structure was land in 1834 and support for a separate 0.2-acre addition.
6. **Implementation:** legal authority for the revised 5.1-acre practical configuration and the work required for an eventual decree.

## Common legal premises

All eight accept the Compact's federal-law character and its distinct allocations. Article First sets the sovereign midwater boundary except as particularly provided; Article Second reserves New York's island jurisdiction; Article Third's commerce/navigation jurisdiction and specified property rights do not simply restore New York sovereignty west of the line. Central Railroad supplies that distinction without being an earlier judgment binding the States on this precise island. Article Third's wharf, fastened-vessel, quarantine/health/passenger-law and fisheries provisions retain their actual separate terms. Articles Fourth–Seventh likewise do not supply an unexpressed territorial transfer. The United States' express reservation is preserved.

The present land additions are artificial. No Justice converts the record into gradual natural accretion merely because filling took years or was federally undertaken. Georgia's application to mixed dredging and sedimentation was factual; Scalia and Kennedy's disagreement there did not reject the governing law. Nor does Louisiana's continuing-island rule decide this Compact's artificial enlargement. Property ownership, federal enclave jurisdiction, residual state functions and interstate sovereignty remain distinct. The 1904 deed is relevant evidence, not a comprehensive title judgment, and the roughly .5672-acre retained-ownership description does not itself settle every territorial issue.

Under Illinois and Georgia, New York bears the preponderance burden on long, continuous possession and assertion of sovereignty over the actual disputed area and New Jersey's long acquiescence. No fixed universal number of years, formal-protest requirement, mandatory state-service checklist or categorical federal-enclave exemption is adopted. A State's officials' statements and conduct matter, and silent acquiescence requires a sufficiently open, adverse territorial claim. A later objection cannot undo prescription already completed; whether completion occurred first is the disputed question. Kansas did not choose a different general compact proof quantum, and its laches holding did not decide categorical availability.

## Enhanced component comparison and provisional conclusions

### 1. Compact: retain the original island, not an automatically expanding artificial enclave

**Supported rule and result:** All eight reject New York's claim that Article Second alone carries sovereignty to these artificial additions. Read together, Articles First–Third leave the underwater territory west of the midwater boundary in New Jersey while preserving New York's existing island enclave. The Compact supplies no agreement that adding fill to that enclave transfers the other State's territory. Ordinary artificial-change principles therefore operate; federal filling changes the physical shore without itself changing interstate sovereignty.

The shared sufficient ground is the whole Compact and the absence of a bargain displacing that consequence, not the assertion that the adjective “present” alone gives metes and bounds. That word supports retention of an existing jurisdictional object, but can also describe the jurisdiction's quality after the federal cession. The Master's federal-enclave explanation of it is not silently adopted as the only reading. The two routes cannot be merged into a fictitious common textual holding.

**Strongest competing path:** New York invokes the named island as an enduring entity, foreseeable reclamation, the lack of a size limit, the 1827 negotiations, existing filling powers and the Compact's navigational purpose. A reservation may sometimes encompass later change; artificial formation does not answer interpretation before the agreement is construed. But foreseeable filling elsewhere establishes no reciprocal agreement to relinquish sovereignty over this substrate. Article Second has real work preserving original islands on New Jersey's side of Article First, unlike the redundancy Kennedy identified in Georgia's differently worded natural-island reservation. The 1889 map and 1921 silence provide no express enlargement provision, and the 1921 agreement preserves the earlier allocation except where actually modified.

Whether the later hospital tracts were temporarily distinct islands need not independently decide the compact question. Pilings, gangways and submerged cribbing are not interchangeable with continuous dry land, but even attachment would not establish a transfer. Conversely, the case establishes no universal rule that every later connected feature is legally a new island. The continuing-island theory is rejected for this artificial enlargement under this Compact.

**Exception:** Overrule New York's compact-construction exception. This decides original entitlement only; prescription remains independent.

### 2. Prescription: a genuine disagreement over the complete record

**Five-Justice provisional conclusion:** O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg find New York has not carried the burden and would overrule its prescription exception. New York has real evidence of state functions directed to fill residents and facilities. The hospital-specific birth/death certificates, electoral records identifying residents of Islands 2 and 3, compensation treatment and particular construction records cannot be dismissed as merely generic island labels. Federal control also means ordinary police, taxation and service patterns cannot be mechanically demanded. Those points make this a close cumulative-evidence question.

The evidence nevertheless does not establish the requisite continuous adverse dominion and acquiescence over the additions. The identified electoral years are intermittent; registration and an actual vote are distinct, and the record's old map outlines complicate what territory the administration openly claimed. Specific hospital records prove genuine acts at identified locations, but sparse vital records are not themselves a continuing, unmistakable territorial assertion throughout the enlarged tract. Federal choice of wage scales and construction standards has varying significance; statutory or obligatory compensation treatment is stronger than a convenient benchmark, yet it cannot turn every federal choice into New York's exercise of sovereignty.

The contrary evidence must also be weighed precisely. The 1904 deed and recordation were assertions of New Jersey's proprietary interest with territorial implications under the construed Compact, not a federal admission deciding all sovereignty. The permits sometimes concerned submerged property, and exempt county rolls are weakened by whole-island descriptions and uncertain notice. Neither is treated as conclusive state possession of the dry fill. The 1934–35 controversy nevertheless publicly challenged a New York-only geographic understanding; the later federal decision favoring New York is considered, not omitted. The 1947–49 New Jersey wage episode was reversed in 1949 and no fictional State communication is added, but that oscillation and the conflicting federal map descriptions undermine a claim of uniformly recognized New York territorial dominion. Norton, Hague, federal labor officials and the States are not treated as interchangeable actors. The significance of an episode may be uncertainty about the openness or exclusivity of the asserted sovereign claim, even where it does not prove a formal New Jersey protest.

The five do not require New Jersey to have issued a particular legal notice or filed this suit earlier. Nor do they hold that a 1955 protest alone would defeat a title already acquired. They find the earlier cumulative evidence insufficient to establish the necessary acquisition before that controversy. The post-1955 record then supplies ongoing disagreement, rather than an unbroken later period curing the deficiency. This is an evidentiary conclusion under the preponderance standard, not an increase in the burden to certainty or a numerical count of governmental acts.

**Three-Justice provisional conclusion:** Stevens, Scalia and Breyer would sustain New York's prescription exception and recognize New York sovereignty over the enlarged island on that independent ground. They accept the same legal test but find its burden met by 1954. Their conclusion rests on decades of residual sovereign administration suited to this federally controlled territory: repeated election registration of identified fill residents, hospital vital records from fill facilities, marriage-law administration to the extent supported, compensation and construction arrangements specifically linked to the additions, and limited actual police/fire activity considered at its proper weight. They do not infer that every listed registrant voted, every marriage occurred on fill, every wage reference was mandatory, or every fireboat response asserted land sovereignty.

Their decisive distinction from Illinois is that the strongest acts here are affirmatively tied to the disputed fill and its resident community, not simply to an acknowledged central sailing channel or undisputed original tract. “Ellis Island” usage alone would be insufficient, but the identification of fill-resident and hospital activity makes the usage more than a label. Public administration of the resident community under New York law across decades supports notice of a practical claim covering the enlarged federal installation. Neither a new quantified yearly frequency nor a finding of personal knowledge by a named New Jersey officer is invented.

They find the competing episodes insufficient to defeat that inference or establish timely nonacquiescence. The 1904 transaction primarily concerns New Jersey's submerged property, which Article Third can preserve separately from administration of the enlarged island. Waterfront permits and water-main work likewise have an independent submerged-land explanation. Undistributed or obscure exempt rolls covering concededly New York land are weak notice of an adverse fill boundary. The 1934–35 jobs dispute contains local and federal positions rather than a decisive State territorial assertion and ended in a federal return to New York jurisdiction; the two-year wage experiment also ended in restoration of New York treatment. The maps are conflicting, sometimes derivative and not equivalent to competing state administration. These matters prevent treating the history as unanimous but, in their assessment, do not defeat the preponderance of long practical New York dominion and acquiescence before the later sustained public contest.

These three do not declare that only a governor or formal original complaint can preserve sovereignty. They reject the rival evidence for its particular object, provenance and circumstances. Nor do they assume federal ownership bars prescription or that possession of the original island automatically possesses every later addition. Michigan's described-tract analogy is supporting context only: the fill-directed activity and asserted coverage of the enlarged installation must do the actual work. Their judgment is an acquisition established by 1954 on the cumulative record, not a newly announced fixed prescriptive term or an invented earlier acquisition date. Under this path later protest does not undo the acquired sovereignty.

**Separate-writing function:** The disagreement must be explained as a disagreement over the territorial significance, continuity and notice of concrete acts. A dissent supporting New York must confront the deed, permits, maps and both federal reversals; a controlling rejection must confront the specific fill-resident and hospital evidence. Neither account may substitute “every immigrant thought New York,” unsupported motives or an inference about destroyed records for the admitted proof.

### 3. Laches: reject the independent defense without inventing lost evidence

All eight support rejecting the pleaded independent laches bar if it is necessary to reach it. Their common sufficient ground is that, assuming a distinct compact-enforcement laches defense could be available here, New York has not established material litigation prejudice attributable to New Jersey's delay sufficiently to bar this sovereignty claim. The claim concerns lost archives, expired retention periods, vandalism, leaking roofs, absent guards and deceased adult witnesses, and both affirmative proof and rebuttal. It is not rejected on the false premise that New York offered no evidence.

The strongest contrary path is that waiting until the immigration-era records and witnesses disappeared materially impaired a defense even if New Jersey had voiced disagreement earlier. That is a legally distinct claim from acquiescence and deserves its own consideration. But the circumstances of loss do not establish that the missing material would supply the disputed adverse dominion or notice, or defeat particular contrary acts. No precise missing document or witness account is required as an impossible universal prerequisite; the problem is that this record does not connect the asserted loss to a sufficiently supported material impairment and its attribution to the delay. The Court may not presume favorable contents merely because archival conditions were poor.

Illinois supplies an additional reason for caution: interstate boundary repose ordinarily operates through prescription and acquiescence. O'Connor and Souter are willing to apply that boundary treatment as an additional reason here. The common opinion need not turn it into a categorical rule for every compact case, which Kansas expressly left open. The common assumed-availability ground does not decide whether New Jersey's delay was inexcusable, choose a different laches remedy, or silently establish any prescriptive element. Stevens, Scalia and Breyer need no laches bar for their preferred prescription disposition, but authorize joining this bounded alternative rejection if the Court resolves the component.

**Exception:** Overrule New York's independent-laches exception. Do not make loss of evidence prove sovereignty or treat a laches dismissal as an ownership decree.

### 4. Historical shoreline: mean high water for the retained original island

If the Court rejects prescription and must fix the retained enclave, all eight would sustain New Jersey's high-water exception. The operative legal line is the original island's mean-high-water shore in 1834, to be geographically located through the accepted historical evidence. This is a construction of this Compact and its record, not a universal constitutional definition of an island or a rule that private tideland title always establishes interstate sovereignty.

The best cumulative construction preserves Article First's sovereign allocation of surrounding waters/substrate and Article Second's existing island, without silently adding a low-water reservation absent from its final text. Article Third expressly uses low-water language for its particular water-jurisdiction allocation; its express term is not mechanically imported into Article Second. The 1808 high-water conveyance and approximate 2.97-acre description are not themselves a sovereign boundary decree, but corroborate the understood fast-land object. The 1829 description and later tideland claim likewise carry evidentiary weight without becoming dispositive concessions. The omitted low-water term from an unsuccessful 1827 proposal is relevant negotiation evidence but is weaker than the text and the entire final allocation.

The strongest low-water path invokes Handly's Lessee, Vermont, physical access to navigable water and the Master's inference about likely original intent. Those cases concern different interstate river arrangements; they do not establish a rule that every reserved tidal island owns sovereignty to low water. This island's federal-enclave setting and the Compact's particular navigational arrangements further weaken an inference that a sovereign strip to low water was indispensable. No obstruction of access is proved. Convenience may illuminate an ambiguous bargain, but cannot supply a missing territorial transfer.

The 1857 Coast Survey's historical contour is the supported starting point, with the Master's control-point analysis and the evidence of negligible relevant change before major filling. Acceptance of that contour does not adopt every non-testifying technician's area computation or establish 2.74, 2.97 or a later 3.5 acres as the decree. A legal line and the acreage enclosed by a precisely authenticated survey remain different jobs.

### 5. Pier: no unproved footprint or independent 0.2-acre award

All eight, if the retained enclave must be located, would sustain New Jersey's pier exception to the extent of rejecting the Master's separate addition and any assumption that the projecting pier's entire footprint constituted land in 1834. The record does not sufficiently establish that proposed inclusion. Squires's fill inference from charted surrounding material and the supposed engineering needs of ammunition traffic must be evaluated against the admitted piling explanation, changing shapes, unproved load/use assumptions and his engineering limitation. Sediment around a structure does not itself prove that the relevant footprint was solid land at the governing date.

The strongest competing path credits the Master's assessment of a functional military facility and the mapped surrounding material as circumstantial proof of early fill. Independent original review does not require eyewitness evidence or a perfect map. But the alternative support mechanism was admitted, the supposed loading demands are not established sufficiently, and the source-description problem cannot be cured by citing an “1839 chart” whose identification is contested. The Court may use the supported 1819 map at its actual scope, not silently invent a different exhibit. The evidence does not justify converting the structure's projected area into retained sovereign land.

The .2-acre allowance also raises a separate calculation question. New Jersey says the 4.69-acre estimate already contained the claimed pier portion; the Master says it supplies the excluded half. The inconsistent transcript citation is not repaired by guesswork. Rejection of the unsupported separate award does not require deciding whose unexplained numerical account is correct, particularly when the 4.69-acre low-water base is not the adopted legal line. The survey must neither add the .2 mechanically nor exclude genuine fast land independently shown within the adopted 1834 high-water contour. No duplication, stipulated survey error or new measurement is found.

**Exception:** Sustain the objection to the separate pier allowance and unproved footprint. Rejecting those recommendations is a merits evaluation of the available evidence, not a research-default sanction or a command to reopen the whole trial. Any necessary technical clarification must implement the chosen historical line and may not revive the allowance without adequate proof.

### 6. Convenience configuration and the later decree

All eight reject judicial replacement of an established Compact boundary with the proposed modified Survey A. Its approximately 5.1 acres, right-angle corridor turns, Main Building and Ticket Office margins, ferry-access arrangement and staircase allocation are changes in what territorial parcels each State receives, not merely a more accurate rendering of the historical tide line. The alternative 4.89-acre plan likewise has no entitlement simply because it matches a different approximate acreage calculation. Neither party's survey assistance, acceptance of Marchuk's accurate execution, or preference among alternatives is consent to changing sovereignty.

The strongest adjustment path invokes original-jurisdiction equity, uncertain ancient mapping and the practical value of avoiding boundaries through buildings. The Court has authority to resolve genuine geographic uncertainty and formulate an effective decree. But Texas v. New Mexico and the actual Kansas holding forbid turning that authority into a new compact bargain. Georgia's equitable maritime work concerned a line the treaty had not fixed; Stevens and Scalia's maritime dissent did not authorize exchanging land fixed by a compact. The absence of demonstrated administrative unworkability, denied access or an NPS operational objection further defeats an inference that these replacements are necessary implementation. Approximate acreage equivalence is not equivalent territorial entitlement.

If the Court adopts the five-Justice prescription rejection, the ensuing direction is for an authenticated proposed survey and decree locating the 1834 mean-high-water boundary consistently with the accepted historical contour and the pier ruling. The States may agree on a conforming technical description; unresolved implementation issues may be returned to the Master for focused work. This does not compel a new evidentiary trial, create a new compact or require an independent full survey where an authenticated conforming description is already available. The Court must approve the eventual decree in a separately supported dated action.

The March 31 proposed decree and May 30 Designated Survey are not adopted or treated as an entered decree. This merits event determines entitlement and necessary implementation directions. It supplies no invented coordinates, exact final acreage, immediate geographically unspecified injunction, execution date or automatic closure of the original docket. A requested injunction against enforcement on New Jersey's territory must be framed consistently with the settled line and federal reservation at the decree stage. The United States' rights, ownership questions and unpresented municipal/regulatory consequences are preserved.

Under Stevens, Scalia and Breyer's preferred prescription result, a decree would instead recognize New York sovereignty over the enlarged island subject to the United States' reserved authority; no historical-line reconfiguration is necessary. They nevertheless authorize the specific alternative shoreline, pier and anti-reconfiguration joins described below if the Court rejects their prescription position. Those conditional joins do not change their preferred territorial judgment.

## Joinder and remedy audit

The compact ground has eight Associate commitments. The prescription rejection has five—O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg—while Stevens, Scalia and Breyer support acquisition by New York. No Stone vote is counted and no final Court count is inferred.

The independent laches rejection has the eight Associates' support on the common assumed-availability ground, if reached. O'Connor and Souter's additional Illinois boundary rationale is not counted as a separate common holding. This avoids disguising a narrow evidence holding as resolution of laches availability for every compact dispute.

The five who reject prescription directly support the historical mean-high-water line, rejection of the unproved pier allowance, rejection of Survey A's territorial reconfiguration, and a separate authenticated decree. Stevens, Scalia and Breyer authorize joins to those legal and technical components as alternative holdings if the Court rejects prescription; they still dissent from the resulting allocation because of their prescription conclusion. Assembly must state those exact relationships, rather than treating preferred sovereignty over the whole island as an unqualified vote for the smaller enclave. No Marks synthesis of different compact rationales or prescriptive theories is offered.

All modeled paths preserve the United States' express Compact reservation and separate private/federal ownership from interstate sovereignty. The prescription split changes which territorial entitlement the eventual decree implements. It does not itself authorize a property-title decree, resolve all municipal law, determine a particular tax, create a new compact, or make the Master's proposed survey operative.
