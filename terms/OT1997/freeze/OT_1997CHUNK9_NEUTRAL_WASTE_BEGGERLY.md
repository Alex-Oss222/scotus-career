## 1. Waste Management, Inc. of Tennessee v. Metropolitan Government of Nashville and Davidson County

### Identity, vehicle, grant, and record

**Scheduled event:** May 27, 1998, an operator scheduling assignment under the user's authorization to review this lower-court matter. There is no assigned Supreme Court docket or historical merits citation. **Court below:** Sixth Circuit, Nos. 95-5863 and 95-5900, 130 F.3d 731, decided November 5, 1997; argued there August 6, 1996. Source: [complete lower opinion](https://static.case.law/f3d/130/html/0731-01.html), mechanically copied in the companion.

The district court permanently enjoined the disposal fee, declined to enjoin the routing provisions, and denied relief on the passenger-car/pickup ordinance. WMIT appealed the latter two rulings; Metro cross-appealed the fee injunction. The Sixth Circuit expressly exercised **28 U.S.C. §1292(a)(1)** jurisdiction over the injunction dispositions. It reversed denial of the routing injunction, affirmed the fee injunction, and vacated/remanded the passenger-vehicle/pickup ruling for takings and ripeness consideration. Do not describe this as an appeal from a single final judgment under §1291 or as a lower-court finding that a physical taking occurred.

The authorized Supreme Court scope consists of two preserved, ripe Commerce Clause issues: (1) whether compulsory routing to this municipally owned facility discriminates against interstate commerce; (2) whether the separate fee on disposal outside Metro's system is discriminatory or a valid compensatory charge. Supreme Court review of a case in the court of appeals is available under §1254(1); the remaining takings proceedings are not a §1257 state-finality obstacle. The grant does **not** include the passenger-car/pickup taking or its ripeness. No additional argument date, filing deadline, grant order, or resolution of the separate property component is invented.

The parties stipulated the facts relevant to this appeal. Preserve the following distinctions:

- Metro owns the waste-to-energy facility; Nashville Thermal Transfer Corporation operates it. NTTC supplies power to downtown buildings. Public ownership is an established fact; the record supplied does not establish the operator's ownership, profit distribution, nationality, procurement terms or a contract transferring commercial risk. A private corporate operator does not itself make the plant privately owned.
- The rules require licensing and approved disposal sites. Unless the Director specifically authorizes otherwise in writing, all residential solid waste collected within Metro must go to NTTC. Nonresidential waste need not go there unless allocated to meet the plant's operating need, reported as 6,300 tons per week. The quoted regulation requires monthly reporting, at-least-quarterly pro rata calculations using best available data, advance NTTC notice and advance notice of each collector's estimated share. The exact quoted qualifications are copied in the companion; do not collapse this into an unconditional requirement for all waste.
- The appellate narrative describes a $36-per-ton NTTC tipping fee and a separate $8-per-ton outside-system disposal fee. **Footnote 2 also reproduces an enacted increase to $44 per ton effective January 1, 1995**, with cubic-yard equivalents. Do not freeze $36 as the only enacted rate or use an unverified rate to calculate competitive losses. The outside-system fee is $2 per cubic yard or $8 per ton, imposed on waste generated/collected within Metro and disposed at a non-Metro facility within Metro or collected within Metro and disposed outside its boundaries. The lower court reports that NTTC and other Metro-owned sites are exempt.
- WMIT has a Davidson County transfer station and landfills in Benton County, Tennessee, and Logan County, Kentucky. BFI has no transfer station and disposes at its Rutherford County, Tennessee, landfill. Sanifill uses NTTC, WMIT's transfer station or BFI's landfill. Thus intrastate competitors also suffer exclusion or charges; actual interstate alternatives exist. That does not by itself answer discrimination classification.
- Metro asserts reliable fuel supply, environmental service and compliance with Tennessee's requirement to reduce by 25 percent the volume disposed in landfills/incinerators. The Sixth Circuit identified competitive tipping charges as an alternative and recorded Metro's concession that increased direct or contracted collection could secure a stream, though legislation and preparation would be needed. The court recorded both parties' acknowledgment of a nondiscriminatory generator/container tax alternative. These are the reported alternatives, not a new finding that every substitute is free, immediate or equally profitable.
- Metro supplied no evidence of a specific intrastate tax burden that the $8 charge compensated. That lower-record deficiency is distinct from a statutory declaration of cost recovery, and from a finding that Metro incurs no waste-management costs at all.

The lower court treated the routing and fee as discriminatory under its understanding of Carbone and Oregon Waste. It reasoned that reserving only part of the demand changes discrimination's extent, not its existence. That is the judgment under review; the fresh model must apply the **actual simulated** versions, whose public/private boundaries differ in legally important ways from an undifferentiated citation.

### Entering law and material forks

**Actual Carbone, May 16, 1994:** Kennedy, with Stevens, Scalia, Thomas and Ginsburg among the six controlling Justices, invalidated coerced private transactions reserved to a selected local **private** processor. Competing local exclusions do not cure that private-service discrimination. A tonnage guarantee and option to purchase did not make the service a present municipal purchase. The Court expressly left a materially different genuinely public system undecided. O'Connor supported the judgment only under Pike; Souter dissented on the municipal-service and insufficient-burden grounds, without categorical proprietary immunity.

**Actual SSC Corp., March 11, 1996:** Kennedy's unanimous opinion permitted a destination term for collection **and disposal actually purchased by the Town**; private ownership and contractor performance did not negate that purchased service. The Court separately applied Carbone to a coercive ordinance reserving other private business, unanimously, with O'Connor and Souter retaining their original classification objections. Investment, financial guarantees and contractual labels alone did not establish public ownership or transform independent purchases into government purchases. Its materially different public-system reservation survives.

**Actual Oregon Waste, April 4, 1994:** Thomas's opinion, joined by all currently seated Associates then serving, except Breyer who was not yet seated, requires an identified intrastate burden, rough approximation without excess, and substantially equivalent taxable events for compensation. General income taxation and revenue comparisons alone fail. It rejects a geographic surcharge at private facilities while leaving government-owned landfill charges and materially different proven costs undecided. Once discrimination is established, the legitimate-purpose/adequate-nondiscriminatory-alternatives inquiry governs; Pike cannot replace it. None of these decisions requires perfect certainty or disproving every imaginable substitute.

The alternatives are meaningful and component-specific:

1. **Apply the discrimination rule to coercive public-facility routing.** Treat protection of this local processing demand, despite public title, as the relevant geographic service preference. Confront the express Carbone/SSC reservation: this would resolve an open extension, not mechanically apply a prior public-system holding. Evaluate the actually recorded environmental purposes and feasible neutral alternatives. If the rule fails, preserve lawful licensing and independently valid public purchases; no substitute program is judicially chosen.
2. **Distinguish a genuine public service and apply Pike.** Explain why public ownership and the program's actual incidence matter even with a separate operator and burdens on interstate providers. The strongest objection is that a sovereign routing command still compels independent private purchases; calling it a service or invoking environmental goals does not establish market-participant immunity. Pike requires a record-based account of interstate burden and local benefit, not mere opposition to a higher price. This path can support upholding the route or a bounded remand if the applicable inquiry was not adequately resolved below. It cannot invent operator economics or an unproved procurement arrangement.
3. **Separate the fee from the routing classification.** The charge may be a discriminatory penalty on use of competing services even if the public route survives; alternatively a materially evenhanded public-system charge may need its own burden analysis. A compensatory-tax defense must meet Oregon Waste's three requirements; the reported absence of an identified equivalent burden is a serious barrier. Neither a public label nor revenue need establishes equivalence. An alternative fee rationale must fit the enacted incidence and existing record; no new matched-cost finding or new assessment is supplied.

## 2. United States v. Cabrales

### Identity, posture, questions, and decisive amendment

**No. 97-643; argued April 29, 1998; scheduled event June 1, 1998.** Eighth Circuit No. 96-3080WM, 109 F.3d 471 (March 24, 1997), as amended at 115 F.3d 621 (June 12, 1997). [Original lower opinion](https://static.case.law/f3d/109/html/0471-01.html) and [amending order](https://static.case.law/f3d/115/html/0621-01.html) are copied in full. The Government appealed pretrial dismissal of Counts II and III for improper Missouri venue; the court of appeals affirmed. The district court had denied dismissal of Count I, conspiracy. The question here is venue for the two substantive laundering charges, not sufficiency, guilt, suppression or sentencing.

**Material source correction:** The original opinion's footnote 2 said Missouri overt acts supported conspiracy venue. On Cabrales's motion, the panel expressly removed that explanation so that no incorrect inference would affect the other prosecution. The operative amended footnote says only that the district court did not dismiss Count I and that Count I is not part of this appeal. The full original is reproduced only with the amendment alongside it. **Do not treat Missouri overt acts, valid conspiracy venue, or any conspiracy merits finding as established.**

Count I alleges §371 conspiracy to violate §1956(a)(1)(B)(ii), the reporting-avoidance branch. Count II charges conducting a financial transaction under that branch; Count III charges a monetary transaction in criminally derived property worth more than $10,000 under §1957. The sanitized objective account supplies a January 1991 Florida bank deposit of $40,000 and four $9,500 withdrawals within a week. The lower opinion supplies the material geographic facts: all charged banking transactions occurred in Florida; the proceeds are traceable to Missouri drug sales. The Government does not dispute that the acts Cabrales performed constituting those laundering transactions occurred outside Missouri. It relies on the Missouri predicate crime as making laundering a continuing offense. **It does not allege she transported the funds from Missouri to Florida.** Counts II–III identify no act of hers in Missouri. No aiding-and-abetting charge or acts are added to cure the indictment.

The authorized analytical question is whether the place of the proceeds-producing crime independently supplies venue for these substantive laundering counts although the charged financial dealings began, continued and ended in Florida. The Government's argument treats the illicit source, an indispensable statutory ingredient, as part of a multistate offense; Cabrales distinguishes the character of the property from where the conduct prohibited by these statutes occurred.

### Entering law and alternatives

Article III, §2, cl.3, the Sixth Amendment and Rule 18 locate criminal trial where the charged crime was committed. The lower opinion quotes the operative statutory language and Anderson, 328 U.S. 699, 703 (1946), tying the locus to the nature of the crime and the acts constituting it. The full 1994 official §3237 source has been read and copied; its amendments establish that the pertinent language predates January 1991. Subsection (a) covers an offense begun in one district and completed in another, or committed in more than one; its separate paragraph addresses offenses involving mails, interstate/foreign transportation or importation. An ordinary interstate-commerce connection in a financial-transaction definition is not automatically proof that this defendant transported these funds or committed conduct in each place they previously passed. The tax-election subsection is distinct and irrelevant to the charged offenses.

**Actual United States v. Thomas, May 19, 1997, Ginsburg Part III, unanimous:** Venue lay where the charged, authorized interstate transportation of obscene images reached the recipient. The Court expressly reserved remote effects alone, every district crossed by signals, unknown viewers and passive pages. Destination knowledge supported that application without becoming a universal extra element. This is directly relevant entered venue law absent from the source input's short list. It requires examining the charged conduct, while leaving room for genuine multistate offenses.

**Actual Gaudin, June 19, 1995, Scalia, unanimous:** A statutory materiality element belongs to the jury under the specified criminal statute. It does not hold that every circumstance element identifies a place of criminal conduct, nor decide this pretrial statutory venue question. Respect for all elements is necessary on either path; it is not an automatic geographic answer.

1. **Conduct-location path:** Treat the Missouri proceeds as a necessary attribute of the property and Florida transactions as the prohibited conduct for these counts. The strongest objection is the Government's argument that removing the proceeds-producing crime would remove the laundering offense altogether. Explain why element status does not invariably relocate the offense. Affirm dismissal of Counts II–III for Missouri venue, without an acquittal or decision about lawful prosecution elsewhere, Count I, transportation, or a different indictment.
2. **Integrated-offense path:** Treat the particular statutory offense as beginning with the Missouri criminal production of proceeds and ending with the Florida transactions. The strongest objection is that this charges Cabrales's financial transactions, not the predicate drug conduct, and alleges no transport by her; a general laundering scheme cannot substitute for statutory language. Explain the limiting principle that prevents venue wherever any historical circumstance arose. If this construction is accepted, reverse the venue dismissal and permit further proceedings on the two counts, making no guilt finding.
3. **Narrow distinction without a universal rule:** Resolve this indictment while reserving a defendant who committed the predicate crime, transported the proceeds, or is properly charged with a broader scheme. Beddow, 957 F.2d 1330, 1336 (6th Cir. 1992), as discussed and quoted below, involved predicate involvement and transport; Sax, 39 F.3d 1380, 1390 (7th Cir. 1994), supported a broader origin theory which the Eighth Circuit rejected to that extent. Their conflict supplies the real alternative; it does not establish missing Cabrales acts. The panel's rehearing correction forecloses relying on its former conspiracy footnote to manufacture a shortcut.

## 3. United States v. Beggerly

### Identity, record, and separate routes

**No. 97-731; argued April 27, 1998; scheduled event June 8, 1998.** Fifth Circuit No. 95-60625, 114 F.3d 484, decided May 29, 1997, corrected on rehearing July 28, 1997. [Full lower opinion and Garza dissent](https://static.case.law/f3d/114/html/0484-01.html), copied in the companion. The district court dismissed under Rules 12(b)(1)/(6), denied the claimants' summary-judgment cross-motion and leave to amend to assert Quiet Title/Tucker Act jurisdiction. The court of appeals reversed, vacated the 1982 consent judgment as to these claimants, treated the Quiet Title Act as an alternative basis, and directed title to be quieted for them. Review must distinguish jurisdiction, entitlement to extraordinary reopening, the time bar, title merits, and remedy.

The lower opinion reports a 1950 tax-sale purchase of part of Horn Island; park legislation in 1971; negotiations begun in 1972; a 1975 contract for $156,500 canceled when the Government asserted it had issued no patent. The Government brought its quiet-title action in 1979. During discovery its officials reportedly searched public land records and formally represented that no private disposition existed. In 1982 the parties settled: $208,175.87 to the claimants and title quieted to the United States. These payment amounts and chronology are distinct.

The claimants continued searching, including records in several States, correspondence and information requests; a genealogical researcher they hired located the Boudreau document in the National Archives in 1991. It was an English translation of a 1781 Spanish grant. The lower majority accepted it as the available copy after destruction of the Spanish archives and relied on Professor Hans Baade's affidavit about Spanish law. It rejected the Government's contention that the paper was only a patent application and treated an early land commissioner's refusal to accept it as nonconclusive. **Those are appellate conclusions contested by the Government and by the dissent's objection to deciding title on an incomplete record; they are not newly stipulated historical facts or an independently adjudicated good chain of title.**

The new suit was filed June 1, 1994. The lower majority described Government misrepresentation, negligence or mistake and found the traditional independent-action conditions satisfied. It did not establish intentional concealment simply by identifying a document later found in government archives. The distinction between an unsuccessful search, an inaccurate discovery representation, intentional concealment, and obstruction of a claimant's own reasonable search remains material. The packet supplies no affirmative bad-faith finding beyond the lower court's stated conclusions.

The lower majority found knowledge of the Government's adverse claim by 1976, when negotiations ceased; the sanitized account identifies knowledge by 1979 at the latest. Both precede the new suit by more than twelve years. Do not confuse first knowledge of the adverse claim with later discovery of evidence to defeat it. The majority tolled from the original search during the quiet-title litigation until the 1991 discovery. The Government's earlier suit and the settlement are notice of its claim; whether misrepresentation warrants equitable suspension is a different question.

### Law entering the case

The 1998 version of Rule 60(b) preserves an independent action, apart from its ordinary motion remedies; the complete lower opinion quotes that savings language and its five traditional requirements: a judgment inequitable to enforce; a good defense; fraud, accident or mistake preventing use of it; absence of the claimant's fault/negligence; and absence of an adequate legal remedy. The lower court derives these from Bankers Mortgage, 423 F.2d 73, and National Surety, 120 F. 593, 599. This is an exceptional equitable route, not a means of converting every later-discovered item into an unrestricted second case. The neutral input identifies extraordinary relief to prevent a grave miscarriage of justice as the proposed governing threshold; the model must explain its application through established equitable principles rather than assume a finding satisfying it.

The lower opinion reports **QTA §2409a(g)'s twelve-year period**, accruing when the claimant knows or should know of the United States' adverse claim. It reports §2409a(b)'s choice, after an adverse title judgment, to deliver possession or retain it and pay just compensation. Full historical statutory pages were not recovered despite direct official-source attempts. The handoff relies on these authoritative reported operative provisions under §14, rather than pretending to quote the complete statute or its unrelated exceptions. If a model needs another unquoted qualification to decide the case, it must obtain it before adopting that ground. No such missing clause is used by the paths below.

**Actual Morgan Stanley (May 23, 1994) and Plaut (April 18, 1995)** uphold the expressly bounded §27A(b) congressional reinstatement procedure while retaining independent judicial eligibility, merits and remedy decisions. Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg joined both controlling opinions; Breyer joined Plaut. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas dissented in both; O'Connor did not participate in Morgan Stanley and joined Scalia's Plaut dissent. These actual cases foreclose importing historical Plaut's absolute Article III rule as controlling law. They equally do **not** turn all completed settlements into discretionary fairness reviews. The difference between legislatively prescribed reopening and longstanding judicial correction remains essential.

**Actual Kokkonen (May 16, 1994), Scalia, unanimous among those then seated:** Mere federal dismissal does not supply jurisdiction to enforce a separate settlement contract. Incorporation/retention can support enforcement of the court's own order within lawful authority. Rule 60 reopening was expressly reserved. Thus the Government's limited-jurisdiction argument deserves consideration, but Kokkonen is not already a holding that a court cannot entertain an independent attack on its own allegedly corrupted judgment.

**Actual Peacock (February 21, 1996), Thomas:** Eight Justices, including all Associates other than Stevens, rejected ancillary jurisdiction for a new action imposing an existing judgment debtor's debt on another person. Authority to effectuate a decree remains distinct from creating new substantive liability. This supports careful differentiation between undoing the original decree and obtaining new title or compensation relief, not automatic denial of every equitable attack.

**Actual Nordic Village (February 25, 1992), Scalia, and Department of Energy v. Ohio (April 21, 1992), Souter:** Federal consent must cover the particular remedy; general jurisdiction, an equitable label, or a narrower waiver does not establish an affirmative monetary obligation. Their actual joins and limits are copied in the companion. They do not decide whether the Government's own original title suit carries continuing jurisdiction to correct its judgment, nor whether a timely QTA claim meets its express waiver.

**Irwin, 498 U.S. 89 (1990), official predivergence opinion, fully read:** O'Connor, Scalia and Kennedy joined the Court's rebuttable presumption that limitations in suits against the Government can be equitably tolled after an express waiver; that presumption does not create a waiver or automatically extend every deadline. Ordinary attorney neglect did not suffice; misleading conduct inducing expiration was a recognized example, with diligence required and no more favorable rule against the Government than private defendants. Stevens expressly agreed with tolling availability and its failure on that record, dissenting on the notice trigger. Souter did **not** participate. Do not attribute an Irwin join to him, Thomas, Ginsburg or Breyer.

**Actual Brockamp, February 18, 1997, Breyer, unanimous on the timing rules:** Assuming Irwin applies, the integrated §6511 filing/allowance/payment scheme rebutted nonstatutory incapacity tolling. Specific exceptions alone and administrative scale alone were not independent grounds. The Court reserved fraud, affirmative governmental inducement and separate recoupment. **Actual Beach, April 21, 1998, unanimous:** The particular §1635(f) text expires the rescission right, subject to its stated agency extension, and cannot be revived by a defensive label. Neither decision classifies every deadline as jurisdictional or decides the QTA. Beggerly requires examination of this twelve-year notice-triggered scheme and alleged interference, not transplantation of a tax or lending outcome.

### Material alternatives and remedies

1. **Same-court corrective jurisdiction but extraordinary relief fails.** Distinguish reopening the Government's own decree from a new independent demand for money, while finding that the alleged search/disclosure failure and subsequent discovery do not satisfy the narrow equitable threshold. The strongest counterargument is official misinformation inducing settlement despite sustained claimant diligence. A rejection must address that causal account without inventing bad faith or blaming claimants for a search they reasonably could not complete. This narrower route need not decide every sovereign-consent issue if its posture permits that treatment.
2. **Corrective jurisdiction and sufficient extraordinary circumstances.** Treat the reported misleading representation, unavailable defense and persistent search as meeting the traditional conditions. The strongest barriers are the final settlement, other remedies/timeliness, and the distinction between material evidence later found and conduct preventing fair presentation. Even if reopening is justified, it does not compel an appellate title judgment. A remand for contested grant validity, title chain and remaining defenses is a separate, narrower remedial choice.
3. **Independent jurisdiction/consent required for the new relief.** Distinguish court power to correct its decree from a renewed QTA title action or affirmative compensation claim; apply the actual waiver precedents to the remedy sought. The opposing argument is that allowing the Government to invoke jurisdiction, obtain judgment through a material error, and then foreclose correction undermines the court's authority over its own process. Do not equate continuing subject-matter jurisdiction with consent to every monetary award, or read Kokkonen's reserved reopening question as settled.
4. **QTA timing either defeats or permits the alternative title route.** One path treats twelve years from actual/constructive knowledge as the enacted balance already allowing time to investigate, and rejects searching until favorable evidence appears as an extra accrual rule. The opposing path applies Irwin's presumption to proven adversary inducement or obstruction and diligence, distinguishing Brockamp's expressly reserved governmental-misconduct question. A decision must state whether it finds statutory rebuttal, inadequate equitable facts, or tolling on established facts; these are not interchangeable. Neither general equity nor a still-valid settlement can simply disappear from this analysis.
5. **Even with jurisdiction and reopening, title may require remand.** Garza's dissent separately objects that the majority decided the cross-motion, grant validity and title on an incomplete record. The Court can reject that objection on an adequately established record or remand for the necessary adjudication. It cannot treat the claimant's affidavit and a found translation as automatic proof of an unbroken title chain or turn a preliminary legal victory into an unspecified damages award.
