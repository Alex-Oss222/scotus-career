## Component definitions and common boundaries

**M — Amendment mootness and judgment disposition.** The obsolete definition-based reporting and corporate-employee theories, the old independent/issue-spending branch of the corporate ban, and the issue-only disclaimer application no longer present the challenged controversy. The independent-spending and KRL/Zoeller branches of the gubernatorial challenge are likewise moot as identified below. This conclusion addresses the obsolete claims; it does not hold that every nonprofit satisfies amended §121.035(3). That permission expressly requires that the nonprofit not derive a substantial portion of its revenue from for-profit corporations. A live challenge to that condition, a genuinely adverse current application, or supported recurrence would require its own record. None is adjudicated here.

The power to reenact is not alone a live controversy. Conversely, an express announcement of reenactment is not an indispensable legal prerequisite to voluntary-cessation analysis. The statutory changes, their removal of the challenged applications, the reported enforcement history, and absence of a surviving relief claim support mootness together. Bancorp and Arizonans keep equitable judgment disposition separate from merits jurisdiction. Preserve dismissals that already denied jurisdiction; direct that moot claims be dismissed for mootness and clear any underlying merits adjudication of those obsolete applications, particularly the former issue-advertisement application. Legislative amendment while the losing challengers sought review is not their voluntary settlement or abandonment. No merits ruling on the repealed scheme is retained through an undifferentiated affirmance. No broader vacatur of still-live judgments is justified by mootness.

**C — Corporate direct candidate contributions.** Affirm rejection of the claimed categorical nonprofit exemption from §121.025's direct candidate-contribution restriction. NRWC's solicitation/contribution setting and MCFL's express distinction support prophylactic regulation of the corporate contribution channel without a finding that this corporation has committed corruption. KRL's separate PAC and the preserved avenue for independent advocacy matter to the character of this restriction; no new operational fact about them is found. Day's actual-characteristics protection concerns independent expenditures, expressly leaves direct corporate gifts open, and does not require a nonprofit exception here. No holding makes nonprofit status a finding of commercial accumulation or a conduit. No blanket endorsement of every conceivable corporate restriction follows.

**D — Paid candidate-advertisement identification.** The surviving question concerns the amended requirement of “paid for by” followed by the payer's **name and address** on the listed paid communications expressly advocating a clearly identified candidate, slate or group of candidates. The issue-only application is moot. Corporate/committee financing, individual pamphleteering, candidate-related disclosure, and actual coordination must remain distinct. Neither presumed corruption in every independent advertisement nor the proposition that reports and on-message identification impose identical burdens is permissible. McIntyre reserves materially different finance and organizational contexts; Colorado Republican prohibits converting actual independence into a contribution merely by identity, but does not invalidate attribution requirements.

**L — Direct $1,000 limits.** Affirm the surviving judgment under Buckley's contribution rule at the preserved scope. The amended ceiling is **in any one election**; §121A.050(1)'s knowing-donation prohibition excepts independent expenditures as statutorily defined. The lower court reports that counsel proposed $1,000 and essentially conceded its constitutionality. That supports confining the question; it is not an unseen general waiver and does not bind the Court to an incorrect constitutional rule. The valid direct-transfer distinction and this preserved challenge supply the disposition without declaring $1,000 a constitutional floor, safe harbor for every office, or annual aggregate cap.

**A — $1,500 all-intermediary annual cap.** Section 121.150(10) limits one person's contributions to **all permanent committees and contributing organizations together** in one year. It does not limit that person's total direct gifts to all candidates. Day requires meaningful collective association and fit to an identified corruption or circumvention mechanism, while allowing realistic candidate-conduit regulation and higher limits. California Medical's four-Justice low-protection reasoning is not itself a categorical controlling answer for every intermediary. KRLPAC's actual candidate-contribution activity supports a genuine preventive concern, but the law's all-recipient aggregate reach and the individual-donation alternative require separate attention. Neither the $1,500 figure nor a general reference to small state campaigns resolves that fit.

**G — Gubernatorial receipts.** Only KRLPAC's direct-contribution challenge survives. KRL and Zoeller are not permanent committees under the amended law; their corresponding challenge and the independent-expenditure theory are moot. A participating slate's permanent-committee receipts may not exceed **25 percent of qualifying contributions received, up to $150,000 per election**. A nonparticipating slate has the separately reported **$150,000 ceiling**. The $150,000 figure does not replace the percentage at a lower fundraising level. The $600,000 privately raised maximum, 2:1 match and $1.8 million spending maximum describe the reported funding arrangement, not an assumption about every slate's actual receipts. The 151st-PAC illustration assumes maximum fundraising and $1,000 gifts; it establishes no universal absence of burden. The district court's reported absence of any indication of actual or likely restriction is a record conclusion, not an inference from unrecovered papers.

### Justice-specific grounds, strongest competing paths and join barriers

**Stevens.** His own California Medical Part III join supports preventive regulation of the candidate-funding intermediary, and his Colorado Republican dissent preserves institution-specific corruption and circumvention analysis without demanding a completed corrupt bargain. His Day Parts III–IV joins are decisive limits: association remains protected, and a nonprofit's actual independent resources cannot be treated as commercial merely from incorporation. He joins M through his Arizonans opinion and Bancorp join, C through the received corporate contribution distinction, and L through the still-valid contribution rule. For D, his McIntyre opinion protects the ordinary issue leafleteer while reserving campaign-finance and organizational settings; this facial organizational/candidate-advertisement challenge implicates direct identification of the financer and checking disguised candidate-connected spending. The address identifies the legal payer and permits tracing; it is a burden requiring justification, not surplus text to omit. He would preserve retaliation-based and materially different individual applications.

For A, the strongest contrary path is Day-based remand because one donor's donations to unrelated committees exhaust the same annual ceiling. Stevens nevertheless finds the received contribution character, KRLPAC's candidate transfers, the specific $1,000 candidate limit, and the broader opportunity for political action sufficient for this facial rejection. He does not adopt the lower court's assertion that intermediary association is categorically unimportant. Day involved an established severe practical pooling burden and a $100 ceiling; it creates no rule compelling invalidity or remand whenever a higher limit's precise empirical effects are not recounted. His G position similarly rests on direct candidate receipts and prevention of candidate dependence on aggregated committee funds, with the reported absence of actual or likely burden supporting rejection of this facial claim. The strongest objection is that some later donor may be excluded solely because others contributed; this does not make every collective receipt ceiling facially invalid. He will not join a claim that the percentage is always $150,000, that the restriction can never burden association, or that equalizing political voices alone justifies it. He does not require the absent funding-eligibility details because he does not rest validity on a public-benefit waiver theory.

**O'Connor.** Her Day joins and Colorado Republican principal opinion distinguish protected expression from genuine contribution channels and require the function of the actual transaction to do legal work. Her join in Kennedy's Austin dissent likewise distinguishes contribution controls from independent-expression bans; it does not commit her to a nonprofit exemption for candidate cash. These support C and L, and her Bancorp/Arizonans joins support M. Her McIntyre join requires exacting attention to on-message attribution, but the paid candidate-finance setting and organizational plaintiffs allow D's facial judgment to stand on source identification and enforcement interests without deeming all independent expenditures corrupt. A substantially different ordinary-individual or reprisal record remains open.

For A, affirmance under California Medical is a serious alternative because KRLPAC actually contributes to candidates. She rejects it here as a sufficient disposition of the whole all-intermediary ceiling: the lower court's rule gives too little independent effect to meaningful pooled association and treats an aggregate reaching unrelated committees as validated by the existence of some possible conduit. Vacate and remand to apply Day to actual recipient functions, the reach of the aggregate and the supported burden. Do not order a new trial, demand transaction-by-transaction bribery proof, or declare the State to have offered no evidence. For G, the opposite disposition is warranted: actual receipts of the gubernatorial candidate are the regulated transaction, and the facial record supplies no demonstrated obstruction. She will join a narrowly stated affirmance covering the 25-percent branch and the separate nonparticipant cap, with future concrete applications left open; she will not join the faulty 151st-PAC generalization or a novel funding-condition holding. Her Colorado Republican refusal to decide all coordinated arrangements supplies no presumption of postponing every facial contribution case.

**Scalia.** His McIntyre dissent supplies a distinct, affirmative basis for D: election-specific attribution is an accountability and enforcement safeguard, subject to appropriate protection against established threats or reprisals. He can also join the narrower candidate-finance/organizational rationale described here; his broader view of ordinary election attribution is unnecessary. His Day joins and Colorado Republican concurrence join preserve actual candidate contributions and genuine conduits while resisting restrictions on an association's own expression. He therefore joins C and L without abandoning either those positions or his Austin dissent. His own Bancorp opinion and Arizonans join support M's separate equitable disposition.

For A, the strongest affirmance ground is the accepted ability to use a PAC for candidate contributions. It does not explain why payments to unrelated recipients exhaust one small common allowance when direct gifts to different candidates remain unlimited in the aggregate. The lower court's categorical speech-by-proxy premise cannot carry that breadth after Day. He joins vacatur and remand on that legal error, not a finding that every application is invalid. He could accept targeted candidate-transfer/conduit regulation supported by the actual scheme. For G, he would also vacate and remand: a further classwide receipt ceiling must have its own fitted corruption ground, beyond labeling every political association a vested interest after each donor already faces the $1,000 ceiling. The strongest counterargument is that a candidate may become dependent on aggregate committee financing even without one oversized gift. That is legitimate, but the lower court's reasoning substitutes a maximum-fundraising illustration for scrutiny of both actual statutory branches. His objection can be answered only by a branch-specific account of fit, not by assuming every contribution can be made or every participant has raised $600,000. He proposes neither invalidating voluntary public financing nor immunizing candidate cash.

**Kennedy.** His Austin dissent, joined by O'Connor and Scalia, treats independent expression as distinct from the candidate-contribution concerns of NRWC and identifies disclosure as a narrower alternative to suppression. His Day and Colorado Republican writings protect an association's own advocacy while preserving actual transfers and sham conduits. These authorities support C and L, and permit D's limited facial affirmance despite his McIntyre join: naming the financer of an organizational candidate advertisement can serve disclosure and detection without limiting the amount or content of its advocacy. The strongest objection is McIntyre's insistence that an on-message name and address is more intrusive than a financial report. He accepts that distinction and will not join automatic validity of every individual pamphlet or a presumption that an independent ad creates corrupt allegiance. The separate legal interest in identifying candidate-election financing supports this facial result.

For A, his Day and Colorado Republican positions require the independent and genuine-conduit functions to remain distinct. A blanket annual pool across all recipients cannot be justified merely by the possibility that some recipient might pass money to a candidate. The strongest competing path is facial affirmance because KRLPAC itself makes candidate gifts and unearmarked funds are fungible. That supports possible regulation, but does not cure the lower court's categorical dismissal of collective association and its failure to assess the aggregate's breadth under Day. He joins bounded vacatur/remand rather than facial invalidation based on facts not established in the published account. For G he joins Scalia's branch-specific remand ground. Candidate-dependence concerns are legitimate, but hostility to pooled interests is not enough, and the first contribution rejected after a cap is reached remains an associational burden even when earlier donors could contribute. Neither the public-funding label nor the $150,000 maximum decides the 25-percent branch at lower fundraising levels. He joins M through Bancorp/Arizonans; he reserves no power to decide dead constitutional claims.

**Souter.** His Day Part III–IV joins, Colorado Republican Parts I–III join and McIntyre join supply his own decisive commitments: contribution regulation survives, independent expression is protected, and the burden and institutional setting must be evaluated without identity-based shortcuts. He joins C and L on direct-transfer grounds. His Bancorp and Arizonans joins support M. He joins D's facial affirmance because organizational candidate-finance attribution serves interests distinct from compelling an ordinary issue leafleteer's identity, while preserving possible individual and reprisal applications. The strongest contrary path is extending McIntyre to every independent candidate advertisement; that would ignore its express reservations and the difference between regulating independent spending and identifying its payer.

For A, he favors vacatur/remand because the lower court gave California Medical's nonmajority low-value rationale the force of a categorical rule and did not apply Day's meaningful-association requirement to the combined-recipient ceiling. The strongest opposing path is that the demonstrated candidate-funding channel establishes all the fit a facial case needs. It establishes an important purpose but not the fit of the aggregate's entire coverage. He does not convert record incompleteness into a finding against the State. For G, actual receipt by one candidate makes the dependency concern materially more direct; together with the facial posture and the reported absence of actual or likely restriction, it permits affirmance. He insists that the percentage branch operate as written and rejects the inference that the cap can affect only a 151st donor. No consent to unknown public-financing conditions is necessary to his conclusion.

**Thomas.** His published McIntyre concurrence rests on the protected tradition of anonymous political pamphleteering and Talley, not the Court's modern balancing rationale. His Colorado Republican concurrence join expressly preserves Buckley's contribution distinction and genuine conduit regulation; it does not adopt the historical Thomas position in that separate case. His Day joins likewise protect collective association and actual nonprofit independent advocacy without eliminating contribution controls. Those actual published commitments support C and L and the Day-grounded remand in A. His Bancorp and Arizonans joins supply M.

For D he would vacate and remand rather than affirm the categorical surviving disclaimer judgment. The strongest affirmance ground is identifying the source of organized paid candidate advocacy and exposing hidden candidate connections. That justifies distinct financial disclosure, but does not answer the surviving statute's inclusion of an individual's independent candidate handbill within the name-and-address command. In his view the protected pamphleteering tradition does not end because the author discusses a candidate rather than a ballot question. The lower court's candidate/issue distinction is therefore insufficient for its undifferentiated facial rejection. This is a request for the narrower protected-pamphlet question to receive proper treatment, not a finding that Zoeller has distributed a handbill or that every organization, radio ad, paid broadcast or financial report is exempt. He selects no unverified statutory exception or severability construction and orders no blanket facial injunction. An opinion premised solely on modern exacting-scrutiny balancing would remain a join barrier even if it reached the same result.

For A he joins the common Day legal-error rationale and bounded remand; the lawful alternative of sustaining candidate-conduit regulation does not justify low protection for every act of pooling. For G he joins the separate-fit remand ground with Scalia and Kennedy. His strongest counterargument is that the statute directly regulates candidate receipts; he accepts that important distinction but concludes that an additional aggregate exclusion, triggered by other donors' decisions, requires its own fitted justification. The faulty maximum-fundraising illustration and generalized disfavor of vested interests cannot substitute for it. He does not claim that valid base limits and aggregate limits can never coexist, or that electing public funding is constitutionally irrelevant. He declines a complete financing-condition judgment on the limited statutory account.

**Ginsburg.** Her McIntyre join protects the ordinary individual's anonymity with the decision's explicit reservations, and her Day joins require actual nonprofit characteristics and a meaningful opportunity for pooled association. Her Colorado Republican dissent join accepts prophylactic institutional contribution-channel regulation while rejecting equalization as a justification for suppressing speech. Those prior published positions support C, L and limited D affirmance. Identifying the payer and address on the listed candidate advertisements has a source-tracing function beyond general curiosity about an issue leafleteer. She preserves concrete retaliation and individual-application questions rather than requiring all speech to bear a label solely because it relates to an election. Her unanimous Bancorp/Arizonans joins support M.

For A she joins Stevens's limited affirmance ground. The strongest contrary path is remand to examine the all-recipient aggregate's practical burden. She finds the preserved facial claim, KRLPAC's candidate contributions and the fit of the aggregate to preventing distribution among several candidate-funding intermediaries sufficient here. Day's actual $100 burden does not establish invalidity of the higher cap, and its reservation of realistic conduit regulation matters. She will not join a blanket declaration that committee association is less worthy of protection or that an independent-only recipient necessarily presents the same conduit risk. For G, the candidate-receipt transaction and the supported absence of actual or likely restriction warrant facial rejection of KRLPAC's claim, with the exact percentage and nonparticipant branches retained. She rejects describing the 151st-PAC example as a universal factual finding and rejects a categorical safe harbor for every future application.

**Breyer.** His Day joins and Colorado Republican Parts I–III join preserve a functional inquiry: meaningful association and genuinely independent speech receive protection, while the State can prevent real evasion without first proving a completed corrupt transaction. His McIntyre join requires attention to the added burden of on-message identification. Those commitments permit C, L and D's limited facial affirmance, with the payer's address included and ordinary individual/reprisal cases reserved. His Bancorp and Arizonans joins support M.

For A he joins vacatur/remand. The strongest affirmance argument is that the aggregate permits meaningful activity and closes an obvious route for funneling money through several PACs. That route is legitimate, but the lower court's categorical low-protection premise bypasses Day's question whether the actual annual all-recipient restriction fits it while leaving meaningful collective participation. Remand corrects that legal inquiry without predetermining empirical answers, imposing a new evidentiary burden, or assuming the absent trial papers contained nothing. For G the regulated funds go directly to one candidate; the source-class receipt limitation has a more immediate dependency rationale and no demonstrated actual or likely obstruction in this facial record. He would affirm both accurately distinguished branches and leave concrete burdens for an appropriate application. He will not adopt either the fictional universal $150,000 threshold or the theory that public subsidy itself eliminates First Amendment scrutiny.
