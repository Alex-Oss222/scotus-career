## Fidelity Financial Services, Inc. v. Fink

**Identity and dates:** Supreme Court docket 96-1370; argument November 3, 1997. Under-review decision: In re Beasley, 102 F.3d 334 (8th Cir. 1996) (per curiam), following 183 B.R. 857 (Bankr. W.D. Mo. 1995) and district-court affirmance. The supplied event anchor is January 13, 1998; it is a schedule datum only.

**Source formulation of question, paraphrased:** Whether the §547(c)(3) enabling-loan exception can apply when the creditor performs state-law perfection acts more than 20 days after the debtor receives the property but within a longer state relation-back or grace period. This is not certified as a verbatim petition or grant-order quotation.

**Objective record:** On August 17, 1994, Diane Beasley bought a 1994 Ford, gave Fidelity a promissory note for its purchase price, and secured the note with the car. Fidelity mailed the Missouri Department of Revenue the application necessary to perfect its interest on September 7, 1994—21 days after the transaction. Beasley sought Chapter 7 relief about two months later; the proceeding was converted to Chapter 13. Trustee Richard V. Fink moved to avoid the lien as a preference.

**Competing positions recorded in the opinion:** Fink invoked the federal 20-day enabling-loan period. Fidelity invoked Mo. Rev. Stat. §301.600(2) (1994), which provides for perfection by delivery of the specified documents to the revenue director and relates its effective date to creation if delivery occurs within 30 days. Fidelity contended that its delivery within that state period yielded perfection as of August 17. The record supplied here establishes the mailing date; it does not independently determine the delivery date or decide whether mailing alone satisfied Missouri law. The source expressly identifies the sufficiency of mailing as a state-law question not raised in this case.

**Judgment and reasons below:** The bankruptcy court avoided the lien, reasoning that Missouri relation back could not extend the federal 20-day period. The district court affirmed on substantially the same grounds. The Eighth Circuit also affirmed and treated federal perfection as occurring when the secured party performed the last step required by state law. These are lower-court holdings, not an adopted simulated rule.

**Relevant statutory context recorded:** The case uses the 20-day text of 11 U.S.C. §547(c)(3)(B), together with the definition of perfection in §547(e)(1)(B). It does not concern a later statutory extension. Statutory quotations in the official report also identify §546(b)(1)(A), whose enumerated trustee powers are §§544, 545, and 549; omission of §547 is statutory text, while the consequence of that omission remains an argument. The original Bankruptcy Reform Act text and full enactment effective-date provisions were not separately retrieved.

## Baker v. General Motors Corp.

**Identity and dates:** Supreme Court docket 96-653; argument October 15, 1997. Under-review decision: 86 F.3d 811 (8th Cir. 1996). The supplied event anchor is January 13, 1998; it is a schedule datum only.

**Source formulation of question, paraphrased:** Whether full faith and credit prevents the Bakers, who were not parties to the Michigan case, from obtaining Ronald Elwell's testimony in their Missouri wrongful-death action. No verbatim petition language is certified.

**Elwell–GM proceeding:** Elwell worked for GM from 1959 to 1989; for 15 years beginning in 1971 he worked in the Engineering Analysis Group, concentrating on vehicle fires and assisting product-liability defense counsel. Difficulties over retirement followed deterioration of the employment relationship beginning in 1987. In May 1991, Elwell was deposed in a Georgia product-liability action and criticized a GM pickup fuel-system design he had previously defended. In June 1991 he sued GM in Wayne County, Michigan, for wrongful discharge and related contract and tort claims. GM counterclaimed for alleged breach of fiduciary duty, disclosure of privileged/confidential information, and misappropriation of documents. These are attributed claims, not independently adjudicated misconduct.

**Orders and settlement — distinct instruments:** On November 22, 1991, after a hearing, the Michigan court preliminarily barred disclosure or discussion of GM trade secrets, confidential information, and attorney-client/work-product matters relating to product-liability litigation that Elwell acquired in his employment. In August 1992, GM paid Elwell an undisclosed settlement amount, and the parties tendered a stipulated permanent injunction. A different judge entered it on August 26, 1992 without another hearing. The permanent injunction substantially repeated the confidentiality restriction and added a broad bar on testifying or consulting in litigation involving GM as owner, seller, manufacturer, or designer without GM's written consent. Its specific exception protected the jurisdiction of the court in the then-pending Georgia action.

The **separate settlement agreement**, not the injunction itself, said that Elwell's testimony in another court or tribunal, if that court or tribunal ordered it, would not form the basis of an action for violation of the agreement or injunction. Do not collapse the decree's narrow Georgia exception into the agreement's broader court-ordered-testimony protection. The original agreement is not independently possessed; its operative provision is reproduced in the reported judicial account.

**Bakers' separate case:** Beverly Garner died after a February 1990 Missouri highway collision involving a 1985 Chevrolet S–10 Blazer. Her sons Kenneth and Steven Baker sued GM in Missouri state court in September 1991, alleging a defective fuel pump caused the fatal engine fire. GM removed on diversity grounds and disputed both defect and causation, contending impact injuries caused Garner's death. The Bakers were not parties to the Elwell–GM Michigan case. They subpoenaed Elwell, and GM objected on the basis of the Michigan injunction. The Bakers asserted the injunction did not override the Missouri subpoena and relied on the separate settlement provision permitting court-ordered testimony.

**Trial and judgment below:** After reviewing the decree and agreement in camera, the federal district court allowed the deposition and trial testimony. It gave two grounds: Missouri policy favored relevant nonprivileged evidence; and the injunction was subject to modification in Michigan and therefore elsewhere. Elwell supported the Bakers' fuel-system theory and identified a 1973 GM memorandum about fuel-fed fires. The jury returned an $11.3 million verdict, and judgment was entered.

The Eighth Circuit reversed, ruling among other things that Elwell's testimony should have been excluded. It assumed arguendo a public-policy exception but found Missouri's policy favoring relevant evidence balanced by an equally strong policy favoring full faith and credit. It also found insufficient evidence that the Michigan court would modify the injunction, noting prior unsuccessful modification requests. These are lower-court positions, not premises the reviewing Court must accept as law.

**Attributed nonobjection fact:** The official report records the Bakers' assertion, undisputed by GM there, that GM did not object to a question or answer in Elwell's Missouri testimony on attorney-client, work-product, or trade-secret grounds. This is a reported uncontested party representation, not independent transcript verification and not a finding that everything Elwell ever knew was nonprivileged. The broad anti-testimony restriction and ordinary particularized privilege objections remain distinct.
