## Hudson v. United States — 522 U.S. 93; No. 96-976

**Date and question.** Argument October 8, 1997; event-date anchor December 10, 1997. The petition question reproduced at 522 U.S. 101–102 n.5 concerned whether the Treasury's in-personam civil monetary penalties, together with other sanctions, constituted punishment for Double Jeopardy purposes. The packet's second issue about the same offense is an analytical contingent issue, not a separately verified granted question. The Tenth Circuit did not pass on the Blockburger question, deeming it unnecessary, 92 F.3d 1026, 1028 n.3.

**Objective administrative record.** In the early/mid-1980s Hudson was chairman and controlling shareholder of the First National Banks of Tipton and Hammon. Rackley was Tipton's president and a Hammon director; Baresel was a director of both. OCC alleged that loans nominally made to others actually benefited Hudson by enabling redemption of bank stock pledged on defaulted loans. Its February 13, 1989 notice alleged violations of 12 U.S.C. §§84(a)(1), 375b and 12 C.F.R. §§31.2(b), 215.4(b). It alleged almost $900,000 in bank losses and contribution to the banks' failures; that notice alleged no government loss. These are agency allegations, not independent findings in this extract.

OCC initially assessed $100,000 against Hudson and $50,000 each against Rackley and Baresel, stating that it considered financial resources, good faith, gravity, prior violations, and justice. On August 31, 1989 it issued notices proposing exclusion from insured depository institutions on the same allegations. October 1989 consent orders settled the assessments at $16,500 for Hudson, $15,000 for Baresel, and $12,500 for Rackley. Each agreed not to participate in banking institutions without written authorization from OCC and other relevant regulators. The orders reserved other federal representatives' and agencies' enforcement authority. The first Tenth Circuit decision held that this reservation did not waive the petitioners' double-jeopardy claim.

**Criminal case and judgment below.** An August 1992 indictment in the Western District of Oklahoma contained 22 counts under 18 U.S.C. §§371, 656 and 2, and 1005 arising from the same lending transactions. Only Rackley faced §1005 false-entry charges. The District Court initially denied the double-jeopardy dismissal motions. The Tenth Circuit, 14 F.3d 536 (1994), affirmed as to the nonparticipation sanction but remanded the monetary-sanction issue. The District Court then dismissed the indictments. On the government's appeal, 92 F.3d 1026 (1996), the Tenth Circuit reversed, applying Halper and finding the actual fines not grossly disproportionate to proved government damages. This states the lower court's legal conclusion; it does not establish the underlying damage measure or punitive character as a fact.

**Support and limits.** [Official opinion](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-522/pdf/USREPORTS-522-93.pdf), objective recitation at 96–98 and question/preservation at 101–102 n.5; [local control text](USREPORTS-522-93.txt). Original OCC instruments, complete indictment, petition, merits briefs, and appendix have not been independently recovered here. Exact bank losses remain alleged; no government-loss amount is supplied by this extract. Published recitation establishes the amounts, chronology, administrative restrictions, and procedural history without reconstruction. A claim requiring the unquoted indictment's exact element-by-element charging language would require a targeted further source; missing originals do not themselves establish default or waiver. No new participation premise is adopted.

## Bay Area Laundry v. Ferbar — 522 U.S. 192; No. 96-370

**Date and questions.** Argument November 10, 1997; event-date anchor December 15, 1997. The reported questions concern when the MPPAA's six-year period begins for collection of withdrawal liability, and whether an untimely first installment prevents recovery of subsequent installments falling within six years. These are source formulations, not a claimed verbatim petition quotation.

**Objective payment history.** Ferbar Corporation and Stephen Barnes owned three laundries. Contributions for one ceased in 1983; contributions for the other two ceased in March 1985 and never resumed. The Fund's December 12, 1986 demand assessed $45,570.80. It offered lump-sum payment within 60 days of receipt or 240 monthly installments of $345.50 starting February 1, 1987. Ferbar sought trustee reconsideration; no response expressly directed to that request was received. Ferbar initiated arbitration on July 8, 1987, but arbitration had not occurred at the reported stage. Ferbar made no payments. An April 14, 1987 letter warned that failure to cure within 60 days would constitute default.

The Fund sued in the Northern District of California on February 9, 1993. Its complaint requested the entire $45,570.80 or, alternatively, $25,375 in then-due installments plus an injunction directing future payments as due. Suit thus followed the first scheduled installment by six years and eight days, but was within six years of subsequent installments.

**Judgments below.** The District Court granted Ferbar summary judgment, first applying the three-year discovery period and alternatively treating the first missed payment as starting the six-year period for the entire debt. The Ninth Circuit, 73 F.3d 971 (1996), affirmed on a different ground: the statutory later-of rule permitted six years, but complete withdrawal in March 1985 triggered that period. It followed Thibodo, reasoning that missed-payment accrual could render the discovery provision superfluous and let the creditor control accrual. Judge Trott separately noted the circuit conflict. Other reported circuit positions were: Joyce, 871 F.2d 1119 (D.C. Cir. 1989), accrual on missed scheduled payment; Kahle, 43 F.3d 852 (3d Cir. 1994), separate installment periods; Navco, 3 F.3d 167 (7th Cir. 1993), a single period from the first missed installment. These describe competing source authorities, not adopted simulated law.

**Material litigation representations.** In the court of appeals and later merits briefing the Fund argued that §1399(c)(2)'s 60-day provision made its first-payment claim timely; it also relied on the demand letter's receipt-based prepayment period. In its certiorari petition, however, it described the disagreement between Kahle and Navco as determinative and represented that Navco's approach barred its suit. These are published accounts of the party's submissions, not an adopted finding of waiver. Ferbar contended that the complaint's demand for the whole debt amounted to acceleration; that is a contention, not an established earlier acceleration event. Neither delinquency nor the April 1987 warning is certified here as an exercise of acceleration.

**Support and limits.** [Official opinion](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-522/pdf/USREPORTS-522-192.pdf), factual/procedural recitation 198–200; party representations 206–207 and 209 n.5; [local control text](USREPORTS-522-192.txt). Original demand, complaint, reconsideration request, petition, briefs and appendix were not independently recovered. The authenticated published accounts adequately support the chronology, request for relief and inconsistent petition/merits positions; their legal effect remains for neutral analysis. Do not invent effective acceleration before suit, an arbitration outcome, or a petition concession beyond what is reported. No reconstruction or participation premise is adopted.

## General Electric Co. v. Joiner — 522 U.S. 136; No. 96-188

**Date and question.** Argument October 14, 1997; event-date anchor December 15, 1997. The grant is reported as asking what appellate standard governs a trial court's admission or exclusion of expert testimony under Daubert. Treat case-specific application as a distinct scope question, not a separately certified grant question.

**Objective record and competing factual positions.** Joiner worked as a Thomasville, Georgia electrician beginning in 1973, servicing transformers with mineral-oil dielectric coolant. His hands and arms entered the fluid; it sometimes splashed into his eyes and mouth. In 1983 the City discovered PCB contamination in some transformers. He developed small-cell lung cancer in 1991 and sued in Georgia state court in 1992. Monsanto manufactured PCBs; General Electric and Westinghouse manufactured transformers and dielectric fluid. He alleged that PCBs, furans and dioxins promoted or accelerated cancer, rather than asserting they were necessarily its sole cause. He had smoked for about eight years, had parents who smoked, and had a family history of lung cancer; their causal significance was disputed.

After removal, defendants sought summary judgment on exposure and expert admissibility. Dr. Schecter linked cancer to smoking and PCB exposure; Dr. Teitelbaum attributed it in significant part to workplace materials. The experts' described method combined scientific studies, exposure information, interviews and medical records rather than asserting that a single study independently proved causation. Animal material described infant-mouse injections, high doses/concentrations and alveologenic adenomas; Joiner was an adult with small-cell carcinoma and alleged lower occupational exposure. The four discussed human studies concerned Italian capacitor workers, Monsanto PCB workers, Norwegian mineral-oil workers and Japanese Yusho patients. These are descriptions of proffered materials, not judicially established medical conclusions. The complete studies and depositions have not been independently examined here.

**Judgments below.** The District Court, 864 F.Supp. 1310 (N.D. Ga. 1994), found a genuine dispute on PCB exposure but no genuine dispute supporting furan/dioxin exposure. It excluded causation testimony as unsupported speculation and granted summary judgment. The Eleventh Circuit, 78 F.3d 524 (1996), reversed. It stated that exclusion attracted particularly stringent review because the Rules favored admission; it faulted evaluating the experts' conclusions instead of legal reliability and found record testimony supporting furan/dioxin exposure. The defendants did not challenge that latter exposure ruling in their certiorari petition. The District Court had not explicitly considered admissibility of causation evidence assuming exposure to furans and dioxins.

**Recorded party position.** Both sides accepted abuse of discretion as the verbal appellate standard. Joiner argued that the Eleventh Circuit's phrase meant closer attention to a dispositive ruling rather than a different standard. Do not treat the furan/dioxin exposure dispute as resolved against Joiner or the PCB exclusion as necessarily disposing of every causal theory.

**Support and limits.** [Official opinion](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-522/pdf/USREPORTS-522-136.pdf), record/procedure 139–141, proffers and underlying study descriptions 143–146, unchallenged exposure ruling 147, further descriptions of deposition methodology and lower opinion 151–154; [local control text](USREPORTS-522-136.txt). Complete record, studies, expert depositions, petition and merits briefs remain unrecovered originals. Published descriptions support identification of the distinct theories and lower rulings without reconstruction. They do not independently establish scientific sufficiency, require admission/exclusion, or resolve whether a record-intensive application is warranted at this review stage. A disposition needing details beyond these descriptions requires targeted retrieval. No participation premise is adopted.

## City of Chicago v. International College of Surgeons — 522 U.S. 156; No. 96-910

**Date and question.** Argument October 14, 1997; event-date anchor December 15, 1997. Source-formulated question: whether a lawsuit combining federal constitutional challenges to local administrative action and state claims for record-based review falls within federal district court jurisdiction after removal. This is not a verbatim petition quotation.

**Objective administrative and judicial record.** ICS owned two North Lake Shore Drive mansions. The Landmarks Commission made a preliminary district designation in July 1988; the city council enacted the designation ordinance in June 1989. A February 1989 sale/redevelopment contract contemplated retaining the facades and constructing a condominium tower. ICS sought demolition permits in October 1990. The Commission denied them on historical/architectural grounds and later denied renewed applications under the economic-hardship exception.

ICS filed two Cook County actions under Illinois's Administrative Review Law. Complaints alleged facial and as-applied federal due-process, equal-protection and takings violations, unconstitutional administrative procedure, Illinois constitutional violations, and grounds for statutory administrative review. The City removed on federal-question grounds; the District Court consolidated the actions, dismissed some constitutional claims, exercised supplemental jurisdiction and entered summary judgment for the City. It ruled that the ordinances/procedures were constitutionally valid and that the Commission's findings had record support and were not arbitrary or capricious. A third action concerning the Lake Michigan and Chicago Lakefront Protection Ordinance was dismissed but is not part of this review.

**Judgment and reasoning below.** The Seventh Circuit, 91 F.3d 981 (1996), reversed and directed remand to state court for lack of jurisdiction. It distinguished de novo agency review from appellate-style review bound to an administrative record. It treated the latter as inconsistent with federal courts' original jurisdiction under §1441(a), even when the complaint also contained independently removable facial federal claims. Illinois §5/3–110 forbids new supporting/opposing evidence and treats agency factual findings as prima facie true and correct; legal/factual review extends to the administrative record. The Seventh Circuit treated as-applied claims and state review as record-bound, facial claims as independent of the record.

**Supported competing arguments and scope.** The City sought reversal of the jurisdiction ruling. ICS maintained that it had selected state statutory administrative review rather than a plenary §1983 action; adding federal grounds to that appellate review, it argued, did not supply original jurisdiction. ICS also asserted abstention and discretionary refusal under §1367(c). The District Court had considered economy, convenience, fairness and comity in retaining the state claims. The Seventh Circuit's jurisdictional disposition did not decide those alternative retention arguments. ICS represented that Illinois appellate courts had not interpreted the local ordinance or decided the relevant state constitutional questions; retain this as its contention, not independently verified comprehensive precedent research.

**Support and limits.** [Official opinion](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-522/pdf/USREPORTS-522-156.pdf), record/lower judgment 159–163, reported positions 166–168, 173–174 and 186–188; [local control text](USREPORTS-522-156.txt). Original complaints, entire administrative record, district opinion, petition and briefs were not independently recovered. Published recitation supports the jurisdictional vehicle and distinction between statutory power and challenged retention. It does not supply a new constitutional merits finding or a compulsory abstention determination. No reconstruction or participation premise is adopted.
