## Underlying conduct, interview, and lower-court posture

- While a union officer in 1987 and 1988, James Brogan accepted cash payments from JRD Management Corporation, a real-estate company employing members of the union. The federal investigators already possessed company records reflecting the payments before the interview. [399,409.]
- **The official record narrative states the interview occurred October 4, 1993. Cornell reproduces that date.** Department of Labor and Internal Revenue Service agents visited Brogan at home, identified themselves, explained their investigation of JRD and other individuals, and sought his cooperation. They said that, if he wished to cooperate, he should have counsel contact the U.S. Attorney's Office and that counsel could be appointed if he could not afford one. He agreed to answer questions. [399.]
- The evening visit was unannounced. One agent testified that no advance warning was given to preserve surprise (App.5). Counsel was not present. When asked whether he had received cash or gifts from JRD while a union officer, Brogan answered “no.” Only after the answer did the agents disclose their contrary records and warn that lying to federal investigators was criminal. Brogan did not change his answers. The agents asked no further questions, and the interview ended shortly thereafter. [399–400,409–410.]
- He was indicted for accepting unlawful employer payments under 29 U.S.C.§§186(b)(1),186(a)(2),186(d)(2), and for violating 18 U.S.C.§1001. A Southern District of New York jury tried him with several co-defendants and found him guilty. [400.]
- The Second Circuit affirmed and rejected the judicially created “exculpatory no” exception categorically, while accepting that Brogan's statement was a true exculpatory no under formulations recognized by other circuits. The relevant published exculpatory-no opinion is **United States v. Wiener, 96 F.3d35 (2d Cir.1996)**, specifically pages37 and40. The target official source repeatedly cites 96 F.3d35. [400–401,416.]
- The published source reports that limitations had expired on four of the five payments Brogan was accused of accepting. This is limited to that count of underlying payments; it does not establish that every charged offense was time barred or that the §1001 statement was a repetition of an earlier charge. [411.]

## Operative statutory text

The source reproduces 18 U.S.C.§1001 (1988 ed.) in full as applicable to Brogan's statement:

> Whoever, in any matter within the jurisdiction of any department or agency of the United States knowingly and willfully falsifies, conceals or covers up by any trick, scheme, or device a material fact, or makes any false, fictitious or fraudulent statements or representations, or makes or uses any false writing or document knowing the same to contain any false, fictitious or fraudulent statement or entry, shall be fined not more than $10,000 or imprisoned not more than five years, or both.

## Reported litigating positions and preserved limits

Brogan did not contest that the answer was false or knowingly and willfully given; his brief at page5 conceded that a literal reading of the text defeated his asserted exception. That concession is about his presented statutory argument, not a blanket concession of every independently material legal issue. [401.]

He sought a limited exception for a simple denial of wrongdoing, arguing that §1001 protected governmental functions from perversion and that such a bare denial did not pervert them. He also invoked the “spirit” of the Fifth Amendment, the choice among admitting guilt, silence, and denial, possible lack of awareness of silence as an option, fear that silence could later be used, and the risk of prosecutors adding false-statement charges to underlying crimes. These are positions attributed to his brief, not adopted rules. [401–405.]

The Second Circuit's published discussion expressly left open whether §1001 requires knowledge that making such a statement is unlawful. It also noted that a factfinder might find an unexpected denial lacked the required criminal intent and that an instruction could permit such an inference. The opinion did not turn mere denial of responsibility into proof of knowledge of unlawfulness. Do not replace those bounded observations with a finding that Brogan lacked intent, an already preserved independent claim, or an entitlement to a new trial. [416, quoting Wiener,96 F.3d40.]

The Solicitor General acknowledged at oral argument that the provision could be used to “escalate completely innocent conduct into a felony” (Tr.36). That is an attributed litigation statement about possible statutory reach, not a finding that Brogan's underlying conduct was innocent or that his prosecution was initiated for an illicit purpose. [411.]

## Legislative and executive materials reported in the source

- The 1863 enactment addressed fraudulent claims against the Government; the 1918 enactment broadened coverage to other statements made with an intent to cheat or defraud it. The 1934 enactment extended the provision to false statements in matters within federal agency jurisdiction following concerns about false regulatory reports. Congress separated false claims and false statements in the 1948 recodification. These are legislative sequence facts; their interpretive force remains open. [412–413 & n.4.]
- The U.S. Attorneys' Manual at the filing of Brogan's charges stated: “Where the statement takes the form of an ‘exculpatory no,’ 18 U.S.C.§1001 does not apply regardless who asks the question.” Manual9-42.160 (Oct.1,1988). The February12,1996 text instead stated a Department policy that it was not appropriate to charge a §1001 violation where a suspect merely denied guilt in response to governmental questioning. September1997 text read: “It is the Department's policy not to charge a Section1001 violation in situations in which a suspect, during an investigation, merely denies guilt in response to questioning by the government.” These are quoted executive policy formulations, not statutory amendments or established judicially enforceable defenses. [415 & n.6.]
- The source identifies an unenacted 1981 Senate criminal-code proposal protecting an unelaborated oral denial during investigation and requiring a warning for certain unvolunteered oral statements. Other proposed reforms would have limited coverage to writings/recorded statements or excluded certain investigative statements. They were proposals, not operative enactments. [417–418.]
- The reported November1997 Sentencing Guidelines application note excluded ordinary nonperjurious denials of guilt from the obstruction enhancement. This sentencing policy is distinct from the elements of §1001 or the specific sentencing disposition here. [415 n.7.]
