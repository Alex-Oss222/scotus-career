## Operative regulation and the disability component

Section 36.104 is a Department of Justice Title III regulation. The lower opinion's references to these Title III provisions as EEOC regulations do not change their actual agency source. The official text expressly includes symptomatic and asymptomatic HIV disease within physical or mental impairment. It also preserves all three disability alternatives and requires substantial limitation of a major life activity for the actual-impairment alternative. Its major-life-activities list uses illustrative language, and its physiological-disorder definition expressly includes the reproductive system. Naming HIV as an impairment does not by itself decide every individual's disability under the first alternative, and naming a body system is distinct from identifying and applying a major-life-activity rule. The appendix contains every disability-definition qualification and exclusion in this section, not just the favorable HIV clause.

The lower court treated Abbott's case as arising under the actual-impairment alternative. It held that HIV was an impairment, reproduction was a major life activity, and the infection substantially limited her reproductive activity. It assumed favorably to Bragdon, without definitively deciding, that a plaintiff must show an individual connection to the asserted major activity. It then relied on Abbott's testimony that her infection caused her decision not to have children and rejected the proposed contrary inference from her answer about impaired life functions. The opinion attributes to Bragdon a concession concerning approximately 25% transmission risk without AZT and 8% with it. Those are historical record descriptions, not current medical guidance. The court's expressed holding concerning a fecund woman's reproduction and its final caution about case-specific facts must both be preserved. It did not decide the United States' alternative regarded-as-disabled contention. See Part III, especially reproduced paragraphs 25–35 and footnote 2.

The lower opinion also quotes the comparative substantial-limitation formulation from Appendix B to Part 36. That quoted formulation is supported here as a quotation in the recovered opinion; it is not contained in the newly copied section 36.104 itself. Do not attribute Appendix B's additional language to the body of section 36.104 or import a later disability definition.

## What the court below actually did on direct threat

**Posture and application judgment.** The parties cross-moved after discovery. The district court granted Abbott summary judgment, and the First Circuit affirmed. Its Rule 56 discussion requires a properly documented motion, a genuine and material factual issue to defeat it, reasonable inferences for the opposing party, and rejection of unsupported speculation. Its direct-threat conclusion was that the reported proffers, separately and together, did not create a genuine issue. That conclusion is the lower-court judgment under review; it is not a Supreme Court conclusion supplied by this packet. See Parts II, IV(C), and V.

**Scope actually argued below.** Footnote 4 expressly reports that Bragdon did not argue that his hospital offer was a practice modification eliminating the alleged threat, did not endeavor below to prove hospital treatment significantly safer, and did not press such a theory in the circuit. He treated the hospital offer as gratuitous and claimed the right to refuse routine office treatment altogether. The court therefore did not decide whether additional precautions for an HIV-positive patient would be unlawful. These are the court's descriptions of the litigated position, not inferences from missing papers. They coexist with the original packet's warning that the offer alone proves neither hospital privileges nor suitable facilities.

**Evidence date and its expressly reserved exception.** Parts IV(A) and footnote 5 use an objective standard tied to medical knowledge and the best objective evidence available when treatment was refused. The court rejected both holding a provider responsible for unknowable later advances and using later discoveries to excuse an earlier unfounded refusal. It did not announce an exceptionless rule: it reserved an extreme case in which a subsequent medical breakthrough or new CDC pronouncement overwhelmingly vindicated the provider's concerns, particularly because relief can have prospective effects. It found no such development in this record. That reservation remains a lower-court qualification, not a finding that an exception applies here.

**The competing deference arguments.** Abbott and the Government urged that an uncontradicted consensus among public-health authorities should bind the provider unless shown medically unreasonable. Bragdon urged deference to a skilled provider's own judgment if reasonable in light of then-current medical knowledge. The First Circuit rejected the first argument's effectively conclusive treatment of the official consensus and did not adopt the second argument's provider-deference rule. See Part IV(B), reproduced paragraphs 45–49.

**The rule the lower court chose.** It gave prima facie force to public-health authorities' reasonable medical judgments while permitting challenge by contrary, properly supported opinions from recognized experts, including peer-reviewed research. It described a rebuttable presumption of correctness for the collective judgment and required competent, directly relevant countervailing evidence rather than an unsupported private opinion. Footnote 6 makes its form explicit: this was an intermediate presumption remaining available to the factfinder after contrary evidence appeared, not one that disappeared upon production of contrary evidence. Therefore the opinion cannot accurately be summarized either as conclusive official deference or as having no presumption at all. Whether this particular presumption and its application are legally sound remains for modeling.

**Foundation credited for the public-health judgment.** Part IV(C) quotes the American Dental Association's 1991 policy as stating that little transmission risk accompanies recommended infection controls and that HIV-positive patients can be treated safely in private offices when those controls are used. It separately describes the CDC's 1993 guidance as prescribing universal precautions that should reduce transmission risk. The court expressly acknowledged that the CDC text did not say either that additional measures were unnecessary or that routine treatment was safe; it inferred those propositions from the detailed office-treatment guidance. It also noted that no public-health authority had said routine office care was unsafe. On that combination it found competent evidence of an uncontradicted public-health judgment that routine care presented no direct threat. The Association's affirmative statement, the CDC's actual statement, and the court's inference are three distinct propositions. The professional association is not transformed into the CDC by this description. See reproduced paragraphs 51–52 and footnote 10.

**Marianos and Kuvin.** Footnote 7 reports Marianos's categorical testimony that no procedures beyond universal precautions were needed and that the patient-to-provider risk in routine care was too low to quantify. The court called that testimony of limited value because the record did not show the CDC had publicly taken so explicit a position by September 1994. It did not say that the testimony was never submitted. Footnote 8 reports retained expert Sanford Kuvin's material, but says Bragdon did not claim it rested on knowledge available when he refused treatment, discussed it in a separate later-evidence section of his brief, and used sources from 1995 onward. This supports the stated temporal treatment, not an inference that no defense expert existed or that every part of an unrecovered expert submission was inadmissible.

The court then expressly considered cumulative probative force, even though it said Bragdon did not expressly make a cumulative-proof argument. It recognized that separately insufficient evidence can establish a material fact together, giving examples of needle-injury likelihood combined with infection likelihood, or several small risks adding up to significant risk. It concluded these proffers did not do so. Thus a claim that the First Circuit never considered cumulative evidence is contradicted by the recovered text. Disagreement with its particular cumulative conclusion is different from failure to conduct the inquiry. See reproduced paragraphs 64–66.

Its conclusion also states that the ADA does not promise absolute safety and cautions against a blanket rule for every HIV-positive patient or treatment. It identifies more invasive treatment, stronger risk evidence, or later scientific improvement as potentially material in another case. See reproduced paragraphs 66–70. These qualifications do not themselves reopen this record or supply a new evidentiary hearing.

## Question 3: supported alternatives without a selected result

The granted provider-deference question permits evaluation of the legal status of the provider's judgment and the court's public-health deference method. Independent whole-record triable-issue Question 5 remains ungranted, as the earlier scope refresh states. A dispute about the legal force assigned to categories of medical evidence can be distinct from independently reassessing all evidentiary sufficiency; the model must explain the connection rather than merely relabel a Question 5 disagreement.

**An affirmance path against the granted challenge is supported for consideration.** The First Circuit used contemporaneous objective evidence, rejected a conclusive official-consensus rule, allowed properly supported private expert disagreement, and examined the actual competing proffers both separately and together. It did not hold that a provider must obey official views regardless of evidence. A model concluding that the provider has no further entitlement to deference and that the identified framework supplies no material legal error can leave the direct-threat judgment undisturbed on the granted challenge. It need not invent a categorical CDC command, treat a professional title as dispositive, or independently decide Question 5 to identify that path. The persistent-presumption language, the inferred CDC conclusion, and the treatment of observation testimony must still be considered; they cannot be omitted in order to characterize the lower method as simply neutral weighing.

**A bounded-remand path requires an identified legal or methodological defect that matters.** The recovered opinion supports examination of these concrete candidates without establishing that any candidate is error:

- Whether the intermediate, continuing presumption of correctness gives public-health views a legal force beyond the ordinary assessment of their expertise, foundation and relevance, and whether it changes the treatment of a properly supported provider judgment or disputed fact. The text expressly supplies the presumption; its legality and materiality are open.
- Whether the court conferred that force on a sufficiently established public-health judgment. It acknowledged the CDC guidance did not expressly assert the inferred safety/no-further-precautions propositions and limited the later categorical Marianos statements by date. The Association's independent affirmative statement remains part of the support and cannot be ignored. A claim that this analytical step was legally inadequate must identify the required foundation and explain why it changes the granted deference analysis; the incomplete original packet alone proves no failure of proof.
- Whether requiring recognized scientific expertise to rebut the official judgment improperly displaced competent evidence about the provider's own treatment conditions, as distinct from unsupported medical risk conclusions. Paragraph 62 supplies the actual language about sharp injuries and lay testimony. The model must distinguish personal observation from infection-risk expertise and assess relevance and materiality, not assume that the provider's title entitles all conclusions to deference.

If a model independently concludes that one of these or another specifically supported method defects is legally erroneous, within the granted question, and material to the direct-threat judgment, it may consider a component-limited remand for application of the corrected rule. Such a remand must identify what the lower court must reconsider and why; it does not itself establish a triable issue, significant dental risk, automatic admission of an expert, a right to new evidence, or a new trial. If the asserted defect is only dissatisfaction with the eight proffers' ultimate combined sufficiency, the Question 5 boundary remains an independent obstacle requiring the scope analysis already frozen. There is no neutral finding that a particular error exists or that remand is necessary.

The disability component, direct-threat component, and state-law claim must remain distinct. The lower court treated federal and state discrimination rules together because no distinction affecting its appeal was suggested; that account does not enlarge the Supreme Court's federal grant or itself determine the state-law judgment's eventual procedural treatment. Any selected remedy must specify its component and legal basis.
