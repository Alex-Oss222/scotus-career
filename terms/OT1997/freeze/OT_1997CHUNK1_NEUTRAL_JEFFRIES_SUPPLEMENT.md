## Chair duties, performance and speech capacity

The reported trial record describes introduced bylaws requiring the chair to carry out policies made by the department, faculty and trustees. Listed duties include keeping departmental records, assigning faculty courses, representing the department at internal CUNY meetings, presiding at department meetings, helping prepare departmental budget proposals and evaluating professors for tenure. These concrete duties, rather than a reconstructed generic job description, supply the functional record.

The Provost reportedly told Harleston that interviewees inside and outside the department considered Jeffries's performance adequate; the reviewed activities included scheduling, meetings, recruitment, recordkeeping and representation. The Social Sciences Dean separately reported adequate ordinary administrative performance while characterizing his own review as perfunctory and promising follow-up. These are administrative assessments described in the opinion, not jury findings of flawless performance or proof of every aspect of the chair's role.

The panels characterized this chair position as essentially ministerial and nonpolicymaking. The 1994 panel grounded that characterization in the bylaws and the task-focused assessment and reported no trial evidence of special loyalty/confidentiality obligations or public contact exceeding that of an ordinary professor. The 1995 panel repeated the characterization and public-contact comparison. Preserve that actual-record premise without turning it into a rule that all department chairs lack discretion, that evaluating tenure is mechanically clerical, or that every conceivable chair function was affirmatively disproved. Equally, the title alone supplies no contrary finding of policymaking, confidential trust or special public representation.

The festival was unaffiliated with CUNY. Jeffries was introduced as department chair and as a former curriculum-review committee member. Public identification by office is therefore established, while institutional sponsorship of the festival is not. Identification by office does not alone settle whether the address was governmental expression, and the reported duties remain relevant to the actual employment balance.

## Institutional concerns and their evidentiary status

Harleston's letter reporting the one-year-term vote stated that the speech threatened recruitment, fundraising and CUNY's community relationship. These are specific asserted institutional interests, rather than an unelaborated disruption label. The published account supplies no quantified expected loss or identified lost recruit, withheld donation or disrupted community program. That reporting limit is not a finding that the underlying Court file contains no additional evidence.

The 1994 opinion also describes possible impaired interaction with Jewish faculty, stigma to the department or university and discouragement of benefactors, and states that Harleston and Reynolds did not investigate those matters before recommending the shortened term. This is the panel's record account. It is neither a separate jury finding nor an adopted conclusion that a constitutional investigation duty existed or was violated.

Extensive media attention and officials' public statements condemning the speech or announcing review are reported events. They do not themselves establish operational failure, reasonable prediction or unconstitutional motive. The distinctions between public offense, genuinely feared institutional consequences and a legally sufficient employment justification remain for application of the entering law.

The jury found both that the speech had not hampered effective and efficient operation of the department, college or university and that defendants acted from a reasonable expectation of disruption. Neither finding may silently replace the other. The reported answers contain no separate quantified findings about recruitment, fundraising, faculty relationships or community programs. The 1994 panel's assessment of meager actual-disruption evidence and absence of threatened-harm evidence before the Board, and the 1995 panel's assessment of prospective disruption, remain appellate evaluations, not additional jury findings.

The Dean and Provost later reported angry exchanges about the term limitation and an outburst during an interview with a Harvard student reporter. Defendants offered ineffectiveness evidence and maintained that the term would have been limited without the speech. The 1994 panel stated that the record did not show the Board considered those incidents as a reason for denying reappointment; the reported record also describes conflicting testimony about performance discussion at the later replacement meeting. Later incidents cannot themselves establish the actual reason for the earlier October action. Their role in a separately preserved later action or independent defense depends on the evidence and actual claim scope; this supplement resolves neither.

## October vote mapping and causal limits

At the late-October 1991 vote, Edith Everett, Herman Badillo and Harold Jacobs voted against reappointment outright. Sylvia Bloom abstained; the published account attributes that abstention to her prior criticism of Jeffries's views. Blanche Bernstein was the fourth original outright-rejection voter; her later death and discontinuance of the claims explain why only three such voters appear among the six remaining defendants identified by the later jury answers. Harleston and Reynolds were nonvoting officials. The nine other remaining defendants voted for the one-year term.

This mapping describes the October vote. It does not establish the separate causal contribution of a defeated vote, abstention, proposal, or successful vote; nor does it decide the effect of any actor's role in later action. The packet's separate inquiries into protected conduct, actual personal cause, an independent lawful Board decision and the proved same-decision defense remain intact.
