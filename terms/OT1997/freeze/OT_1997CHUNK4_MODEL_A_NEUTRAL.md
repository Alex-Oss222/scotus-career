* **Case Name:** United States v. Kirk (William Joseph Kirk v. United States)
* **Citation or Docket:** 105 F.3d 997 (5th Cir. 1997) (en banc); lower-court docket: 94-50472; no simulated Supreme Court docket number assigned.

* **October Term:** OT_1997 — new simulation

* **Simulated Event Date:** 1998-02-14 — SIMULATION SCHEDULING ASSIGNMENT. First matter of Chunk 4, after MacArthur (February 13, 1998) and before Johnson v. Rodriguez (February 17, 1998). This is not a historical Supreme Court opinion or grant date.

* **Question Presented:** Whether Congress exceeded its authority under the Commerce Clause by prohibiting Kirk’s possession of a machinegun under 18 U.S.C. § 922(o) where the possession count requires no transaction-specific showing that Kirk, the weapon, or the charged possession crossed state lines. [Source formulation; not certified as a verbatim petition quotation.]
* **Question Granted:** Whether § 922(o)’s possession prohibition is within Congress’s Commerce Clause authority as applied to Kirk’s preserved possession conviction.
* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit, sitting en banc, No. 94-50472, decided February 3, 1997. The en banc court divided evenly, eight to eight with Judge Benavides recused, and by that division affirmed the judgment of the United States District Court for the Western District of Texas (Judge Sam Sparks) convicting Kirk on the possession count. 105 F.3d at 997–98 & n.*. No en banc opinion commands a majority.
* **Relief Requested:** Kirk seeks reversal of his § 922(o) possession conviction. The United States seeks affirmance.
* **Material Record:** A December 21, 1993 indictment charged four § 922(o) counts: possession of a machinegun on January 4, 1989 (Count One), transfer on January 4, 1989 (Count Two), possession on February 21, 1989 (Count Three), and transfer on February 21, 1989 (Count Four). United States v. Kirk, 70 F.3d 791, 793 (5th Cir. 1995); 105 F.3d at 1006. The possession counts make no mention of interstate commerce or of any connection between Kirk’s machinegun, or his possession of it, and commerce. 105 F.3d at 1006. Kirk moved to dismiss, contending that § 922(o) exceeds the commerce power and that the indictment alleged no connection with interstate commerce; the district court denied the motion. Id.; 70 F.3d at 793. Kirk then pleaded guilty to Count One, reserving his right to appeal the denial of his constitutional challenge, and the transfer counts were dismissed under the plea bargain. 105 F.3d at 1006; 70 F.3d at 792, 796 n.8, 797. The reported account of the January 4, 1989 transaction states that Kirk agreed to sell Donald Mueller “an M-16 machinegun” for $1,200; that the two then obtained conversion parts at a rifle range in Dripping Springs, Texas; that Kirk used the parts to convert a semiautomatic EA Company .223 rifle, model J-15, into a machinegun; and that the converted weapon was test-fired with blank ammunition and the transaction completed. On February 21, 1989, at the same range, an Action Arms UZI carbine converted to a machinegun by the addition of an UZI machine bolt was exchanged for $1,100 and a commercial welder. 70 F.3d at 792. The reported opinions do not describe those transactions as admitted elements of the plea, and the conviction does not depend on them. They identify no interstate movement of the converted rifle, its parts, or the charged possession; the opinion favoring reversal describes the conviction as one for “the wholly intrastate possession of a machinegun.” 105 F.3d at 1005. The conviction therefore presents the validity of the possession prohibition without requiring the Court to treat an alleged transfer as an element of the offense. No Second Amendment claim appears in the reported panel or en banc opinions.
* **Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** The Commerce Clause question was raised by pretrial motion, preserved by the conditional plea, and can be resolved on the existing record. 105 F.3d at 1006. The Court need not reconstruct any uncharged interstate transaction or decide the constitutional sufficiency of a hypothetical jurisdictional element. Count One is the only conviction under review; the dismissed transfer counts are not before the Court and cannot be substituted for it. Kirk’s other appellate contentions, decided in the panel opinion that rehearing en banc vacated (specific performance of an earlier plea agreement, double jeopardy following dismissal of an earlier 26 U.S.C. § 5861(d) conviction, and an obstruction-of-justice sentencing enhancement), 70 F.3d at 793–98, are outside the question granted. The en banc opinions are historical lower-court reasoning; where they describe Lopez or Hale differently from the simulation’s controlling decisions, the simulation’s Supreme Court law governs.

Indictment: December 21, 1993; sentence imposed June 24, 1994. 70 F.3d at 793. ## B. LAW ENTERING THE CASE

**Current through:** February 13, 1998. Read the [verbatim Kirk entering-law slice](../entering-law/OT_1997CHUNK4_01_KIRK.md), which supplies the full controlling holdings, their proposition-level authority, limits, and the current Commerce standard. It contains the earlier current-term Rybar and Parker public law; no prepared later matter is authority.

**Lopez:** Identify the enacted regulated class and a constitutionally sufficient interstate mechanism before aggregate magnitude. General downstream crime, fear, insurance, or productivity costs cannot provide the missing connection. The necessary-inclusion route permits genuinely necessary and proper intrastate regulation to prevent evasion or material impairment of a valid interstate regime. Fit must remain judicially identifiable; neither least-restrictive-means review nor a universal legislative-findings requirement applies. Courts may not add a missing commerce element to an unambiguous offense.

**Hale:** The unanimous commerce holding permits possession within the post-May 19, 1986 machinegun transfer/stock regime where possession and transfer are sufficiently integrated that exclusion would permit acquisition, retention, concealment, or continued circulation of prohibited stock and materially impair enforcement. Commerce and Necessary and Proper authority supply this ground, without a transaction-specific interstate element. Marketability alone, remote movement, and generalized gun-crime effects do not.

**Rybar:** Its unanimous Part I applies Hale to possession of two completed machineguns associated with consecutive gun-show sales. Those facts make the market relationship concrete but do not introduce a transaction-specific interstate-crossing element. Rybar adds no new commerce rule. Its separate arms questions and remand do not create an unpresented Second Amendment claim here. **Parker** concerns a legally fixed cross-border support obligation and does not provide the machinegun rule.

**Operative standard:** “Commerce connection, mechanism, and class-wide effects.” Present operation effective June 30, 1997, Mussari/Schroeder, for the latest register application; controlling architecture from Lopez (April 26, 1995), anti-evasion branch from Hale (February 12, 1996). Rybar and Parker supply the subsequent applications reproduced in the slice. The sequence is enacted class, interstate connection and mechanism, constitutional eligibility, magnitude, latitude within valid power, fit, and enforceable boundary.

**Statutory boundaries:** Section 922(o)(2) excepts transfers to or by and possession by or under authority of the United States or its departments/agencies, and a State or its departments/agencies/political subdivisions. It separately excepts lawful transfer or possession of a machinegun lawfully possessed before May 19, 1986; manufacture before that date alone does not suffice. No exception is established for Count One. Section 5845(b), incorporated by §921(a)(23), includes conversion parts and readily restorable weapons in the machinegun definition, but this case concerns the reported converted and test-fired weapon, not every application of that definition.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

* **Reasoning and disposition below:** By an equally divided court, the en banc Fifth Circuit affirmed. 105 F.3d at 998. Judge Parker, for eight judges, would affirm because Congress had a rational basis to conclude that post-1986 manufacture, transfer, and possession of machineguns substantially affect commerce, distinguishing § 922(q) by the history of federal firearm regulation and § 922(o)’s national scope. Id. Judge Higginbotham, for four of those judges, would affirm on a legislative judgment that post-1986 machinegun possession substantially affects interstate commerce, particularly by facilitating the drug trade. Id. at 998, 1002–05. He stressed that Congress “froze in place the market in machine guns,” that a proscribed possession must ordinarily result from a post-1986 transfer apart from worn or converted guns, and that conversion makes tracing often impossible, id. at 1001, 1004, and he gave weight to, but did not find controlling, the absence of congressional findings, id. at 999. Judge Jones, for the other eight judges, would reverse: § 922(o) contains no jurisdictional element and no findings, reaches wholly intrastate possession including weapons made automatic by conversion or wear, and regulates possession that is neither economic activity nor essential to a larger commercial regulatory regime. Id. at 1005, 1010–11, 1014–17. The Government argued below that § 922(o) regulates the channels of, or things in, interstate commerce and is part of a comprehensive approach to the regulation of machineguns. Id. at 1010, 1013–14.
**Kirk's strongest supported argument, developed analytically:** A possession conviction cannot be turned into a transfer conviction by importing dismissed counts or background sale allegations. Hale requires an actual relationship between the enacted class and impairment of a valid interstate regime; ease of enforcement, general marketability, and gun-crime consequences cannot dispense with that constitutional showing. Kirk can dispute whether inclusion of local conversion and subsequent possession has the required relationship, and whether the Government's proposed boundary would authorize possession bans for every object traded nationally. These are arguments about the mechanism and the class's fit, not findings that every local conversion lies outside a market regime.

**The Government's strongest supported argument, developed analytically:** Local conversion creates additional prohibited machinegun stock; requiring proof of a prior sale could leave that stock available for acquisition, retention, concealment, or future circulation. Congress may include that possession class to prevent material impairment of the valid transfer/stock restriction. Hale already rejects an individual interstate-element requirement, and Rybar's concrete sale evidence need not become a constitutional prerequisite. The argument must identify that mechanism rather than rely solely on marketability or general crime costs. Neither side's reconstructed argument is represented as a recovered brief or concession.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

1. **Apply the class/regime rule.** Affirm if Hale's integration and material-impairment conditions encompass the charged possession of prohibited stock produced by local conversion. Explain why the class fits the valid regime without converting background transfer evidence into an element or finding an interstate crossing.
2. **Distinguish on the required mechanism or fit.** A narrower decision could conclude that the Government's supported theory fails Hale's own conditions for this application while preserving possession regulation where those conditions are met. Such a decision must identify the precise limiting distinction and answer the government's local-addition/retention argument. It does not automatically require a new statutory element. Conversely, a universal requirement of a defendant-specific interstate crossing conflicts with Hale and Rybar; a universal proved-sale prerequisite would require explaining how the class/regime anti-evasion rule survives.
3. **Reconsider existing law expressly.** A rule excluding all intrastate possession absent an individual interstate nexus would alter the existing anti-evasion holding and could not be presented as an unchanged application. No need to reconsider Hale is assumed.

**Record and remedy limits:** The absence of an interstate allegation is established; it is not itself an unresolved factual question or proof of constitutional invalidity. The published transaction narrative is not an admitted element of the plea. Its use must preserve the offense actually charged and the conditional-plea posture. No new finding about interstate movement, stock provenance, market statistics, or an unreported transfer may supply a judgment. A disposition rejecting the constitutional basis must identify its consequence for Count One; dismissed transfer counts and the ungranted appellate issues remain outside the judgment. A narrower remand requires a specific legally material unresolved issue, not a demand for missing originals merely because they are unavailable. A weapon becoming automatic through wear is a lower-opinion hypothetical, not this case.

The intermediary examines the cost report, audits it when found necessary, and issues a written “notice of amount of program reimbursement” (NAPR), which determines the total amount payable for Medicare services during the reporting period. The NAPR is subject to review by the Provider Reimbursement Review Board (PRRB), the Secretary, and ultimately the courts. By regulation, the Secretary may reopen, within three years, any determination by an intermediary, the PRRB, or the Secretary herself to recoup excessive (or correct insufficient) reimbursement for a given year. In 1986, Congress changed the method for calculating reimbursable GME costs. In lieu of discrete annual determinations of “reasonable cost . . . actually incurred,” 42 U. S. C. § 1395x(v)(1)(A), the “GME Amendment” now requires the “Secretary [to] determine, for [a] hospital's cost reporting period that began during fiscal year 1984, the average amount recognized as reasonable under [the Act] for direct [GME] costs of the hospital for each full-timeequivalent resident,” § 1395ww(h)(2)(A), and directs the Secretary to use the 1984 amount, adjusted for inflation, to calculate a hospital's GME reimbursement for subsequent years, § 1395ww(h)(2). Based on indications that some “questionable” GME costs had been “erroneously reimbursed” to providers for their 1984 base year, the Secretary's “reaudit” regulation, 42 CFR § 413.86(e), interprets the GME Amendment to authorize intermediaries to conduct a second audit of the 1984 GME costs to ensure accurate reimbursements in future years. The reaudit rule permits no recoupment of excess reimbursement for years in which the reimbursement determination has become final. Rather, the rule seeks to prevent future overpayments and to permit recoupment of prior excess reimbursement only for years still within the three-year reopening window. Petitioner Regions Hospital (Hospi tal) is elig ible for GME cost reimbursement. A reaudit commenced in late 1990 yielded a determination that the Hospital's total allowable 1984 GME costs were $5,916,868, down from the original NAPR of $9,892,644. The Secretary did not attempt to recoup excessive reimbursement paid to the Hospital for its 1984 GME costs, for the three-year window had already closed on that year. Appealing to the PRRB, the Hospital challenged the validity of the reaudit rule. The PRRB responded that it lacked authority to invalidate the rule. On expedited review, the District Court granted the Secretary summary judgment, concluding that § 1395ww(h)(2)(A)'s language was ambiguous, that the reaudit rule reasonably interpreted Congress' prescription, and that the reauditing did not impose an impermissible “retroactive rule.” The Eighth Circuit affirmed.
