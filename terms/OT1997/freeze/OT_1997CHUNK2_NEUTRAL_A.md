### Event, record, and question

Argument was October 8, 1997. Hudson chaired and controlled the First National Banks of Tipton and Hammon; Rackley was Tipton's president and a Hammon director, and Baresel directed both. OCC alleged loans nominally made to others benefited Hudson by enabling redemption of pledged bank stock. Its February 13, 1989 notice alleged violations of 12 U.S.C. §§84(a)(1), 375b and 12 C.F.R. §§31.2(b), 215.4(b), nearly $900,000 in bank losses, and contribution to bank failures. Those losses are allegations; the notice alleged no government loss.

Initial assessments were $100,000 against Hudson and $50,000 each against Rackley and Baresel, with financial resources, good faith, gravity, history, and justice considered. August 31 notices proposed exclusion from insured institutions. October 1989 consent orders settled the assessments for $16,500 (Hudson), $15,000 (Baresel), and $12,500 (Rackley); each agreed not to participate in banking institutions without written regulatory authorization. Other federal enforcement authority was reserved. The first Tenth Circuit decision held that this reservation did not waive the Double Jeopardy claim; no new waiver may be inferred from settlement.

An August 1992 indictment contained 22 counts under 18 U.S.C. §§371, 656 and 2, and 1005 arising from the transactions; only Rackley faced §1005 false-entry charges. The District Court initially refused dismissal. The Tenth Circuit, 14 F.3d 536 (1994), affirmed the nonparticipation-sanction ruling and remanded the monetary issue. After the District Court dismissed, the Tenth Circuit, 92 F.3d 1026 (1996), reversed under Halper, concluding that actual fines were not grossly disproportionate to proved government damages. This is the lower court's legal conclusion, not an independently established government-loss figure.

The verified petition question concerns whether Treasury's in-personam civil monetary penalties, together with other sanctions, constituted punishment. The same-offense issue is contingent analytical work, not a separately verified grant question; the Tenth Circuit expressly did not decide Blockburger. Petitioners seek reversal of the reinstated prosecution; the United States defends it. Classification must address the actual combined-sanction submission while respecting the earlier nonparticipation ruling's procedural scope. Excessive Fines and independent due-process claims are not added.

3. **Kurth Ranch (June 6, 1994):** The combined criminally conditioned, arrest-linked, extraordinary tax on confiscated contraband was punishment after criminal punishment on the preserved same-offense premise. ## 3. Bay Area Laundry and Dry Cleaning Pension Trust Fund v. Ferbar — December 15, 1997

### Event, record, and question

The Fund is the petitioner seeking reversal of a limitations dismissal; the runtime's description of the employer's position as the position supporting requested change is incorrect. Ferbar defends dismissal. Argument was November 10, 1997. Ferbar Corporation and Stephen Barnes owned three laundries; contributions for one stopped in 1983 and for the others in March 1985. The December 12, 1986 demand assessed $45,570.80, offering full payment within sixty days after receipt or 240 monthly installments of $345.50 beginning February 1, 1987. Ferbar sought trustee reconsideration without receiving an expressly responsive answer, initiated arbitration July 8, 1987, and made no payments. Arbitration had not occurred at the reported stage. An April 14, 1987 letter warned that failure to cure within sixty days would constitute default.

The Fund sued February 9, 1993, demanding the entire $45,570.80 or alternatively $25,375 in then-due installments plus an injunction for future payments as due. The District Court applied the three-year discovery period and alternatively found the whole obligation untimely six years after the first missed installment. The Ninth Circuit, 73 F.3d 971 (1996), recognized the later-of formulation but held that the six years ran from complete withdrawal in March 1985. It followed Thibodo, reasoning that missed-payment accrual could make the discovery provision superfluous and give the creditor control of accrual; Judge Trott noted the conflict. Joyce, 871 F.2d 1119 (D.C. Cir. 1989), used a missed-payment trigger; Kahle, 43 F.3d 852 (3d Cir. 1994), separate installment periods; Navco, 3 F.3d 167 (7th Cir. 1993), one period from first missed payment. Those are competing lower authorities, not adopted simulated Supreme Court law.

The Fund's appellate and merits submissions invoked §1399(c)(2)'s sixty-day provision and the demand's receipt-based prepayment period to argue even the first payment timely. Its petition, however, represented that the Kahle/Navco conflict determined the case and Navco's rule barred the suit. Preserve those attributed representations when assessing question scope; do not silently turn them into either waiver or permission to ignore the petition. Ferbar argued that the complaint's full-debt demand amounted to acceleration. No earlier acceleration is established merely by delinquency, the April warning, or the complaint's alternative request.

The questions concern accrual under the enacted limitation and whether an untimely first installment bars subsequent installments within six years. The Court is not choosing a borrowed period, recalculating liability, deciding an uncompleted arbitration, or assuming every assessment defense resolved.

### Law entering the event

Under 29 U.S.C. §1381(a), covered withdrawal produces withdrawal liability. Section 1399(b)(1) requires notice, assessment, schedule, and demand as soon as practicable. Section 1399(c) separately governs actual payment commencement, installments, overdue interest, default, and optional acceleration. Section 1451(a)(1) permits suit by an adversely affected fiduciary; §1451(f) provides the longer of six years after the cause arose or three years after actual or constructive knowledge of the cause, with its fraud/concealment qualification if actually implicated. These distinct events cannot be collapsed merely because the underlying debt originated at withdrawal. Nor does the existence of a schedule itself establish timely notice, acceleration, or a particular maturity date for the entire debt.

**Milwaukee Brewery Workers' Pension Plan v. Jos. Schlitz Brewing Co. (February 21, 1995)** is directly material and omitted from the runtime selection. Breyer's unanimous opinion distinguishes an assumed computational starting date from actual demand, payment, delinquency, and acceleration. Actual payments begin no later than sixty days after demand, notwithstanding review or appeal; intervals may differ if the plan specifies them. Default based on nonpayment requires that failure remain uncured sixty days after written notice; an alternative plan-defined default must indicate substantial likelihood of inability to pay. Default and election to accelerate are separate from the first overdue payment. Its installment-calculation holding does not already decide §1451(f) accrual.

**Concrete Pipe (June 14, 1993)** separates sponsor assessment/enforcement from neutral adjudication and preserves ordinary statutory preponderance rebuttal. It supplies no conclusive finding that this assessment is correct. **North Star Steel (May 30, 1995)** governs borrowing for WARN, not an express federal limitation such as §1451(f); it decides no accrual question here. **Inter-Modal (May 12, 1997)** separates protected substantive plan rights from action predicates and available relief, but its §510 holding is not a withdrawal-liability accrual rule.

Relevant exact modules: **Neutral adjudication and withdrawal-liability rebuttal** (Concrete Pipe, June 14, 1993), **Borrowing limitations periods for WARN claims** (May 30, 1995), and **ERISA interference with prospective welfare-plan rights** (Inter-Modal, May 12, 1997). The latter two are limiting comparisons, not substitutes for §§1399 and 1451. Milwaukee's exact rule is in its canonical Holdings entry; no invented additional Standards module is supplied.

### Supported competing paths

The Fund can distinguish statutory liability at withdrawal from an enforceable failure to pay, argue that separate due installments produce separate claims, and resist acceleration by implication. The employer can invoke statutory repose and the concern that unilateral delay in assessment cannot postpone limitations indefinitely; it can also defend a single cause upon initial default, if the enacted payment structure permits that construction. The strongest answer to delayed-demand concerns is to examine the “as soon as practicable” duty and any properly preserved delay defense on their actual predicates, without rewriting accrual. The strongest answer to automatic separate accrual is the effect of a validly invoked acceleration on later maturity; no such invocation is presumed.

Lawful resolutions include a withdrawal-based rule if text and action availability support it; a first-default rule for the entire matured claim if legally justified; or separate accrual for independently due installments, with any actual acceleration analyzed separately. A remand can permit collection of timely claims while leaving earlier installments and remaining defenses for ordinary adjudication. The distinction between a payment becoming due, a statutory grace/cure mechanism, and acceleration must be resolved from verified facts before declaring even the first installment time-barred. No final amount, all-installments recovery, tolling finding, forfeiture, or payment order follows solely from rejecting the Ninth Circuit's rationale.

## 4. City of Chicago v. International College of Surgeons — December 15, 1997

### Event, record, and question

Argument was October 14, 1997. ICS owned two North Lake Shore Drive mansions. The Commission preliminarily designated a landmark district in July 1988; the City enacted the designation ordinance in June 1989. A February 1989 sale/redevelopment contract contemplated retaining the facades and building a condominium tower. ICS applied for demolition permits in October 1990. The Commission denied them on historical/architectural grounds and denied renewed applications under the economic-hardship exception.

Two Cook County complaints under the Illinois Administrative Review Law asserted facial and as-applied federal due-process, equal-protection, and takings claims, unconstitutional administrative procedure, state constitutional claims, and statutory review grounds. After removal, the District Court consolidated the cases, dismissed some constitutional claims, retained state claims, and granted the City summary judgment, upholding the ordinances/procedure and finding the administrative decisions supported and not arbitrary. A third Lakefront Protection Ordinance action is outside this review.

The Seventh Circuit, 91 F.3d 981 (1996), directed remand for lack of jurisdiction. It distinguished de novo review from appellate-style review confined to an agency record, treating the latter as inconsistent with §1441(a)'s original-jurisdiction requirement even alongside independently removable facial federal claims. Illinois §5/3–110 bars new supporting or opposing evidence and treats agency factual findings as prima facie true and correct; legal and factual review extends to the administrative record. The circuit classified as-applied and state-review claims as record-bound and facial claims as independent of that record.

The City seeks reversal of this jurisdictional judgment. ICS argues that its chosen statutory administrative-review proceeding, rather than a plenary §1983 action, does not acquire original jurisdiction merely by presenting federal grounds. ICS separately urges abstention and discretionary refusal under §1367(c); the District Court considered economy, convenience, fairness and comity, while the Seventh Circuit did not decide those alternative retention arguments. ICS's assertion that Illinois appellate courts had not construed the ordinance or decided the state constitutional questions remains its contention, not a verified exhaustive precedent search. No reversal of the jurisdictional ground automatically resolves these retention questions or the constitutional merits.

### Law entering the event

Sections 1331 and 1441(a) supply original federal-question jurisdiction and removal of a qualifying civil action; federal claims must arise from the well-pleaded complaint and be substantial. Section 1367(a), subject to express exceptions, supplies supplemental jurisdiction over other claims so related to claims within original jurisdiction that they form the same Article III case or controversy, including claims involving additional parties. Section 1367(c) separately authorizes declining a claim for novel/complex state law, substantial predominance, dismissal of all original-jurisdiction claims, or exceptional circumstances presenting other compelling reasons. Statutory power and its discretionary exercise are different questions. Gibbs, 383 U.S. 715 (1966), supplies common-nucleus background and the values of economy, convenience, fairness, and comity; it is not permission to disregard the enacted conditions.

**Peacock (February 21, 1996)** denies new full-debt liability against a new judgment debtor without an independent jurisdictional basis. It distinguishes ordinary execution from a later controversy involving new facts and duties. Its original action predates §1367's effective boundary and the later case supplies no original claim; it does not decide same-case supplemental review of administrative decisions. Thomas's ancillary holding has eight votes; Stevens's restorative-enforcement position is noncontrolling.

**Quackenbush (June 3, 1996)** unanimously separates statutory jurisdiction from abstention and the appellate route. Section 1447(d) covers remands actually resting on covered §1447(c) grounds; a separate abstention remand is not converted into a jurisdictional defect. Conclusive surrender of the federal forum supports the specified §1291 routes. Burford permits outright dismissal/remand only when relief is equitable or otherwise discretionary; an independent legal-damages claim may be stayed on a proper showing but cannot simply be surrendered. Neither the existence of abstention predicates nor a stay was decided universally. These limits must be matched to the actual relief here.

Exact modules: **Covered removal grounds and the bar on appellate remand review** (Things Remembered, December 5, 1995; Quackenbush clarification June 3, 1996); **Appeal of an abstention order surrendering the federal forum** and **Burford abstention and independent legal-damages claims** (Quackenbush, June 3, 1996); **Ancillary enforcement and a new judgment debtor** (Peacock, February 21, 1996). **Article III power before disputed statutory merits** (Steel Co., June 2, 1997) prevents merits-first avoidance of a genuine jurisdictional issue, but does not itself decide supplemental power here.

The City can rely on substantial pleaded federal claims, common facts, and §1367(a)'s broad coverage of related claims; a state procedural label does not by itself alter federal statutory jurisdiction. ICS can argue that the demanded appellate-type review of a local adjudication falls outside the original civil action Congress authorized for district courts, and that federalism and state administrative structure matter to the statute's scope. The City must answer that structural argument; ICS must answer the enacted same-case language and distinguish actual state-court judgments from administrative action.

The Court may recognize power and return for properly preserved discretionary or abstention questions; it may conclude that the particular review claim lies outside the statutory grant and direct the corresponding remand; or it may resolve a supported narrower claim-classification or procedural ground. A decision recognizing supplemental power need not mandate its exercise or reinstate the District Court's entire merits judgment. No categorical rule concerning every administrative appeal, diversity action, §1447 remand, or state judgment is assumed.

## 5. General Electric Co. v. Joiner — December 15, 1997

### Event, record, and question

Argument was October 14, 1997. Joiner worked as a Thomasville electrician from 1973, servicing transformers whose mineral-oil coolant contacted his arms and hands and sometimes his eyes and mouth. PCB contamination of some transformers was discovered in 1983. He developed small-cell lung cancer in 1991 and sued in 1992. Monsanto manufactured PCBs; General Electric and Westinghouse manufactured transformers and dielectric fluid. His theory is promotion or acceleration by PCBs, furans and dioxins, not necessarily sole causation. Eight years of smoking, parental smoking, and family lung-cancer history are part of the record; their significance is disputed.

After removal, defendants sought summary judgment on exposure and expert admissibility. Schecter linked cancer to smoking and PCB exposure; Teitelbaum attributed it in significant part to workplace materials. Their described method combines scientific studies, exposure information, interviews and medical records rather than relying on one study as independently dispositive. The described animal studies involved infant-mouse injections, high doses/concentrations and alveologenic adenomas; Joiner was an adult with small-cell carcinoma and allegedly lower occupational exposure. Four human studies concerned Italian capacitor workers, Monsanto PCB workers, Norwegian mineral-oil workers and Japanese Yusho patients. These are descriptions of proffered materials, not judicial medical findings. Full studies, depositions, and the precise cumulative inferential explanation remain unrecovered by this source review.

The [sanitized study supplement](OT_1997CHUNK2_NEUTRAL_FACTS_JOINER_STUDIES.md) supplies these reported observations. Bertazzi's Italian capacitor-worker study found excess lung-cancer deaths in small numbers without statistical significance; its authors did not associate that excess with plant exposure. Zack and Musch's Monsanto study found a somewhat elevated but statistically nonsignificant rate and did not suggest the PCB link. Ronneberg's Norwegian study found a statistically significant increase in mineral-oil-exposed workers, did not mention PCBs, and limited its observations to the oil studied. Kuratsune's Yusho report found a statistically significant increase in a PCB-exposed group that also ingested toxic rice oil and encountered multiple possible carcinogens, without isolating an independent PCB effect in the supplied recitation. No missing sample size, confidence interval, confounder adjustment, or equivalence of exposures is invented.

Teitelbaum described a weight-of-evidence method: assess general methodological/statistical adequacy, consider collected data against knowledge of the substance and exposure, and form an opinion to a reasonable medical probability using interviews and medical records. No single study was asserted to decide individual causation. The Eleventh Circuit described the combined reasoning and cited work of at least thirteen researchers and WHO reports. The District Court characterized the animal-study response as defending animal studies generally. Preserve that characterization without turning an unrecovered complete study-by-study bridge into an established failure to offer one. Species, age, dose, route, tumor type, and co-exposure differences are relevant to foundation and fit, but neither a categorical animal-study exclusion nor a universal statistical-significance prerequisite is entering law.

The District Court, 864 F.Supp. 1310 (N.D. Ga. 1994), found a genuine PCB-exposure dispute but no genuine furan/dioxin-exposure dispute, excluded causation testimony as unsupported speculation, and granted summary judgment. The Eleventh Circuit, 78 F.3d 524 (1996), reversed, using particularly stringent review because of the Rules' admission policy, criticizing assessment of conclusions instead of legal reliability, and identifying testimony supporting furan/dioxin exposure. Defendants did not challenge the latter exposure ruling in the certiorari petition. The District Court had not explicitly considered causation admissibility assuming furan/dioxin exposure. The packet must therefore preserve that separate theory even if it concludes that PCB-only exclusion was permissible.

The reported grant addresses the appellate standard for admission or exclusion of Daubert expert evidence. Case-specific application is a distinct review-scope question, not a separately verified grant. Both parties accepted “abuse of discretion” as the verbal standard; Joiner argued that the circuit's phrase meant closer attention to a dispositive ruling, not a different standard. Petitioners seek reversal and reinstatement, while the Joiners defend the circuit judgment. Agreement on a verbal standard does not settle its lawful content or authorize factfinding.

### Law entering the event

**Daubert (June 28, 1993)** displaces mandatory Frye general acceptance. Under the original Rules 104(a) and 702, the proponent must establish by a preponderance a preliminary foundation of scientifically reliable knowledge fitting the disputed question and assisting the factfinder. Relevant considerations are flexible and nonexclusive: testing, peer review/publication, error information and operational standards, and acceptance. The inquiry concerns principles, methodology, and valid application; no single consideration is invariably required. Admissibility does not itself decide ultimate causation or sufficiency. The opinion expressly leaves appellate review unresolved and adopts neither later Rule 702 amendments nor a universal technical-expertise rule.

The original Rule 702 permits qualified expert testimony where scientific, technical, or other specialized knowledge will assist the factfinder to understand evidence or determine a fact in issue. Rule 104(a) governs the judge's preliminary determination; Rule 703 separately regulates bases, and Rules 403 and 706 remain available. Rule 56 governs the distinct summary-judgment inquiry. No criminal reasonable-doubt burden or de novo factfinding follows.

**Koon (June 13, 1996)** supplies a task-specific analogy: abuse-of-discretion review corrects legal error independently, respects historical findings under their proper standard, and defers to supported case-specific judgment. Its Guidelines categories do not transfer to Rule 702. **Ornelas (May 28, 1996)** adopts independent review of ultimate warrantless-search probable cause and reasonable suspicion while protecting facts and giving grounded inferences due weight. It expressly does not resolve all mixed questions. Pierce v. Underwood, 487 U.S. 552 (1988), and Cooter & Gell, 496 U.S. 384 (1990), are the surviving task-sensitive appellate-review background identified by those holdings.

Exact modules: **Scientific expert evidence under original Rules 104(a) and 702** (Daubert, June 28, 1993); **Guidelines departure classification and appellate review** (Koon and Powell, June 13, 1996); **Independent appellate review of suspicion and nonwarrant probable cause** (Ornelas, May 28, 1996). The latter two are analogies, not an existing Rule 702 appellate holding.

### Supported competing paths

Petitioners can defend ordinary discretionary evidentiary review and insist that a judge may test whether a scientific inference actually connects its data to the proposition offered. The Joiners can argue that the District Court substituted its assessment of persuasiveness for the flexible foundation inquiry, relied on scientific dissimilarities that do not undermine the experts' actual promotion theory, or incorrectly resolved exposure evidence. Neither side may transform Daubert into an automatic jury entitlement or a requirement that an expert produce an identical published study.

A uniform abuse-of-discretion rule, with independent correction of legal mistakes, can be paired either with sustaining a supported exclusion or with remand for reconsideration. A more independent review of a distinct legal boundary remains possible if justified by the appellate task; outcome-dispositive effect alone does not establish that justification. Even if PCB causation exclusion is sustained, unresolved alternative exposures or mixtures must be treated at their actual procedural scope. Conversely, finding an abuse does not establish causation or command admission of every opinion. The remedy must track which ground the appellate court reached and which issues remain.
