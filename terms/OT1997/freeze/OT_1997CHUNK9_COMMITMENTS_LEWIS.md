## Record and reach

The presented claim concerns Smith's own pursuit and unintended collision, not a constitutional duty to protect the victim from private violence. Plaintiffs assert no Fourth or Eighth Amendment claim. Under Brower the absence of termination by intentionally applied means matters; an unsuccessful command to stop and an unintended collision do not establish a seizure. Graham and the actual Lanier specific-provision rule remain applicable when a specific provision does cover the conduct. Albright's actual seven-vote judgment supplies no new general displacement rationale.

The plaintiff-favorable account is retained: roughly seventy-five seconds over 1.3 miles, exceptional speed and inadequate following distance, residential roads and intersections, unhelmeted minors, danger to other travelers, no established dangerous-felony predicate, and alleged or conceded pursuit-policy violations. Smith did not intend the collision. No invented benevolent finding, purpose to injure, hearing of Stapp's command or known violent offender changes that account. Policy breaches may be evidence of disregard but do not themselves enact a federal constitutional rule or prove particularized federal notice.

The municipal judgments were affirmed below and no cross-petition reopens them; state dispositions were not appealed. The present result addresses only the reviewed federal individual claim. It orders no damages, trial, municipal retrial or state-law disposition.

## Tested paths

**A. Emergency-sensitive constitutional disposition.** The Due Process Clause protects against an official's arbitrary abuse of power, not every grave error causing bodily injury. For a rapidly evolving high-speed pursuit requiring decisions without a practical opportunity for considered deliberation, the conscience-shocking culpability necessary for this substantive-due-process claim is a purpose to cause harm unrelated to a legitimate law-enforcement objective. Recklessness or deliberate indifference to the pursuit's dangers does not alone meet that standard. Whitley's competing-obligation analysis supplies the emergency analogy; it did not itself decide civilian pursuit liability. Farmer's actual-awareness rule and Doe's supervisory rule address settings with a different duty and meaningful opportunity to respond. Their distinct operation is preserved.

The strongest objection is that this pursuit was discretionary and allegedly unnecessary from its beginning, and that declining or stopping it could have removed the danger. A law-enforcement label cannot establish good faith or categorically excuse deliberate official injury. Nevertheless, deciding whether to pursue and how to respond during this continuous seventy-five-second flight involves contemporaneous competing demands, rather than the practical opportunity for unhurried consideration that makes deliberate indifference adequate elsewhere. The absence of a serious original offense and the policy violations support an allegation of very bad judgment; without evidence of the requisite harmful purpose they do not supply this constitutional wrong. This is a classification of the supported encounter, not a factual finding that the officer correctly weighed its risks.

The rule does not cover a contrived encounter used to injure, a purposeful ramming, or every action that an official calls an emergency. Nor does it decide the precise culpability required for materially different conduct allowing actual deliberation. No exhaustive seconds-based test, universal intent requirement for all executive action, or universal sufficiency of deliberate indifference outside emergencies follows.

**B. Deliberate-indifference merits path.** The direct creation of an extreme risk, its apparent lack of necessity, and the opportunity to desist could support a constitutional inference under a serious actual-awareness rule. The direct-deprivation distinction in Pinder and Lanier prevents rejecting this path solely for lack of custody. But Farmer and Doe do not erase context: custodial safety and a supervisor's opportunity to address deliberate sexual abuse cannot simply furnish the culpability rule for an evolving chase. Canton concerns municipal attribution, not the underlying individual right. This path's possible validity for a deliberative encounter is reserved; it does not establish that this supported seventy-five-second pursuit satisfies the needed constitutional culpability.

**C. Immunity-only disposition.** Independently, the law on May 22, 1990 did not make this unintended pursuit collision's substantive constitutional unlawfulness sufficiently apparent. All relevant authorities must be considered, without demanding an identical pursuit case. Garner and Brower concern intentional force or intentionally applied stopping means; they do not supply an obvious rule for this nonseizure. Wood II concerns abandonment after taking control of a driver's vehicle and passenger's protective circumstances; Fargo's gross-negligence formulation was questioned and modified. Checki addresses intentional misuse of a police car to terrorize, materially different from the established pursuit purpose here. Roach and Jones rejected the actual claims before them; their recognition that some sufficiently egregious pursuit might be actionable does not identify this particular constitutional boundary. Daniels expressly reserved recklessness questions. Later Farmer, Doe, Collins and current decisions cannot retroactively furnish notice.

The strongest objection is that Wood II plus the prohibition on unjustified deadly force gave sufficiently particular warning when Smith knowingly created extreme danger for no established serious offense. The objection is substantial but does not remove the distinction between intentionally using lethal means and an unintended collision during a pursuit, nor the unsettled due-process culpability in that context. The contrary notice judgment cannot stand merely because injuries are severe or a departmental directive is violated. Uhlrig, Elder, Lanier and Smith v. Mattox supply the current method, not retroactive substantive rules. No exact-case or numerical-consensus requirement is imposed.

## Proposition and remedy audit

All eight support reversal of the reviewed individual-immunity denial and termination of the presented federal individual claim. Stevens rests solely on notice and does not join the new merits rule. Seven support the specific rapid-pursuit purpose-to-harm proposition and its application. Of those seven, Scalia and Thomas withhold assent from an unrestricted judicial methodology based on subjective moral appraisal; a concise opinion grounded in inherited authority and the precise pursuit rule can retain their substantive join. O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer support the context-sensitive explanation. No final topology or authorship is assigned here, and no Stone vote is included.

The common judgment leaves the district court's favorable individual judgment in effect through the bounded appellate remand. It does not revive municipal or state counts. The merits coalition finds no requisite constitutional culpability on the assumed record, not a final historical finding about every aspect of the chase. Stevens's notice-only position supplies no additional merits authority. No Marks aggregation is proposed: if the final opinion cannot preserve the recorded join limits, proposition-level authority must be counted directly.
