## Parties, conduct and supported chronology

Plaintiff Peter I. Diamondstone sued Trooper Christopher A. Macaluso, supervisors Leroy Prior and David R. Stanton, Public Safety Commissioner A. James Walton, Jr., and Vermont. The candidate's reference to absence of a license is inaccurate: the repeated asserted predicate was lack of automobile INSURANCE. License/registration refusal occurred at the final stop and does not retroactively justify initiating it. All identified cars were insured and carried valid inspection stickers; the reported Vermont inspection process required proof of insurance. What Macaluso knew at each stop remains distinct from Diamondstone's actual coverage.

Earlier stops in 1987, by other officers, led state district courts to sustain a Fifth Amendment refusal to answer. Vermont decriminalized minor traffic violations in 1989. The new insurance violation carried at most a $100 penalty and no imprisonment. Those earlier rulings must not be described as still-controlling criminal-law determinations after decriminalization or as preclusive federal judgments.

## Claims and lower-court treatment kept separate

Count I asserted Fourth/Fifth/Fourteenth Amendment claims under section1983; Count II parallel state constitutional claims; Counts III/IV asserted federal/state speech interference because June5/17 stops interrupted travel to peace vigils; Count V asserted assault, battery, false arrest/imprisonment, malicious prosecution, trespass to chattels and defamation. Filing was April7,1995. The defendants asserted legality, qualified immunity and Vermont's sovereign immunity. Diamondstone sought preclusion from eight no-insurance rulings. The courts declined preclusion because defendants lacked the necessary full/fair opportunity under Vermont law; notice from an actual ruling is analytically different from preclusion.

The appellate court held stops3-10 and12 unsupported by reasonable suspicion on the undisputed initial-stop facts. It relied on the old/refused information, time lapse, changed insured vehicles, valid stickers and absence of new evidence; anticipated future refusal after detention did not itself justify detention. It affirmed Fifth Amendment rejection because the insurance scheme was civil/nonpunitive without a supported criminal exposure. These are lower legal conclusions available for review, not assumed federal holdings.

Macaluso said supervisors encouraged further tickets and that he consulted the hearing officer. The court rejected superiority instructions as sufficient justification for ignoring contrary judicial rulings but left open whether actual further communications showed a good-faith effort to comply. It remanded immunity instead of finally denying it. Supervisors' involvement/knowledge were not sufficiently settled; their immunity judgment was vacated for individual assessment of involvement and material factual disputes. The case cannot fairly be framed as an appellate finding that all four are personally liable or categorically ineligible for immunity.

The appellate court also vacated the state malicious-prosecution verdict for a new trial because the instruction that Macaluso had probable cause to stop could mislead the jury on probable cause to prosecute insurance violations, an essential tort element. Vermont's listed elements also include institution of proceedings, malice, favorable termination and damages; invalid stop alone proves none of those others. It upheld excessive-force and zero-defamation-damages verdicts and the other components. Its recusal ruling rejected an inference of judicial bias from failure to return greetings and incidental knowledge of public peace vigils; the record did not establish knowledge of a disputed evidentiary fact. Those matters retain their separate preservation and review boundaries.

## Lawful alternatives, remedy and specific gaps

The Court can sustain or reject the appellate reasonable-suspicion analysis by applying the objective totality to each stop rather than treating refusal to cooperate as an all-purpose detention license. It can distinguish lawful fresh observed violations from later renewed stops, address Fifth Amendment exposure separately, and decide whether supported contemporaneous law/communications permit immunity for a particular defendant. A legal mistake may remain reasonable despite an eventual violation; actual knowledge of contrary authoritative rulings may defeat that claim when the facts and federal right support it. Reliance on a superior, ambiguous communication with a hearing officer, and judicial notice/preclusion are three separate matters.
