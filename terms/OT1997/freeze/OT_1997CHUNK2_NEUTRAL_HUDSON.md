## 2. Hudson v. United States — December 10, 1997

### Event, record, and question

Argument was October 8, 1997. Hudson chaired and controlled the First National Banks of Tipton and Hammon; Rackley was Tipton's president and a Hammon director, and Baresel directed both. OCC alleged loans nominally made to others benefited Hudson by enabling redemption of pledged bank stock. Its February 13, 1989 notice alleged violations of 12 U.S.C. §§84(a)(1), 375b and 12 C.F.R. §§31.2(b), 215.4(b), nearly $900,000 in bank losses, and contribution to bank failures. Those losses are allegations; the notice alleged no government loss.

Initial assessments were $100,000 against Hudson and $50,000 each against Rackley and Baresel, with financial resources, good faith, gravity, history, and justice considered. August 31 notices proposed exclusion from insured institutions. October 1989 consent orders settled the assessments for $16,500 (Hudson), $15,000 (Baresel), and $12,500 (Rackley); each agreed not to participate in banking institutions without written regulatory authorization. Other federal enforcement authority was reserved. The first Tenth Circuit decision held that this reservation did not waive the Double Jeopardy claim; no new waiver may be inferred from settlement.

An August 1992 indictment contained 22 counts under 18 U.S.C. §§371, 656 and 2, and 1005 arising from the transactions; only Rackley faced §1005 false-entry charges. The District Court initially refused dismissal. The Tenth Circuit, 14 F.3d 536 (1994), affirmed the nonparticipation-sanction ruling and remanded the monetary issue. After the District Court dismissed, the Tenth Circuit, 92 F.3d 1026 (1996), reversed under Halper, concluding that actual fines were not grossly disproportionate to proved government damages. This is the lower court's legal conclusion, not an independently established government-loss figure.

The verified petition question concerns whether Treasury's in-personam civil monetary penalties, together with other sanctions, constituted punishment. The same-offense issue is contingent analytical work, not a separately verified grant question; the Tenth Circuit expressly did not decide Blockburger. Petitioners seek reversal of the reinstated prosecution; the United States defends it. Classification must address the actual combined-sanction submission while respecting the earlier nonparticipation ruling's procedural scope. Excessive Fines and independent due-process claims are not added.

### Law entering the event

1. **Ursery / United States Currency (June 24, 1996):** For the three forfeiture provisions decided there, determine intended civil character, then require the clearest proof of sufficiently punitive purpose or effect to overcome that intention. The civil label and in-rem form are not conclusive; deterrence, burden, offense connection, and owner defenses are not singly decisive. O'Connor's opinion has Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer's five direct votes. It expressly retains Halper's exceptional fixed-personal-civil-penalty accounting, including reasonable remedial approximation, and Kurth Ranch. It does not resolve all personal penalties, attachment, offense identity, settlement waiver, or coordinated proceedings.
2. **Hendricks (June 23, 1997):** Breyer, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, and the Chief adopt civil-intent/clearest-proof analysis and cumulative examination of the actual confinement operation. Prisonlike custody and insufficient initial treatment established punishment in that application; neither the outcome nor its findings transfer to bank regulation. The case concerns Ex Post Facto classification and leaves Double Jeopardy attachment and identity open. No mandatory exhaustive checklist, treatment-like requirement for banking regulation, or burden shift follows.
3. **Kurth Ranch (June 6, 1994):** The combined criminally conditioned, arrest-linked, extraordinary tax on confiscated contraband was punishment after criminal punishment on the preserved same-offense premise. It creates no universal compensation ceiling, single-factor rule, or holding on reverse sequencing.
4. **Halper, 490 U.S. 435 (1989), survives:** Its personal civil-penalty setting remains independently material. A penalty with no rational relationship to the Government's loss can require a supported remedial accounting, allowing reasonable approximation. Whether this record fits that exceptional rule and how it interacts with Ward must be addressed; one may not describe Halper as already overruled. Ward, 448 U.S. 242 (1980), and Mendoza-Martinez, 372 U.S. 144 (1963), inform classification at their received scope.
5. **Dixon (June 28, 1993) and Felix (March 25, 1992):** Blockburger's elements protection remains, and Grady remains governing: a later prosecution may be barred if proof of an essential element requires conduct constituting an offense already prosecuted. Mere reuse of evidence or a common episode is insufficient; an independently punishable conspiracy remains distinct. A classification holding alone establishes neither attachment nor identity.

### Supported competing paths

The Government may defend a civil supervisory design and protective occupational exclusion, with monetary amounts consistent with legitimate regulatory and remedial functions. The strongest objection is that personal financial exactions occupy Halper's expressly retained field and must be evaluated on their own operation rather than by analogy to forfeiture alone. Conversely, petitioners may invoke Halper and cumulative punitive effect, but punishment cannot follow solely from deterrence, scienter, the large nominal authorization, occupational burden, or offense overlap.

A civil classification can sustain rejection of the bar without reaching identity. A conclusion that the monetary assessment may be punitive can require a bounded remand if its statutory/accounting predicates are unresolved; debarment must still be evaluated separately. A final bar requires supported findings or accepted predicates concerning punishment, first jeopardy, the relevant offenses under retained law, and separate proceedings. Reconsidering Halper is a distinct precedent-treatment choice requiring an expressly justified overruling analysis; neither Ursery nor Hendricks supplies such an overruling by implication. No path automatically dismisses all charges or adjudicates an unpresented fines claim.
