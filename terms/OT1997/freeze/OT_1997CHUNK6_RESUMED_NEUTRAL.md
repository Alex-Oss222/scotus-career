## 3. United States v. Bird — April 13, 1998

### Event, facts and component scope

The Fifth Circuit affirmed Bird's criminal judgment on September 24, 1997, No. 95-20792, 124 F.3d 667. Ordinary §1254(1) review of that judgment is the supplied channel. Petition No. 97-1270 is a verified filing identity; no historical Supreme Court grant or question is imported.

On December 13, 1994 Bird threw a bottle at Dr. Theodore Herring's car while the doctor entered America's Women Clinic in Houston, shattered the windshield, and threatened to kill him. No physical injury occurred. Herring, the physician witness, lived in Texas; no evidence established this clinic's out-of-state patients, staff or supplies. A jury convicted Bird under §248(a)(1), and apart from the statutory and constitutional objections he did not dispute guilt under its terms. The special condition excludes him from within **1,000 feet of any abortion clinic, specifically America's Women Clinic**. ### Current law, strongest arguments and limits

Jurisdictional words alone are insufficient, and courts cannot insert an omitted element. The strongest Government answer is the same direct interference with provision of an actual interstate service that American Life League already sustained. The supplied facts connect the attack to Herring's provision of clinic services. The alternative §5 question remains separate and ordinarily unnecessary if Commerce supplies power. ### Treaty/default record and competing paths

The federal petition was filed in August 1996, after AEDPA. The Fourth Circuit's January 20, 1998 decision, 134 F.3d 615, enforced Virginia's successive-petition default for the consular claim and found no cause because the treaty and factual basis were available through reasonable diligence. It expressly did not reach prejudice after rejecting cause. The lower court also found no established crime-innocence or death-eligibility gateway. The district court's prejudice view is not an appellate finding or an independently proved counterfactual.

The source reports a State stipulation that consular-rights notice was not given; this does not concede prejudice or judicial remedy. Counsel's affidavit reports a noncapital offer, advice to accept, Breard's refusal and insistence on testifying. Whether consular advice would have altered those decisions is disputed. The treaty's Article 36(1)(b) requires consular notice on the detained national's request, forwarding communications without delay, and advising the individual of those rights without delay. Paragraph (1)(c) protects consular access and arrangements for representation but requires abstention if the national expressly opposes action. Paragraph (2) allows exercise under receiving-State law **subject to enabling full effect to the rights' purposes**. The complete exact Article 36 in the earlier validated packet remains required reading; neither half of paragraph (2) may be omitted.

One supported path applies the ordinary default/cause framework: receiving-State procedural rules remain operative, the asserted right's treaty source alone creates no immunity from default, and lack of awareness of an available claim is not necessarily external cause. A treaty violation alone proves neither a successful habeas claim nor causally material loss. Its strongest counterargument is that notice failure can prevent timely invocation of the very right and that a bar denying any effective remedy may frustrate paragraph (2)'s express proviso.

The competing path gives paragraph (2)'s full-effect condition substantive work in deciding whether this default may be enforced, or finds cause under Reed v. Ross's legal-novelty doctrine. Its strongest counterarguments are the treaty's express recognition of domestic laws, the distinction between unfamiliarity and objective legal unavailability, and the need to show a legally available remedy and any required prejudice. The text and general default authorities do not predetermine which construction prevails. Coleman v. Thompson, 501 U.S. 722 (1991), Murray v. Carrier and Reed are surviving pre-divergence sources; Trest's December 9 no-duty-to-invent-default holding does not erase the defense actually asserted here. Treaty interpretation, individual enforceability and the scope of an effective domestic remedy must be kept separate from the conceded notice failure.

The authenticated state judicial quotations corroborate the operative statutory distinctions. ## 5. Carriger v. Stewart — April 16, 1998

### Actual judgment and record distinctions

Ordinary §1254(1) review concerns the Ninth Circuit en banc judgment, No. 95-99025, 132 F.3d 463 (December 17, 1997), which vacated the panel opinion, reversed the district court, and remanded with instructions to grant habeas relief requiring a new trial. It did not merely order a hearing or an acquittal and fixed no retrial deadline. No Supreme Court docket or argument is inferred. The operative federal petition was filed in August 1995 before AEDPA and followed earlier federal/state proceedings; the case is capital. The district court treated the new claims as abuse of the writ. The lower court applied former §2254(d)(8).

The en banc majority distinguished an assumed freestanding innocence claim (insufficient at the assumed very high level), the Schlup procedural gateway (satisfied), and the independently material Brady violation (warranting the writ). Those are three distinct inquiries. Its judgment is a Brady new-trial remedy following a gateway, not a freestanding innocence writ.

Central prosecution witness Robert Dunbar confessed in 1987, implicated his wife Joyce, recanted approximately three weeks later, and died in 1991. A later cellmate reported another confession. The state court's 1988 findings credited trial testimony and rejected the confession. Former §2254(d)(8) asks whether the finding is fairly supported by the record as a whole; it neither commands automatic deference despite an applicable exception nor allows displacement merely because later evidence exists. The majority found support inadequate; the dissent defended the state finding and the contrary evidence.

The DOC file described lying, shifting blame, violence and armed burglaries beyond the six disclosed burglary felonies and disclosed false gun-form statement. The majority relied on cumulative impeachment, investigative and preparation uses, the confession and weakened corroboration. The dissent emphasized defense knowledge and access, including Carriger's April 18, 1978 letter identifying Dunbar's informant history and directing counsel to get the file. Knowing a file exists is not knowing every material item; knowing particular evidence independently cannot be recast as suppression. Detectives' knowledge of his informant history likewise is not proof the defense knew none of it.

The fingerprint on adhesive tape is disputed corroboration. A later expert offer placed it 21.5 inches from the tape's end. The majority considered the reconstruction and lack of defense testing reasons for little weight. The dissent reports exclusion following withdrawal of the defense fingerprint challenge and a relevance ruling, rather than a finding of scientific unreliability. The later offer was not trial evidence. Both accounts remain part of whole-record assessment.

Joyce's and the children's accounts are potentially inculpatory as well as impeachment. > The record is not conclusive as to whether the individual prosecutors in this case ever actually possessed Dunbar's corrections file. See id., 436-38, 115 S.Ct. at 1567.   Because the prosecution is in a unique position to obtain information known to other agents of the government, it may not be excused from disclosing what it does not know but could have learned.  See id., at 438-40, 115 S.Ct. at 1568.   The disclosure obligation exists, after all, not to police the good faith of prosecutors, but to ensure the accuracy and fairness of trials by requiring the adversarial testing of all available evidence bearing on guilt or innocence.  See id., at 438-42, 115 S.Ct. at 1568-69;  Brady, 373 U.S. at 87, 83 S.Ct. at 1196-97.

##### Falconer announces a new rule for this collateral proceeding

**Holding:** Falconer’s specific requirement governing the interaction of these murder and voluntary-manslaughter instructions was not dictated by precedent at Taylor’s finality and is therefore a new rule under Teague. The State’s concession of error under Falconer does not waive its preserved retroactivity objection, and this conclusion does not approve the charge on direct review or exclude all defense-instruction errors from due process.

**Proposition-level authority:** O’Connor’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (7 Justices). Direct majority support at this level of generality; no Marks synthesis.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S. 288: finality-era dictated-by-precedent inquiry applied.
- Cupp v. Naughten, 414 U.S. 141: whole-charge fairness preserved but not treated as dictating this specific rule.
- Connecticut v. Johnson, 460 U.S. 73: plurality discussion of elemental presumptions distinguished; it is not treated as a controlling holding compelling Falconer.
- Boyde v. California, 494 U.S. 370: later capital-instruction reasonable-likelihood analysis distinguished; its date does not itself supply a 1987 finality-era source.
- Patterson v. New York, 432 U.S. 197, and Martin v. Ohio, 480 U.S. 228: offense-element burdens distinguished from affirmative-defense allocations.
- Estelle v. McGuire, December 4, 1991: the whole-charge reasonable-likelihood holding preserved; it neither decides Falconer nor supplies a source existing at 1987 finality.
- Graham v. Collins, January 25, 1993: the capital-mitigation holding distinguished on its earlier capital authorities.

##### Neither then-existing Teague exception permits this new instructional rule

**Holding:** The Falconer rule regulates trial procedure rather than removing conduct or a class of persons from the State’s substantive power to punish, and it is not a watershed procedural rule essential to the fundamental accuracy and fairness of the criminal process. Neither exception recognized by Teague at this event’s date permits its retroactive use to obtain the reviewed habeas relief.

**Proposition-level authority:** O’Connor’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (7 Justices). Direct majority support at this level of generality; no Marks synthesis.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The State preserved Teague despite its Falconer-error concession. Direct-review validity of the charge and harmlessness are not decided; no categorical exclusion of defense-instruction errors from due process is adopted.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Reverse the conditional habeas judgment; the writ cannot issue on this new procedural rule.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Teague, 489 U.S. 288: both the substantive and watershed exceptions applied in their event-date form.
- Sawyer v. Smith, 497 U.S. 227: the demanding watershed category applied; importance of a procedural protection alone is insufficient.
- Brecht v. Abrahamson, April 21, 1993: the preserved-Doyle Chapman holding remains distinct; harmlessness is not reached here.
- Sullivan v. Louisiana, June 1, 1993: the absence of a valid reasonable-doubt verdict is a different instructional defect and does not bypass Teague in this case.

#### Caspari v. Bohlen, 510 U.S. 383

**Docket or dockets:** No. 92-1500.

**Decided:** 1994-02-23.

##### The preserved nonretroactivity defense is fairly included here

**Holding:** In this habeas case, Missouri's consistently asserted Teague defense is fairly included as a subsidiary limit on applying the very constitutional rule and granting the writ challenged by the petition, although the stated question names the double-jeopardy issue rather than Teague. The Court therefore considers that nonjurisdictional defense; preservation, disclosure in the petition's body or logical priority alone does not make every independent omitted question reviewable.

**Proposition-level authority:** O'Connor's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg; eight Justices. Stevens disagrees with fair inclusion and would decline the defense under Rule 14.1(a).

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Yee v. City of Escondido: applied to distinguish subsidiary legal arguments from distinct questions; preservation and fair inclusion remain separate requirements.
- Izumi Seimitsu Kogyo Kabushiki Kaisha v. U.S. Philips Corp.: preserved; an omitted independent question does not become reviewable merely through notice, logical priority or ordinary economy. This confined same-rule habeas limitation is distinguished from its separate intervention determination.
- Schiro v. Farley: distinguished on presentation; the defense there first appeared at merits briefing after earlier omissions. Its discretionary treatment of a nonjurisdictional defense remains intact.

##### The requested extension was a new rule at finality

**Holding:** Bohlen's conviction and sentence became final on January 2, 1986, and the rule forbidding renewed proof of persistent-offender status in this noncapital proceeding was not dictated by the precedent then in force. Federal habeas relief therefore depends on a recognized Teague exception; the Court does not decide whether the proposed double-jeopardy protection should govern a case eligible for a decision on the constitutional merits.

**Proposition-level authority:** O'Connor's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg; eight Justices adopt the finality-specific new-rule determination, not a prospective rule permitting every noncapital sentencing rehearing.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Teague v. Lane: applied to the date at which the state judgment became final and the distinction between dictated applications and new rules.
- Graham v. Collins: applied in its controlling form; a dictated application does not become new merely because the facts differ or courts disagree.
- Gilmore v. Taylor: applied to preserve the possibility of a genuinely new noncapital extension after substantive comparison.
- Bullington v. Missouri and Arizona v. Rumsey: preserved for acquittal-like capital adjudications; their extension to this noncapital status proceeding was not dictated at finality, and their ultimate reach is not decided.
- United States v. DiFrancesco: applied as prefinality support for distinguishing sentencing determinations from offense acquittals.
- Griffith v. Kentucky: used for the finality convention; direct review was exhausted and the certiorari period expired on January 2, with January 1 a legal holiday.
- United States v. Dixon: its retention of Grady remains in force but does not answer whether this sentencing-status proceeding has the necessary jeopardy character.

##### Neither Teague exception permits this writ

**Holding:** The proposed rule falls within neither the exception for rules excluding conduct or a class of persons or punishments from the State's substantive power, nor the exception for watershed procedures essential to accuracy and fundamental fairness. Its protection against renewed litigation of offender status does not remove the robberies or these authorized sentences from lawful punishment and does not supply a watershed accuracy procedure; the habeas grant on this new rule must therefore be reversed.

**Proposition-level authority:** O'Connor's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg; eight Justices apply both exceptions as they exist at this decision's date. Neither exception is eliminated or prospectively narrowed out of existence.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Teague v. Lane: applies both contemporary exceptions after finding a new rule; neither is abolished.
- Penry v. Lynaugh: applies the substantive exception's protection against punishment beyond the State's constitutional power; this claim does not satisfy it.
- Sawyer v. Smith: applies the distinction between an important procedural protection and the exceptional accuracy-and-fairness requirement.
- Bullington v. Missouri: preserved; refusing retroactive extension does not overrule its established capital-acquittal protection.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Both contemporary Teague exceptions remain. The State's consistently presented defense is subsidiary here; preservation, mention in the petition body and logical priority do not automatically make an omitted independent question reviewable. The ultimate constitutional reach of double jeopardy to the noncapital status hearing is not decided.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The Eighth Circuit's conditional habeas grant on this claim is reversed, 8–1, under Teague. No new State proof opportunity, replacement sentence or release deadline is prescribed, and unrelated properly preserved claims are not disturbed.
