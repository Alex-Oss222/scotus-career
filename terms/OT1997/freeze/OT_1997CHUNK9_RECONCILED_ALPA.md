## Shared propositions, objections and remedial limits

Six Associates—O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg—support affirming on exhaustion. Stevens and Breyer support reversal of the categorical prohibition with bounded remand. These are eight-member commitments, not a final Court tally.

The affirming group may share the holding that these nonconsenting objectors need not exhaust the private Hudson arbitration before bringing their federal claims. Its sufficient explanation distinguishes this protective option from a congressionally allocated administrative process, answers the nonbinding-first-step argument, and preserves normal litigation-specific objection requirements and the union's germaneness burden. It does not hold that absence of consent alone invalidates every legal exhaustion requirement. The union's process remains available and must satisfy its actual governing safeguards.

The two contrary commitments require actual promptness, impartiality, adequacy and genuinely independent judicial adjudication after arbitration. Neither treats the award as preclusive or entitled to contractual finality. Completed participants have already taken the preliminary step; no automatic loss follows for nonparticipants absent a lawful, compliant procedure and consideration of the actual posture. Both accept that a discretionary judicial pause for prompt arbitration can alleviate practical concerns, while distinguishing that pause from a union-imposed prerequisite.

Affirmance preserves the D.C. Circuit's bounded remand; it awards no refund, sets no final chargeable fee, resolves no particular expense or audit, and prescribes no discovery plan. Access to court does not eliminate the district court's balanced discretion to defer discovery or other proceedings pending prompt arbitration (historical p. 879 n.6), a compatibility limit already accommodated by the neutral packet's preservation of ordinary case management.
