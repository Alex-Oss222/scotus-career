## 6. Tanford v. Brand — December 22, 1997

### Event, record, and question

The full Seventh Circuit opinion, 104 F.3d 982, lower docket 96-3053, decided January 16, 1997 after argument December 10, 1996, affirms the District Court's merits judgment for University President Brand and Chancellor Gros Louis. Rehearing was denied February 25, 1997. Those are lower-court dates, not invented Supreme Court dates. Plaintiffs seek to enjoin the invocation and benediction at Indiana University's Bloomington commencement. A preliminary injunction was denied in May 1995; the amended action ended in a July 1996 summary judgment for defendants.

A faculty/staff/student committee planned commencement. In 1995 approximately 5,000 of 7,400 graduates attended the official stadium ceremony, with 150 officials/faculty and 25,000–30,000 guests. Attendance was voluntary without penalty. The program placed a nonsectarian invocation after the national anthem and a benediction at the close. A local religious leader, selected each year, gave both; the practice dates to 1840. Recent clergy came from Catholic, Unitarian Universalist, Presbyterian, Methodist and Hillel institutions. In 1995 Father Ralph Sims delivered the exercises. The reproduced benediction expressly invites prayer, addresses God, requests divine help and strength, and ends with Amen. Its devotional content is record evidence, not merely plaintiffs' label. The only reported instruction was to provide an uplifting, general message; no University-written prayer script, denominational exclusion, or detailed content editing is established.

President Brand described secular solemnity and dignity as the purpose, disavowing endorsement. Most stadium attendees stayed seated during the prayers. Movement, late arrival and early departure were common, and the director of field services offered easy seating accommodations for exit/reentry. The law school's separate afternoon recognition ceremony contained no invocation or benediction. The lower courts relied on adult maturity, voluntary attendance, practical freedom to abstain or leave, alternative ceremonies, tradition, limited clergy contact and the stated ceremonial purpose. They found neither coercion nor a Lemon violation and used Marsh, Lynch and the Seventh Circuit's Sherman reasoning. Those conclusions are the reasoning below; they do not replace controlling simulated Lee, Ingebretsen or Sherman.

### Law entering the event

**Lee (June 24, 1992)** has two independently majority-supported rules. Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, and the Chief prohibit secondary-school officials' selection of clergy, request for prayer, guidance of religious content, and incorporation of worship into the official program; optional attendance and nonsectarian wording do not cure this sponsorship and quantified pressure is unnecessary. Kennedy, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, and Souter independently prohibit school-directed graduation prayer that substantially pressures adolescents to participate, appear to participate, or forgo the occasion. Neither rule decides every adult ceremonial setting. The live-controversy holding rests on a student's concrete future exposure, not generalized objection.

**Ingebretsen (March 19, 1997)** is materially omitted from the runtime. Ginsburg, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Breyer, and the Chief prohibit organization, adoption, direction, or religious supervision of worship at the school's own event despite student delivery or a disclaimer. Ginsburg, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, and Breyer independently adopt the actual-pressure ground. Graduation receives no categorical safe harbor. All nine preserve genuinely private expression and independently lawful provisions in shaping relief. This school statute and preliminary posture do not themselves decide the mature university setting.

**Sherman (February 12, 1996)** permits the particular voluntary civic Pledge, with protected silence and no forced exit, because it is civic allegiance rather than prayer, devotional instruction, or compulsory religious profession. It does not authorize worship merely because embedded in a civic program. O'Connor's contextual purpose/effect/entanglement reasoning has Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer; Kennedy, Scalia, and Thomas join the concrete civic conclusion without that comprehensive methodology.

**Good News (December 11, 1995), Rosenberger and Pinette (June 29, 1995), and Kiryas Joel (June 27, 1994)** preserve contextual attribution and religious neutrality. Genuine private viewpoint equality differs from governmental selection and conduct of worship. Neutral availability or a disclaimer alone does not cure actual official sponsorship. Lemon remains governing as Agostini modified it; Marsh's legislative setting and historical function must be evaluated rather than treated as universal ceremonial permission. Engel and Schempp remain the school-worship background. No later establishment framework enters this event.

Agostini v. Felton, June 23, 1997, retains Lemon as the governing Establishment Clause test and modifies its operation. Purpose, effects and entanglement are evidence within one inquiry, not freestanding prongs or an alternative inquiry. A specific prohibition or distinct doctrine controls conduct it resolves; an established permissible relationship defeats the claim on that ground; an otherwise legally sufficient unresolved allegation receives the attribution-and-function inquiry. Governmental organization of worship is prohibited. Agostini leaves unpresented ceremonial applications to their own facts and preserves distinct legislative-prayer, private-expression and accommodation doctrines; it creates no universal exception for an adult audience.

That argument must answer the separate official-worship rationale; plaintiffs must address Lee's defined secondary-school scope and the actual adult practice rather than treating age as legally irrelevant in every context.
