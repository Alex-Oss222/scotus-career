## J — Limited jurisdiction to consider correction of the same decree

The United States invoked the original court's jurisdiction and obtained its quiet-title judgment there. An independent action brought in that same court to obtain relief from that judgment does not require a second independent jurisdictional grant merely because it is procedurally a new action. The traditional ancillary character, preserved by Rule 60(b)'s savings language and illustrated by Pacific Railroad, treats the corrective request as a continuation for jurisdictional purposes.

This decides actual authority to entertain the request; jurisdiction is not assumed to reach merits. The Rule preserves an existing equitable procedure, not general subject-matter jurisdiction or a universal waiver for new claims. The Government's original invocation supports correction of its own decree, not unlimited affirmative liability. Kokkonen's separate settlement-contract enforcement holding expressly reserved reopening. Peacock's new-debtor case distinguishes decree effectuation from imposing a different substantive obligation. Neither case requires the Government's proposed blanket independent-jurisdiction rule here.

Nordic Village and DOE v. Ohio continue to require consent to the particular affirmative monetary remedy. No new damages, Tucker Act jurisdiction or timely QTA cause follows merely from corrective authority. The contrary jurisdictional argument is therefore rejected at this bounded scope, while its legitimate warning about new relief survives.

## E — The independent action fails the extraordinary standard

An independent action may relieve a completed judgment only to prevent a **grave miscarriage of justice**. The traditional equitable requirements must establish that exceptional need; they do not create a routine second opportunity whenever favorable evidence later appears. Rule 60(b)'s preserved independent action must coexist with ordinary motion procedures and their limits, including the one-year limit for the relevant mistake/new-evidence/misconduct motions. Allowing the alleged failure to provide relevant information alone to bypass those limits would defeat their operation.

Hazel-Atlas supplies the exceptional injustice principle; Marshall's alleged forged-document case illustrates its force without making intentional forgery an indispensable condition in every independent action. The rule is not a categorical ban on correcting consent judgments, a universal intentional-fraud requirement, or a claim that only one form of process corruption can qualify.

The Government's unsuccessful archive search and inaccurate representation, the claimants' sustained efforts and the representation's influence on settlement are evaluated at their supported level. The allegations of failure to search thoroughly and make full disclosure concerning the Boudreau material do not approach the required extraordinary injustice. That conclusion neither finds the Government's search adequate nor blames the claimants for failing to locate the material earlier. A document's presence in federal archives does not establish intentional concealment or obstruction; no such new finding is supplied.

The strongest contrary path emphasizes official misinformation and unusual diligence, rather than merely disappointment with settlement. It still does not establish the necessary exceptional ground for this independent action on the supported allegations. Whether these allegations could have supported a **timely Rule 60(b)(3) motion** is not decided. Whether the grant evidence would have changed the original result is also not decided; failure of the extraordinary-relief threshold makes that additional inquiry unnecessary. The five traditional conditions are not each converted into a new adverse factual finding.

Actual Morgan Stanley and Plaut do not supply historical Plaut's categorical Article III prohibition. They instead authorize a defined legislative entitlement to renewed adjudication while preserving independent judicial decisions. Nothing in this case denies that law. But no analogous legislative reinstatement command governs this decree, and those holdings do not displace the traditional equitable threshold or Rule 60's distinct structure.

## Q — Statutory rejection of additional equitable tolling

For the private title claim presented, **§2409a(g)'s twelve-year period runs from when the claimant or predecessor knew or should have known the United States' adverse claim. Additional nonstatutory equitable tolling is incompatible with that notice-triggered, unusually generous limitations period and the Act's protection of certainty in land ownership.** The trigger concerns knowledge of the Government's claim, not later discovery of convincing evidence supporting the claimant's own title.

Irwin supplies a rebuttable equitable presumption, not an additional period regardless of the enacted scheme. The QTA already accounts for lack of knowledge through its accrual rule. The length of the permitted period and the special need to know when land rights cease to be challengeable reinforce the statutory conclusion. Brockamp supplies the method of testing compatibility with the particular statute; its tax provisions are not imported. Beach likewise confirms provision-specific analysis without turning this QTA limitation into §1635(f)'s substantive-right expiration rule.

The claimants knew the adverse claim by 1979 at the latest; the lower majority used 1976. Either supported date precedes the June 1, 1994 suit by more than twelve years. Their continued search and 1991 discovery do not move the accrual date or justify an additional equitable interval from the original search to discovery. The decision does not make an independent adverse diligence finding or require an affirmative bad-faith finding to reject this ordinary tolling theory. The statutory ground is sufficient; the freeze's narrower assumption that tolling remains available but these equities fail is **not retained as the complete QTA holding**.

This is not a universal proposition that no equitable doctrine can ever affect any QTA dispute. It decides the presented private claim under the reported notice provision, not unquoted statutory qualifications or special classes of claimant. It supplies no general classification of all QTA timing provisions as jurisdictional, no different deadline, and no ruling on a materially different legal doctrine merely because someone labels it equity.

### Stevens/Souter reservation: distinct doctrines, no relief here

Stevens and Souter fully join the statutory holding and explain its boundary. A hypothetical case in which outrageous governmental misconduct prevents a person, already aware of the Government's claim, from discovering her own claim might present **fraudulent concealment or equitable estoppel**, doctrines distinct from the additional equitable tolling rejected here. Such doctrines conceivably could apply; the Court neither recognizes a definitive exception nor resolves that hypothetical.

The present record does not establish that hypothetical. The concurrence supplies no fraud finding, affirmative estoppel, postponed accrual, successful tolling, new hearing or amendment entitlement. The other six need not adopt its affirmative observation about conceivable availability to join the common opinion's limited decision. They also do not decide the distinct hypothetical adversely merely by adopting the statutory no-additional-tolling ground. The reservation must not be rewritten as an exception to the tolling rule that the Court has granted.

## T — Title and monetary relief remain distinct; exact mandate

Reverse the Fifth Circuit judgment and remand for proceedings consistent with rejection of the presented independent action and untimely alternative QTA route. The appellate order vacating the 1982 judgment and quieting title for the claimants is removed; the original consent judgment remains operative. The district court's blanket suggestion that it lacked power to entertain any same-court independent action is not adopted as the reason for denying relief.

Even lawful reopening would not by itself adjudicate the Boudreau document's validity, the complete title chain or the summary-judgment merits. Those contested questions are not resolved in either direction here. Once the asserted reopening and statutory routes fail, no alternative title-merits trial or extraordinary-relief hearing is required. No new compensation valuation, entitlement to amendment on rejected grounds or inverse-condemnation jurisdiction is supplied.

The 1975 canceled $156,500 agreement differs from the 1982 settlement and its $208,175.87 payment. Neither repayment nor a second payment is ordered. The lower court's QTA §2409a(b) possession-or-compensation option depended on an adverse title judgment that no longer stands; it is not activated here. No new district-court Tucker Act jurisdiction for the claim exceeding $10,000 is recognized, and no available alternative recovery forum or successful independent claim is found.
