## Historical comparison and the Ritchie correction

Historically, Jefferson dismissed the writ for want of jurisdiction. Seven current Associates supported that historical majority, whose opinion Ginsburg wrote. Stevens dissented, would take jurisdiction, and voted to reverse on the federal damages question. His stated preference to overrule Ritchie and dismiss was not his actual vote. The opinion's concluding operative disposition on page 84 is jurisdictional; an earlier improvidently-granted formulation does not convert it into a purely discretionary disposition. Historical Rehnquist's participation supplies no simulated Chief Justice vote.

The record and review question remain materially the same. The Alabama Supreme Court answered an interlocutory federal damages question and returned the same petitioners' case for further proceedings. Liability, causation, and related claims remain disputed; no final state judgment or preordained outcome is supplied. The City's late jurisdictional objection cannot create jurisdiction by waiver. Dismissal would not affirm the correctness of Alabama's federal remedial rule.

The fresh addendum correctly supplies a previously omitted controlling source: [Pennsylvania v. Ritchie, 480 U.S. 39](../research/preflight-c/ritchie-reconciliation.txt), Part II, pages 47–50 and notes 7–8 through page 51, with Stevens's dissent at pages 72–78. Its majority did not require absolute legal impossibility of every later appeal. Note 7 expressly considered a possible new-trial appeal and state law-of-the-case treatment, yet concluded that repeated appellate presentation would not serve finality's purposes in the unusual circumstances. The surrounding opinion also considered disclosure injury, prosecution outcomes, completed trial and appellate review, and the inadequacy of a contempt detour. Confidentiality was not independently dispositive. Neither a legal-impossibility test nor a confidentiality-only exception accurately states its holding.

Seven Associates accept the addendum's particular distinction: later federal review can effectively correct Jefferson's damages restriction, while adjudicating disputed causation, conduct, and the same parties' remaining claims serves a substantive purpose. Waiting does not establish the loss of the contested federal interest through disclosure or an equivalent irreversible event. This is a comparison of consequences and proceedings, not a rigid requirement that every future practical-finality case contain Ritchie's facts. They preserve Ritchie, including note 7, and reject a freestanding exception for every issue governed by state law of the case.

## Stevens: why the frozen prediction changes

The strongest case for retaining Stevens's frozen dismissal is his own 1987 dissent, his later joins in carefully bounded simulated finality cases, and Jefferson's effective later corrective route. That case was independently modeled and is legally supported. But the historical comparator supplies unusually specific contrary evidence: Stevens considered this very distinction and insisted on respecting Ritchie's jurisdictional holding despite his personal disagreement with it. His original dissent therefore cannot be used as if it independently settled his treatment of the still-binding majority holding.

The concrete-change test does not establish a sufficient new premise to retain the predicted departure. The simulated [Sullivan](../../OT1992/output/OT_1992CHUNK7.md#sullivan-v-louisiana-no-92-5129), [Casarotto and Richards](../../OT1995/output/OT_1995CHUNK7.md), and [Labron/Kilgore](../../OT1995/output/OT_1995CHUNK10.md#pennsylvania-v-labron-no-95-1691-pennsylvania-v-kilgore-no-95-1738) holdings make their own practical-finality predicates explicit. They do not decide the scope of Ritchie's note 7, overrule it, or establish that its treatment of futile renewed state appeals is unavailable here. The fresh addendum expressly preserves that point. The remaining Jefferson proceedings and potential later review are unchanged facts already addressed by the competing historical positions.

Accordingly, the existing immediate-review alternative is the better supported final Stevens position. He would not dismiss for lack of jurisdiction while treating Ritchie as binding; his personal preference for stricter finality does not itself supply an overruling. This uses historical evidence together with unchanged operative law and his own public position, rather than transferring a tally mechanically. No new factual ground is required. His broader historical damages conclusion is not automatically imported: the independent handoffs supply no final Associate merits commitment, and an objection to the jurisdictional dismissal does not require resolving the merits that the Court does not reach.
