## 1. Parties, records, and actual district disposition

**Judicial recitation.** Lindblom and the Foundation sued the FBI. Lindblom invoked the Privacy Act and constitutional provisions; the Foundation's appellate theory was constitutional. The court describes 23 pages of FBI material concerning Lindblom, five pages elsewhere referring to the Foundation, and no separate Foundation file. Some released material described Lindblom's associational activity. App. 2a–3a (PDF 36–37).

**Government's more detailed record account.** The FBI located 23 responsive documents comprising 36 pages, with 13 duplicates. The account cites C.A. App. 111 ¶19 and is compatible with 23 nonduplicate pages. FOIA and Privacy Act access requests went to headquarters and New York, Chicago, and Washington field offices. Opp. 2 (PDF 7), citing C.A. App. 21–26, 40–41. Pet. 3–4 (PDF 13–14) describes Lindblom's foundation work, contacts with foreign political figures, and the 1989 response disclosing records concerning his association with Kim Dae Jung.

**Actual district order, not a party paraphrase.** App. 20a (PDF 54), image verified, states that the court considered the FBI's summary-judgment motion, plaintiffs' and amici's opposition, and its September 1, 1995 in-camera examination. It identifies three conclusions: the claimed FOIA exemptions were properly invoked; the documents were compiled for legitimate law-enforcement purposes; and the investigations were of other people with whom plaintiffs had incidental and apparently innocent contact. On September 6, 1995, it granted summary judgment and dismissed the complaint with prejudice, “for essentially the reasons advanced by defendant.”

**Limits of that order.** It contains no separately articulated ruling on current pertinence of retention, statutory adverse effect, damages entitlement, an amendment request/refusal/review, or a Privacy Act remedial exemption. Its adoption of the defendant's reasons is not a license to supply unquoted arguments from the unrecovered district motion. The petition describes the order as failing to address retention's pertinence to ongoing activity (Pet. 4–5); that is also consistent with the actual order's limited text.

## 2. Claims and requested relief; what can and cannot be said about money

**Original prayer is affirmatively supported.** The court states that the action sought monetary damages, an injunction, expungement, and an order against future maintenance of records. App. 3a (PDF 37). The government independently states that Lindblom sought release of information, a declaration that maintaining files on his First Amendment activities was unlawful, expungement, a future-maintenance injunction, **and damages**, citing C.A. App. 10. Opp. 3 (PDF 8), image verified. Thus it would be inaccurate to characterize the original action as seeking only expungement or only prospective relief.

**Exact counts remain incompletely recovered.** App. 3a identifies FOIA, First, Fifth, and Ninth Amendment claims and Lindblom's § 552a(e)(7) claim. It reports abandonment of FOIA and Fifth/Ninth Amendment claims on appeal, while Privacy Act and First Amendment claims were pursued. These are express judicial characterizations of appellate scope. The full complaint and its allocation of damages to particular counts, damages amount, actual-damages allegations, and intentional-or-willful allegations are not reproduced. The original damages request therefore does not by itself establish a particular § 552a(g)(1)(D)/(g)(4) theory.

**Monetary relief on further review is unresolved in these sources.** The lower judgment dismissed the whole complaint, and the appellate court affirmed; the question here is what relief Lindblom continued to seek upon further review. All three complete party papers and the appendix contain no express statement that he abandoned damages, and no express confirmation that a particular statutory damages demand remained pressed through the petition. The petition focuses on retaining/expunging records, and its conclusion requests certiorari (Pet. i, 1, 4, 19, 21). That emphasis and silence do **not** prove abandonment, waiver, default, or an exclusively injunctive suit. Nor does the old prayer alone prove a live damages claim sufficient for redressability. The distinction must remain unresolved unless another source or an authorized procedural premise supplies it.

**Jurisdiction pleaded, as reported by petitioner.** Pet. 2 says district jurisdiction rested on 28 U.S.C. §§ 1331 and 1361, appeal on § 1291, and petition jurisdiction on § 1254. This reports invoked jurisdiction; it does not establish that a particular nonstatutory cause of action or sovereign-immunity waiver was pleaded or preserved.

## 3. Amendment requests, refusals, review, and exemptions

**Administrative access history is supported; amendment history is not identified.** Opp. 2–3 says FOIA and Privacy Act requests sought information maintained about Lindblom, followed by exhaustion of administrative appeals and suit. It does not identify a § 552a(d)(2) request to amend or expunge, the contents/date of an agency refusal of such a request, or a § 552a(d)(3) review decision. The quoted exhaustion statement follows the access-request account and cannot silently be expanded to establish amendment exhaustion. Pet. 4 describes requesting records and then suing for expungement; it supplies no additional administrative-amendment sequence.

**What is actually exempted in the recovered record.** App. 3a and Opp. 3 n.2 explicitly identify **FOIA** exemptions 1, 2, 7(C), and 7(D); the district order sustains the “FOIA exemptions claimed.” Opp. 3 n.2 describes classified material, internal rules/practices, third-party privacy, and confidential sources. App. 10a–11a says the requested discovery was largely classified and sustains the discovery ruling. These are not findings that the particular records were exempt from Privacy Act subsections (d) or (g).

**Privacy Act statutory references are not record-specific exemption decisions.** App. 5a–6a discusses § 552a(k)(2) while criticizing *Becker*; App. 16a and Pet. 16 cite (j)(2)(A), (k)(2), and (k)(5) as textual comparisons. Pet. 9 n.4 says a later release identified the file as “199.C,” citing a 1979 Federal Register source. None of these passages identifies an operative Privacy Act regulation applied to Lindblom's actual records or an adjudication exempting his amendment/damages remedy. A classification reference cannot substitute for the applicable regulation and its scope.

**Coverage-bounded unresolved proposition.** After reading all three papers and the full appendix, no specific amendment request/refusal/review or case-specific Privacy Act (d)/(g) exemption assertion or determination was located. This is a limit on the recovered source set, not proof that no such event, pleading, argument, or record exists.

## 4. Personal injury, statutory adverse effect, and constitutional standing

**Lindblom's own complaint allegation is affirmatively reported.** Pet. 4 (PDF 14), image verified, states that he alleged retention chilled his First Amendment freedoms and sought expungement, citing J.A. 7; the Privacy Act allegation is cited to J.A. 9. This is petitioner's report of a pleaded personal injury, not a recovered affidavit, fact finding, or concession of monetary loss. Pet. 7 and 11–13 further argue that retention invades privacy and increases future disclosure/use risks. Those passages include general legislative and policy reasoning and predictions of future harm; they do not identify an adjudicated concrete disclosure injury to Lindblom.

**Actual constitutional-standing characterization has a defined scope.** App. 11a–14a treats the separate First Amendment claims. For the Foundation, the court discusses the Lies and Hallett affidavits concerning potential employee job harm and possible deterrence of grantees, and finds the showing insufficiently concrete/imminent. App. 13a–14a says Lindblom supplied no separate standing arguments or affidavits, did not claim limited employment prospects, and failed to show personal injury; it treats his constitutional claim as derivative of the Foundation's. App. 13a is image verified. These are the appellate court's descriptions and conclusions about the constitutional claims. They are not Lindblom's own admission that he suffered no statutory adverse effect, nor an express § 552a(g)(1)(D) adverse-effect adjudication. Tatel's separate discussion of causation concerns the Foundation, App. 17a–19a.

**Disclosure evidence is limited.** Opp. 11–12 n.8 says petitioner identified no instance of the FBI's public release of information in his file. That is the government's characterization of the showing, not petitioner's admission that no disclosure to anyone occurred. Pet. 4 says discovery was sought concerning dissemination to any other party and was barred. No fact of actual harmful dissemination, specific economic loss, or quantified damages to Lindblom is established by these papers. No contrary negative finding should be invented from the source gap.

**Net record limit.** The papers supply a pleaded personal chilling claim, records concerning associational activity, and the lower constitutional-standing characterization. They do not supply a separate, express statutory adverse-effect finding or admission resolving the statutory damages pathway. Whether supported allegations/evidence meet statutory and Article III requirements is a subsequent legal inquiry, not this extraction's conclusion.

## 5. Concessions, current pertinence, and scope of the statutory dispute

**Express concession on initial collection.** Pet. 19 says Lindblom does not challenge initial collection, only ongoing retention. Reply 5 (PDF 9) describes him as having conceded the propriety of collection. App. 4a states that the court assumes the information was pertinent when collected because collection is not challenged. This is an express limitation on his challenge; it does not concede continuing pertinence or adequate justification for all later retention/use/dissemination.

**Contested present-pertinence assertions.** Pet. 7 asserts that the file was closed over a decade earlier. Opp. 9 n.6 (PDF 14), image verified, quotes C.A. App. 66 ¶13 to the effect that some targets/subjects may no longer be of present interest, but certain individuals/organizations remain of investigative interest. The government argues that even under petitioner's proposed construction it could suffice that some current investigative activity might use the existing file. The underlying declaration is not fully reproduced; the statement is not a district finding that these particular retained records satisfy a current-pertinence standard. Neither closing Lindblom's file nor continued interest in some other subject alone supplies an uncontested resolution.

**Government's descriptions of how files arise.** Opp. 3–4 cites agents Pitts and Llewellyn and reports intelligence work under Executive Order 12,333 § 1.14 and Attorney General guidelines. It says a file “subject” can be a victim, property, another target of a scheme, or a body of information of law-enforcement/counterintelligence interest; the designation need not signify unlawful conduct. Pet. 9–10 and n.5 cites the government's description of intelligence investigations and former agent Rodriguez's declaration on practices concerning innocent contacts. Treat these as descriptions of declarations, not new fact findings or blanket admissions covering every record in this case.

**Petitioner's narrowed articulation.** Reply 2 (PDF 6) expressly says authorized law-enforcement activity need not be limited to an active or ongoing investigation; it requests a present, articulable use for keeping the information. Reply 3–5 rejects continual review as the demanded remedy, suggests review on closing an investigation or processing a Privacy Act request, and argues for an individualized determination concerning his records. These are actual arguments, not reconstructed litigant positions. The government disputes their textual basis and practical consequences, Opp. 7–13.

## 6. Review scope, discovery, and independent prospective equity

The petition's single question concerns whether § 552a permits permanent retention once information is no longer pertinent to authorized law-enforcement activity (Pet. i / PDF 3). The government frames its question as the propriety under (e)(7) of maintaining information lawfully collected (Opp. i / PDF 2). Only Lindblom is the petitioner; the Foundation is listed as a party below (Pet. ii / PDF 4).

App. 10a–11a sustains the refusal of discovery for abuse-of-discretion reasons. Opp. 6 n.3 expressly says the petition does not challenge either the discovery ruling or the First Amendment standing rulings. The petition and reply argue the (e)(7) retention issue; Pet. 4's recounting of denied discovery is not itself an express renewed question seeking reversal of that ruling. No contrary express challenge was identified in the complete papers. That permits reporting the government's stated review-scope position, without enlarging it into a judicial waiver ruling.

**Prospective relief was requested.** Both the judicial account and government account support an original injunction against future maintenance, and an expungement request. **An independent prospective cause is not established by that remedy description.** The papers do not identify an independently pleaded/preserved APA claim, nonstatutory ultra vires claim, or other freestanding equitable claim surviving apart from the Privacy Act and constitutional counts. Section 1331/1361 jurisdiction and citation of *Peters v. Hobby* in Reply 3 cannot silently supply one. In the reply, *Peters* supports the argument that an agency ordinarily cannot retain unlawfully collected records; it is not stated as a new count or separate route around the Privacy Act's remedial provisions. This is a source limit, not an adjudication of equitable authority or waiver.

**Postjudgment declaration episode, as the government reports it.** Opp. 6–7 n.4 says the government alerted the appellate court on Dec. 23, 1996 to Pitts's arrest; Lindblom sought rehearing and district Rule 60(b) relief; the government submitted other agents' declarations affirming descriptions and lawful collection; appellate rehearing was denied Apr. 11, 1997; and the Rule 60(b) motion was withdrawn four days later. The government states that the petition raises no issue concerning Pitts's arrest/conviction. The rehearing orders themselves are reproduced at App. 21a–22a; the Rule 60(b) filings and replacement declarations are not. This is not administrative amendment-request history and establishes no new statutory adverse-effect finding.
