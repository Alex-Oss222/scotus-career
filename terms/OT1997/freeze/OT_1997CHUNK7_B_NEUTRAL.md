# Chaudhuri v. Tennessee

# SECTION I: NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

* **Case Name:** Chaudhuri v. Tennessee
* **Citation or Docket:** No historical U.S. Reports merits citation; lower-court docket: 96-5538; no simulated Supreme Court docket number assigned.

* **October Term:** OT_1997 — new simulation
* **Entry Stage and Event:** Scheduled Supreme Court review event; the supplied question and lower judgment identify the matter. No present Supreme Court disposition is supplied.

* **Simulated Event Date:** 1998-04-27 — SIMULATION SCHEDULING ASSIGNMENT. This is not a historical Supreme Court opinion or grant date.

* **Question Presented:** 1. Whether officially arranged, nonsectarian invocations at public-university functions violate the Establishment Clause when attendees are adults and attendance is not formally compulsory, including where a university employee alleges practical employment pressure to attend.
  2. Whether an officially designated moment of silence adopted as an alternative to spoken prayer violates the Establishment Clause. [Source formulation; not a verbatim petition quotation.]

* **Question Granted:** 1. Whether officially arranged, nonsectarian invocations at public-university functions violate the Establishment Clause when attendees are adults and attendance is not formally compulsory, including where a university employee alleges practical employment pressure to attend.
  2. Whether an officially designated moment of silence adopted as an alternative to spoken prayer violates the Establishment Clause.

* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Sixth Circuit rejected Chaudhuri’s claims concerning prayers and moments of silence at Tennessee State University events.
* **Relief Requested:** Chaudhuri seeks reversal, declaratory relief, damages, and an injunction against university-sponsored devotional exercises.
* **Material Record:** The record facts below are taken from the Sixth Circuit's opinion; bracketed references are to its paragraph numbering in the primary-source text cited in Section I.E.
  1. Tennessee State University (TSU), a state university in Nashville, formerly included invocations and benedictions at certain university events. Chaudhuri objected to prayers at functions such as graduation exercises, faculty meetings, dedication ceremonies and guest lectures. [130 F.3d 232, paras. 2, 4 (6th Cir. 1997)]
  2. Chaudhuri, born in India and a naturalized citizen, is a tenured professor of mechanical engineering at TSU and an adherent of Hinduism. [para. 4]
  3. Responding to Chaudhuri's complaint, the general counsel of the State University and Community College System issued an opinion letter in May 1988. The letter said prayers are not unconstitutional if they do not appear to favor or endorse a particular religious view, and it suggested that a moment of silence would be an "appropriate vehicle for allowing the tradition of prayer without running afoul of the Constitution." [para. 5]
  4. TSU officials then decided that the prayers should be generic and nonsectarian. Many were delivered by Dr. James Haney, a clergyman and TSU associate professor of history; others were delivered by local religious leaders at TSU's invitation. TSU did not review the prayers in advance and, apart from requesting that they be nonsectarian and omit references to Jesus Christ, gave no content guidelines. [paras. 6, 39]
  5. The opinion sets out Haney's May 1991 commencement prayer as typical. The prayer opens "Let us pray" and repeatedly addresses the "Heavenly Father." [paras. 7-12]
  6. In January 1991 Chaudhuri sued the State of Tennessee, TSU and TSU administrators Annie Neal, Decatur Rogers, James A. Hefner and Arthur C. Washington. He alleged Title VII failure-to-promote claims and Establishment and Free Exercise Clause claims, and he sought compensatory damages and declaratory and injunctive relief. [caption; para. 13]
  7. Chaudhuri asserted that faculty were required to attend university functions at which prayers were offered, and that his evaluations included a "university service" component. Defendants responded that attendance was encouraged but not required or monitored, and that no employee had suffered adverse consequences for non-attendance. [paras. 14-15] The engineering dean attested that Chaudhuri's attendance or non-attendance never affected his evaluations, and the Sixth Circuit described TSU's non-monitoring and no-penalty representations as made "without contradiction." [para. 47]
  8. In April 1993 Chaudhuri moved to enjoin prayer at the May commencement. At about the same time, President Hefner announced that there would be no verbal prayers at those exercises and that a moment of silence would be acceptable "to solemnize the occasion." The district court denied the injunction. [para. 16]
  9. At the May 1993 commencement, when the speaker asked everyone to rise and remain silent, someone or a group began reciting the Lord's Prayer aloud. Many audience members joined in, spontaneously by all accounts, and applause followed. TSU officials said they had no advance knowledge. [para. 17]
  10. Chaudhuri filed a second injunction motion, noting that prayers continued at non-graduation functions and asking that moments of silence also be enjoined. Hefner responded by affidavit that moments of silence would replace prayers at all university functions where prayers had been offered, and that he had told staff to clarify that faculty attendance was encouraged but not mandatory. The district court denied the injunction. [para. 18] Judge Jones's separate opinion states that the president ordered the prayer practice ceased on June 8, 1993. [para. 59 (Jones, J.)]
  11. At the August 1993 summer commencement, the keynote speaker was Dr. David Jones, Jr., a Nashville public-school educator identified in the program as a local pastor. When he asked the audience to stand for a moment of silence, part of the audience again recited the Lord's Prayer. TSU officials denied complicity. [para. 19] Chaudhuri conceded that there was no evidence of university complicity in either recitation. [para. 34]
  12. In January 1995 the district court granted summary judgment for defendants on the Establishment and Free Exercise claims, 886 F. Supp. 1374 (M.D. Tenn. 1995). The Title VII claims were resolved for defendants, by summary judgment on one and by judgment after a bench trial on the other. Chaudhuri appealed only the First Amendment rulings. [para. 20 and n.1]
  13. The Sixth Circuit decided the appeal on November 18, 1997, before this Court's December 22, 1997 decision in Tanford v. Brand. It stated that the prayer challenge remained live insofar as Chaudhuri sought money damages, and that his declaratory and injunctive claims regarding the prayers appeared to have become moot. [para. 22, at 234]
  14. Applying Lemon and distinguishing Lee v. Weisman, the Sixth Circuit held that the former nonsectarian prayers and the moments of silence did not violate the Establishment Clause. It rejected Chaudhuri's contention, based on the 1988 letter, that the moments of silence were a ruse to foster prayer. [para. 33] It held that the audience recitations were not attributable to TSU and that the Free Exercise claim lacked merit, and it affirmed the judgment for all defendants. [paras. 27-55]
  15. The Sixth Circuit's opinion cites the Seventh Circuit's decision in Tanford v. Brand, 104 F.3d 982, as supporting authority on the legitimacy of the prayers' secular purposes [para. 27], the de minimis character of the religious gesture [para. 36] and of any entanglement [para. 40], the absence of coercion to participate [para. 47], and Chaudhuri's freedom, even if obliged to attend, not to participate in the prayers [para. 48]. It does not describe Tanford's facts, and its separate discussion of the audience's age cites Lee v. Weisman, Marsh v. Chambers and other decisions, not Tanford. [paras. 50-51]
  16. The opinion does not address immunity, the capacity in which the individual defendants were sued, or the statutory basis of the damages claim. [entire opinion]
  17. Judge Jones concurred as to the moments of silence. He dissented as to the prayers: he would have held the prayer challenge not moot and found an Establishment Clause violation warranting declaratory and injunctive relief. [paras. 57-64, at 240-41]
* **Relevant Dates:** Verified lower-court decision: 1997-11-18 (United States Court of Appeals, Sixth Circuit.). New-run scheduled event: 1998-04-27. The user authorizes placement within OT1997, including after the final historical merits-opinion date.

* **Companion or Related Matters:** None.

* **Case Name:** Edwards v. United States
* **Citation or Docket:** Supreme Court docket: 96-8732

* **October Term:** OT_1997 — new simulation

* **Question Presented:** May a sentencing judge determine the types and quantities of drugs attributable to a conspiracy after a general verdict referring to cocaine and cocaine base? [Analytical formulation, not a verbatim petition quotation.]
* **Question Granted:** Scope: May a sentencing judge determine the types and quantities of drugs attributable to a conspiracy after a general verdict referring to cocaine and cocaine base? This is an analytical formulation checked against the reported proceeding, not a claimed verbatim quotation of an unavailable petition or grant order.

* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** Review of a Seventh Circuit criminal sentencing judgment.
* **Relief Requested:** The defendants argue that an ambiguous verdict prevents sentencing based on the more severely treated drug.
* **Material Record:** At petitioners' trial under 21 U. S. C. §§ 841 and 846 for “conspir[ing]” to “possess with intent to . . . distribute [mixtures containing two] controlled substance[s],” namely, cocaine and cocaine base ( i. e., “crack”), the jury was instructed that the Government must prove that the conspiracy involved measurable amounts of “cocaine or cocaine base.” (Emphasis added.) The jury returned a general verdict of guilty, and the District Judge imposed sentences based on his finding that each petitioner's illegal conduct involved both cocaine and crack. Petitioners argued (for the first time) in the Seventh Circuit that their sentences were unlawful insofar as they were based upon crack, because the word “or” in the jury instruction meant that the judge must assume that the conspiracy involved only cocaine, which is treated more leniently than crack by United States Sentencing Guidelines § 2D1.1(c). However, the court held that the judge need not assume that only cocaine was involved, pointing out that, because the Guidelines require the sentencing judge, not the jury, to determine both the kind and the amount of the drugs at issue in a drug conspiracy, the jury's belief about which drugs were involved—cocaine, crack, or both—was beside the point. # Edwards objective record supplement


The indictment charged cocaine and cocaine base; the jury instruction used the alternative, and the general verdict did not specify the drug. The sentencing judge found each defendant's conduct involved both substances. The claim that the alternative jury instruction required cocaine-only sentencing was raised for the first time on appeal. The related assertion that a cocaine-only assumption could have changed the judge's factual findings was not presented to the sentencing court.

The published source reports, citing the United States brief at 15–16 and notes 6–7 and appendix pages 42–47, 72–82, 107–112, 136–141, 163–169, that every sentence was within the statutory limits for a cocaine-only conspiracy given the cocaine quantities attributed to that petitioner. This is a supported published recitation; the underlying brief and appendix have not yet been read here. It does not state the individual sentences or authorize inventing them.

The record is described as a series of interrelated cocaine and crack transactions. Petitioners did not contend that their crack activity fell outside the same course of conduct or common scheme or plan. No acquittal of a separate crack offense is reported: the ambiguity arose from the alternative instruction and general conviction. The sentence calculations used evidence presented to the jury, but the published source describes the judge's findings as independent of a particular assumption about the jury's selection of a conspiracy object. These are source characterizations, not new factual findings or a conclusion that every possible sentencing challenge was waived.

* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Ninth Circuit recalled its mandate and granted Thompson habeas relief.
* **Relief Requested:** California seeks reversal and reinstatement of the original mandate denying habeas relief.
* **Material Record:** In 1983, respondent Thompson was convicted of rape and murder and sentenced to death in a California state court. The special circumstance of murder during the commission of rape made him eligible for the death penalty. In ruling on his first federal habeas petition in 1995, the District Court, inter alia, granted relief on his rape conviction and the rape special circumstance, thus invalidating his death sentence. A Ninth Circuit panel reversed the grant in June 1996, and it denied Thompson's petition for rehearing and suggestion for hearing en banc in March 1997. In June, the Ninth Circuit issued a mandate denying all habeas relief. The State then set an August execution date, and the State Supreme Court denied Thompson's fourth state habeas petition. Two days before the execution, however, the en banc Ninth Circuit recalled its mandate sua sponte, based on claims and evidence presented in Thompson's first habeas petition. The court had delayed action in the interests of comity until the conclusion of his fourth state habeas proceeding. It asserted it had recalled the mandate because procedural misunderstandings at the court prevented it from calling for en banc review before the mandate issued, and because the original panel's decision would lead to a miscarriage of justice. In granting habeas relief, the court found that Thompson was denied effective assistance of counsel at trial by his attorney's failure to contest the conclusions of the State's forensic expert and to impeach the credibility of two jailhouse informants. # United States v. Estate of Romani

# SECTION I: NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

* **Case Name:** United States v. Estate of Romani
* **Citation or Docket:** Supreme Court docket: 96-1613

* **October Term:** OT_1997 — new simulation
* **Entry Stage and Event:** Scheduled Supreme Court review event; the supplied question and lower judgment identify the matter. No present Supreme Court disposition is supplied.

* **Simulated Event Date:** 1998-04-29 — VERIFIED HISTORICAL DATE, used as this new simulation’s event anchor. Preparation is not a completed simulation decision.

* **Question Presented:** Does the federal priority statute displace the protection Congress gave a previously perfected judgment lien against a later federal tax lien? [Analytical formulation, not a verbatim petition quotation.]
* **Question Granted:** Scope: Does the federal priority statute displace the protection Congress gave a previously perfected judgment lien against a later federal tax lien? This is an analytical formulation checked against the reported proceeding, not a claimed verbatim quotation of an unavailable petition or grant order.

* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** Review of a Pennsylvania judgment preferring the established judgment lien.
* **Relief Requested:** The United States invokes the general priority of federal claims in an insolvent estate.
* **Material Record:** After a third party perfected a $400,000 judgment lien under Pennsylvania law on Francis Romani's Cambria County real property, the Internal Revenue Service filed notices of tax liens on the property, totaling some $490,000. When Mr. Romani died, his entire estate consisted of real estate worth only $53,001. Because the property was encumbered by both the judgment lien and the federal tax liens, the estate's administrator sought the county court's permission to transfer the property to the judgment creditor in lieu of execution. The court authorized the conveyance, overruling the Federal Government's objection that the transfer violated the federal priority statute, 31 U. S. C. § 3713(a), which provides that a Government claim “shall be paid first” when a decedent's estate cannot pay all of its debts. The Superior Court of Pennsylvania affirmed, as did the Pennsylvania Supreme Court. The latter court determined that there was a “plain inconsistency” between § 3713 and the Federal Tax Lien Act of 1966, which provides that a federal tax lien “shall not be valid” against judgment lien creditors until a prescribed notice has been given, 26 U. S. C. § 6323(a). The court concluded that the 1966 Act effectively limited § 3713's operation as to tax debts, relying on United States v. Kimbell Foods, Inc., 440 U. S. 715, 738, which noted that the 1966 Act modified the Government's preferred position in the tax area and recognized the priority of many state claims over federal tax liens. [Factual and procedural account from the reported opinion’s introductory material; disputed submissions remain disputed.]

# SECTION I: NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

* **Case Name:** Ricci v. Village of Arlington Heights

* **Question Presented:** Whether full custodial arrest pursuant to an automatic municipal policy is reasonable for the fine-only offense of operating a business without a license. [Analytical formulation, not a verbatim petition quotation.]
* **Question Granted:** Scope: Whether full custodial arrest pursuant to an automatic municipal policy is reasonable for the fine-only offense of operating a business without a license. This is an analytical formulation checked against the reported proceeding, not a claimed verbatim quotation of an unavailable petition or grant order.

* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Seventh Circuit affirmed summary judgment for the defendants, 116 F.3d 288 (June 20, 1997), No. 96-2229. Supreme Court No. 97-501 was argued April 21, 1998. The underlying ordinance’s civil/regulatory character must be retained rather than recasting it as a breach-of-peace misdemeanor.
* **Relief Requested:** Ricci argues that a fine-only licensing violation by an identified business operator provides no proportionate basis for automatic station-house custody, invoking the limited common-law arrest power and Fourth Amendment reasonableness.
* **Material Record:** Randall Ricci operated Rudeway Enterprises, a telemarketing business. Officers investigating complaints arrested an employee on an outstanding warrant, then asked Ricci about his own business license. He admitted he lacked one. Under Village policy they took him to the station, confined him in an interview room for about an hour, prepared paperwork, and released him on recognizance. His wife obtained the license and the charge was dismissed. The separate business-premises search claim was settled; Ricci did not appeal dismissal of his no-probable-cause claim. The live challenge concerns custodial processing under the licensing ordinance, not the existence of probable cause.
