# Home Builders - public-ground compatibility audit packet

This packet contains only the proposed public legal ground, frozen Associate positions and date-eligible public position evidence. It contains no private supplement. The event is the plenary merits decision of June 8, 1998, lower docket 96-5354. The historical comparator is the D.C. Circuit opinion; no historical Supreme Court merits comparator exists.

## Proposed public action and opinion

Vacate and remand, 5-4: Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer support the judgment. Stevens/Souter would affirm; Scalia/Thomas would reverse and remand for confined relief. The Chief Justice delivers the Opinion of the Court. Part I (the enacted class and rejection of the economic-subset ground) is joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas: five. Part II-A (the regime route) is joined by those four and Ginsburg and Breyer: seven. Part II-B (the independent externality route), Part III (the Government's support responsibility and legislative latitude) and Part IV (vacatur/remand and statutory limits) are joined by O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer: five in each. Ginsburg/Breyer do not join Part I. Scalia/Thomas do not join Parts II-B, III or IV. Stevens/Souter join no part.

Kennedy writes a concurrence joined by O'Connor, joining the entire Court opinion. Breyer writes an opinion concurring in Parts II, III and IV and the judgment, and dissenting from Part I, joined by Ginsburg. Stevens writes a dissent joined by Souter. Scalia writes an opinion concurring in Parts I and II-A and dissenting from Parts II-B, III and IV and the judgment, joined by Thomas. Each part states a distinct legal question: enacted class, regime necessity, independent externality, evidentiary responsibility, and remedy. These divisions follow the legal questions, not assignment counts. Stone, O'Connor and Kennedy are the only full-opinion eligible authors.

The Court treats the enacted taking class as noneconomic: injury to listed wildlife is its predicate, not the actor's exchange, expenditures or development function. Genuine economic classes remain regulable under Wickard, Perez and Hodel. Those authorities do not themselves authorize replacing the taking class with an economic subset whose characteristics Congress did not enact. Sweet Home's construction remains binding and does not settle the separate constitutional question.

Congress may include noneconomic intrastate conduct when inclusion is reasonably necessary to prevent evasion or material impairment of a valid federal regulation of interstate commerce. Congress may also reach such conduct when it reasonably identifies, on a materially adequate legislative or adjudicative basis, a substantial interstate externality generated by the regulated class that individual States cannot reasonably confine or internalize. The Government must identify and support whichever route it invokes. Under the second route it must identify the regulated class; the concrete cross-border ecological, commercial or comparable interstate effect; its substantial rather than speculative character; the reason ordinary territorial authority cannot reasonably internalize or control that effect; and the reasonable fit of the federal prohibition to that spillover. No fixed number of States, incidents, dollars or species, market share or scientific threshold applies. Courts independently determine constitutional eligibility; Congress receives substantial latitude on prediction, magnitude and design once an eligible mechanism is identified. There is no universal requirement of formal findings, defendant-specific congressional findings or clear-and-convincing proof. Courts cannot invent a market, causal pathway, externality or regime necessity. National importance, biodiversity, possible future scientific or economic value, tourism, development pressure, comprehensiveness, or parallel problems in many States is insufficient by itself.

Existing judgments do not resolve the material interstate mechanism under this rule. The lower courts must identify the mechanism actually invoked and test the existing legislative and adjudicative record, rather than assume a cross-border ecological finding or a necessary interstate-regime relationship. This is neither affirmance nor categorical invalidation. No new event, blanket permit, taking finding, permit entitlement, deadline, numerical benchmark or judicially manufactured factual premise follows. Actual interstate sale, transport, trafficking, migratory movement and another independently adequate Commerce mechanism remain distinct. The Court does not make every ecosystem national or every species a commodity.

## Associate-specific compatibility

O'Connor's frozen Path B objection demands enacted-class fit and an actual mechanism. Her Hale opinion and Rybar/Kirk joins supply the first route; her Hinds join rejects an owner's economic relations as a substitute for the statutory predicate. The Court accepts that objection. The second route requires a concrete effect that escapes ordinary territorial control, with an actual record and reasonable statutory fit, so it does not revive general biodiversity-benefit aggregation. Her support is a reasoned inference from that mechanism-first architecture and her Kennedy Lopez concurrence join, not a claim that Hale already decided the externality route. Because the Court articulates an available constitutional mechanism whose adequacy the judgments did not determine, she accepts vacatur for application of it rather than final invalidity. Her joined concurrence insists that national desirability and interstate inability are different propositions, and each factual link remains subject to review.

Kennedy's frozen Path B objection likewise demands an operation connecting the enacted class itself to commerce. His American Life League, Rybar, Robinson and Bird opinions recognize different concrete mechanisms rather than one exhaustive commercial-act taxonomy. The externality route supplies that connection only when the effect actually crosses jurisdictional lines and cannot reasonably be contained by the States. His historical Lopez concurrence with O'Connor rejects rigid formal economic classifications while requiring an enforceable national/local boundary. The Court's independently reviewed boundary, record support and fit answer his objection without turning hospital labels or development expenditure into constitutional facts. He joins both routes and vacatur; his concurrence explains the genuine interstate mechanism and the practical territorial limit without adding a commercial-transaction prerequisite that the Court does not adopt.

Ginsburg retains the frozen Path A analysis and does not join Part I. Her Sweet Home and Hinds joins treat the habitat-altering development as the actual economic function; she continues to regard that as a sufficient as-applied ground for affirmance. She recognizes, however, that Part I has five independent votes (Stone, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas) rejecting that route as a matter of the enacted class. Her further votes therefore concern the legally available consequence of that Court holding, rather than a concession that her own classification is mistaken. She affirmatively joins both routes in Part II, the support/deference rule in Part III and the actual vacatur/remand in Part IV. The narrower constitutional inquiry preserves federal power over materially supported interstate relationships without assuming such a relationship here. Her own Hinds vote accepted an existing-record remand when the controlling legal predicate required a more precise examination of the record; here the controlling Part I prevents an affirmance on the predicate she prefers, while a reasoned test of alternative eligible mechanisms remains possible. Her historical join of Breyer's Lopez dissent supports class-wide practical analysis and legislative respect within that inquiry. She does not use aggregate national importance as a substitute for the mechanism under governing Lopez. She supports this final remand because it permits a lawful federal application if supported and avoids a constitutional invalidation the existing record inquiry does not establish. Her assent is an independent judicial choice of the legal consequence, not a vote automatically compelled by Part I. Her concurrence with Breyer expressly preserves the economic-application position while accepting Part I as the operative rule for this case; she casts no simultaneous vote to affirm the judgment.

Breyer likewise retains Path A and dissents from Part I. The proposed opinion does not assert that deference safeguards persuade him to abandon his analysis of actual economic development. Rather, an independent five-Justice Part I settles that statutory-class question for the Court, so his vote on the consequence confronts a different legal premise. His historical Lopez dissent and simulated Sweet Home, Hinds, Robinson and Bird joins favor practical operation and legislative judgment rather than categorical immunity for noneconomic local activity. He accepts the two affirmative grounds in Part II as a lawful response to the Court's classification and independently accepts Part III's material-support requirement because it requires a reasoned legislative or adjudicative basis without de novo judicial environmental science, formal findings in every case or a numerical benchmark. His Hinds join supports examining the existing record under the Court's adopted predicate instead of assuming missing facts or invalidating without the proper inquiry. Part IV therefore represents his chosen operative judgment: vacatur/remand for the alternatives that remain after Part I. The conjunction matters: genuinely interstate mechanisms, legislative latitude on supported predictions, and no invented finding or categorical invalidity. He does not cast a vote merely to avoid a split or supply five. The separate writing states his continuing disagreement with Part I and his full agreement with Parts II-IV and the judgment; it makes no conditional future vote and supplies no additional holding on the economic ground.

Stevens retains Path A and affirmance. His Hinds opinion's actual-function inquiry and Sweet Home, Robinson and Bird positions distinguish the very injury-causing development from an owner's unrelated purchases. He regards an as-applied economic class as a permissible identification of conduct actually regulated, not addition of an element. Although the supported routes need not be rejected in all cases, they are unnecessary here and do not justify vacating a judgment supported by economic conversion. He therefore joins no part of the Court's opinion; he declines to make the Court's class conclusion the premise for his own remedial vote because he regards a supported economic application as dispositive and the additional mechanism inquiry as unnecessary. No extra route or remedial vote is inferred from possible abstract agreement.

Souter retains Path A and joins Stevens's dissent. His Sweet Home opinion identifies habitat-causing activity itself and confines liability through actual injury and proximate cause. His Hinds and Robinson joins allow examination of that actual function. He is not persuaded to replace the economic-application ground with a record inquiry into an additional mechanism, especially where the record supplies no established externality. The Court's deference limits do not answer his disagreement about what conduct is actually regulated. He joins no part of the Court opinion and votes to affirm. He chooses to retain that disposition rather than accept Part I as the premise for a further inquiry; unlike Ginsburg and Breyer, he sees no reason to displace the sufficient actual-injury economic application with an alternative record examination.

Scalia retains Path B, joins Parts I and II-A, and writes an opinion dissenting from Parts II-B, III and IV and the judgment, joined by Thomas. His Lopez no-insertion and Hale/Rybar/Kirk joins allow an existing valid interstate regime to carry necessary local components. He finds the Court's second route still insufficiently tied to commerce: inability of States to confine a substantial ecological burden is not itself an exchange or an indispensable component of a commerce regime. The record supports neither Path A's reconstructed economic class nor Hale necessity. He would reverse for confined relief; the absence of a proven mechanism is not for him a reason to remit the case under a newly recognized independent route. He respects Sweet Home as binding statutory law and proposes no categorical intrastate immunity or facial invalidation of the ESA.

Thomas retains Path B, joins Parts I and II-A, and joins Scalia's separate opinion. His Lopez and application joins require an identified commerce mechanism, and his historical Lopez concurrence gives an additional Justice-specific reason to reject a free-standing externality basis resting on regulatory need. He does not use this case to overrule the controlling Lopez/Hale architecture and does not revive his Sweet Home statutory disagreement. He agrees that no demonstrated valid regime supplies the missing connection and prefers confined reversal/remand to the Court's inquiry. His partial Court join and separate join are coextensive with Scalia's.
