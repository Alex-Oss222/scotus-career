## Event

Tanford v. Brand, review of the Seventh Circuit's judgment in No. 96-3053, 104 F.3d 982. Decided December 22, 1997, on the merits of the challenge to Indiana University's official commencement invocations and benedictions. No separate Supreme Court docket or argument date is stated.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated on submission and at decision. No Justice was absent.

## Public Action

The Seventh Circuit's judgment is reversed, and the case is remanded for declaratory and appropriately limited prospective relief against the official prayer practice.

## Judgment & Remedy

Reversed and remanded, 6–3. Supporting: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Opposing: Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, who would affirm. Relief must bar the University's selection or conduct of invocation and benediction prayers as official worship while preserving independent prayer and lawful private expression. No damages, fee ruling, automatic hearing, mandatory new forum or general ban on religious speech is ordered.

## Holdings

**1. The live controversy concerns the University's own devotional program.** The continuing policy and Urbanski's concrete expectation of attending his 1999 graduation support prospective review. The University selects a religious leader and incorporates actual invocation and benediction prayers into its official ceremony; independent composition of the words does not make the exercises private equal-access speech. Part I is unanimous.

Lee's prospective-exposure principle supports the live claim without treating other plaintiffs as perpetual students or relying on ideological objection alone. The program places the invocation after the national anthem and the benediction at the close. The reproduced benediction invites prayer, addresses God, seeks divine assistance and concludes with Amen. The University need not have written a full script for the exercise to be its own: official selection and incorporation establish attribution. The limited instruction to offer an uplifting general message and the clergy's independent wording remain part of the record, not evidence of private-forum access.

**2. Official university commencement worship violates the Establishment Clause on this record.** A public university may not select a religious leader to conduct actual invocation and benediction prayers as components of its official commencement. Governmental presentation gives these exercises the prohibited primary religious effect; mature attendance, meaningful freedom to abstain, nonsectarian language and a sincere ceremonial purpose do not cure it. Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer directly adopt Parts II–III.

Lee and Ingebretsen establish official sponsorship as an independently sufficient ground under the retained Lemon framework. Today's decision applies that principle to the previously unresolved university setting. The audience's maturity and genuine freedom to remain seated, leave or attend another ceremony bear materially on coercion, but do not change whose worship is presented. Most attendees remained seated, ordinary movement was common, and no penalty followed nonattendance. The Court accepts those facts without finding adolescent pressure or requiring a separate coercion showing for the sponsorship ground.

The practice's long history and purpose of solemnity receive their actual weight but supply no authority for this governmental devotional act. Marsh remains a distinct legislative-prayer setting, not a universal adult-ceremony exemption. Sherman permits a particular civic pledge and expressly distinguishes actual prayer; placing worship in a civic event does not convert it into civic acknowledgment. The ruling finds no sham purpose, denominational exclusion or excessive entanglement and does not replace Lemon with a new test.

**3. Relief must address the supported official practice and preserve private expression.** The lower courts must provide declaratory and appropriately bounded prospective relief against the University's official selection and inclusion of these prayers. Independently chosen private religious expression, neutral access to a genuine forum and nondevotional ceremonial remarks remain distinct; the judgment requires no new forum and no suppression of otherwise lawful viewpoints.

Ingebretsen supplies the remedy's official/private boundary. The decree must reach the established institutional practice, not every religious reference, speaker or activity at the University. Private remarks by graduates, historical acknowledgment without devotional operation and a moment of silence are not prohibited by this holding. The lower courts implement the decree without prescribing new ceremonial content or treating these reservations as universal approval of arrangements not before the Court.

## Precedent Treatment

Lee and Ingebretsen's independent official-sponsorship principle is applied to the actual university commencement program; their separate substantial-pressure rules remain unchanged and need not be applied to find a violation here. Engel and Schempp retain their official-worship protections despite nonsectarian wording and permission to abstain. Lemon remains the governing framework, applied through primary religious effect. Marsh is distinguished by its legislative context, and Sherman by its civic exercise. Pinette, Rosenberger, Good News and Mergens preserve genuinely private religious expression; their neutral-access holdings do not authorize governmental worship. No precedent is overruled.

## Law After Decision

Effective December 22, 1997, the University's official selection and incorporation of these devotional prayers is unconstitutional even for the mature voluntary audience shown here. This extension rests on official sponsorship and primary effect, independently of a coercion finding. The separate pressure protections, private-speech rules, legislative-prayer precedent and ordinary ceremonial acknowledgment retain their existing limits. The decision creates no general prohibition on religion at universities or on every religious feature of an adult ceremony.

## Separate Writings

Justice Scalia's dissent, joined by Justices Kennedy and Thomas in Part I, would affirm because this brief traditional adult ceremony, with genuine freedom to abstain and no demonstrated unconstitutional pressure, falls outside the established secondary-school prohibitions. They acknowledge that the exercises are official and devotional; their disagreement concerns the constitutional consequence of the adult setting.

Justice Thomas also joins Part II of Justice Scalia's dissent, which emphasizes historical ceremonial accommodation and the absence of the compulsion required by their account. That position does not become a universal historical exemption or an opinion of the Court, and Kennedy does not join its coercion methodology.

Justice Kennedy separately explains that Lee protects against substantial practical pressure without requiring a legal penalty. The mature audience, optional attendance, ordinary movement, seated abstention and available exit or reentry do not establish that pressure here. He also declines to extend the independent sponsorship prohibition to this university setting; he does not call the prayers private or reject practical coercion as a constitutional concern.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns for declaratory and limited prospective relief against the established official prayer program. The Court decides no damages, fees or new ceremony design, and requires no automatic evidentiary hearing. The continuing policy and Urbanski's expected exposure support the present relief; no later enrollment fact is presumed. No Supreme Court jurisdiction is retained or return date set.
