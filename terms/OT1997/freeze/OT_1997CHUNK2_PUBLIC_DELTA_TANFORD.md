## Event

Tanford v. Brand, review of the Seventh Circuit's judgment in No. 96-3053, 104 F.3d 982. Decided December 22, 1997, on the merits of the challenge to Indiana University's official commencement invocations and benedictions. No separate Supreme Court docket or argument date is stated.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated on submission and at decision. No Justice was absent.

## Public Action

The Seventh Circuit's judgment is reversed, and the case is remanded for the lower courts to determine appropriate prospective relief against the official prayer practice, subject to the plaintiffs' continuing standing and the requirements for an injunction.

## Judgment & Remedy

Reversed and remanded, 6–3. Supporting: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Opposing: Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, who would affirm. Any prospective relief the lower courts find warranted, subject to the plaintiffs' continuing standing and the requirements for an injunction, must be limited to the University's selection or conduct of invocation and benediction prayers as official worship and must preserve independent prayer and lawful private expression. No damages, fee ruling, automatic hearing, mandatory new forum or general ban on religious speech is ordered.

## Holdings

**1. The live controversy concerns the University's own devotional program.** The continuing policy and Urbanski's concrete expectation of attending his 1999 graduation support prospective review. The University selects a religious leader and incorporates actual invocation and benediction prayers into its official ceremony; independent composition of the words does not make the exercises private equal-access speech. Part I is unanimous.

Lee's prospective-exposure principle supports the live claim without treating other plaintiffs as perpetual students or relying on ideological objection alone. The program places the invocation after the national anthem and the benediction at the close. The reproduced benediction invites prayer, addresses God, seeks divine assistance and concludes with Amen. The University need not have written a full script for the exercise to be its own: official selection and incorporation establish attribution. The limited instruction to offer an uplifting general message and the clergy's independent wording remain part of the record, not evidence of private-forum access.

**2. Official university commencement worship violates the Establishment Clause on this record.** A public university violates the Establishment Clause when it incorporates officially arranged religious prayer into its commencement exercises, as the University did by selecting a religious leader to conduct actual invocation and benediction prayers as components of its official commencement. The violation does not depend on whether attendance is compulsory, whether the prayer is sectarian, or whether the ceremony also serves secular purposes. This governmental organization of worship falls under the specific-prohibition route of Lemon as Agostini modified it; mature attendance, meaningful freedom to abstain, brevity, nonsectarian language and a sincere ceremonial purpose do not cure it. Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer directly adopt Parts II–III.

Agostini retains Lemon as the governing test while treating purpose, effects and entanglement as evidence within one inquiry, not independent prongs. A specific prohibition or distinct doctrine governs conduct it resolves; an established permissible relationship defeats the claim on that ground; otherwise a legally sufficient unresolved allegation receives the attribution-and-function inquiry. Lee and Ingebretsen supply the official-worship prohibition applied here. The University selects and presents actual devotional exercises as its own commencement activity, so that specific prohibition resolves this case without a separate primary-effect test or a residual inquiry. Today's decision applies that principle to the previously unresolved university setting. The audience's maturity and genuine freedom to remain seated, leave or attend another ceremony bear materially on coercion, but do not change whose worship is presented. Most attendees remained seated, ordinary movement was common, and no penalty followed nonattendance. The Court accepts those facts without finding adolescent pressure or requiring a separate coercion showing.

The practice's long history and purpose of solemnity receive their actual weight but establish no permissible relationship authorizing this governmental devotional act. Marsh protects a distinct legislative-prayer setting; it does not establish an exception for every adult ceremony. Sherman permits a particular civic pledge and expressly distinguishes actual prayer; placing worship in a civic event does not convert it into civic acknowledgment. A secular ceremonial objective cannot justify the prohibited means. The exercises' brevity, and the view below that any advancement or entanglement was de minimis, do not determine whether the worship is officially organized. The ruling requires no sham-purpose, denominational-exclusion or excessive-entanglement finding. It applies Lemon as Agostini modified it and decides no other adult ceremonial arrangement.

**3. Relief must address the supported official practice and preserve private expression.** On remand, the lower courts shall determine appropriate prospective relief against the University's official selection and inclusion of these prayers, subject to the plaintiffs' continuing standing and the ordinary requirements for an injunction. Independently chosen private religious expression, neutral access to a genuine forum and nondevotional ceremonial remarks remain distinct; the judgment requires no new forum and no suppression of otherwise lawful viewpoints.

Ingebretsen supplies the remedy's official/private boundary. Any decree must reach the established institutional practice, not every religious reference, speaker or activity at the University. Private remarks by graduates, historical acknowledgment without devotional operation and a moment of silence are not prohibited by this holding. Any decree the lower courts enter must not prescribe new ceremonial content or treat these reservations as a ruling that arrangements not before the Court are permissible.

## Precedent Treatment

Lee and Ingebretsen's official-worship prohibition is applied to the actual university commencement program; their separate substantial-pressure rules remain unchanged and need not be applied to find a violation here. Engel and Schempp retain their official-worship protections despite nonsectarian wording and permission to abstain. Lemon remains the governing test as Agostini modified it: the specific-prohibition route resolves this governmental organization of worship, and primary religious effect is evidence rather than an independent prong. Agostini's onsite secular-service holding does not authorize governmental worship. Marsh is distinguished by its legislative context, and Sherman by its civic exercise. Pinette, Rosenberger, Good News and Mergens preserve genuinely private religious expression; their neutral-access holdings do not authorize governmental worship. No precedent is overruled.

## Law After Decision

Effective December 22, 1997, a public university violates the Establishment Clause when it incorporates officially arranged religious prayer into its commencement exercises; the violation does not depend on compulsory attendance, sectarian content or the ceremony's secular purposes. The University's official selection and incorporation of these devotional prayers is unconstitutional even for the mature voluntary audience shown here. Lemon, as Agostini modified it, resolves this governmental organization of worship through its specific-prohibition route, independently of a coercion finding. The separate pressure protections, private-speech rules, legislative-prayer precedent and ordinary ceremonial acknowledgment retain their existing limits. The decision creates no general prohibition on religion at universities or on every religious feature of an adult ceremony.

## Separate Writings

Justice Scalia's dissent, joined by Justices Kennedy and Thomas in Part I, would affirm because this brief traditional adult ceremony, with genuine freedom to abstain and no demonstrated unconstitutional pressure, falls outside the established secondary-school prohibitions. All three joined Agostini's modification of Lemon. They regard this particular historical ceremonial relationship as permissible within that test, drawing on Marsh while recognizing that its holding concerns legislative prayer. They acknowledge that the exercises are official and devotional; they disagree with the Court over whether the official-worship prohibition or a permissible ceremonial relationship governs this adult setting. The Court rejects that extension of Marsh. Their position does not make every longstanding adult prayer practice permissible.

Justice Thomas also joins Part II of Justice Scalia's dissent, which emphasizes historical ceremonial accommodation and the absence of the compulsion required by their account. That position does not become a universal historical exemption or an opinion of the Court, and Kennedy does not join its coercion methodology.

Justice Kennedy separately explains that Lee protects against substantial practical pressure without requiring a legal penalty. The mature audience, optional attendance, ordinary movement, seated abstention and available exit or reentry do not establish that pressure here. Consistently with his full join in Agostini, he accepts the modified Lemon test but regards this concrete voluntary adult ceremony as a permissible relationship, declining the Court's application of the official-worship prohibition to this university setting. He does not call the prayers private or reject practical coercion as a constitutional concern.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns for the lower courts to determine appropriate prospective relief against the established official prayer program, subject to the plaintiffs' continuing standing and the requirements for an injunction; the suit sought injunctive relief. The Court decides no damages, fees or new ceremony design, and requires no automatic evidentiary hearing. The continuing policy and Urbanski's expected exposure keep the claim for prospective relief live now; no later enrollment fact is presumed. No Supreme Court jurisdiction is retained or return date set.
