## Event

Rogers v. United States, No. 96-1279. Argued November 5, 1997; decided January 14, 1998. The writ concerns the Eleventh Circuit's affirmance of two silencer convictions under 26 U.S.C. §§5861(d) and (i). Review was granted to consider whether an omitted-element instruction may be harmless when the defendant admitted the element at trial.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision. No Justice was absent.

## Public Action

The writ of certiorari is dismissed as improvidently granted because the intended omitted-element question is not adequately presented by this charge. The Court publishes the writings identified below; no separate numerical tally is announced.

## Judgment & Remedy

The Eleventh Circuit's judgment remains undisturbed without a Supreme Court merits affirmance. The two silencer convictions are left in place. The separate machinegun conviction, reversed below, is not reinstated. No remand, retrial, acquittal, release or resentencing is ordered.

## Holdings

The Court decides no omitted-element harmless-error question. Its procedural conclusion is confined to the mismatch between the intended omission/admission question and the actual charge. Part I has the named joins above; neither competing charge construction becomes a controlling merits ruling.

The charge defined firearm to include a silencer and required knowing possession of the defined firearm. Later wording addressed the need for knowledge that the item was a firearm the law requires to be registered, while separately describing knowing possession and firearm status. Those formulations raise the distinct questions of factual characteristic knowledge and knowledge of registration law. The requested instruction and subsequent objection do not disappear merely because counsel earlier accepted insertion of a knowledge term. Rogers's identification of the object is evidence, not automatic intentional waiver of jury determination or an admission of knowing possession of the bag.

The dismissal does not establish that every instruction was correct, that an actual omission was harmless, that every omission is structural, or that the claim was forfeited. It adopts no extension of Staples to every silencer or §5861(i) issue. The grant's vehicle problem, rather than a general preference to avoid difficult constitutional issues, supplies the disposition.

## Precedent Treatment

Staples retains its distinction between knowledge of the relevant weapon characteristics and knowledge of registration law within its actual machinegun scope. Gaudin retains jury allocation of actual elements. Sullivan retains its rule for a vitiated reasonable-doubt standard; Roy retains its qualified actual-verdict analysis for the omission category it decided. Olano and Johnson preserve the difference between forfeiture and intentional waiver and their distinct error-review conditions. The Court does not apply those merits rules to sustain these convictions or announce an admission exception. Izumi's question-presentation discipline remains intact. No precedent is overruled or modified.

## Law After Decision

There is no new substantive rule governing elements, silencer knowledge, harmlessness, structural error or waiver. The appellate judgment stands without this Court's approval of its merits reasoning. The affirmative charge reading, ambiguity explanation and opposing presentation analysis retain only the status of their identified writings.

## Separate Writings

Justice Thomas's Part II, joined by Justices Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer, reads the defined-firearm knowledge requirement as submitting knowledge of the silencer characteristics and the later registration disclaimer as addressing legal knowledge. The requested formulation's possible legal-classification demand therefore does not, in that view, establish the omitted factual premise. This is their vehicle reading, not a finding that the defense requested only legal knowledge, that every instruction was proper or that admission cures error.

Justice O'Connor, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justice Scalia, declines that affirmative construction. The competing readings make the intended omission question sufficiently uncertain to warrant dismissal. The registration-language disclaimer can address ignorance of law rather than characteristics, but the writing does not definitively resolve the whole charge. It preserves the requested-instruction objection and distinguishes an evidentiary admission from intentional waiver.

Justice Kennedy, joined by Justice Souter, would reach the granted question. The later charge language, recorded colloquy, then-existing circuit-law context and the Eleventh Circuit's actual omission-and-harmlessness ruling sufficiently present the factual-knowledge issue. The dissent objects to using the competing construction to avoid that question. It expresses no present Supreme Court disposition on whether the assumed omission was harmless.

## Procedure After Action

The writ is terminated. The lower appellate judgment is undisturbed, including the separate reversal of the machinegun count. This Court retains no further review, sets no return date and issues no remedial instruction concerning sentence or custody.
