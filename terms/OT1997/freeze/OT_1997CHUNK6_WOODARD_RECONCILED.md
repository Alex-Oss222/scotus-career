## Result and corrections to the provisional topology

All eight Associates retain their judgment commitments: **Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer reject the Fifth Amendment claim on the supported voluntary-interview premises. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer reject the due-process claim on these pleadings; Stevens would preserve a due-process remand.** No historical disposition departure is supported.

Three formulations require clarification or correction after full historical review, without rewriting the provisional freeze:

1. **The five-Associate minimum-process proposition is not confined to total access denial.** O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer historically preserved some minimum safeguards because life remains, illustrating both arbitrary exclusion from the process and deciding mercy by a coin flip. Stevens expressly agreed with that basic principle and sought further proceedings. Current Sandin and Johnson do not justify deleting arbitrary decisionmaking from the floor. The reconciled proposition covers those minimal safeguards, while preserving the difference between arbitrary process and judicial review of discretionary mercy on its merits.
2. **Stevens does not join a general rejection of the established-process analogy in Evitts.** His historical writing at 292–295 and note 3 expressly relies on it: when the State creates a process integral to its final decision to take life, that process must satisfy basic fairness, even though it need not replicate a trial. His earlier Dumschat dissent at 470–472 supports that account. Current Johnson addresses initial parole without a substantive release constraint and preserves independent rights; it does not displace this distinct retained-life premise. Retain his basic minimum-process support and remand, and remove any implication that he joins the broad dismissal of the analogy.
3. **The Fifth Amendment result survives the limited adverse-inference assumption.** The historical unanimous Part III does more than observe that no actual use of silence was proved: it assumes an adverse inference in the voluntary civil clemency process and still finds no compulsion. The frozen commitments reject a universal inference license, which is sound, but must not imply that the mere possibility of an ordinary civil inference defeats their noncompulsion conclusion. All eight accept that limited alternative assumption, with Baxter's distinction between an inference and an automatic sanction or compelled waiver preserved. No actual Ohio use of silence is found.

These corrections retain the published historical positions where no concrete changed premise supports narrowing them. They do not import historical authorship or require any particular opinion arrangement.

## Reconciled operative propositions

**A — no state-created entitlement.** Ohio's mandatory investigation, hearing and advisory recommendation do not meaningfully constrain the Governor to grant clemency on specified substantive facts. They therefore create neither a right to mercy nor the claimed full adjudicatory procedure. Dumschat distinguishes seeking clemency from being entitled to receive it; the actual Sandin rule, Doe's procedure/substance distinction and Johnson's initial-parole holding reinforce that boundary. Mandatory procedures alone are insufficient. The lack of such an entitlement does not extinguish independently retained constitutional rights.

**B — the appellate analogy does not itself require the requested procedures.** First appeal as of right and discretionary executive mercy perform different functions. Evitts does not require Ohio to import appellate representation, triallike evidence procedures or a new guilt adjudication into this clemency process merely because mercy follows conviction. Seven Associates reject the Sixth Circuit's general integral-stage theory as a basis for the due-process remand here. Stevens preserves the narrower established-process and retained-life analogy described below; he does not demand trial machinery or an automatic process formula based on distance from trial.

**C — minimum safeguards protecting remaining life.** A condemned prisoner remains alive and retains a constitutionally protected life interest. Although the process due after valid conviction is reduced and the ultimate mercy choice remains discretionary, some minimal procedural safeguards apply to an established capital-clemency process. Arbitrarily denying any access, or replacing consideration with an entirely arbitrary device such as a coin flip, can warrant judicial intervention. This principle creates no right to a favorable recommendation, no accuracy code for every disputed file item, no judicial reassessment of the merits of mercy and no fixed list of trial rights. Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer support this common proposition. Stevens's broader account of the established process and his different application remain separately identified.

**D — application and due-process remedy.** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer would reverse the Sixth Circuit's due-process remand and sustain rejection of the present claim. O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer apply C: the notice and offered personal interview provide access, and the particular objections do not establish failure of the minimum safeguards. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas reach the same judgment on A and B without joining C as a general procedural-life ground. Stevens would retain a remand to examine whether the actual process affords basic fairness and meaningful participation, without finding a constitutional violation already established or prescribing each requested safeguard.

**E — voluntary interview and self-incrimination.** An optional clemency interview is not compelled self-incrimination merely because the inmate must choose between giving information that might hurt his clemency or postconviction position and remaining silent. The absence of counsel at that interview, confidentiality or immunity does not by itself compel answers. The conclusion remains true on the assumption that the Authority considers an ordinary adverse inference from silence in the civil clemency evaluation, as distinguished in Baxter from an automatic penalty for asserting the privilege. All eight Associates reject the Fifth Amendment remand on those grounds. No obligation to attend or answer, automatic ineligibility for remaining silent, compelled waiver, actual criminal use of compelled answers, or particular unlawful silence sanction is established. LaChance and Murphy preserve analysis of a materially supported compulsion or penalty; Johnson prevents treating the discretionary-benefit label as a complete defense to such a claim.

## Changed entering law and historical-premise audit

The historical Part II at 283 invokes historical Sandin's atypical-and-significant-hardship formulation as an additional ground. That formulation is not the law entering this event. The current Court instead requires a genuine substantive state constraint on meaningful deprivation and leaves direct constitutional interests independent. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas supported a hardship alternative in their earlier public Sandin dissent, but their present common state-entitlement ground cannot publish it as controlling law. Each applies the current rule to Ohio's wholly discretionary ultimate mercy choice. The required steps do not compel a favorable recommendation or grant of clemency; Dumschat and the substantive distinction made in Johnson therefore continue to support their dispositions. This is a concrete premise substitution, not a silent import of the historical Court's law or a reason to change their judgment.

**Historical departure:** Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas retain their historical rejection of the due-process claim but cannot adopt historical Woodard's reliance on a controlling Sandin hardship test: that test received only four votes in the earlier public decision, and the controlling substantive-constraint rule now governs. Their own Sandin dissent positions explain why they retain the narrower entitlement/adjudication approach, while their full joins in Johnson supply the current-law basis for finding Ohio's mandatory process insufficient to create a mercy entitlement. The altered legal premise changes that supporting authority, not their judgment or their refusal to join the affirmative retained-life floor.

The five supporters of C are not historical departures: the full O'Connor and Stevens writings already support that minimal proposition, including arbitrary consideration as well as access. Nor does recognizing C contradict Johnson: ordinary initial-parole accuracy rights are distinct from minimum safeguards for a retained life interest, and Johnson expressly reserves independent-right wrongs. This distinction explains O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer's present adequate-process application and Stevens's remand without inventing a new state-created entitlement.

Herrera's current controlling proposition is record-specific. Neither its historical broader discussion nor the historical Woodard citation makes executive mercy an exclusive constitutionally sufficient substitute for all innocence claims. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas may note clemency's established independent executive function, but cannot use Herrera to announce that broader rule. Stevens's life-interest and established-process position stands on his eligible Dumschat/Ford positions and the sources cited above, without depending on a historical Herrera holding absent here.

The Fifth Amendment comparison leaves no material judgment or operative-rule departure after the limited-inference clarification. The official report at 286 expressly acknowledges the missing account of actual Ohio practice and assumes adverse inferences favorably to Woodard. Baxter, 425 U.S. 316–320, distinguishes ordinary civil evidentiary inferences from automatic consequences attached to silence and compelled criminal testimony. Murphy, 465 U.S. 434–439, distinguishes a command backed by an actual or implied privilege penalty from a difficult choice. Current LaChance expressly preserves genuine compulsion and does not impose a universal ban on lawful civil inferences. Johnson addresses a different protected litigation penalty and does not turn every evidentiary disadvantage into compulsion. These actual premises support every Associate's E commitment; they do not support an unlimited rule approving punishment for silence.

## Exact record and remedy limits

The District Court granted judgment on the pleadings; the asserted three-day interview notice, ten-day hearing notice, ongoing postconviction preparation difficulty, no interview counsel, chair-controlled counsel participation at the hearing, and inability to testify or submit documents at that hearing remain allegations in that posture. The seven reversing Associates conclude that the pleaded restrictions, considered with notice and the offered interview, do not require further due-process proceedings. They do not find an unmentioned alternative document channel, resolve disputed credibility or assume that a material allegation is false. Stevens would allow bounded consideration of the relevant evidentiary materials and the process as a whole, not decide every objection independently in Woodard's favor.

Ohio's required original review and recommendation continue even after an execution stay; a later hearing or revised recommendation on additional information is permitted at the Authority's discretion. The Governor's decision remains discretionary and the recommendation advisory. Woodard did not request the offered interview. The absence of immunity and confidentiality is supported by the Authority's stated position; actual use of answers or silence is not established. The hypothetical ordinary-inference analysis supplies no new factual finding, waiver, sanction, admission or criminal-use ruling. No equal-protection claim is presented. The historical denial of certiorari mentioned in the report is not imported as a current-timeline Court act.

The seven-Associate overall position reverses the adverse Sixth Circuit directions and returns for a disposition consistent with rejection of the presented claims; the part already rejecting an independent entitlement is not disturbed. Stevens agrees with reversal of the Fifth Amendment direction but would preserve a due-process remand. Neither position grants or denies clemency on the merits, sets an execution date, adjudicates a stay, orders immunity, supplies an automatic new hearing or counsel right, or retries guilt or innocence.
