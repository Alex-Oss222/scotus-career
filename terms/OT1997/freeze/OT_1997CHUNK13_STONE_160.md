## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: A fair opportunity, former habeas law, and an adequate record

* **Stone's Legal Position:** Meaningful judicial consideration does not guarantee relief. Lucas’s first federal petition and the appellate certificate already provided access; the present task is to test the actual claims with the correct law. Former §2254 factual presumptions attach to qualifying historical findings, not constitutional conclusions or every mixed question. Revised Wright preserves independent legal assessment. Revised Lindh supports the chapter-153 temporal analysis, while the reported absence of chapter-154 qualification remains a distinct case predicate.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The reported state and federal evidentiary proceedings addressed the central record disputes, including the other-suspect allegation and the possible source of knowledge about the Florida fire. Stone examines their described findings and limitations; he does not assume that the existence of any prior hearing satisfies every claim. No specific material gap requiring another hearing is established in the supplied presentation. Revised Keeney would require necessary development of a properly presented claim under its former-law rule; ordinary counsel negligence would not itself defeat that entitlement. That protection does not compel repetition of adequately developed issues. Stone imposes no new exhaustion hurdle and uses no Article III power to dispense with the applicable statutes.

### Issue 2: Innocence evidence, official statements, and access to underlying claims — claims 1–2 and gateway aspects of claims 3–7

* **Stone's Legal Position:** Consider the employment audit, neighbors, expert explanation of false confessions, investigative report, confession incentives, polygraph presentation, and Florida-fire evidence together with the prosecution record. Do not reject them simply because parts corroborate an existing defense or would be inadmissible at trial. Revised Schlup controls the gateway if it must be decided; the verdict does not conclusively answer it.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The described record contains evidence of unreliable work attendance practices and a dispute about when the neighbors could observe Lucas, as well as federal-hearing testimony that acquaintances could have supplied the fire information. Those points limit the force of the alibi inference but do not establish that every defense witness is false. A polygraph conclusion needs a reliability assessment, not categorical exclusion, and an explanation of confession incentives deserves weight without itself proving police coercion or innocence. Stone need not resolve those competing credibility questions to deny the independently insufficient constitutional claims discussed below. For that analysis he assumes the gateway in Lucas’s favor, as the historical court also did, while neither finding it satisfied nor relying on a finding that it failed. Procedural default is nonjurisdictional; this approach neither excuses it in another case nor bypasses a missing jurisdictional basis. Revised Lambrix preserves the distinct, nonrigid character of default sequencing, without itself deciding this merits route.

The requests in claims 1–2 that the State be prevented from executing an allegedly innocent person no longer present that remedy after commutation. Their evidence remains relevant to live claims. The saved record also reports that the Attorney General’s office made no official innocence determination and that the cited former Attorney General’s statements followed his tenure. Stone therefore does not treat those statements as a conclusive judicial admission. He does not adopt the historical court’s categorical rejection of every freestanding innocence claim or its undefined general burden: revised Herrera left that question open. Nor does he silently transform the execution-focused claim into a newly pleaded freestanding right to release from life imprisonment. Commutation expresses executive clemency, not a binding finding either way on guilt.

### Issue 3: Offense proof, victim identification, and counsel — claims 3–6

* **Stone's Legal Position:** Texas corroboration requirements and federal Jackson sufficiency are separate. Under Jackson, as preserved by revised Wright, the trial evidence must permit a rational finding of every element beyond reasonable doubt; rejecting the alibi alone cannot supply a missing element. Ordinary Strickland performance and reasonable-probability prejudice govern counsel claims.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The presented challenge asserts absence of independent corroboration of the sexual-assault element. On the reported trial record, Lucas’s admitted account describes the charged murder during an actual or attempted aggravated sexual assault. Stone does not infer that element solely from disbelief of his alibi or presume every confession admissible; the confession objections receive separate analysis below. The federal claim supplies no basis to exclude a constitutionally admissible confession from Jackson’s trial-record inquiry merely because state law may demand additional corroboration. Failure to establish the victim’s name likewise does not negate proof that a person was murdered. Stone therefore finds no federal sufficiency defect on these identified grounds. The execution-only aspect of claim 4 is moot; its conviction-related contention is considered here.

The victim-identification omission does not show Strickland prejudice because the proposed objection was not a legally required element of the prosecution’s case. The corroboration-performance allegation rests on counsel’s failure to anticipate the later Gribble development, where the reported contemporaneous law did not clearly establish that objection. Stone assesses reasonable professional performance at the time rather than treating every unsettled issue as automatically excusing counsel. The supplied allegation identifies no additional available argument establishing deficient handling on that record. Revised Fretwell supplies no general replacement for reasonable-probability prejudice and no additional unfairness burden; Stone does not adopt the historical opinion’s broader temporal shorthand. These identified counsel claims therefore do not establish both necessary Strickland elements. No unreported additional counsel claim is invented or adjudicated.

### Issue 4: Prosecutor’s remark and impeachment of the alibi witness — claims 7–8

* **Stone's Legal Position:** A comment asking the jury to draw guilt from failure to testify violates the trial right. Determine the remark’s contextual meaning before reaching harmlessness. A state evidentiary error in impeachment requires its own federal due-process analysis.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The prosecutor discussed an inconclusive handwriting comparison on a matchbook, acknowledged uncertainty whether it belonged to Lucas or the victim, and said Lucas alone would know. Stone recognizes that the final phrase can suggest the defendant has information; he reads the reported surrounding argument as explaining why ownership of the matches does not determine guilt, rather than asking the jury to treat silence as inculpatory proof. On that context he would reject the claimed direct or necessary indirect comment on failure to testify. He does not select the historical alternative that any error lacked a substantial and injurious effect, and does not assert that a violation would automatically be harmless. Revised Brecht and O’Neal retain their category-specific Chapman holdings; this no-error conclusion does not enlarge or evade them. A materially different argument transcript would call for renewed analysis.

The alibi witness Caulder was impeached with convictions too stale under state law, but their constitutional validity was not challenged. The jury also heard independent evidence from coworker Emery concerning falsified attendance and kickbacks, and Lucas’s own account of that practice. The stale convictions were not the only basis on which the jury could question the employment alibi. Stone therefore finds the described evidentiary mistake insufficient to make the trial fundamentally unfair. He distinguishes use of constitutionally invalid convictions from this stale-but-valid record and does not treat every state evidentiary mistake as a constitutional error requiring a harmlessness burden. The importance of the witness has been considered, not dismissed merely because other inculpatory evidence existed.

### Issue 5: February interview, earlier confessions, and waiver — claims 9 and 11–13

* **Stone's Legal Position:** Review the actual counsel choice and voluntariness independently. Revised Withrow prevents the State from defeating these claims merely by pointing to an earlier opportunity for review. Revised Davis requires neutral clarification of a reasonable possible present counsel request after waiver, even without an unequivocal demand. Clear invocation requires cessation under Edwards and Smith; later cooperation cannot undo a violated invocation.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The February 1984 interview concerned the work-record system after a protective order. The reported findings describe a subsequent agreement concerning attorney notice, the permitted subject of questioning, and Lucas’s waiver of counsel during the relevant interview. Stone accepts supported historical findings under former §2254 but independently assesses the constitutional consequence. Attorney agreement alone could not surrender Lucas’s own constitutional choice; the reported personal waiver and absence of an identified ignored request are therefore essential. Willingness to speak is not, standing alone, a substitute for a knowing and voluntary waiver. No contrary warning, invocation, or coercion facts defeating the reported waiver are established in the supplied presentation. A breach of the protective order alone, without a federal violation, would not warrant the writ.

The June 22 and July 31 statements concerning this case, and the July 28 statement concerning the Abilene matter, preceded formal adversarial proceedings for their respective offenses on the reported chronology. Representation on a different offense does not alone establish Sixth Amendment attachment here. The separate custodial counsel inquiry remains necessary: the state findings report neither a verbal request nor another indication of a wish for counsel before these statements, rather than merely the absence of an unequivocal request. Thus Stone identifies no reasonable possible present request left unclarified under the simulation’s Davis rule, and no clear request overridden in violation of Edwards. He does not use subsequent answers to erase an earlier request. The accepted interview/waiver record supplies no established constitutional ground for excluding these statements. Confession reliability as evidence, counsel protections, and police-coerced involuntariness remain distinct; the incentives and disputed truthfulness identified above do not by themselves prove the required coercion. No unpleaded coercion theory is conclusively adjudicated.

### Issue 6: Recorded handcuffs and references to other offenses — claims 10 and 14

* **Stone's Legal Position:** Teague requires comparison of the precise requested rule with law at finality, not the mere label of novelty. Evidentiary context and limiting instructions matter to the separate fairness inquiry.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Claim 10 seeks to extend the rule governing courtroom restraints to exclusion of a brief recorded interview showing handcuffs. At the reported 1990 finality, the existing protections concerning the accused’s continuing appearance before the trial jury did not dictate that categorical recording rule. A recording that discloses custody differs from keeping an accused visibly shackled throughout the proceedings. The request changes neither the conduct punishable nor the State’s substantive power to convict, and exclusion of that brief image is not the exceptional foundational procedural rule needed for the other then-existing Teague exception. Stone accordingly denies this particular retroactive extension. Revised Lambrix requires the actual comparison and does not make every application of an older principle new; Stone adopts no universal approval of prejudicial restraint images.

For claim 14, the defense’s selected portions of the July 31 statement created the misleading impression the responsive portions addressed. The challenged other-offense references were implicit, and the jury received an instruction against using other crimes to determine guilt. Stone weighs their corrective purpose, limited character, and instruction together and finds no demonstrated denial of fundamental fairness. “Opening the door” is not an unlimited license to introduce propensity evidence; the conclusion is confined to the reported use here.

### Issue 7: Allegedly withheld other-suspect evidence — claim 15

* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply revised Kyles to actual favorable evidence and its collective defense uses, including investigation and impeachment. Neither a prosecutor’s personal ignorance nor a conclusion that an alternative suspect is unconvincing automatically ends Brady analysis.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The state evidentiary hearing found no other credible suspect, and the reported presentation identifies neither contrary evidence overcoming the supported findings nor a former-§2254 exception making those findings unreliable. Stone gives those historical findings their lawful weight without treating “credible suspect” as an extra Brady element. More fundamentally, the supplied claim identifies no particular suppressed lead, statement, inconsistency, or investigating-team information whose lawful defense uses, assessed cumulatively with the alibi and confession challenges, establish material suppression. On that presentation, a completed Brady violation has not been shown. The conclusion is not that a lead must itself prove another person guilty. If suppressed favorable information were actually identified, its collective effect would require analysis despite the label given the suspect; once a material Brady violation was established, Kyles would forbid a second duplicative harmlessness hurdle. Stone therefore affirms on failure to establish the alleged violation, not an invented post-materiality test.

### Issue 8: Capital mitigation and Fourth Amendment exclusion — claims 16–17

* **Stone's Legal Position:** A live remedy must support review of the death-selection claim. Powell’s restriction on Fourth Amendment exclusion claims remains confined to its full-and-fair-opportunity setting, with Withrow preserving distinct trial protections.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Commutation has removed death as an available punishment in this case. No concrete remaining effect of the challenged capital-mitigation charge is established. Stone treats claim 16 as moot rather than approving the historical theory that any consideration under deliberateness or future dangerousness necessarily satisfies mitigation law. Revised Graham and Johnson remain intact, as does their actual treatment of retroactivity. The mootness disposition does not proclaim that the original instructions were lawful or prevent use of relevant mental-condition evidence in a properly available conviction claim.

Claim 17 seeks exclusion of the confessions as fruits of an arrest allegedly unsupported by probable cause. The supplied proceedings establish available state litigation of the arrest and confession issues and identify no concrete denial of a full and fair opportunity to present that Fourth Amendment theory. Powell therefore bars that exclusion-based habeas request. Stone does not decide that the arrest was lawful or extend the bar to the separately examined Miranda, counsel, coercion, and Brady claims. A demonstrated defect in the state opportunity would require a different Powell inquiry; none is invented here.
