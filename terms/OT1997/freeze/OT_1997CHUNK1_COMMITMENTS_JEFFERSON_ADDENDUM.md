## What Ritchie actually decides

Ritchie's Part II sustains review of Pennsylvania's finally resolved federal disclosure ruling despite an outstanding prejudice inquiry and possible new trial. Its main text invokes Cox's third category: favorable dispositions would leave the Commonwealth without its own occasion for review, while an acquittal following retrial would bar review. The claimed injury from disclosure of the confidential child-protection file would also occur before later correction could restore secrecy.

**Note 7 does additional legal work.** It addresses the dissent's proposed immediate appeal from a new-trial order. The majority does not rest on an absolute legal impossibility of later Supreme Court review: it acknowledges that state law-of-the-case treatment would not bar this Court. It nevertheless regards another round of state appeals over the already settled federal issue as serving no useful finality purpose on the unusual facts. That conclusion is part of the jurisdictional reasoning, not a citation that may be ignored merely because a conceivable later appeal exists.

The surrounding opinion bounds that reasoning. The majority stresses the actual prosecution outcomes, the disclosure injury that would occur irrespective of the ultimate disposition, and the fact that a trial and state appellate review had already occurred. It expressly says the confidentiality consideration is not dispositive by itself. Note 8 distinguishes ordinary discovery orders and explains why requiring defiance and contempt would needlessly repeat litigation in this posture. It does not create a general right to appeal every discovery order or every authoritatively resolved state-law-of-the-case question.

The holding therefore cannot fairly be restated either as requiring an absolute legal bar to every possible later appeal or as making futility of persuading the same state courts a freestanding exception to §1257. Ritchie permits a practical assessment in its identified setting, including the usefulness of delayed review and the risk that waiting will defeat the interest being reviewed. This account does not turn its contextual reasoning into a new mandatory multifactor test. It also does not declare confidentiality, completed trial, or any other single feature an indispensable prerequisite for every future practical-finality case.

Stevens's dissent, joined by Scalia among the present Associates, disputed the application rather than the existence of Cox's limited categories. It identified contempt and new-trial appeals, emphasized that law of the case does not preclude Supreme Court review, and argued that possible mootness and case-specific efficiency do not justify relaxing statutory finality. Page 78 specifically rejects using the expenditure of resources after a case has been heard as a jurisdictional reason. O'Connor joined the majority's Part II. Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer had no personal vote in Ritchie. The dissent supplies individual evidence but does not replace the binding holding.

## The two supported jurisdiction paths after Ritchie

**Immediate review through Ritchie's practical rationale.** Alabama has conclusively decided the federal damages issue, and the trial court is bound. If petitioners must proceed to judgment and repeat the same federal argument through the state appellate courts, law-of-the-case treatment may make that repetition fruitless. Their alleged federal death claim is deprived of compensatory recovery by the ruling; a later victory on another ground could remove the occasion for federal review. Ritchie note 7 means that simply pointing to a later appeal does not dispose of this argument. Immediate correction might avoid expenditure on litigation conducted under the disputed remedial rule.

The strongest version does not depend on pretending that the case already has a final judgment or that the City is liable. It contends that Ritchie's concern with useless repetition applies to a finally determined legal obstacle while the rest of the case continues. Nor can it be rejected merely because factual work remains: Ritchie itself required substantive prejudice review and potentially another trial. Finally, because Ritchie says confidentiality is not dispositive, its absence cannot alone resolve Jefferson.

**Dismissal after giving that rationale its full weight.** In Jefferson, no supplied order requires irreversible disclosure or comparable destruction of the contested federal interest while review is postponed. A legally erroneous restriction on damages can still receive effective correction through review of an appropriate adverse final judgment. That is a functional distinction, not just the nominal existence of an appellate route. The ordinary adjudication of disputed conduct, death, causation and the same parties' remaining claims can establish that the remedy issue need not determine their rights. That work has a substantive judicial purpose beyond reasking the state courts to change their settled legal answer.

This conclusion accounts for Ritchie's contrary treatment of its prejudice inquiry: there the majority also identified disclosure injury regardless of the ultimate prosecution outcome and explained why both another appeal and a contempt detour failed to serve finality's purposes after the completed trial and review. Here the supplied record establishes ordinary continuing civil litigation with later corrective relief, not that combination. Neither a universal first-trial rule nor a confidentiality-only rule follows. The materially different consequences of postponement, considered with the actual remaining proceedings, support different applications of the same practical doctrine.

The strongest objection is that Ritchie note 7's efficiency reasoning can be read independently of disclosure and therefore covers this already decided federal issue. That reading would make most binding state interlocutory rulings immediately reviewable whenever counsel could characterize later appellate presentation as futile. Ritchie's express reliance on its unusual circumstances and note 8's limits do not require that result. The original Cox applications remain relevant: Richards required actual practical completion of the reviewed parties' claims; Sullivan required the conviction issue to survive every lawful sentencing outcome; Labron/Kilgore involved the State's actual loss of effective later review; Casarotto protected a forum interest threatened by the very litigation to come. None expressly overrules Ritchie or converts its reasons into a general law-of-the-case exception.

**Selected treatment.** All eight Associates would preserve and distinguish Ritchie, including note 7, and reject immediate review on Jefferson's supplied posture. The original handoff's statement that no categorical later-review barrier is established remains a true record observation but is not independently dispositive. It must be coupled with the assessment of effective later correction, meaningful continuing proceedings, and the absence of a demonstrated loss of the contested federal interest through waiting. There is no holding that every civil damages dispute must await judgment or that only confidential-information disputes can qualify for Ritchie's practical treatment.

No subsequent simulated authority is claimed to have erased or overruled Ritchie. The cited public decisions retain their actual dated applications: Sullivan, June 1, 1993; Casarotto, May 20, 1996; Richards, June 10, 1996; and Labron/Kilgore, July 1, 1996. Their limits help test the proposed extension but do not silently supply a contrary holding about Ritchie's facts.
