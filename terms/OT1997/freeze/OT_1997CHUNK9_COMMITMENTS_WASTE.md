## Reach and preserved distinctions

All eight Associates reach only the two Commerce Clause injunction components: compulsory routing and the outside-system disposal fee. The Sixth Circuit exercised §1292(a)(1) jurisdiction; §1254(1) permits this review. The passenger-car/pickup takings and ripeness remand is outside the grant and remains untouched. No public/private operator economics, contract terms, profit distribution or risk transfer is inferred.

Metro owns the facility; NTTC operates it. Residential solid waste must be delivered there unless the Director specifically authorizes another destination in writing. Nonresidential waste is required only to the extent allocated to meet the plant's operating need, reported as 6,300 tons weekly, with monthly reports, at-least-quarterly pro rata calculations from best available data, and advance notices to NTTC and collectors. The fee applies to covered Metro-generated/collected waste disposed at a non-Metro facility inside Metro or to waste collected in Metro and disposed outside its boundaries; NTTC and other Metro-owned facilities are exempt. Its rate is $2 per cubic yard or $8 per ton. The narrative's $36 NTTC tipping price must be read with the enacted $44 rate effective January 1, 1995; no calculation or outcome here assumes one verified uniform competitive price.

The alternatives reported below—competitive tipping prices, increased direct or contracted collection with needed preparation and legislation, and a nondiscriminatory generator/container tax—are real alternatives on this record, not costless substitutes or judicial prescriptions. The absence of evidence identifying an equivalent intrastate burden for the outside-system fee is accepted as the record's deficiency, not a finding that Metro bears no public costs.

## Operative alternatives

**D: Discriminatory reservation of compulsory private demand.** The local government's present title changes the beneficiary but not the relevant transaction: independent generators and haulers must purchase the selected local service, or pay an added charge when using a competing service. A government may buy disposal and may own a plant, but neither status alone exempts its coercive reservation of others' private purchases. The public-system reservation in Carbone and SSC makes this an express resolution of an open category, not mechanical application of an already decided public-ownership holding. Under this path both measures discriminate in disposal services even though other private local facilities lose business too.

For D, Metro's environmental, reliable-fuel and statutory waste-reduction purposes are legitimate, but the record does not establish that reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives cannot adequately serve them. A need for dependable revenue does not itself justify excluding competitors. No perfect-certainty burden or duty to defeat every imaginable alternative is imposed. The separate fee also fails the compensatory-tax defense: Oregon Waste requires an identified intrastate burden, rough approximation without excess, and substantially equivalent taxable events; the first predicate is missing, and revenue need does not supply the rest. The result affirms the two reviewed injunction rulings, preserving ordinary licensing, independent valid regulation and actual government purchases.

**P: Genuine public provision receives incidental-burden review.** Present municipal ownership is a material public-system distinction explicitly reserved by Carbone and SSC. Preference for Metro's own public facility over all independent processors, local and interstate, need not be classified as geographic commercial protection of a private business. Coercion prevents treating the scheme as exempt market participation, but it does not necessarily determine discrimination. The route and fee instead require separate Pike review of their actual interstate burdens and public benefits. The fee's exemption follows the public system; its compensation deficiency defeats a discriminatory-tax defense if that framework applies, but cannot itself decide the antecedent classification.

For P, the lower court's discrimination premise prevented the necessary separate public-system burden inquiry. Vacate the two reviewed Commerce Clause injunction rulings and remand for that inquiry; do not decree either measure valid. The interests in predictable public service and fuel, the interstate alternatives excluded, conditional allocation, competitive cost evidence and lawful less restrictive arrangements must be assessed on the supported record. No exact quantitative burden, new cost-equivalence fact or inevitability of victory is inferred. No mandatory evidentiary hearing or trial follows.

**Adversarial conclusion for D:** Its principal objection is that it could erase Carbone's public-system reservation and constitutionalize all municipal service finance. The adopted limit answers that objection: the reservation is now resolved for the particular command and alternative-service penalty imposed on independent private transactions; actual public purchases, direct public provision outside those coercive transactions and ordinary public pricing remain distinct. Five Associates accept D at this common level of generality.

**Adversarial conclusion for P:** Its principal objection is that the same sovereign mechanism reserves the same local processing demand, regardless of title, and neutral alternatives exist. P does not deny coercion or alternatives. It treats a publicly owned system as a different classification and requires actual scrutiny of the burdens before maintaining constitutional injunctions. O'Connor, Souter and Breyer accept P; none adopts categorical proprietary immunity or automatic approval of either measure. Their separate-position function is to preserve the classification disagreement and component-specific remand, not to add an alternative controlling holding.
