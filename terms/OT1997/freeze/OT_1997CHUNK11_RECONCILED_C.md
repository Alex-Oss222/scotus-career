## 1. United States v. Bajakajian — June 22, 1998

### Historical comparison and existing-law screen

The verified historical judgment affirmed, 5–4. Thomas wrote for Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer; Kennedy dissented with Rehnquist, O'Connor, and Scalia. The eight historical Associate dispositions match the independent commitments. The complete Court opinion, its nineteen footnotes, and Kennedy's complete dissent were read. The comparator's broad description of historical property forfeitures cannot be detached from notes 4 and 6, which preserve coverage of modern forfeitures punitive even in part. Kennedy expressly accepts gross disproportionality and substantial legislative deference as the standard, while disputing the application and the historical classifications.

The actual Austin holdings already reject an exemption based on civil or in-rem form for the forfeitures they address, preserve partly punitive coverage, and leave more than instrumentality relevant. Alexander requires a separate fines inquiry for criminal forfeiture but supplies no preexisting complete proportionality formula. Ursery and Hudson preserve the different Double Jeopardy inquiry and do not turn a cost comparison into the Excessive Fines test. Those premises do not displace any Associate's historical answer to this direct criminal forfeiture. The historical references to Ursery's separate opinions must not replace the actual simulated joins or holdings.

No adjudication here may treat the property as unlawful proceeds, manufacture a money-laundering finding, convert dismissal of the section 1001 count into acquittal, or describe $5,000 as the statutory maximum. The findings establish lawful source and debt payment and no other-crime connection; willful reporting failure remains the offense. The $250,000/five-year statutory maxima and $5,000/six-month applicable Guidelines maxima perform different work.

### Firm propositions and remedies

**Coverage and standard.** All eight accept that section 982(a)(1)'s forfeiture imposed on this convicted offender as a criminal sanction is a fine. A punitive forfeiture is unconstitutional when grossly disproportionate to the gravity of the offense for which it is imposed. Substantial respect for legislative punishment judgments and the imprecision of gravity assessments rule out strict proportionality. Neither statutory property connection nor the label instrumentality settles the constitutional comparison. Instrumentality is not a categorical prerequisite making all currency-reporting forfeiture impossible.

**Review.** The constitutional proportionality application receives independent appellate review; supported underlying factual findings receive clear-error review. This distinction does not authorize fresh adverse findings or a universal standard for every mixed question. The historical Court's express review rule confirms the frozen distinction between factual review and independent legal application; the opposing Associates' acceptance of the legal standard and disagreement over application supplies no contrary review rule.

**Affirmance ground.** Stevens, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer support the coverage rule, demanding proportionality standard, and conclusion that the full $357,144 is grossly disproportionate on these findings. The offense causes a reporting-information loss, not the established proceeds or revenue loss of another crime. The lawful source and purpose, lack of another crime, applicable sentencing assessment, and mismatch between the whole fund and this offense's gravity support the conclusion together. No feature creates an automatic exemption, mandatory checklist, numerical ratio, or compensation ceiling. The broader statutory maximum and reporting system's importance are acknowledged but do not conclusively establish proportionality.

**Contrary application.** O'Connor, Scalia, and Kennedy accept punitive coverage and gross disproportionality but would permit full statutory forfeiture. The substantial concealed amount measures the scale of the deliberately unreported transaction; Congress may punish the reporting wrong seriously without proving a separate offense. The authorized imprisonment and fine, mandatory property forfeiture, and reporting system's preventive function make the full exaction constitutionally permissible in their view. The Guidelines fine supplements rather than silently displaces the forfeiture authorization. They reject a classification that would exclude a fine merely because it also serves a remedial purpose.

**Remedy and reservations.** Affirmance rejects the Government's demand for the full amount and leaves the existing judgment in place. It does not decide that $15,000 is constitutional, that $15,001 is excessive, or that a court may disregard the mandatory statute by selecting a different sum: respondent did not cross-appeal. Conviction, probation, and ordinary fine are not reopened. The opposite disposition would reverse the refusal of full forfeiture, not reopen those matters. Wealth, income, and deprivation of livelihood were not supported as issues here and remain undecided. No comprehensive innocent-owner or every-in-rem-forfeiture rule is adopted.

## 2. Pennsylvania Board of Probation and Parole v. Scott — June 22, 1998

### Historical comparison and existing-law screen

The verified historical Court reversed and remanded, 5–4. Thomas wrote for Rehnquist, O'Connor, Scalia, and Kennedy. Stevens dissented separately; Souter dissented with Ginsburg and Breyer. ### Firm propositions and remedies

**Exclusion group.** Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer would require exclusion where parole officers search their known supervisee in violation of the Fourth Amendment and the parole authority seeks to use those fruits to revoke parole. The agency's own expected use supplies a direct deterrent target. Criminal-trial exclusion does not necessarily remove the benefit when revocation is the practical proceeding used to return the person to custody. The genuine loss of reliable evidence and administrative burden are insufficient to withhold the remedy in this supported setting. This rule does not require all criminal-trial procedure or decide unrelated-officer and unknown-status situations.

**No-extension group.** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas would reject a federal exclusion requirement in the ordinary parole-revocation setting presented, including searches by parole officers who know the person's status. Exclusion is a distinct remedy, and the conditional-release system's need for reliable evidence and flexible administration outweighs the additional deterrence that this extension would supply. The institutional connection is acknowledged, not denied. Their ground does not depend on a finding that parole officers never investigate crime, that the search was lawful, or that alternative remedies were proved effective. Exceptional abuse not established in this record is not independently adjudicated.

**Stevens's qualification.** His assent to the institutional-deterrence explanation does not adopt an assertion that the exclusionary remedy is wholly detached from constitutional necessity. His historical separate writing and date-eligible Leon/Segura positions support a constitutional remedy needed to make the Fourth Amendment effective. He can join the particular exclusion holding and its deterrence explanation while preserving that additional basis. Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer support the case-specific remedy through deterrence analysis; their votes do not adopt Stevens's constitutional formulation. A final opinion must not collapse those grounds.

**Remedy.** Affirmance preserves the reviewed federal suppression and the reversal of revocation on that ground; it enters no new release order. Reversal and remand removes that federal exclusion requirement but does not enter a new revocation, decide search legality, guarantee admissibility under every other source, or extinguish properly available state law. No prior procedural or hearsay ruling outside the reviewed federal question is silently reversed.

## 3. Wisconsin Department of Corrections v. Schacht — June 22, 1998

### Historical comparison and existing-law screen

### Firm propositions and remedy

All eight join the following complete ground. An action containing substantial federal claims within section 1331 is removable under section 1441(a) despite an Eleventh Amendment bar applicable to particular State or official-capacity claims. Assertion of that bar prevents adjudication of the barred claim; it does not eliminate otherwise valid original/removal jurisdiction over the remaining federal claims. Unlike absence of complete diversity, the State's immunity is capable of waiver and does not automatically extinguish the federal jurisdictional basis for every claim. These features do not establish that immunity is identical to ordinary personal jurisdiction in every respect.

Section 1447(c) does not require remand of the entire action simply because a particular claim is barred. Even assuming immunity is subject-matter jurisdiction for this analysis, the statute's reference to lack of jurisdiction over the case supplies no rule erasing the remaining federal claims. Its distinction between a timely nonjurisdictional-defect motion and subject-matter remand does not create a new removal defect or a new timeliness finding here. The question whether immunity is properly classified as subject-matter jurisdiction remains undecided.

The complaint actually alleges federal claims. Supplemental state-law claims furnish an analogy from City of Chicago, not a new factual component of Schacht's action. The dismissal of the State and official-capacity claims was not appealed. Vacatur removes the appellate whole-action remand and returns the personal-capacity appeal to the Seventh Circuit; personal-capacity liability, summary-judgment merits, waiver by removal, and new relief against Wisconsin remain undecided.

## 4. Pearson v. Hines — June 22, 1998

### Comparator and actual-law screen

### Firm common ground and remedy

All eight Associates join the following bounded propositions and affirmance. A correctional officer may not knowingly fabricate a disciplinary accusation in retaliation for a prisoner's protected filing or threatened filing of a grievance where that accusation causes the concrete disciplinary consequences established here. Protected conduct and its substantial or motivating causal role require ordinary proof; the officer may prove by a preponderance that the same action would have occurred without that protected conduct. A conceivable lawful ground is not that proof. Valid prison restrictions and actual nonretaliatory discipline remain available.

Hill's some-evidence review of a disciplinary adjudication does not immunize the accusing officer's knowingly false retaliatory initiation. The established confinement and loss of television supply concrete adverse consequences; the claim does not additionally require a deprivation independently sufficient under due process, actual abandonment of grievance activity, or loss of a nonfrivolous court case. This rule does not decide whether every adverse comment, trivial inconvenience, consequence-free allegation, or subjective offense is actionable.

Ordinary review of the jury verdict governs the sufficiency question. Evidence of Hines's many grievances and reputation, his statement that morning that he would file a grievance, and Pearson's failure to challenge the jury's falsity finding support the verdict without a new credibility finding or heightened motive quantum. The conduct occurred January 19, 1992. Qualified immunity is not presented; present law is not converted into conduct-time notice. Pearson's $100 compensatory and $1,000 punitive judgment remains in the reviewed respect. No new damages, injunction, release, expungement, credit restoration, or disposition against another officer follows.

## 5. Swidler & Berlin v. United States — June 25, 1998

### Full comparator and changed-premise inquiry

The verified historical judgment reversed, 6–3. Rehnquist wrote for Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer. O'Connor dissented with **Scalia and Thomas**. Both the complete Cornell syllabus and dissent identify those joins, independently cross-checked against the official Reports. Ginsburg did not join the dissent. The entire Court opinion, all four footnotes, and O'Connor's entire dissent were read.

The historical majority preserves ordinary post-death attorney-client privilege under Rule 501, distinguishes testamentary disclosure serving the client's intention, and rejects later criminal usefulness as a general balancing exception.
