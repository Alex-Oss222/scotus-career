**F1 preserves review of actual applications without deciding every future case.** The five Justices reject facial invalidation on the limited statutory construction and selective arts context. ## National Endowment for the Arts v. Finley

### Reachability, text, and alternative paths

All eight Associates accept review of the surviving facial First/Fifth Amendment challenge. NAAO's representative controversy concerning member organizations remains live on the supplied record; subsequent grants to two artists and restrictions on some individual grants do not establish that every affected member organization is ineligible or that the entire dispute disappeared. This conclusion neither adjudicates all 1998 appropriations exceptions nor gives settled individual claims new life. No prior grant denial is attributed to a statutory provision enacted afterward.

Section 954(d)(1) requires the Chairperson's regulations and procedures to use artistic excellence and merit as criteria, taking account of general decency and respect for diverse public beliefs and values. Taking account is mandatory; the statute specifies neither an automatic disqualification nor a fixed weight. It cannot be read as requiring no consideration at all. Panel diversity and an agency resolution are not a binding construction eliminating the statutory duty. Section 954(d)(2)'s obscenity prohibition is separate and is not substituted for the contested clause.

**F1 — Narrow facial-validity ground.** In this competitively selective arts program, the statutory considerations can operate as contextual aspects of artistic evaluation without establishing a categorical viewpoint exclusion, a penalty for expression outside the program, or an automatic denial whenever an official finds offense. Scarcity and artistic selection permit judgments about content and suitability within genuine program purposes, but are not a universal immunity from viewpoint constraints. On that construction the facial challenge does not establish invalidity; an actual discriminatory application remains separately reviewable. The construction preserves meaningful consideration while refusing to add a mandatory exclusion absent from the text.

**F2 — Vagueness.** Indeterminate artistic judgment is inherent in this selective grant scheme. The considerations, read with artistic excellence/merit and the program's actual selection function, do not impose a criminal prohibition, licensing requirement, or objective entitlement to a grant that demands the specificity of a coercive speech rule. This limited setting defeats facial vagueness invalidation. It does not make vague grant standards universally valid or excuse a discriminatory use, and does not declare all artists' speech unaffected in practice.

**F3 — Broader subsidy ground.** Scalia and Thomas would sustain these discretionary appropriations as the Government's selection of what art to subsidize, including its own judgment about decency and respect, without treating every refusal as unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination. An applicant remains free to produce the work without this subsidy. Their position distinguishes Rosenberger's generally available private-publication benefit and Forbes's forum for competing candidates, and does not authorize conditions controlling independently financed speech, punishing dissent, or transferring all forum law into government subsidy selection. Their broader ground need not join F1's full reservation of viewpoint-neutral selection as an across-the-program rule.

**F4 — Facial-invalidity ground.** Souter would affirm the injunction against the challenged decency/respect criterion because it directs public decisionmakers to judge private expression against public beliefs and values and therefore makes offensive viewpoints an adverse consideration. A requirement to take that assessment into account cannot be saved by calling it only advisory, and panel diversity supplies no limiting rule. A public program supporting varied private arts does not become the Government's own message. Its selective nature permits genuine artistic judgment, not a mandatory direction to assess conformity to public sentiment. His disposition rests on the First Amendment ground; no separate Fifth Amendment vagueness holding is necessary to affirm the actual relief.
