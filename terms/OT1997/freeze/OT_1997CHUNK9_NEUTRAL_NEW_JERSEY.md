## 1. Channel, questions, participation and requested relief

This is an Article III original controversy between States under 28 U.S.C. §1251(a). There is no lower appellate judgment to affirm or reverse. New Jersey invoked original jurisdiction in 1993. A twenty-three-day trial before the Master ran from July 10 through August 15, 1996, producing more than 4,000 transcript pages and almost 2,000 documents. Both States filed exceptions to the Master's March 31, 1997 report and May 30 supplement. The current Court consists of Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; no event-specific recusal or nonparticipation is supplied. With nine participants, five support the judgment; no ordinary appellate equal-division affirmance is available in this original action.

The separable questions are:

1. Whether Articles First–Third of the 1834 Compact place the artificial additions to Ellis Island within New Jersey's sovereignty, or Article Second's reservation of New York's island jurisdiction embraces its subsequent enlargement.
2. If the Compact otherwise favors New Jersey, whether New York acquired the fill by prescription and New Jersey's acquiescence.
3. Whether laches can independently bar this boundary claim and, if available, whether New York proved the required lack of diligence and prejudice.
4. Whether New York's retained original-island territory extends to the 1834 mean high-water or mean low-water line.
5. Whether the evidence justifies including the historic pier's footprint as land in 1834, and whether the Master's additional 0.2-acre allowance is supported.
6. Whether the Court may substitute the Master's practical 5.1-acre configuration for the established historical boundary, and what survey/decree work follows the resolution of these exceptions.

New Jersey seeks a declaration that the original island to its former mean high-water line belongs to New York and the artificial additions belong to New Jersey, and an injunction against New York enforcing laws or asserting sovereignty on the New Jersey portions. Its proposed 1857-survey contour, excluding the pier, gives New York approximately 2.74 acres. New York seeks a declaration of sovereignty over the entire island. New Jersey accepts New York's sovereignty over the original island; federal title and jurisdiction remain distinct. No party's acceptance of survey accuracy, compliance with the Master's survey directions, or alternative preference within his plan is consent to the opposing merits position.

## 3. Compact text and uncontested physical setting

The agreement was executed September 16, 1833 and received congressional consent June 28, 1834. Its first three articles provide:

> **Article First.** The boundary line between the two states of New York and New Jersey, from a point in the middle of Hudson river, opposite the point on the west shore thereof, in the forty-first degree of north latitude, as heretofore ascertained and marked, to the main sea, shall be the middle of the said river, of the Bay of New York, of the waters between Staten Island and New Jersey, and of Raritan Bay, to the main sea; except as hereinafter otherwise particularly mentioned.
>
> **Article Second.** The state of New York shall retain its present jurisdiction of and over Bedlow's and Ellis's islands; and shall also retain exclusive jurisdiction of and over the other islands lying in the waters above mentioned and now under the jurisdiction of that state.
>
> **Article Third.** The state of New York shall have and enjoy exclusive jurisdiction of and over all the waters of the bay of New York; and of and over all the waters of Hudson river lying west of Manhattan Island and to the south of the mouth of Spuytenduyvel creek; and of and over the lands covered by the said waters to the low water-mark on the westerly or New Jersey side thereof; subject to the following rights of property and of jurisdiction of the state of New Jersey, that is to say:
>
> 1. The state of New Jersey shall have the exclusive right of property in and to the land under water lying west of the middle of the bay of New York, and west of the middle of that part of the Hudson river which lies between Manhattan island and New Jersey.
> 2. The state of New Jersey shall have the exclusive jurisdiction of and over the wharves, docks, and improvements, made and to be made on the shore of the said state; and of and over all vessels aground on said shore, or fastened to any such wharf or dock; except that the said vessels shall be subject to the quarantine or health laws, and laws in relation to passengers, of the state of New York, which now exist or which may hereafter be passed.
> 3. The state of New Jersey shall have the exclusive right of regulating the fisheries on the westerly side of the middle of the said waters, Provided, That the navigation be not obstructed or hindered.

Article Fourth gives New York specified quarantine and passenger-law jurisdiction over the Kill Van Kull to Shooter's Island and, for vessels bound to New York ports, the sound to Woodbridge Creek. Article Fifth gives New Jersey jurisdiction over the specified southern sound and western Raritan Bay, subject to New York's underwater property, Staten Island wharves/improvements and aground/fastened-vessel jurisdiction, with New Jersey's quarantine/health/passenger-law exception, and New York fisheries rights conditioned on unobstructed navigation. Articles Sixth and Seventh separately permit the two States' identified criminal and civil process on the other's jurisdictional waters, except for persons or property on vessels aground or fastened to the other shore or wharf, or already under that State's arrest or seizure. These are particular jurisdictional allocations, not interchangeable exceptions. Article Eighth conditions binding effect on both legislatures' confirmation and Congress's approval. Congressional consent includes: "nothing therein contained shall be construed to impair or in any manner affect, any right of jurisdiction of the United States in and over the islands or waters which form the subject of the said agreement."

Ellis Island lies west of the Article First midwater line. Its original fast land was approximately three acres. The United States held the original island under New York's 1808 conveyance, following New York's 1800 jurisdictional cession with a reservation for service of process. Beginning about 1890, federal works enlarged the island by approximately 24.5 acres, principally for immigration facilities. The additions were artificial fill, a formation history the report treats as undisputed; New York's central answer is that the Compact displaced the ordinary boundary consequence, not that the substantial additions were natural gradual accretion. The present island was approximately 27.5 acres; the 1995 Marchuk survey measured 27.40 acres. High-water fast-land acreage is not the same quantity as acreage measured to mean low water. (Report 3–9, 90–99, 159–162.)

The 1904 New Jersey deed transferred underwater property to the United States, expressly preserving all United States rights and privileges as if no grant had been accepted (NJ Exceptions, Appendix D,9a–12a). The reported trial stipulation identified .5672 acres outside the grant along the northeast seawall, with additional narrowly described western seawall encroachment. The Master associated the approximately .57 acres with retained New Jersey ownership. This case need not transform that finding into a comprehensive property-title decree. Ownership, federal enclave authority, residual state jurisdiction and interstate sovereignty must be treated separately.

## 4. Law entering each issue

**Original forum and review.** The entering Holdings entry *Mississippi v. Louisiana*, December 14, 1992, and Standards and Tests **Exclusive interstate jurisdiction and private boundary-related claims**, **Present operation effective: 1992-12-14**, reserve an actual interstate controversy to this Court under §1251(a). An otherwise lawful private-title adjudication can locate a boundary incidentally without binding the States on sovereignty. Accordingly, *Collins v. Promark Products*, 956 F.2d 383 (2d Cir. 1992), supplies a lower-court interpretation and context for this litigation, not a binding sovereign boundary. *Louisiana v. Mississippi*, October 31 and December 4, 1995, requires independent original-action review, appropriate evidentiary weight for the Master's technical work, and an authenticated, internally consistent decree. Rule 52 appellate deference does not bind this Court to the Master. That case preserved an established island boundary despite a navigation-channel shift, but did not settle new islands, artificial enlargement of this enclave, prescription, or a universal island definition.

**Compact construction.** *Central Railroad Co. of New Jersey v. Jersey City*, 209 U.S. 473, 478–479 (1908), construes Article First as a sovereignty boundary and Article Third's New York authority as serving commerce and navigation rather than restoring all sovereignty to New York. The Court's construction remains precedent; the States' absence as parties to that tax case means it is not an earlier binding adjudication of this island boundary. The disputed reach of Article Second remains to be resolved. *Texas v. New Mexico*, 462 U.S. 554, 564 (1983), forbids relief inconsistent with the express terms of a constitutionally valid congressionally approved compact. *Cuyler v. Adams*, 449 U.S. 433 (1981), supplies its federal-law character; *Oklahoma v. New Mexico*, 501 U.S. 221, 234 n. 5 (1991), permits relevant negotiation history where ambiguity warrants it. All precede divergence.

The current *Kansas v. Colorado*, May 15, 1995, unanimously enforces the actual compact obligation and distinguishes its substance from remedial flexibility; neither state practice nor preferred policy creates a different bargain. Its liability determination does not choose a general proof quantum for every compact-enforcement factual dispute. Current *Hess*, November 14, 1994, and Standards and Tests **State immunity of bistate compact entities**, **Present operation effective: 1994-11-14**, examine an entity's actual compact, legal, financial and institutional relationship. They do not decide territorial boundaries or establish that a compact may be rewritten for convenience. *Hess*'s controlling author is Stevens, joined by Stone-Zsela, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; do not substitute the historical writing. Current *Alaska v. Native Village of Venetie*, February 25, 1998, unanimously distinguishes political status, title and statutory territorial predicates; its §1151(b) requirements do not become a test for Ellis Island.

**Formation and prescription.** *Georgia v. South Carolina*, 497 U.S. 376, Part IV, reads its own island reservation with the treaty's enduring-boundary terms and rejects a newly shifting boundary for later-emerging islands; it does not construe this different Compact. Part VII distinguishes gradual accretion/erosion from avulsion and treats the particular mixture of federal dredging, sedimentation and filling as predominantly avulsive on a close record. Federal activity alone is not a universal classification. Part III establishes prescription through sufficiently long possession and dominion with acquiescence, considering concrete sovereign acts and notice. A later protest does not undo sovereignty already acquired, but whether acquisition occurred first must be proved.

*Illinois v. Kentucky*, 500 U.S. 380, 384–389 (1991), places on the claiming State the preponderance burden for long, continuous possession and assertion of sovereignty over the disputed territory, followed by proof of the other's long acquiescence. Acts within an undisputed area do not establish control over a disputed adjacent sliver. Officers' statements as well as conduct matter. Its boundary discussion rejects an independent laches defense, explaining that prescription and acquiescence implement the relevant repose policy. It also separates entitlement from later precise geographic determination. Souter wrote for a unanimous Court; Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia and Kennedy participated and joined. Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer did not participate.

**Laches.** Current *Kansas v. Colorado*, May 15, 1995, unanimously assumes possible laches availability in compact enforcement and rejects it for failure to establish inexcusable lack of diligence; categorical availability remains open and no independent prejudice holding follows from that case. Thus *Illinois*'s boundary rule cannot be recast as an absolute exemption for every compact dispute, and *Kansas* cannot be recast as having authorized laches here. If the Court reaches the proposed independent defense, both lack of diligence and prejudice require their own support.

## 5. Compact-construction alternatives and their record support

**Original island plus New Jersey fill.** Article First, *Central Railroad*, New Jersey's underwater sovereignty and the retention of the existing island jurisdiction can be read to leave artificial additions on New Jersey's side. The ordinary avulsion principle then prevents enlargement of New York sovereignty by federal filling. The Master adopted this route, while treating "present" principally as recognizing preexisting federal jurisdiction rather than relying on that adjective alone as a precise geographic limitation. He found the Compact silent about subsequent landfill. Report 53–99.

New Jersey separately maintains that Article Second's present-tense retention itself limits the reservation geographically to the 1834 island. Its brief expressly distinguishes its interpretation from the Master's federal-enclave explanation (NJ Exceptions 9–11, especially10 n. 5; Report 60–63). A judgment for NJ fill could adopt either interpretation, but a common result does not establish a common compact rationale.

The strongest objection is New York's Article Second argument: a named island may be reserved as an enduring geographic entity without a fixed acreage; "present" can describe the quality of retained jurisdiction after the federal cession. The Compact expressly contemplates improvements elsewhere and contains no metes-and-bounds restriction here. The fact that the fill was artificial does not answer whether the Compact displaced the default rule.

**Continuing island reservation.** New York argues that retaining island jurisdiction without a size limitation encompasses foreseeable enlargement and serves the Compact's commerce, health and navigation settlement. It adduces the Dongan and Montgomerie charters' filling powers, historic Manhattan reclamation and New Jersey waterfront development; *City of Hoboken v. Pennsylvania Railroad*, 124 U.S. 656 (1888), and *Bell v. Gough*, 23 N.J.L. 624 (1852), document the latter context. New York disputes the Master's finding that evidence of accepted filling at the time was too ambiguous, and argues that contractual good faith and practical limits answer hypothetical unlimited expansion. NY Exceptions 11–21; Report 91–99.

The strongest objection is that foreseeable private or municipal filling does not establish an agreement to transfer the other State's sovereign territory. Article Third's jurisdictional allocation remains distinct from territory, and a court cannot infer that New York's conceded original enclave expands whenever federal land is attached. The reported 1827 proposals, 1889 interstate map and 1921 Port Authority materials support different inferences but contain no express award of later Ellis fill. The 1921 agreement preserves the earlier boundary/jurisdiction except as specifically modified; its silence about Ellis Island is not an express settlement in either State's favor.

**One island or several.** The Master found three physically distinct islands for a period, relying on the prelitigation NPS history, gangways on pilings and a 1901 photograph, including Unrau's references to a new hospital island and Squires's concession that a pile breakwater is not fill. Report 94–97. New York answers that submerged cribbing connected Island No. 2 to the original tract and the photograph was taken at high tide; Island No. 3 was always connected by a gangway, and contemporary descriptions treated both as additions to Ellis Island. NY Exceptions 18–20, T2963–2985, T3065, T3110, NY952. Physical connection by a bridge is distinct from continuous dry land. New York alternatively invokes *Michigan v. Wisconsin*, 270 U.S. 295, 313–314 (1926), on possession of part of a described tract under color of title. That analogy requires deciding whether this Compact supplies color of title to the enlarged tract; naming a later formation Ellis Island cannot itself decide that question. Neither the number-of-islands dispute nor ordinary wharfing rights necessarily decides the whole case.

## 6. Prescription, notice and acquiescence: evidence for both paths

The relevant claim concerns **the artificial additions**, not New York's conceded original island. The Master concentrated on 1890–1955; New York seeks an acquisition complete by 1954, before the later public controversy. Federal ownership reduces the opportunities for ordinary local government but, on the Master's approach, is not a categorical bar to prescription: *Arkansas v. Tennessee*, 310 U.S. 563, 568–571 (1940), and *New Jersey v. Delaware*, 291 U.S. 361, 377 (1934), supply counterexamples. New Jersey presses the further argument that New York's particular cession left it too little governmental authority to prescribe (NJ Exceptions 14 n. 8, citing *United States v. Unzeuta*, 281 U.S.138,142 (1930)); the Master's narrower treatment does not resolve that exception by assertion. Conversely, federal adoption of a standard is not automatically an act by the State or proof of exclusive sovereign control. The strongest objection is that the claimed acts were intermittent, federally chosen or referable to the original enclave, while conflicting official records and NJ conduct negate the necessary acquiescence. A prescriptive period cannot be assembled merely by counting unrelated acts.

**Path rejecting prescription:** Find that NY has not proved the requisite possession/dominion over the specific fill and NJ's long acquiescence, considering federal control, mixed descriptions, original-island ambiguity, the deed, permits and contrary assertions. *Illinois* supplies the need to connect activity to the disputed area; *Georgia* supplies the notice inquiry and makes silence meaningful in context. The strongest objection is that this approach may discount documented fill-resident elections and hospital acts, impose more governmental activity than a federal enclave permits, or treat unknown federal/union conduct as an NJ sovereign protest. It must answer those objections specifically. Neither direct formal notice from NJ nor filing an original lawsuit is automatically the sole means of nonacquiescence. No fixed universal number of years is supplied.

## 7. Laches as a distinct component

The Master initially declined to add the pleaded defense but admitted the prejudice evidence and preserved the issue for the Court. His final analysis cites the general interstate-boundary rule, assumes possible availability for this compact setting, and finds no established prejudice; he also reasons that prescription addresses the controversy. Report 27–28,103–105. Do not reduce that treatment to a finding that NY introduced no evidence or a categorical application of the current *Kansas* holding.

New York's distinct theory is unreasonable litigation delay causing loss of evidence even if NJ maintained verbal disagreement, whereas prescription requires acquiescence in actual dominion. It argues coequal States have competing public interests and invokes *Rhode Island v. Massachusetts*, 40 U.S. (15 Pet.) 233,272–274 (1841), *Guaranty Trust*, 304 U.S.126 (1938), and *Patterson v. Hewitt*, 195 U.S.309 (1904). NY Exceptions 40–45. These arguments require confronting *Illinois* and the limited current *Kansas* reservation; they do not automatically displace either.

For prejudice, NY cites records abandoned after 1954, loss of utilities/guards, vandalism, leaking roofs, historical papers still scattered in 1978, expired tax-retention periods, deaths of adult immigration-era witnesses, and inability to question officials whose acts NJ relies upon. It argues those circumstances explain sparse surviving records and impair both affirmative proof and rebuttal. NY Exceptions 46–50, T1957–1959,2478, NY74, NJ162. The Master finds the inference that lost material would establish additional sovereign conduct or defeat contrary acts speculative. Report 104–105. No identified missing document's contents or deceased witness's testimony may be invented; the loss circumstances are not proof of those contents. Available paths include applying *Illinois*'s boundary treatment, assuming possible laches but finding an element unproved, or recognizing an independently justified compact defense and deciding both elements on the actual evidence. A grant of laches relief must specify its consequence; it cannot silently become proof of each prescriptive element.

## 8. Historical shore, pier and lawful implementation

**High versus low water.** NJ relies on Article Third's underwater-land allocation, its sovereign tideland ownership to mean high water, Article Second's retention of the 1834 island, the approximately 2.97-acre 1808 deed and the 1857 Coast Survey. NY and the Master invoke the earlier 1827 proposal reserving islands to low water, *Handly's Lessee v. Anthony*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.)374 (1820), *Vermont v. New Hampshire*, 289 U.S.593 (1933), and access to navigable water. The Master regards low water as the more plausible original intent, while acknowledging uncertainty and that the express 1827 phrase did not appear in the final Article Second. Report 151–155. The high-water path must distinguish interstate territorial boundaries from ordinary ownership rules; the low-water path must explain why an omitted negotiation term and nonidentical river precedents govern this tidal island. Convenience can inform interpretation of an uncertain term without itself authorizing replacement of an established line.

NJ Exceptions 28–38 supplies the principal contrary grounds: the 1827 offer was rejected and further litigation/negotiations followed; Article Third expressly uses low-water language that Article Second omits; its 1829 complaint described NY possession as fast land, without necessarily conceding acquired sovereignty; the 1808 NY conveyance stopped at ordinary high water whereas the 1904 NJ deed included intervening tidelands; and *Handly's Lessee* concerned a State bounded by a river, unlike an island exception within the other State's midwater territory. NJ points to its demands beginning in 1892 (P383(a),P405,P1 p. 11). Its modern-access answer is that Ellis already was a federal enclave surrounded by NJ territory, and no evidence shows access had been obstructed. These support inference, not an express compact high-water clause or an independently adjudicated boundary. The conditional Moody letter and federal-rights reservation remain applicable.

**Historical mapping.** The Master credits the 1857 Coast Survey as the best available depiction, based on Castagna's account of survey quality and control points linking the Liberty Island seawall/Fort Gibson to the accurate 1980 map and 1995 Marchuk survey. He accepts negligible relevant change before major filling and rejects NY's preferred 1837 map as less precise. He adopts NY experts' estimate of 4.69 acres to mean low water and adds 0.2 for the pier, yielding 4.89. Report 156–162. The expert's broader 4.5–6-acre low-water range expresses measurement uncertainty, not permission to allocate any shape within that acreage.

NJ accepts the historical contour but disputes precision of area calculations by non-testifying Sander Prisloe: experts could not verify the equipment/methods, Swanson was absent for part, and some discarded calculations were unrecorded (NJ Exceptions 40–41 n. 18, T2940,3041–3042). That challenge does not necessarily defeat the contour's usefulness. The 1857 mean-high-water estimate of 2.74 acres, the 1808 deed's approximate 2.97, and NPS's later rough 3.5-acre description are different sources/measurements, not interchangeable judicial findings.

**Pier dispute.** The Master credits Squires's inference that part of the L-shaped pier was filled before 1834, based on the charted surrounding material and asserted need for a sturdy ammunition-rail facility, with inadequate early piling technology. Report 158–161. NJ Exceptions 46–48 responds that pile-supported structures trap sediment; Squires acknowledged dense pilings as a possible explanation; changing pier shapes suggest piles; no persuasive evidence establishes ammunition/carts' actual use or weight; and Squires disclaimed engineering expertise to calculate the required supports (T2831–2832,2928–2929,3023,3026–3027,3061–3062). The report's reference to an "1839 chart" is challenged at NJ Exceptions 47 n. 20: NJ says no such chart is in the record and the relevant document is the 1819 map. This remains a source-description dispute, not a new finding that a nonexistent exhibit proves anything.

NJ additionally claims the 4.69-acre estimate already included the purported filled pier and that adding .2 duplicates it (Exceptions 48 n. 21). The Master says the addition covers the half not included in Swanson's conservative calculation (Report 161). The brief's transcript citation, "T3061-6 to 3052-8," is internally inconsistent and is not silently repaired. Do not resolve that conflict or adopt a numerical allowance twice without addressing the evidence. The viable paths are a supported finding of actual land within the original boundary, rejection of the unsupported footprint/addition, or a bounded technical determination if necessary to implement a selected legal line. A structure projecting over water does not automatically make its entire footprint sovereign land. The available merits record permits evaluation of the proof; exact geographic implementation can remain for the decree.

**Convenience adjustment.** The Master proposes reconfiguring the historical area to include the entire Main Building, adjacent land, ferry access and Fort Gibson while avoiding boundaries through principal buildings. His supplement selects a modified Survey A of about **5.1 acres**, rather than Survey B's 4.89. He changes the Baggage/Dormitory corridor crossing to right angles, preserves margins around the Main Building and a fifty-foot Ticket Office margin, and treats the full stairway as NJ despite approximately five inches of railing across the line. Report 162–170; Supplement 1–15. These are proposed equitable adjustments with legal consequences, not merely location of the original tide line.

New York submitted four map alternatives rather than a separate new survey; it did not dispute Marchuk's execution of Surveys A/B and preferred A within the Master's choices. NJ furnished both without surrendering exceptions. Supplement 3–8. That procedural history establishes neither default nor consent to moving sovereignty. The strongest argument for the adjustment is that uncertain ancient mapping and the Court's equitable original jurisdiction allow a practical implementation faithful to the Compact's allocation, avoiding split-building administration and preserving approximate entitlement. Its strongest objection is that acreage equivalence and administrative usefulness do not authorize exchanging territorial parcels fixed by a congressional compact. *Texas v. New Mexico* and current *Kansas* constrain relief; *Georgia* Parts VI/IX concern locating unresolved lines, not rewriting a fixed land boundary. The alternative is to determine the lawful historical line from the supported evidence and require an authenticated implementing survey/decree, leaving the States to negotiate any lawful boundary change themselves.

NJ acknowledges that the historical template intersects buildings and leaves a strip between the Main Building and ferry landing, but disputes proven administrative harm: no evidence of obstructed access, no showing that a fixed irregular line is harder to identify, federal preservation consultation already includes both States, and NPS expressed no operational objection to divided jurisdiction (NJ Exceptions 42–44). It also challenges a proposed decree that identifies all fill as NJ but reallocates some in the practical survey (p. 46 n. 19). The Court must either justify the adjustment under the actual compact/remedial authority or carry the chosen historical line into a consistent decree; presumed convenience is not record proof that the historical line is unworkable.
