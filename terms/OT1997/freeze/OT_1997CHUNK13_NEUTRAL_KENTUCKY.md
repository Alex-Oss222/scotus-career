## Record and component scope

KRL is a nonprofit ideological corporation, KRLPAC its separate political committee, and Zoeller a past contributor who wishes to contribute freely. The caption also includes PAC treasurer Robertson. Their suit challenges Kentucky campaign-finance restrictions. The district court consolidated requested preliminary relief with the State's summary-judgment request, dismissed the original definitional and corporate-employee theories for lack of a controversy, and rejected other constitutional claims. During the appeal, 1996 legislation changed definitions, independent-spending permission, identification coverage and the candidate contribution ceiling. The Sixth Circuit dismissed the affected challenges as moot and rejected the surviving facial challenges.

| Component | Exact material scope and lower disposition |
|---|---|
| Definitions, reporting and corporate employees | The amended permanent-committee definition requires a primary purpose of expressly advocating election or defeat of clearly identified candidates, slates or political parties, and excludes the separately listed committee categories. Independent expenditures are excluded from contributions. The court dismissed the challenge to §121.180(6)(a) reporting and §121.035(2) employee handling in full, and the independent/issue-spending branch of §121.025. The amendments permit corporate contributions concerning constitutional amendments, ballot public questions and issues of public importance. The nonprofit independent-expenditure permission applies to a nonprofit **which does not derive a substantial portion of its revenue from for-profit corporations**; do not omit that condition or adjudicate a different entity's facts. |
| Corporate candidate contributions | The surviving §121.025 challenge concerns nonprofit corporate **direct** candidate contributions. The court rejected a categorical nonprofit exception, relying on NRWC and distinguishing MCFL's independent expenditures. There is no finding that KRL is a commercial treasury or a conduit. |
| Advertisement identification | Amended §121.190(1) covers the listed paid communications expressly advocating election or defeat of a clearly identified candidate, slate or group of candidates. It requires “paid for by” plus the paying individual or committee's **name and address**. The issue-only application is moot; the independent candidate-election application survives. The lower court distinguished campaign reports from an on-message disclaimer but held this requirement narrowly tailored to source information, corruption and detection of disguised coordination. Those are the lower court's conclusions, not independently found facts that every advertisement involves corruption. |
| Direct $1,000 limits | Amended §§121.150(6) and 121A.050(1) impose $1,000 limits **in any one election**, not a general annual limit. The latter's knowing-donation prohibition expressly excludes independent expenditures as statutorily defined. The court treated the challenge as controlled by Buckley's contribution distinction and reported that plaintiffs' counsel had proposed $1,000 below and essentially conceded its constitutionality at oral argument. That reported concession is material; no unseen transcript or broader waiver is invented. |
| Aggregate intermediary limit | Section 121.150(10) limits a person's contributions to **all permanent committees and contributing organizations together** to $1,500 in one year. It does not cap that person's aggregate gifts to all candidates. The court relied on circumvention prevention, committee discretion over contributed funds and differences between state/local and federal campaigns. These are its reasoning, not proof that each donation is earmarked or that every affected organization transfers funds to candidates. |
| Gubernatorial receipts | Section 121A.030(4) concerns permanent-committee gifts to a gubernatorial slate. For a Fund participant, the text limits such gifts to **25% of qualifying contributions received, up to $150,000 per election**. The lower court describes a maximum $600,000 privately raised sum, a 2:1 match, and $1.8 million total spending; $150,000 is the resulting maximum, not a substitute for the percentage at a lower fundraising level. For a slate declining Fund disbursements, footnote 25 reports an explicit $150,000 ceiling. The challenge survives for KRLPAC only; it is moot for KRL and Zoeller as non-permanent-committee plaintiffs, and the independent-expenditure theory is moot. The district court found no record indication the provision had limited PAC contributions or was likely to do so. The appellate court's 151st-PAC illustration assumes the maximum $600,000 private receipts and $1,000 donations; it is not a fact about every candidate. |

* **McIntyre, April 19, 1995:** Exacting scrutiny means narrow tailoring to an overriding interest for the ordinary individual's anonymous issue leaflet. Its holding reserves candidate finance, committees and organizational spending; it does not decide this case by labeling everything election speech. Standard: **Anonymous individual political leaflets**, effective April 19, 1995.
* **Day v. Holahan, May 15, 1995:** The unanimously controlling intermediary holding requires meaningful collective association and fit to an identified corruption or circumvention mechanism. It invalidated the particular $100 ceiling, created no dollar floor, and left higher ceilings and realistic conduits open. Its nonprofit holding turns on actual political resources, economic claimants and insignificant commercial/corporate funding, and does not decide direct corporate gifts. The responsive-financing judgment has no unified controlling rationale. Standards: **Intermediary contribution limits and meaningful association** and **Nonprofit political spending outside a literal safe harbor**, each effective May 15, 1995.
* **Colorado Republican, June 26, 1996:** Genuinely independent expression is not transformed into a candidate contribution by party identity; the specific candidate-connection predicates and separate earmark/republication provisions remain distinct. The contribution/expenditure distinction survives. Standard: **Independent political-party campaign expression and candidate connections**, effective June 26, 1996. This is relevant by analogy, not a decision that Kentucky's different corporate or disclosure rules are invalid.
* **Bancorp, November 8, 1994; Arizonans, March 3, 1997:** Mootness forbids constitutional merits adjudication of a dead claim; §2106 judgment-disposition authority remains equitable, not automatic. Standard: **Equitable vacatur after settlement moots review**, effective November 8, 1994, preserves its settlement-specific scope. Legislative amendment is not settlement. Arizonans confirms corrective disposition without creating merits jurisdiction or a surviving damages claim.
* **Surviving pre-divergence authorities:** Buckley's contribution/expenditure distinction and appropriately fitted corruption, circumvention and disclosure interests; NRWC's corporate contribution/solicitation setting; MCFL's distinct nonprofit independent expression; California Medical's actual plurality and decisive concurrence, not a categorical declaration that all intermediary contributions lack protection; Citizens Against Rent Control on collective advocacy; NCPAC on independent expression; Talley on anonymous handbills; Kremens, Burke, Oakes and Mesquite on genuinely live claims and voluntary cessation. 5. The $1,500 all-intermediary cap may be sustained on an adequately fitted circumvention rationale or rejected because its broad pooling burden lacks the fit Day requires.
