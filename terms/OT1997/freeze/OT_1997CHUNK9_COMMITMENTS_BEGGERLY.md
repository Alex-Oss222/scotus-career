## Common judgment and four separate propositions

All eight Associate Justices would **reverse the Fifth Circuit's judgment and remand with directions to give effect to rejection of the presented independent action and untimely alternative QTA route**. The appellate order vacating the 1982 consent judgment and directing title for the claimants cannot stand. The consent judgment remains operative. The result does not adjudicate the validity of the 1781 document, the ultimate title chain or a new compensation claim.

**1. The original court has limited power to consider an independent attack on its own decree.** The United States invoked that court's jurisdiction and obtained the challenged quiet-title judgment there. A traditional independent action directed to correcting that same judgment may be entertained as a continuation of the original controversy; a second independent jurisdictional grant and newly enacted sovereign consent are not required merely to ask that court to remove its own allegedly inequitable decree. Rule 60(b)'s savings clause preserves the existing equitable procedure; it does not itself confer general subject-matter jurisdiction or waive immunity for new substantive relief.

Kokkonen concerns enforcement of a separate settlement contract and expressly reserves reopening; its jurisdictional caution does not eliminate a court's authority over the validity of its own judgment. Peacock distinguishes effectuating an existing judgment from a new action imposing another person's debt; this bounded corrective inquiry creates no new debtor or affirmative damages obligation. Nordic Village and Department of Energy v. Ohio continue to demand consent to the particular affirmative monetary remedy sought. The same-court jurisdictional conclusion therefore does not establish a QTA claim, a Tucker Act remedy or entitlement to money. Jurisdiction is actually resolved at this limited level rather than assumed to reach the merits.

**2. An independent action requires extraordinary injustice, not merely evidence that the earlier settlement may have been mistaken.** The preserved equitable procedure is available only in exceptional circumstances sufficiently grave to justify setting aside a completed judgment outside ordinary remedies. The traditional requirements—an inequitable judgment, a good defense, fraud/accident/mistake preventing its use, absence of claimant fault or negligence and absence of an adequate legal remedy—must establish that exceptional need together. They do not create a broad second opportunity whenever a better document is later found. The operative limit is prevention of a grave miscarriage of justice, not a categorical prohibition on correcting settlements or a requirement that only intentional fraud could ever qualify.

The Government's reported archive search failed and its representation that the island had never been privately granted was inaccurate. Accepting the claimants' sustained search and the representation's influence on settlement, those facts do not establish extraordinary interference with their ability to present their title claim of the kind necessary for this independent action. The later discovery of a material translation does not by itself establish a corrupted adjudicative process, a guaranteed good chain of title or an exceptional equitable right to reopen. No finding of intentional concealment or obstruction is supplied; none is inferred simply from the document's presence in federal archives. The claimants are not blamed for failing to locate it sooner, and the Court does not find that the Government searched adequately. The asserted search/disclosure failure and later evidence, taken at their supported level, are insufficient for the extraordinary remedy demanded.

Actual Morgan Stanley and Plaut permit expressly bounded legislative reinstatement with independent judicial adjudication; they do not establish historical Plaut's categorical Article III bar, and no such bar is used here. They likewise do not convert final judgments into open-ended fairness review. The independent equitable procedure is preserved while this attempt fails its demanding conditions.

**3. The alternative QTA route is late on the stated facts; no universal anti-tolling rule is necessary.** Section 2409a(g)'s twelve years begins when the claimant knows or should know of the United States' adverse claim, not when the claimant finds persuasive evidence to defeat it. The lower majority placed knowledge in 1976; even the latest supplied notice in 1979 preceded June 1, 1994 by more than twelve years. Continued research and discovery of the translation in 1991 do not restart that trigger.

Assuming Irwin's rebuttable presumption leaves room for an appropriate equitable claim under this statute, the facts supplied do not justify suspending the whole interval from the original search until favorable evidence emerged. The government representation concerned the strength of the competing title positions in an already known dispute; its influence on settlement does not itself establish that the Government prevented timely assertion of the known claim. Sustained diligence is relevant but does not by itself supply the missing extraordinary barrier or inducement concerning timely litigation. This is a failure of the asserted equitable basis, not a new finding that the claimants were negligent or that intentional wrongdoing is always indispensable.

Brockamp assumed Irwin and found rebuttal from its particular integrated tax scheme while reserving governmental inducement; Beach applies the particular expiration text for rescission. Neither establishes a universal sovereign-deadline rule. This commitment **does not adopt a categorical statutory holding that §2409a(g) can never be equitably tolled**, does not classify every QTA deadline as jurisdictional, and does not decide a different record of affirmative concealment, obstruction or inducement. Full historical code pages were not recovered; the authoritative published notice trigger and twelve-year period suffice for this narrower resolution without an unquoted statutory exception.

**4. Reopening and title relief are distinct.** Because neither route supplies relief here, the appellate title direction fails. Independently, judicial permission to reopen would not itself resolve the contested grant, complete title chain, remaining defenses or the summary-judgment record. The Court does not use the lower majority's conclusion that title was certain as a substitute for the separate reopening predicates. It need not issue an alternative title-merits remand after rejecting the legal routes on which that direction depends.

## Enhanced alternatives and Justice-specific conclusions

The strongest reopening alternative relies on a formal official representation inducing settlement, a seemingly decisive document retained in public archives, and unusual claimant diligence. Those facts deserve full consideration and are not relabeled as a failure to prosecute. The competing exceptional-relief rule nevertheless requires more than a materially mistaken litigation representation and subsequently found proof: the record does not establish the grave process failure needed to reopen this completed settlement through an independent action. This conclusion accepts the supported conduct rather than inventing a clean search or resolving the contested title facts.

The strongest jurisdictional alternative would require new consent for every independent action. It fails for this narrowly corrective same-court request because it treats an attack on the court's own decree as equivalent to new affirmative liability, despite Kokkonen's reopening reservation and Peacock's distinction. It remains correct that new title or compensation relief requires its own lawful authority. The strongest tolling alternative invokes Irwin and Brockamp's reserved governmental-inducement issue; it fails on these asserted facts without foreclosing a materially different claim of interference with timely suit.
