## United States v. Estate of Romani

### Validated record, scope and distinctions

The full Pennsylvania Supreme Court opinion in the saved `research/chunk7_neutral_b/romani_lower_opinion.md` was read, including all footnotes. The saved objective extraction `research/chunk7_neutral_facts/ROMANI_DISTINCT_LIEN_CONCESSION.md` was also read in full. The two sources support distinct propositions and do not erase one another.

Romani died January 13, 1992. His sole estate asset was Cambria County real estate valued at $53,001; debts exceeded that value. A $400,000 judgment was entered January 25, 1985 and revived June 7, 1989. Later federal tax-lien notices concerned unpaid taxes and an unpaid civil penalty totaling $489,720.04 with interest. The estate administrator sought permission to transfer the land to the judgment creditor in lieu of execution. The county court authorized that transfer July 7, 1994; the Superior Court affirmed; the Pennsylvania Supreme Court affirmed the priority judgment. These are lower-court events, not present Supreme Court dispositions.

The lower opinion's finality explanation is that transfer of the estate's sole asset effectively ends administration, although there was no separate final-distribution order. This supports review of the final federal priority determination under the ordinary state-judgment channel; no independent state ground is identified that disposes of the competing federal statutes.

Three matters must remain separate:

1. **State lien and property:** the creditor held a valid judgment lien on the identified existing real estate before Romani's death and before federal notice. This is not McDermott's subsequently acquired property.
2. **Section 6323:** the published procedural recitation records the Government's acknowledgment that its federal tax liens were not valid against this earlier judgment lien. The source therefore supplies the concrete protected-creditor predicate for the statutory conflict presented; it does not leave ordinary section 6323 priority wholly unproved.
3. **Independent section 3713 exception:** footnote 19 of the Pennsylvania opinion records a separate Government argument that the lien was not sufficiently specific and perfected to displace federal insolvency priority. The lower courts had not addressed that argument, and the Pennsylvania Supreme Court declined to consider it. No concession about section 6323 establishes that a different federal insolvency exception involving title, possession or additional perfection requirements has been satisfied.

**Correction to preliminary framing:** references to a previously perfected judgment lien must be read as the established state lien and section 6323 protection, not as an admission that every independent section 3713 choateness exception was met. Conversely, footnote 19 is not a reason to pretend section 6323 protection itself is contested on this record. This distinction matters both to the substantive rule and to whether a further inquiry is necessary.

### Entering law and statutory conditions

The entering-law slice supplies controlling McDermott, March 24, 1993: federal notice precedes a judgment lien's attachment to land acquired only later; the Court did not decide existing-property priority contrary to section 6323. Its Standards and Tests module, **Federal lien notice and after-acquired property priority**, has that same present-operation date. McDermott preserves existing-property choateness and other statutory creditor protections. Its application does not automatically decide the separate section 3713 conflict here.

Section 3713(a)(1)(B) directs payment of a federal claim first when a deceased debtor's estate, in an executor's or administrator's custody, cannot pay all debts. Insolvency alone is not a substitute for all statutory triggers in other settings. Subsection (a)(1)(A) separately concerns an insolvent living debtor with a voluntary assignment, attachment of an absent debtor's property, or an act of bankruptcy. Subsection (a)(2) excludes cases under title 11. Subsection (b) separately makes a representative, other than a title-11 trustee, liable to the extent the representative pays another debt before an unpaid federal claim. No personal-liability award under subsection (b) is presented here.

Section 6323(a) protects a purchaser, security-interest holder, mechanic's lienor or judgment lien creditor against a section 6321 federal tax lien until the Secretary files notice satisfying subsection (f). This proceeding concerns the established judgment lien and the Government's concession about its tax liens. The detailed protections for other creditor categories, later advances or other property are not adjudicated simply because the lower opinion discusses their legislative context.

The saved lower opinion gives the operative competing statutory provisions and explains the opposing readings. The official 1994 section 3713 source independently confirms all of that section's operative conditions: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1994-title31/html/USCODE-1994-title31-subtitleIII-chap37-subchapII-sec3713.htm . ### Supported alternatives for independent assessment

**Apply the specific tax-lien protection to this conflict.** The Court could hold that section 3713 does not permit collection of this tax claim from the same property ahead of the judgment creditor whose priority section 6323 protects. The specific and later tax-lien scheme would govern this collision without repealing federal priority generally. The lower court relies on Kimbell Foods, 440 U.S. 715, for Congress's rejection of unrestricted tax-lien preference and the stability supplied by the notice system; it acknowledges that Kimbell itself concerned consensual federal lending and solvent debtors. Morton v. Mancari, 417 U.S. 535, supplies harmonious construction and specific/general comparison; Randall, 401 U.S. 513, supplies an example in which a specific statutory distribution scheme controls over general federal priority. Those authorities do not by themselves establish a universal rule for all federal debts.

The strongest objection is that section 6323 regulates lien validity while section 3713 independently regulates insolvency distribution; Congress did not expressly remove tax debts from the latter. A defensible answer must explain the operative conflict on this property, not merely invoke commercial fairness or call every later provision an implied repeal. If this path resolves the statutory conflict through the conceded section 6323 protection, the distinct and unaddressed extra requirements for an independent section 3713 exception need not be found satisfied. Affirmance of the priority judgment is legally available on that bounded rationale; a remand to decide an irrelevant independent exception is not automatic.

**Give the independent insolvency command priority.** The Court could distinguish lien priority outside insolvency from section 3713's distribution command, relying on Emory, 314 U.S. 423, and Key, 397 U.S. 322, for the demanding requirement of inconsistency before an implied exception, and on the distinction the lower court acknowledges between Kimbell's lending question and the insolvency question here. This path must answer why Congress's protected judgment lien retains meaningful effect when the tax debtor's estate cannot pay all creditors, and why payment from this encumbered property is consistent with section 6323's operative protection.

That path cannot summarily assert that the creditor failed a separately sufficient section 3713 exception: the preserved specificity/perfection argument was not adjudicated below. Reversal of the statutory-priority rule with appropriate further consideration of a material independent exception is available if the Court chooses this construction. The actual scope and remedy depend on what the Court holds about that exception; no new evidence or particular hearing follows merely from remand.

**Recognize a sufficiently specific earlier lien exception independently.** This is a different possible ground, not another description of the section 6323 reading. The older cases discussed in the lower opinion repeatedly reserved a sufficiently perfected-and-specific exception while finding the lien before them deficient. A court choosing this ground must define its federal requirements and establish their application; it cannot use the section 6323 concession to dispose of the distinct objection. The present source supports preserving that question, not a new finding of title transfer, possession, execution or federal choateness.

## Ricci v. Village of Arlington Heights

### Validated record and preserved scope

The complete Seventh Circuit opinion, including its four footnotes, was read at https://openjurist.org/116/f3d/288/ricci-v-arlington-heights-illinois . The objective extraction `research/chunk7_neutral_facts/RICCI_PRESERVATION.md` was read in full and checked against that opinion.

The Seventh Circuit affirmed summary judgment June 20, 1997, No. 96-2229, 116 F.3d 288. This is a final federal appellate judgment suitable for ordinary section 1254(1) review. The scheduled question concerns full custodial processing under the automatic business-license policy, with the probable-cause predicate accepted. The local ordinance is regulatory and fine-only; the lower opinion's references to a misdemeanor do not establish a breach of the peace or authorize a new criminal penalty.

The officers had investigated complaints and confirmed the absence of a business license. While at the premises to arrest an employee on a warrant, they asked Ricci about the license and he acknowledged he lacked it. They took him to the station, locked him in an interview room for about an hour while preparing the arrest sheet, local-ordinance complaint and bond receipt, and released him on recognizance. His wife acquired the license during custody, and the charge was dismissed after the license was presented. The lower court recites Ricci's assertion that officers already knew licensing, release and dismissal would follow; that remains his assertion, not an independent finding about all officers' intentions.

Section 9-201 carried a fine between $5 and $500 for each offense, with each day of violation a separate offense. The lower court noted possible accumulated exposure based on extended operation; no specific accumulated fine was adjudged. Section 14-3002 supplied the business licensing requirement. Ricci did not contest probable cause or state/local authority to arrest; the opinion identifies Illinois section 5/107-2(1)(c) and the Village's paperwork policy. State authorization and federal constitutional reasonableness remain distinct legal questions.

The search claim was settled. Ricci did not appeal the dismissal with prejudice of his no-probable-cause claim. Neither claim is reopened. At appellate oral argument he advanced a distinct warrant-clause theory that had not appeared in his brief; the Seventh Circuit held that theory waived. Its footnote 4 also records his concession that an arrest under a warrant would have been reasonable. Those are concrete preservation and concession limits. A preserved common-law argument about the reasonableness of fine-only custody is not the same thing as a properly preserved independent warrant requirement.

The opinion does not decide individual qualified immunity and does not establish that the defendants waived it. The record supports a challenge against the Village policy and officers, but constitutional invalidity would not itself award damages, establish every officer's responsibility or resolve immunity. The proceedings concern one hour of station-house custody, not handcuffs, shackles, strip searches, body-cavity searches, deadly force or a home entry; hypothetical practices are not findings about Ricci.

### Supported alternatives for independent assessment

**Sustain this custody under an objective arrest rule or bounded application.** The probable cause, accepted local arrest authority, continuing violation and ordinary short processing support affirmance. The Seventh Circuit invokes its Trigg rule and cites Fisher and Higbee as analogous circuit cases permitting processing for minor offenses. Robinson and Gustafson concern searches following lawful custodial arrest and help describe ordinary arrest consequences; they cannot simply prove the antecedent lawfulness disputed here. The strongest objection is that a state statute and probable cause establish grounds to intervene, not unlimited federal constitutional authority to take an identified person into custody for administrative paperwork. A defensible affirmance must explain the legal significance of the actual custody and its limits, rather than announce that state authorization answers every Fourth Amendment question.

**Reject automatic custody for this fine-only licensing policy.** The Court could distinguish initial intervention from the greater station-house intrusion and require a constitutionally adequate reason for that additional restraint. The fine-only regulatory setting, known identity and limited paperwork/compliance objectives support a narrow unreasonable-custody claim under Whren's reserved execution limits and Robinette's separate-justification principle. Wilson permits historical arrest limits to inform the inquiry. The strongest objection is the absence of a controlling rule that fine-only punishment, by itself, bars ordinary processing, coupled with the admitted continuing offense, statutory arrest authorization and actual limited duration. Any requirement of individualized enforcement need would be a new application or rule here, not a rule already dictated by Whren. The Court must state any operational exceptions it adopts; no unbounded least-restrictive-means requirement follows from the existence of a possible citation.

**Correct an overbroad legal rule without conclusively deciding this application's reasonableness.** A reasoned vacatur could reject the view that probable cause plus state authority invariably suffices and remand for the actual policy, custody and supported enforcement needs. That path requires identifying a material application or legal inquiry left unresolved by the present judgment; it is not justified simply because more facts could always be collected. The lower court already discussed continuing operation, fines and processing time. A remand must preserve those supported facts and identify what further question matters, rather than manufacture missing facts or automatically order a hearing.

**Threshold limits.** The waived independent warrant-clause theory cannot silently become the decisive merits ground. A judgment on the preserved reasonableness claim remains available without deciding whether a warrant was required. If a proposed ground necessarily depends on an unpreserved question or conflicts with the recorded concession, model the appropriate reachability limit expressly; do not rewrite the concession, imply waiver of all reasonableness objections, or invent a new vehicle defect. An unexplained procedural dismissal would establish no constitutional holding.

If Ricci prevails on constitutional reasonableness, the ordinary consequence is reversal or vacatur of the adverse summary judgment within the reviewed claim and further proceedings on remaining liability and remedy conditions. No damages amount, independent immunity rejection, new licensing policy, reopening of the settlement, or reinstatement of the abandoned probable-cause claim follows automatically. An affirmance likewise need not establish the constitutionality of every fine-only arrest or every method of custody.
