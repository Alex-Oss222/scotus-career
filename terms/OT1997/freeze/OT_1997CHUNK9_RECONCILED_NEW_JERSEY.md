## Reconciliation conclusion

Five Associates—O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer—support New Jersey sovereignty over the later artificial fill, reject New York's three exceptions, reject New Jersey's high-water and pier exceptions, and sustain New Jersey's objection to exchanging territory for convenience. They support an original-island boundary at mean low water, including the historical pier area found to have been filled land, with a proposed conforming decree to follow.

Stevens supports New York sovereignty over the enlarged island by prescription. Scalia and Thomas support that territorial result through interpretation of the Compact in light of its structure and the parties' practical construction; they do not decide that New York proved prescription. The three do not form a common sufficient-rationale coalition.

The material provisional changes are justified below by source-specific review, not by importing a historical margin. The supplied record is the same underlying record that the historical writings evaluated. Its recovery, fuller presentation, and explicit preservation of competing inferences are not a changed factual premise. The current Kansas and Louisiana decisions materially constrain remedy and explain original-action review, but neither changes this Compact, the prescriptive elements, the general shore-boundary rule, or the circumstantial pier evidence. No new neutral fact or reconstructed evidentiary premise is adopted.

## Six components: grounds, counts and operative limits

### 1. Compact interpretation and original entitlement

**Six support NJ original entitlement:** Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Article First supplies the midwater sovereignty boundary; Article Second preserves New York's original island jurisdiction. Article Third's commerce/navigation jurisdiction does not restore New York's underlying sovereignty west of that line. The Compact does not displace the ordinary rule that artificial filling leaves the interstate boundary unchanged. Foreseeability of reclamation and omission of metes and bounds do not establish an agreement transferring the submerged sovereign territory whenever fill is added.

This is the whole-Compact/background-law ground, not a claim that the word “present,” by itself, gives metes and bounds. The Master understood that word partly in terms of retained jurisdiction after the federal cession; the geographic conclusion does not require treating his explanation as the only possible meaning. Central Railroad, 209 U.S. 473, 478–479, remains precedent distinguishing sovereignty from Article Third jurisdiction, without becoming a prior judgment binding these States on the precise present island. Artificial enlargement is distinct from natural accretion, and no universal proposition about every federal dredging operation or every subsequently connected feature is adopted.

**Scalia and Thomas support NY interpretation.** The Compact's integrated structure and its commerce/navigation arrangement make the scope of the retained island sufficiently ambiguous for practical construction to matter. Article Third informs the meaning of Articles First and Second even though the State did not advance an independent Article Third title claim. They regard the decades of state and federal administration as establishing the Compact's meaning: New York governed the enlarged island as a single jurisdiction, while New Jersey did not supply comparable island administration. They do not treat Article Second as an automatic transfer clause for every imaginable future fill or treat prescription as proved merely by relabeling it interpretation.

Their source ground is the historical Scalia dissent, joined by Thomas, 523 U.S. 829–832, including its distinction between interpreting an ambiguous agreement and ousting an otherwise undoubted owner. Its date-eligible interpretive support includes Air France v. Saks, 470 U.S. 392, 396, Choctaw Nation, 318 U.S. 423, 431–432, and Vermont v. New Hampshire, 289 U.S. 593, 619. The supplied Master already discusses Vermont's practical-construction principle, and the New York exceptions invoke the same boundary practice; the neutral packet supplies the relevant structural argument and competing conduct. This is no newly reconstructed neutral fact. Scalia and Thomas would also consider the City's argument rather than characterize it as the State's preserved independent claim. Their sufficient ground here remains the integrated Compact and practical construction; no separate independent Article Third holding is supplied.

**Why the provisional unanimity changes:** Scalia's own Georgia and Illinois positions do not establish that this Compact is unambiguous; his historical dissent directly addressed the same text and practical record. Thomas's Kansas, Louisiana and Venetie joins likewise do not forbid interpretation by practice where the agreement is ambiguous. Kansas forbids replacing the bargain, not determining what a disputed bargain meant. Louisiana does not settle this artificial enlargement. No changed premise supports their original rejection of this interpretive route after that direct source review.

### 2. Prescription and acquiescence

**Five reject prescription:** O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. **Stevens sustains it. Scalia and Thomas do not reach it as a necessary ground** and are not added to either evidentiary coalition. In particular, Thomas's original rejection is no longer a necessary merits commitment after his sufficient interpretation ground is corrected; Scalia's original finding of prescription is withdrawn.

The five and Stevens apply the preponderance standard to New York's affirmative claim of long, continuous possession/assertion of sovereignty over the actual disputed fill and New Jersey's acquiescence. Acquiescence requires knowledge of the adverse claim or sufficiently open, notorious, visible and uninterrupted adverse acts to support that inference. No fixed universal number of years, mandatory municipal-service checklist, requirement of a formal State protest, or rule that federal occupation categorically precludes prescription is adopted. The latter general question remains open.

The five acknowledge specifically located fill-resident electoral and hospital records. They do not dismiss them as wholly unspecified references to “Ellis Island.” They nevertheless find the cumulative adverse claim and notice inadequate: the identified civil acts are intermittent, often performed elsewhere, and coexist with mixed federal understandings and episodes recognizing New Jersey's interests. Original-island New York jurisdiction makes generic references equivocal. Federal control constrains both the acts available to New York and the notice or protest reasonably expected of New Jersey. These are contextual reasons why the affirmative burden is unmet, not a new elevated proof quantum.

The 1904 deed is not an unqualified sovereign concession, the 1933 permit's object must be respected, and federal or local conduct is not automatically State conduct. The 1935 federal return to New York and the 1949 wage reversal are included. They prevent a claim of permanent contrary federal control, but do not erase the underlying uncertainty. A late protest cannot undo title already perfected; the five find the requisite acquisition unproved before that later controversy. No missing document's content, witness testimony, or unrecorded act is invented.

**Stevens's retained position:** The actual fill-directed records, civil administration, census treatment and residents' public affiliation show a continuing sovereign understanding suited to a federally administered installation. Sparse surviving examples do not negate a consistent practice. He draws notice from the activity's public character and significance and treats the contrary episodes as isolated, property-related, local/federal rather than State assertions, or ultimately abandoned. He need not adopt every broad historical factual phrase as a new finding: registrations are not automatically actual votes, every marriage is not located on fill, and every federal wage benchmark is not obligatory State law. His conclusion rests on the cumulative approved evidence, not a newly invented count. Inconvenient building divisions reinforce his evidentiary criticism; they do not establish an independent authority to exchange territory.

**Breyer correction and Ginsburg concurrence function:** Breyer's historical concurrence, joined by Ginsburg, specifically rejected the argument that the modest acts possible under federal control necessarily established acquisition. Virtually exclusive federal administration made it unreasonable to expect a major New Jersey protest against each modest assertion; treating this proof as sufficient would make the acquiescence protection unfairly easy to defeat. The Wyzanski statement is evidence of a federal understanding, not a correct holding that federal enclaves are outside all States. Stevens's contrary inference and the later federal reversals were part of the same historical dispute. Current Hess's entity-specific analysis does not turn those modest acts into adequate notice; Kansas and Louisiana alter no prescriptive element. Breyer's provisional contrary inference lacks a material changed premise after this source review and is corrected. Ginsburg retains her rejection and may join this explanation.

Breyer and Ginsburg also find no **relevant** ambiguity displacing background avulsion in Articles First and Second. They do not declare every Compact provision unambiguous, or every silence incapable of contextual interpretation. Their additional explanation can coexist with a complete join of the five's opinion; the historical author's identity is evidence of the ground, not a present assignment.

### 3. Laches

All eight reject a separate laches bar if the component is reached, but not on a common eight-Justice rationale. The five directly support **ground B**, and Stevens supports it conditionally as part of the legal analysis with which he agrees. Scalia and Thomas retain the frozen **ground P** as an alternative; their preferred interpretation decree needs neither ground.

**Ground B:** New York identifies evidentiary loss affecting proof of its own affirmative acquisition by prescription. On that issue it bears the plaintiff's burden even though prescription was pleaded as a defense. Laches cannot excuse proof of that affirmative entitlement. The claimed losses do not identify lost evidence defeating the Compact/avulsion entitlement itself. The Court need not determine whether some other interstate compact controversy permits laches. Even assuming the historical loss circumstances made prescriptive proof harder, this asserted defense does not establish or substitute for the missing prescriptive elements.

This corrects the five's provisional independently sufficient finding of inadequate prejudice. Historical pages 806–807 expressly did not make that factual finding necessary; they allowed that New York might be right about lost evidence and rejected the asserted legal use of laches. Current Kansas also leaves general availability open, but its decision on lack of inexcusable delay does not require an evidentiary-prejudice holding here or displace New York's affirmative burden. Illinois's boundary-repose discussion supplies context; it is not enlarged into an absolute rule covering every compact action.

**Ground P, retained only for Scalia and Thomas if needed:** Assuming availability, New York has not adequately established material prejudice from the delay on this record; circumstances of loss do not prove the contents of missing documents or testimony. This is the original bounded alternative, not a historical attribution. It does not decide inexcusable delay, resolve all compact cases, supply prescription, or convert laches dismissal into an ownership decree.

### 4. Original-island shore

**Five direct low-water commitments:** O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. **Stevens conditionally supports low water. Scalia and Thomas retain conditional high-water objections.** Only the five presently favor the divided-island entitlement that requires this implementation. Do not publish an invented historical eight-Justice shoreline vote.

The five's rule: Article Second preserves the original island to its **mean low-water line in 1834**. Handly's Lessee, 18 U.S. 374, 380–383, and Vermont, 289 U.S. 593, 606, supply the general sovereign-boundary rule used to construe this reservation. The parties' silence is read against that rule. The conclusion does not depend on treating the unsuccessful 1827 proposal's omitted language as enacted text.

Article Third's express low-water limit on the New Jersey shore does not imply a high-water boundary for Ellis Island. Its specific language resolves whether New York's water/submerged-land jurisdiction may move inland over New Jersey's shore as the tide rises. Around an island already within New York's retained jurisdiction, that problem differs. The limited conveyance of property to high water and the approximate deed acreage do not themselves fix the interstate boundary. The different river facts of Handly and Vermont require explanation but do not supply a changed rule excluding a tidal island. Actual Louisiana decided continuity even under a proposed high-water test and expressly did not establish a universal island definition; it therefore cannot compel a high-water territorial reservation here. A showing of present-day obstructed access is not a condition of the governing interpretive default.

**Correction basis:** The five directly joined the historical low-water analysis on this same Compact and record. Kansas's exact-bargain principle and Louisiana's independent review do not change its predicate. The provisional contrary construction is a permissible argument already available historically, not a newly changed premise sufficient to support their departure. Stevens's express agreement with the legal analysis likewise corrects his conditional high-water position.

**Scalia/Thomas alternative:** If a divided enclave must be fixed despite their interpretation, they retain the frozen view that the final text, Article First's allocation, Article Third's express wording, the high-water conveyance and the nonidentical river precedents support high water for this reservation. They do not turn the conveyance into a sovereign decree or Louisiana into a universal high-water rule. Their historical interpretation dissent did not determine this alternative legal question; its silence is not a specific low-water commitment that can be imported against the independent freeze.

### 5. Historical pier and numerical implementation

**Five accept the supported filled-pier finding:** O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. **Stevens, Scalia and Thomas retain the frozen conditional pier objection.** The latter three do not favor a divided-island decree on their sufficient grounds.

The five overrule New Jersey's pier exception. The supported 1819 map appears to show filled material around the pier location, and the credited evidence about the relative use of piling and ease of shallow-water filling supports the likely conclusion that the historical pier rested on fill. A possible pile-supported explanation does not require rejecting that inference. This is independent original-action evaluation of circumstantial proof, not Rule 52 deference, a demand for certainty, or a presumption that every projecting structure is land.

The Master's “1839 chart” description is not silently converted into a different exhibit; the five rely on the identified 1819 map. The ammunition-load assumptions, engineering qualification objection, changing shape and admitted alternative support mechanism are considered, but do not negate that narrower map-and-construction inference. Louisiana requires appropriate weight and independent review, which the historical Court expressly exercised by finding the likely inference persuasive. Kansas's rejection of an unreliable water estimate creates no categorical ban on supported circumstantial mapping evidence. Thus neither entering decision supplies a changed premise for the five's provisional pier rejection.

The finding includes the historical pier land within the retained original island; it does **not** enter an exact acreage award. The Master explains the 0.2-acre allowance as the portion excluded from his 4.69-acre base; New Jersey disputes that explanation and alleges duplication. No independently measured resolution of that arithmetic is invented. The implementing proposal must locate the accepted historical land once, show the treatment of the pier, and reconcile its description and acreage. This technical requirement does not reopen the resolved finding that the pier rested on fill, reject it merely because the operator lacks original measurements, or authorize a new merits trial as a research default.

The three alternative objectors retain their frozen view that the admitted piling explanation and limitations of the inference do not establish the footprint/separate addition adequately. Stevens's broad historical disagreement with factual assessment does not prove a pier finding either way; Scalia and Thomas did not reach it. Their conditional rejection is an independent present position, not a claimed historical holding. They preserve genuine independently established original land and prohibit double counting; they do not turn every uncertainty into a litigant default.

### 6. Convenience adjustment, decree and remaining proceedings

All eight reject the judicial exchange of an otherwise fixed Compact boundary merely for administrative convenience. For the five, sustain New Jersey's exception to the proposed practical reconfiguration. Scalia and Thomas's use of practice to interpret an ambiguous agreement does not authorize changing a boundary after its meaning is established. Stevens's prescription conclusion and criticism of a building-splitting line likewise do not repeal current Kansas's constraint on a judicial exchange.

Texas v. New Mexico, 462 U.S. 554, 564, and current Kansas require enforcement of the congressionally approved bargain; Cuyler supplies its federal-law status. Georgia's treatment of an undefined maritime line, including Stevens and Scalia's disagreement about that line, is not authority to redistribute land fixed by this Compact. Technical uncertainty permits locating the boundary, not allocating substitute parcels of equivalent area.

The five reject the modified **5.1-acre Survey A** as a convenience adjustment, including its building margins, corridor turns, access arrangement and staircase treatment. **4.89-acre Survey B** is not made lawful merely by matching an approximate acreage. Survey assistance, lack of objection to the surveyor's execution, or preference among the Master's options is not consent to sovereign exchange. The March 31 proposed decree and the May 30 Designated Survey remain proposals.

**Direction supported by the five:** Recommit the matter to the Special Master for preparation of a proposed decree consistent with the adjudicated mean-low-water original-island boundary and filled-pier finding. The technical description must be authenticated, internally consistent and free of duplicate acreage. Existing supported work may be used; no unnecessary new evidentiary trial or new full survey is mandated. The Court must separately consider and enter the eventual decree. May 26 supplies no invented coordinates, exact final acreage, geographically undefined operative injunction, decree date, or automatic docket closure.

Stevens would instead implement New York's whole-island entitlement by prescription; Scalia and Thomas would implement it by interpretation. Their conditional objections concern what a decree must do if their preferred entitlement does not prevail. Their support for no judicial exchange is common legal ground; it is not a vote for the five's territorial result.

Every path preserves the United States' express Compact reservation. Sovereignty does not resolve private/federal ownership, the precise extent of federal enclave jurisdiction, every aspect of New York's retained jurisdiction over the original island, or unpresented municipal/regulatory/tax consequences. A request to enjoin exercise of authority over New Jersey's territory requires a geographically supported decree consistent with those reservations; no present unspecified injunction is fabricated.
