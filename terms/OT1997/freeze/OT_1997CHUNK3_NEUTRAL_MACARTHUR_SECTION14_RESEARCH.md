No historical Supreme Court docket or action supplies the hypothetical review. This packet determines source support and available analytical paths, not a judgment, vote, or reconstruction choice. **Research conclusion:** The published opinion and newly recovered party papers establish a personal statutory retention dispute and an original demand by Lindblom for money as well as equitable relief. They do not establish that statutory relief was unavailable, that a money claim was abandoned, or that Lindblom failed to prove statutory adverse effect. Conversely, discussion of expungement does not conclusively establish every civil-action or sovereign-consent predicate. A substantial claim to a personal statutory privacy interest cannot be rejected for want of standing merely because the plaintiff may ultimately lose the retention construction, damages elements, or request for a particular injunction. An asserted remedy that is legally unavailable, rather than simply unproved or disputed, cannot supply redressability. The new research removes the blanket “original papers unavailable” characterization; specified originals remain unrecovered, and their absence is not a litigation-record deficiency. No factual reconstruction is necessary to prepare the supported narrower paths below.

## 2. Article III: concrete personal invasion and meaningful redress, without proving the merits first

The actual in-world June 2, 1997 **Steel Co.** decision governs. It prohibits assuming disputed Article III power to resolve contested statutory liability, but also applies **Bell v. Hood** to distinguish a substantial federal claim from its ultimate success. Its specific redressability ruling concerned a fully corrected reporting default, no continuing or imminent violation, and relief that would not repair the plaintiff's injury. Here, the reported claim concerns continuing retention of the claimant's own records. That factual distinction matters; it does not independently establish a remedy.

**Bell v. Hood, 327 U.S. 678, 681–685 (1946),** holds that a substantial federal claim does not cease to arise under federal law merely because the court may conclude that it does not authorize recovery. The exceptions concern claims immaterial and made solely to obtain jurisdiction or wholly insubstantial and frivolous. Bell is not a waiver of sovereign immunity and does not permit a court to assume constitutional injury. Its statements about choosing remedies cannot override the Privacy Act's specific relief limits or the selected Meyer holding.

**Warth v. Seldin, 422 U.S. 490, 499–502, 514 (1975),** says that standing does not depend on success of the contention that conduct is illegal, though the nature and source of the claimed right matter; statutory recognition can make a previously uncognizable personal invasion actionable. A distinct and palpable injury to the claimant remains necessary. Warth's unfavorable causation result concerned unsupported links through others' housing development decisions. It does not require an additional employment loss when the alleged protected interest is personal informational privacy. Its footnote 13, at 503, expressly separates the relevant exclusion from downstream objective or subjective disadvantages. That is a limited analogy, not a Privacy Act holding.

**Havens Realty Corp. v. Coleman, 455 U.S. 363, 373–375 (1982),** is a concrete illustration: a person actually given the discriminatory misinformation prohibited by the statute could claim an injury to her own statutory right even though she did not intend to rent and expected the answer. The white tester who received accurate information lacked that particular injury. Havens concerns an express Fair Housing Act cause and a damages request against private defendants, at the pleading stage. It supplies no federal sovereign-consent rule, no Privacy Act remedy, and no exemption from Lujan's summary-judgment evidence requirement. Its recognition that a live damages claim can survive the loss of prospective issues also depends on an actual continued request for legally compensable relief, 370–371.

Under the selected **Lujan** and actual **Lee** rules, the facts supporting challenged standing elements must be established at summary judgment, with favorable reasonable inferences. The actual continued keeping of identified personal associational records is reported, rather than merely hypothesized. Whether that act invades a concrete personal interest Congress protected against unnecessary retention is a legal question for the court. If that interest is cognizable, traceability is direct: the FBI itself keeps the information. Proving prospective employers know of the file is then unnecessary to that different theory. **Laird**'s subjective-chill rule remains relevant if the asserted injury is only subjective fear; it did not adjudicate Congress's later-created §(e)(7) right.

Petition 4's actual personal-chill allegation is relevant but does not, without more, prove injury at summary judgment. The direct privacy-interest theory rests on the established personal recordkeeping and the legally asserted retention protection; it must not depend on silently promoting that allegation into medical, economic, or reputational evidence. Nor is current absence of a statutory violation a standing prerequisite: whether lawful collection authorizes continuing retention is the merits dispute. No later cessation or destruction is established by the recovered papers, and none is invented either to preserve or extinguish the controversy.

Redressability asks whether relief within judicial power would likely repair at least part of the supported personal injury **if the claimant prevails**. It does not require a prior finding that the claimant will prove a statutory violation, intentional or willful conduct, the precise amount of actual damages, or every record's lack of pertinence. The actual **Alexander** holding confirms that meaningful partial relief may support a controversy despite uncertainty about ultimate downstream recovery, with procedural-stage and defendant-specific limits preserved. Removing prohibited retained material would address an ongoing retention injury; an authorized personal monetary award could address a compensable past injury. Neither observation proves that this action legally offers either remedy.

The strongest contrary argument is consequently narrower than “a statute was invoked, so there is no injury” or “damages are unproved, so no standing.” It is that the actual action may offer no legally available relief for the particular supported invasion, or that the claimed personal invasion is not established beyond an abstract statutory objection. If every actual remedy is barred as a matter of law, a prayer cannot create redress. The selected **Arizonans** holding supplies that boundary; a monetary claim against a defendant the substantive statute does not make liable cannot be manufactured by a general prayer or consent. Fees and litigation expenses alone are not an independent compensatory injury under Steel Co.

## 3. The three possible remedial routes must be kept distinct

### A. Section (g)(1)(D) and (g)(4): money for a statutory violation

The official 1997 text permits a civil action when an agency fails to comply with another Act provision “in such a way as to have an adverse effect on an individual.” A §(e)(7) claim can use this catch-all route. The text then makes the United States liable in a (C) or (D) suit where the agency acted “intentional[ly] or willful[ly],” for “actual damages sustained ... as a result” and the stated $1,000 minimum for a person entitled to recovery, plus costs and reasonable fees. An “adverse effect” under (D) is not the “adverse determination” under (C).

**Doe v. FBI, 936 F.2d 1346, 1359–1361 & nn.1, 4 (D.C. Cir. 1991),** is especially important for source discipline. Doe did not appeal denial of his damages claim, and only statutory expungement was before the court. His direct §(e)(7) theory failed because the district court actually found no adverse effect and he did not challenge that finding. The court refused an end run around (g)'s remedies. Those are **Doe's** record and appellate limits, not Lindblom's. The original MacArthur opinion cites Doe on retention, but does not transplant Doe's findings, abandoned money claim, or subsection-specific disposition.

The correctly recovered **Albright v. United States, 732 F.2d 181, 184–190 (D.C. Cir. 1984),** refines the money-route analysis. At 186 it expressly holds emotional trauma can be an “adverse effect,” while requiring proof that the challenged recordation caused the claimed injury. It affirmed the actual factfinder's contrary causation and intent findings; it did **not** decide whether nonpecuniary injury is “actual damages” or whether the statutory minimum can be recovered without pecuniary loss. At 184 it describes the fourth proposed recovery prerequisite as actual damages “or, alternatively,” entitlement to the minimum. Thus this packet does not import a later Supreme Court requirement concerning proof of actual damages, or a later limit on nonpecuniary damages. Nor does it assert that emotional trauma was proved in Lindblom's record.

At 188–190, Albright distinguishes a voluntary agency act from an intentional or willful violation: acts without grounds to believe them lawful, or flagrant disregard of statutory rights, can satisfy the specified standard. This is a lower-court liability rule, not a new Supreme Court holding or a factual determination about the FBI here. A serious disagreement about retention's meaning may matter to ultimate fault; it is not itself a proved finding of lawful reliance or of willfulness.

**Necessary distinction:** An actual presented money claim, a personal injury it could compensate, and a legally applicable statutory consent-to-suit route must be identified. But absence of an already-entered favorable damages finding is normal before trial or after summary judgment on a different legal ground. It is not itself an Article III defect. A court may need to decide the legal scope of the monetary waiver; it need not adjudicate every disputed liability or damages fact merely to recognize a substantial action within that scope. The exact text and then-effective law control any jurisdictional characterization; this packet imports no later universal rule classifying all §(g) conditions as merits or jurisdictional.

**Presentation and preservation are not a missing-facts veto.** The affirmative sources show Lindblom's statutory retention claim, his own request for damages, dismissal of the whole complaint, and continued appeal of the Privacy Act claim. They expressly identify other abandoned claims and identify no abandonment of statutory money. Taken together, these sources are sufficient to identify a substantial potential §(g)(1)(D)/(g)(4) monetary route for jurisdictional analysis. They do not conclusively prove ultimate entitlement, but a renewed prayer recital or express citation of those subsections is not a further evidentiary prerequisite created by this packet. Section (g)(1)(D) itself authorizes an individual civil action for the qualifying violation; Bell requires examination of the substantial federal claim rather than an assumption that failure to demonstrate recovery defeats federal-question jurisdiction. Applying the statute's remedy to the pleaded statutory wrong and actually requested money does not, just by identifying the correct subsection, invent a new claim. Neither Bell nor ordinary treatment of a claim and its requested relief supplies omitted injury facts, enlarges sovereign consent, or excuses an actual legally material forfeiture.

The competing proposition would need a concrete legal or record basis: for example, an actual limitation allocating money exclusively to abandoned counts, an express abandonment, a preservation rule demonstrably triggered by an identified omission in the actual appellate presentation, or a legal conclusion that §(g) offers no monetary remedy for the supported personal injury. No such count-allocation or abandonment fact is established in the recovered sources. The historical petition's focus on expungement does not establish it, and its historical scope does not replace the authorized hypothetical review. **No additional preservation premise is indispensable merely because the full complaint is unrecovered or later advocacy omits subsection numbers.** If a later adjudicative analysis identifies a genuinely operative preservation rule, it must explain that rule and the supported triggering event before treating clarification as necessary; it cannot infer both from source silence.

Separately, §(g)(1)(D)'s express “adverse effect” must receive its own construction. Havens's different statute does not decide whether the same personal privacy invasion satisfies both Article III and this additional statutory text. The answer is a legal question on the supported circumstances, not a fact that can be reconstructed by calling every violation adverse. If a distinct harmful consequence is legally required, its actual support must be identified; this source packet supplies no new evidence of emotional or pecuniary loss. That substantive statutory issue is the material fork; absence of a magic-word damages citation is not.

### B. Sections (d), (g)(1)(A), and (g)(2): amendment or expungement

Section (d)(2) provides for an amendment request; (d)(3) supplies agency review; (g)(1)(A) addresses refusal or a failure of that review; and (g)(2)(A) authorizes a de novo judicial amendment order in that suit. Access under (g)(1)(B)/(g)(3) is a separate route. A request for FOIA access does not, by its label alone, prove a request for Privacy Act amendment.

**Nagel v. HEW, 725 F.2d 1438, 1440–1442 & nn.1–5 (D.C. Cir. 1984),** requires exhaustion for an ordinary amendment claim, but expressly says a suit to redress illegal recordation of protected speech does not require exhaustion and describes damages as well as amendment or expungement as appropriate §(e)(7) relief. Its explanation emphasizes that a damages claimant need not exhaust. It resolved the actual case on the law-enforcement character of the work-performance records. One cannot erase its express favorable discussion; equally, it did not decide that every §(e)(7) plaintiff can obtain an injunction without a cause, adverse effect, or applicable waiver.

**Clarkson v. IRS, 678 F.2d 1368, 1375–1377 & n.11 (11th Cir. 1982),** treats amendment/expungement of §(e)(7) records as available through (d)(2)–(3), including records outside a name-retrieved system. It records actual exhaustion, 1370, and its footnote expressly confines statutory injunctive relief to wrongful withholding and wrongful refusal to amend. It reserves actual jurisdictional facts on remand. Its conditional “may be entitled” is not an automatic purge command. Nagel, Clarkson, and Doe together establish a serious route question; they do not justify silently selecting the most favorable sentence from one case while discarding the others' predicates.

**Exemptions:** Neither §(j) nor §(k) authorizes exemption from (e)(7). The 1997 FBI Central Records System regulation lists (d) and (g), among other provisions, but only insofar as the statutory exemption authority applies; it permits, rather than requires, FBI waiver in the specified circumstances. **Doe**, 1351–1354, distinguishes lawful exemption from (d), which defeats a derivative (g)(1)(A) claim, from an attempted remedial exemption for a nonexemptible duty, which cannot defeat Congress's mandatory protection. It requires a supported law-enforcement purpose for records claimed to be exempt and does not treat the CRS label as conclusive. The burden and test are those of the cited circuit decision, not a new fact-specific Supreme Court ruling.

A FOIA withholding exemption, lawful collection under (e)(7), and a lawful Privacy Act (d) exemption are related but distinct inquiries. Neither the published in camera finding nor the fact of lawful original collection alone decides all regulatory predicates. Conversely, if the actual record establishes the applicable exemption, it must be given its lawful effect; no invented waiver follows from disclosure of some documents or the agency's failure to discuss an exemption in a published appellate passage.

### C. Section 702 and independent prospective equity

The selected **Reich** holding recognizes prospective equitable restraint of implementing officers acting beyond statutory authority unless Congress displaced that remedy. Section 702 waives immunity for nonmoney official action even without an APA review cause, but preserves independent review limits and statutes expressly or impliedly forbidding the relief sought. Section 702 is not itself a new damages action or unlimited permission to bypass §552a(g).

There are serious competing readings. Lindblom can point to an express, nonexemptible duty and the actual request to stop an asserted ongoing invasion; the Government can point to the carefully differentiated Privacy Act remedies and **Doe**'s refusal to create an end run. This is a legal remedial/preclusion dispute, not evidence that a record request was never made. The court must determine whether the existing action actually presents a permissible equitable theory; this packet develops an analytical possibility and does not certify that counsel pleaded or preserved a separate APA count.

Section 702 expressly covers an action alleging that an agency **or** an officer acted officially and permits naming the United States. Its command that a mandatory decree specify the responsible officer, by name **or title**, is a constraint on the decree. The mere absence of an officer's personal name from the caption does not, from that text alone, prove a fatal defect in every nonmoney agency suit. At the same time, Reich does not automatically resolve the cause/preclusion question for this FBI-only action. **Meyer** forbids manufacturing direct constitutional damages against the agency from a waiver; it does not turn all statutory or equitable relief into agency Bivens damages.

## 5. Supported narrower paths before reconstruction

These are analytical alternatives, not dispositions entered by this packet.

1. **Decide a supported threshold or remedial question on the existing record.** The constitutional claim-specific traceability question can be considered separately. A supported legal determination that a particular route is unavailable may narrow relief without deciding the continuing-retention merits. It must not silently eliminate an independently surviving route. A comprehensive statutory dismissal needs a comprehensive reason, not an inference from missing papers.
2. **Recognize a live statutory controversy without deciding ultimate entitlement.** If the actual action presents a nonfrivolous, legally available remedy for the supported personal privacy invasion, the retention question is not barred merely because later proof will determine fault, amount, or record-specific relief. This requires reasons grounded in the actual claim, waiver, and governing law, not hypothetical jurisdiction.
3. **Use a confined corrective disposition where a genuinely necessary threshold was not resolved below.** The selected Arizonans and actual Lee holdings permit appropriate jurisdictional correction without adjudicating the disputed retention merits. Any remand must identify the independent judicial question that needs resolution. It need not order a hearing, new evidence, amendment of the complaint, production of classified material, or a particular result. Operator inability to retrieve a document is not an independent judicial reason for remand.
4. **Reserve ultimate relief after a lawful merits determination.** If jurisdiction and the action are established, correcting a rule of statutory construction can leave actual continuing pertinence, lawful exceptions, willfulness, monetary entitlement, and the scope of any permissible injunction to ordinary further proceedings. Not knowing the ultimate remedy does not itself require reconstruction before a legal question can be decided.

A conditional set of alternatives does not, by itself, complete adjudication. But neither does every alternative's possible dependence on further facts stop all available narrower adjudication. Any proposed §14 premise must identify why the **selected scope of review** cannot be resolved from supported facts or a narrower question.
