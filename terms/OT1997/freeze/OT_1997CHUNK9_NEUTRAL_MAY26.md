## Calderon v. Ashmus — No. 97-391

### Identity, vehicle and questions

Argued March 24, 1998; proposed decision May 26, 1998. Certiorari under §1254(1) to the Ninth Circuit, 123 F.3d 1199 (August 18, 1997). The circuit reviewed the preliminary injunction under §1292(a)(1), not a final declaratory judgment under §1291: other district-court claims remained pending. It reviewed the declaration's premises as bound up with the injunction, affirmed the injunction with a clarification, and denied appellate §1988 fees because it had reached no merits of the §1983 claim.

**Corrected scope:** The granted issues concern the Eleventh Amendment and the First Amendment objection to an injunction prohibiting state officials from invoking chapter 154. Article III is an antecedent question that the Court must resolve when disputed; it is not inaccurately recast here as the sole question actually granted. If Article III is satisfied, the granted immunity and speech/petitioning questions remain live. California's actual statutory qualification is the premise of the lower injunction, but a merits decision on every qualification defect is not automatically within the grant. The precise formal grant text is not separately recovered; this description follows the sanitized official source recitation.

### Record and the judgment below

Ashmus filed on April 24, 1996, for a class of California death-sentenced prisoners awaiting execution while pursuing state and federal challenges, except those whose convictions and sentences had been affirmed on direct review before June 6, 1989. California asserted that its unitary-review system qualified under §2265 and threatened to invoke chapter 154. The lower court found that more than 130 class members lacked appointed counsel and that appointment could take years. The class also includes prisoners with counsel. These are reported lower-court findings and descriptions, not proof of each class member's personal filing deadline.

The district court declared California unqualified and enjoined the officials from seeking chapter 154 benefits in proceedings involving class members. The circuit clarified the injunction to address the current state of California law in capital habeas cases; it permitted later modification if events changed. It treated the threatened application as a present pressure to file an inadequately developed petition or risk the shorter period, and held that prospective officer relief was available. It rejected the First Amendment objection because officials remained free to criticize the ruling; their effort to claim the adjudicated litigation benefit was restrained. The circuit did not reach the §1983 merits.

The circuit identified separate qualification deficiencies: nonbinding recommended competency standards rather than a highest-court rule; inadequate compensation for investigation beyond the appellate record; and failure actually to appoint counsel after an indigent prisoner's acceptance. It expressly rejected an automatic requirement of prior habeas experience for appointed counsel. These findings must not become a Supreme Court merits holding merely because review of the injunction presents them. The lower opinion's paraphrase suggesting any late first petition becomes successive is not the statutory text and is not adopted as entering law.

### Entering snapshots

1. **Article III and declaratory relief.** Seminole Tribe (March 27, 1996), Part I, has seven direct joins—O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer—for conclusive adjudication of legal relations despite later administrative steps. An immediate coercive decree is not necessary to judicial power. Raines (June 26, 1997) requires concrete personal injury but decides an individual-legislator claim, not all anticipatory litigation. Steel Co. (June 2, 1997), with its exact exception and sequencing limits, requires Article III before contested statutory merits. Current Texas (March 31, 1998) and Ohio Forestry (May 18, 1998), both unanimous, examine fitness and actual present hardship; neither requires physical injury or violation of law before review. Neither decides the different concrete pressure asserted here. The Declaratory Judgment Act, §§2201–2202, supplies a remedy in a controversy otherwise within federal jurisdiction; §1331 does not remove Article III.
2. **State officials and remedy.** Seminole Tribe's officer holding (Souter, Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Ginsburg, Breyer) permits relief against a connected officer for an ongoing federal duty within that statute's complete remedial limits; it supplies no universal statutory exception. Coeur d'Alene (June 23, 1997; the same five) rejects a categorical sovereign-lands exception but requires an actual ongoing federal violation, a connected official, and proper prospective relief. Green v. Mansour, 474 U.S. 64 (1985), preserves the absence-of-ongoing-violation objection. Neither a federal litigation position nor a future federal court's possible error is automatically an officer's present violation. No IGRA burden, mediation step or contempt limit is transplanted to AEDPA.
3. **AEDPA.** The annex supplies the exact modules “Qualifying State counsel systems for special capital habeas procedures” and “Capital habeas filing and claim scope under chapter 154,” both effective April 24, 1996. The unitary route's transcript deferral, express counsel-continuity request, appointment order, compensation and competency predicates are separate. The 180-day period and each tolling provision are preserved; no prisoner's clock is calculated here. Chapter 154's applicability to pending cases does not prove California's qualification. Chapter 153's one-year limitation remains a separate regime.

### Meaningful lawful alternatives and limits

**Article III path A:** A standalone declaration selects a procedural rule for future custody claims without adjudicating their substantive entitlement. Aetna Life v. Haworth, 300 U.S. 227 (1937), requires a definite dispute and conclusive relief; Coffman v. Breeze Corporations, 323 U.S. 316 (1945), and Public Service Commission v. Wycoff, 344 U.S. 237 (1952), support testing whether an isolated defensive issue is an advisory fragment. The strongest objection is the present forced filing choice, counsel delay and potentially irreversible loss asserted below: a declaration may settle a concrete legal relation even though habeas merits remain. Seminole forecloses dismissal merely because the relief is declaratory. If jurisdiction fails, vacate the operative injunction/declaration as appropriate and remand for jurisdictional treatment; do not pronounce California qualified or decide the underlying convictions.

**Article III path B:** The common qualification issue determines an immediate statutory filing burden and can be conclusively settled between adverse parties. Texas and Ohio Forestry permit review of established present burdens even if other proceedings follow. The strongest objection is that qualification can be litigated in an actual habeas proceeding and this suit attempts to pre-adjudicate only one component. A model supporting jurisdiction must identify the legal relation settled and whose concrete injury is redressed, rather than rest on expense or aggregate efficiency.

**If jurisdiction exists:** Compare the connected-officer/ongoing-violation route with the argument that asserting a disputed procedural entitlement for another court to resolve is no present violation of federal law. A speech-based invalidation requires distinguishing a legitimate injunction enforcing an actual adjudication from a prohibition on seeking judicial consideration of a disputed legal argument. A narrower remedy could remove an impermissible anti-assertion injunction while preserving a declaration, but only if that declaration independently satisfies jurisdiction, immunity and statutory remedy. The speech objection cannot supply jurisdiction or silently eliminate the granted immunity issue. No general censorship holding or statewide finding of bad faith is supplied.

## County of Sacramento v. Lewis — No. 96-1337

### Identity, channel and preserved case

Argued December 9, 1997; proposed decision May 26, 1998. Certiorari under §1254(1) to the Ninth Circuit, 98 F.3d 434 (October 9, 1996). The lower appeal followed final summary judgment under §1291, rather than an interlocutory evidentiary-sufficiency appeal. The question concerns the culpability required for the substantive-due-process claim from this pursuit; individual qualified immunity is an independently available, potentially dispositive ground. The circuit reversed Smith's individual immunity judgment but affirmed the municipal judgments. Plaintiffs did not appeal the state-law dispositions. No cross-petition enlarging those municipal or state components is established. The County's appearance in the caption does not reopen its favorable judgment.

### Record, assumed facts and lower rationale

On May 22, 1990, Smith and Stapp had finished responding to a fight when a motorcycle driven by minor Brian Willard, with sixteen-year-old Philip Lewis as passenger, passed. Neither boy was involved in the fight. On the plaintiff-favorable account, Smith could not hear Stapp's command; the motorcycle passed slowly between the patrol cars and then accelerated. Smith made a three-point turn and pursued. The chase lasted about seventy-five seconds over 1.3 miles, averaged about sixty miles per hour, reached approximately one hundred, and involved residential roads, traffic lights and turns. Neither rider wore a helmet. At points Smith followed 100–150 feet behind despite a substantially longer stopping distance. Two cars and a bicyclist were forced from the road in the account credited for summary judgment.

The motorcycle stopped after a crest and turn; whether Lewis remained seated or dismounted is unclear. Smith braked but struck Lewis, who died. The lower opinion expressly reports it undisputed that Smith did not intend the collision. Plaintiffs asserted neither a Fourth nor Eighth Amendment claim. Smith's motives cannot be newly characterized as a purpose to injure merely from dangerous speed, and the account cannot be silently converted into a finding that the boys were known dangerous felons.

The departmental order required evaluating seriousness, necessity and danger, broadcasting specified pursuit information, and aborting when the hazards outweighed immediate apprehension. The lower opinion reports noncommunication conceded and other violations alleged/supported on the favorable view. Those rules may inform evidence of disregard; their breach is neither a federal constitutional rule nor the relevant law that establishes federal notice. The district court assumed a violation and granted individual immunity. The circuit used deliberate indifference/reckless disregard, found a triable issue and treated pre-May-1990 cases as adequate notice. It relied on Wood II, Fargo as modified, Roach, Jones, Checki, Garner and Brower, preserving their respective facts and outcomes.

### Entering snapshots

1. **Constitutional source and underlying wrong.** Albright (January 24, 1994) has no controlling rationale; its seven-vote judgment is not a general displacement rule. Graham v. Connor, 490 U.S. 386 (1989), and actual unanimous Lanier (March 31, 1997) require use of a specific provision where it covers the conduct, but do not exclude all noncustodial official wrongdoing. Brower v. County of Inyo, 489 U.S. 593 (1989), requires termination through intentionally applied means for seizure. An unintended collision and failed command to stop do not automatically establish that predicate. Daniels, 474 U.S. 327 (1986), excludes negligence while reserving recklessness/gross-negligence questions; Rochin, 342 U.S. 165 (1952), supplies the conscience-shocking official-abuse tradition.
2. **Context and culpability.** Collins (February 26, 1992) separates a substantive wrong from municipal attribution, and its eight-Justice alternative arbitrariness holding is confined to the pleaded voluntary-workplace omissions. Pinder (May 21, 1996) distinguishes affirmative deprivation from an omitted protection duty; it reserves the former's merits and culpability. Uhlrig (February 18, 1997) likewise decides conduct-time notice without adopting the lower court's five-part test. Farmer (June 6, 1994) requires actual awareness and unreasonable disregard for the prison-safety claim, with reasonable-response and permissible-inference limits. It is an analogy, not an already controlling police-pursuit test. Doe v. Taylor (October 16, 1995) concerns actual supervisory knowledge, authority, reasonable opportunity and causation in deliberate sexual abuse; its five-vote rule is not a universal emergency standard.
3. **Emergency analogy.** O'Connor's Whitley v. Albers, 475 U.S. 312 (1986), distinguishes competing emergency obligations from ordinary deliberate-indifference settings, while expressly reserving the Fourteenth Amendment culpability question outside prison security. Stevens joined Marshall's dissent except its final due-process footnote, emphasizing wantonness and preservation of disputed emergency facts. Whitley therefore supplies both a serious analogy and a material limit; neither its holding nor the dissent already resolves this civilian pursuit.
4. **Immunity and remedy.** Harlow/Anderson, actual Elder, Pinder, Uhlrig and Lanier require all relevant law and particularized apparent unlawfulness, without a factual twin or numerical consensus. The notice cutoff is May 22, 1990. Later clarity cannot supply earlier notice. Current Smith v. Mattox (January 12, 1998) permits obvious applications on complete assumed facts, rather than injury alone, and retains immunity separately from underlying liability. It supplies no Fourth Amendment merits rule for this nonseizure claim. The annex includes the relevant exact qualified-immunity module and each authority limit.

### Meaningful alternatives and objections

**Path A—emergency-sensitive constitutional rule:** Require a culpability showing that distinguishes genuine abuse from even grave bad judgment during a rapidly developing pursuit. Whitley's competing-obligation analysis supports asking whether a harmful purpose unrelated to a legitimate enforcement aim or comparably arbitrary abuse is present. Its strongest objection is that pursuit was voluntary, no violent offense justified immediate capture on the favorable record, and the officer allegedly created and prolonged the danger; no custody or riot analogy makes all such choices immune. Any purpose-based rule must explain its precise trigger and what changes if there was time for actual deliberation. A model cannot invent proof of good faith as an adjudicated fact.

**Path B—conscious disregard may suffice on this account:** Apply a serious actual-awareness rule where an officer creates life-threatening danger without a supported necessity and has a meaningful opportunity to desist. Farmer, Doe, the direct-deprivation distinction and the preconduct circuit sources support examining culpability rather than categorical custody. Its strongest objection is that those settings supply different duties, time for reflection and causal structures; subjective awareness of pursuit danger is not necessarily arbitrary abuse. The model must identify whether the summary-judgment account permits the requisite inference, without making policy violation negligence per se under the Constitution or importing Canton’s municipal attribution standard as the underlying right.

**Path C—notice-only disposition:** Even if the assumed conduct could support a constitutional violation, the May 1990 law may not have made this pursuit's unlawfulness apparent. This route preserves the culpability merits and reverses only the individual immunity denial. The strongest objection is that Checki, Wood II and the existing deadly-force principles may have supplied particularized warning or obvious coverage. Roach and Jones rejected liability on their actual facts; their cautionary language is not a judgment sustaining this claim. A finding that no exact pursuit precedent exists is insufficient. Any affirmance of immunity denial still leaves actual culpability and causation for ordinary proceedings; no damages award or automatic trial command follows.

## Kiowa Tribe of Oklahoma v. Manufacturing Technologies — No. 96-1037

### Identity, posture and material record

Argued January 12, 1998; proposed decision May 26, 1998. Certiorari under §1257 to the Oklahoma Court of Civil Appeals, First Division, following state high-court refusal of review confirmed by control. The supplied packet's attribution of the merits holding to the Oklahoma Supreme Court is corrected. The trial court denied the Tribe's immunity motion and entered judgment for Manufacturing Technologies; the intermediate court affirmed on an off-reservation commercial-contract exception. The Tribe seeks reversal on immunity. No individual officer, separate corporation or independently pleaded enforcement route is substituted for the Tribe.

The federally recognized Tribe's industrial development commission agreed to purchase stock from Manufacturing Technologies. Its business-committee chairman signed a $285,000 note plus interest in the Tribe's name. The note recites Carnegie, where the Tribe has a trust-land complex, as execution place; the creditor asserts execution and delivery in Oklahoma City and payment there. Location remains disputed. The note lacks a governing-law clause and expressly preserves sovereign rights. Default and the monetary judgment supply a concrete controversy. The present question is whether federal tribal immunity permits this direct contract action assuming the off-reservation commercial setting asserted below, absent waiver or congressional authorization. A categorical immunity rule could make the location dispute immaterial; a geographic limitation could make it indispensable.

### Entering snapshots

**Tribal immunity is distinct from state immunity and substantive regulation.** Potawatomi, 498 U.S. 505 (February 26, 1991), unanimously preserved immunity absent clear tribal waiver or congressional abrogation, rejected waiver merely from initiating suit and rejected a commercial-business limitation for the trust-land cigarette operation. It distinguished a substantive state tax obligation from an action against the Tribe. Its majority treated trust land as Indian country and distinguished Mescalero's off-reservation setting. It did not directly decide this off-reservation commercial contract.

Stevens's separate Potawatomi writing accepted the established damages-immunity rule but expressly questioned whether it extended to off-territory commerce and distinguished prospective equitable relief. His Puyallup III opinion, 433 U.S. 165 (1977), applied tribal immunity to the Tribe while preserving properly obtained jurisdiction over individual members; an order addressed to the Tribe required its own lawful basis. Participation representing members did not waive immunity. Santa Clara Pueblo, 436 U.S. 49, 58–59 (1978), supplies the requirement of an unequivocal waiver and preserves congressional responsibility. Mescalero Apache Tribe v. Jones, 411 U.S. 145 (1973), concerns applicable nondiscriminatory law beyond reservation borders; applicability is not itself a holding about a direct damages remedy.

Actual Seminole Tribe concerns State/connected-officer amenability under IGRA and establishes no reciprocal tribal consent, common immunity rule for every sovereign or direct holding about private tribal contracts. Actual Chickasaw Nation separates member residence, legal tax incidence and express authorization. Current Alaska v. Venetie (February 25, 1998) separates political status from land classification; it does not make tribal recognition disappear off Indian country or decide immunity. Current Montana v. Crow Tribe (May 18, 1998) separates invalid state taxation from a tribe's entitlement to another payer's money; neither automatic tribal recovery nor automatic private recovery follows from it.

No statutory abrogation or actual waiver is established in the neutral materials. That is a limit on this claim as presented, not an invented finding that no law or document could ever support one. Contract liability, power to make the contract, immunity from suit and execution against particular assets are separate questions.

### Supported alternative paths

**Path A—preserve immunity absent authorized exception.** The Tribe is the defendant and the sought relief is a money judgment. The established status-based immunity, unequivocal-waiver requirement, congressional treatment and distinction between substantive obligation and amenability support rejecting a new commercial/geographic exception. Strongest counterargument: the cited holdings largely arose on tribal lands or involved collective tribal rights, and an off-territory commercial actor imposes risk on nonmembers outside its internal government. A rule preserving immunity must confront that scope argument rather than pretend Potawatomi already adjudicated these facts. It may leave the geographic dispute unresolved because neither asserted location changes its legal result.

**Path B—confine the common-law immunity in off-reservation commerce.** Stevens's Potawatomi reservation, Mescalero's ordinary-state-law principle and the limited factual reach of earlier immunity cases support examining whether immunity follows the tribe into this market transaction. Strongest counterargument: substantive regulation and forum amenability have been deliberately separated; a judicial exception may undermine an established federal protection on which Congress legislates. A model must specify whether it distinguishes or limits precedent and why congressional authority does not foreclose the common-law refinement. If the exception depends on off-reservation execution/performance, the contested location cannot be found against the Tribe without the actual lower factual resolution; a bounded remand is available. A broad commercial exception, including reservation activity, faces Potawatomi directly.

**Path C—waiver or different defendant:** Not presently supported. Signing a payment obligation, defaulting or promising payment in a city is not itself an unequivocal consent to suit; the express sovereign-rights clause points the other way. No independent official, entity or in-rem claim is invented to circumvent the actual defendant. A request to rely on such a path requires the particular document, authorization and preservation source first.

### Individual source anchors

The fresh model must test the same two paths for each Justice, and identify additional personal authority if it is necessary to justify a material exception.
