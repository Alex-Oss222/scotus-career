## Enhanced paths and individual commitments

### Common alternatives and limits

**Facial path A — sustain the threshold.** Polling retains practical uses even after reasonable doubt permits withdrawal: it may confirm actual preferences, avert disruption, and guide an employer that does not wish to withdraw merely because it can defend a charge. The Board can rationally weigh disruption and employee protection; competing reasons for higher or lower polling thresholds do not compel a distinct numerical threshold. The strongest counterargument is that safeguards and the comparatively limited consequences of a poll make the unitary threshold unnecessarily restrictive and deficient in statutory grounding.

**Facial path B — reject the threshold.** Sections 7 and 8(c), §8(a)(1)'s interference/coercion limits, safeguarded polling, and the different consequences of polling, withdrawal, and a binding Board election support invalidation of this restriction. Serious speech concerns reinforce statutory construction under surviving DeBartolo, Catholic Bishop, Gissel, Thomas v. Collins, and Virginia Electric principles. The strongest counterargument is that the Act establishes no unrestricted polling entitlement, Gissel reserved whether particular allowances were mandatory, and rational labor-stability choices need not maximize investigative convenience. The rejecting commitments do not decide a freestanding First Amendment violation or substitute a court-designed circuit formula; the Board retains responsibility to formulate a lawful policy.

**Application path A — deny enforcement.** Under §§10(e)–(f), APA §706, and Universal Camera, the question is objectively supported reasonable uncertainty, not established minority. Seven accepted or assumed individual expressions, Marsh's ambiguous dissatisfaction, Bloch's night-shift account, and Mohr's informed report must receive their cumulative probative force. Mohr's union role and substantial workforce continuity make the categorical old-workforce objection untenable. No headcount is required, group reports are not mechanically added as nonoverlapping employees, and the postannouncement 19–13 poll supplies no retroactive predicate. The strongest counterargument is the ALJ's actual combined assessment and its source, interview, departmental, and retained-workforce limitations. The reversing Justices find those concerns inadequate to sustain the particular no-doubt conclusion, especially where the Board itself gave Mohr's old-workforce report no weight. They find the record sufficient to direct denial of enforcement, rather than merely obtain a better explanation.

**Application path B — affirm.** The Board may employ lawful, experience-based caution about interview remarks, dissatisfaction versus repudiation, and unsubstantiated secondhand reports, while considering all the evidence. The ALJ's full combined assessment supplied actual reasons; the opinion did not simply omit Mohr or demand an express majority count. Universal Camera reserves choices between fairly conflicting inferences and contemplates rare Supreme Court intervention when the appellate standard was misapprehended or grossly misapplied. The strongest counterargument is that specialized practice cannot turn reasonable uncertainty into certainty of opposition or treat the successor presumption as proof of unanimity. The affirming Justices reject that characterization of this supported application. They retain the scope objection as a separate ground and do not depend on a categorical bar to all circumstantial evidence.

**Remand-only alternative.** An actual legal-standard defect leaving more than one lawful evidentiary outcome could warrant reconsideration. The reversing coalition instead finds the existing finding unsustainable on this record; the affirming coalition finds no remand-triggering error. No Associate retains the provisional intermediate mandate. No new hearing, election, current-preference determination, automatic decertification, or separate charging determination is supplied.
