## Caron

Massachusetts restored Caron's civil rights by operation of law rather than by an individualized pardon. The parties agreed to restoration apart from the disputed federal unless-clause effect. Massachusetts permitted him to possess rifles and shotguns: he had the required firearm-identification permit and his convictions were more than five years old. Massachusetts did not completely prohibit handgun possession; the remaining restriction barred possession outside his home or business. The reported provisions are Mass. Gen. Laws §§140:123, 140:129B, 140:129C and, for the remaining handgun restriction, §§140:121, 140:131, 269:10.

The initial federal sentence counted one California and three Massachusetts convictions. The First Circuit en banc, 77 F.3d 1 (1996), rejected the district court's view that restoration must be offender-specific and remanded. On remand the district court excluded the Massachusetts convictions under its interpretation of the federal unless clause, 941 F. Supp. 238, 251–254 (D. Mass. 1996). The presently reviewed First Circuit judgment counted them because a significant firearm restriction remained. The federal jury had convicted Caron on four §922(g)(1) counts; the December 1993 search seized six rifles/shotguns and 6,823 rounds of ammunition. No separate constitutional firearms holding is part of this record extraction.

## Gebser

In October 1992 the parents of two other students complained to the high-school principal about Waldrop's classroom comments. The principal arranged a meeting. According to his account, Waldrop denied believing his remarks offensive, apologized, and said they would not recur. The principal cautioned Waldrop about his comments and told the guidance counselor about the meeting. He did not transmit the parents' complaint to the superintendent, who was the district's Title IX coordinator. This was a complaint about comments, not a report of a sexual relationship.

Gebser did not herself report the relationship to school officials. Police discovered the relationship in January 1993, and the district terminated Waldrop. Policy evidence requires qualification: superintendent Collier's affidavit said the district had a policy, App. 44a–45a; her deposition in plaintiffs' summary-judgment materials was described as saying there was no formal policy, Record 332, exhibit 2, pp.42,44. Collier did not remember whether a handbook informing students about grievance procedures had been distributed, App.72a–73a. Gebser testified that she probably would have reported at the beginning had she known what to do, and that she was bewildered and frightened, App.64a–65a. These are evidentiary accounts, not findings of institutional fault, knowledge of the relationship, or causation.

The district court granted judgment on all claims against the district. Petitioners appealed only Title IX. Other claims against Waldrop were remanded to state court. The regulatory requirements implicated include adopting and publishing a procedure for prompt and equitable resolution of complaints, 34 C.F.R. §106.8(b), and notice of nondiscrimination, §106.9(a). Whether a regulatory omission supplies discrimination, damages liability, knowledge, or causation is a legal issue, not an independently established fact.

## Bajakajian

On June 9, 1994, customs officers found $357,144 carried by Bajakajian and family as they prepared to leave the United States. Count One charged willful failure to report under §§5316 and 5322; he pleaded guilty. Count Two charged false statements under §1001 and was dismissed by agreement, not resolved by acquittal. Count Three sought forfeiture, tried to the court. The district court found that the funds were lawful and intended for a lawful debt and were unconnected to another crime. Those findings do not mean that no false statements occurred.

The statutory maximum under §5322(a) was five years and $250,000. The applicable Guidelines maxima were six months and $5,000. These must remain distinct. The actual sentence was three years' probation, $5,000 fine, and $15,000 forfeiture. The United States appealed seeking forfeiture of the full $357,144; Bajakajian did not cross-appeal. The lower panel majority rejected currency as an instrumentality and therefore did not reach its proportionality prong; Wallace concurred in the result on proportionality despite considering the currency an instrumentality. The validity of the unchallenged $15,000 amount and authority to substitute it are outside the scope supplied by that appeal.

## Pearson / Hines

The conduct occurred January 19, 1992. Hines was in boxer shorts, hands cuffed behind his back, being escorted from his shower. Pearson reported a prohibited stop to receive an unidentified object and a threat. Hines denied stopping and later grieved Pearson's alleged force/threat. Evidence supporting the jury included other guards' descriptions of Hines as a frequent complainer, his prior grievances, and evidence that Hines told Pearson that morning he would file a grievance. Pearson did not contest the jury's finding that the accusation was false. The question was whether sufficient evidence supported retaliatory motive and whether the other asserted legal bars applied.

The jury awarded $100 compensatory and $1,000 punitive damages against Pearson. The disciplinary consequence was ten days' confinement and television loss. A separate jury award against hearing officer Szmaciarz was set aside by postverdict judgment as a matter of law. The lower appellate proceeding included Hines's cross-appeal concerning that officer, but the supplied present matter reviews Pearson's judgment. Do not enlarge it to decide the other officer's liability.

The original petition presents two questions: whether allegedly trivial prison consequences can support constitutional retaliation, and whether Hill's some-evidence standard applies to an allegedly false and retaliatory accusation. Petition Part III requests coordination with Crawford-El's retaliation/qualified-immunity questions. The opposition at p.18 expressly states that qualified immunity is not raised in Hines. That is an attributable party representation consistent with the published appeal; it is not authority to invent an immunity issue.
