## 1. United States v. Bajakajian — June 22, 1998

### Review scope and supported legal alternatives

The Government's appeal seeks the entire $357,144. Respondent did not cross-appeal the $15,000 forfeiture. The Court can reject the lower panel's instrumentality-only categorical reasoning while affirming its refusal of full forfeiture on the supported constitutional ground. Neither the validity of the unchallenged $15,000 nor the statutory power to substitute that amount is independently decided. The reporting conviction, three years' probation, and $5,000 fine remain outside the requested change.

The district court found lawful source, lawful intended debt payment, and no connection to another crime. Those findings are accepted, not enlarged into innocence of the willful reporting offense or an acquittal of false statements. The separate section 1001 count was dismissed by agreement. Statutory maximum exposure was five years and $250,000; the applicable Guidelines maxima were six months and $5,000. The latter express the particular offense's assessed sentencing seriousness but do not replace Congress's maximum or mechanically cap forfeiture.

**Path A — affirm the refusal of full forfeiture on proportionality.** A criminal forfeiture imposed on the convicted offender as punishment is a fine. A fine violates the Clause when grossly disproportionate to the gravity of the offense for which it is imposed. Legislative penalty judgments receive substantial respect, but coverage and statutory property connection do not themselves resolve constitutional excessiveness. Here the full exaction is radically out of relation to the supported reporting offense, its limited harm and culpability assessment, lawful funds, lack of other-crime connection, and the applicable sentencing range. The broader statutory maximum must be confronted expressly; it does not establish that taking every dollar in this particular transaction is proportionate. The strongest objection is that deliberate concealment of a very substantial currency transfer seriously obstructs a reporting system directed at hard-to-detect financial crime, and Congress authorized the entire involved property rather than a fixed multiple of the Guidelines fine.

**Path B — reverse the refusal and permit the full statutory forfeiture.** The amount of concealed currency defines the scale of the reporting failure; the Government need not prove a separate unlawful source to enforce that distinct offense. A large, willful failure to disclose can be gravely punishable although the property could lawfully have been transported after reporting. Congress's $250,000/five-year maximum and the expressly forfeitable property support a deferential constitutional application; the full amount is not grossly disproportionate. The strongest objection is that this combines a general maximum with the statutory forfeiture authorization to deprive individualized excessiveness review of meaningful work and treats lawful assets too much like criminal proceeds. The judicial comparison must answer the record's lesser culpability assessment, not assume money laundering.

**Instrumentality-only alternative.** Austin leaves relevant considerations beyond instrumentality available, and Alexander independently requires examination of punitive criminal forfeiture in relation to its supporting offenses. The lower court's rule that reporting currency can never be forfeited because it is not a traditional instrumentality is therefore rejected by every Associate at this criminal-forfeiture scope. Scalia's earlier separate instrumentality account concerned the in-rem forfeitures presented in Austin; it does not establish that Congress may never impose a personal fine payable with lawful property. No Justice here commits to a universal rule governing every historical in-rem forfeiture.

## 2. Pennsylvania Board of Probation & Parole v. Scott — June 22, 1998

### Review scope and supported legal alternatives

The federal exclusion question is decided assuming the violation on which the lower judgment rests. The searchers were parole officers, knew Scott's status, and searched his residence without obtaining consent. The record does not establish a special plan to evade suppression at criminal trial, harassment, effective alternative discipline, or the adequacy of civil remedies. No vote is premised on any such finding. The state-law dispute about construction of the parole condition concerns search validity; the expressly federal suppression ground supplies the presented question. No independent state exclusion rule is inferred.

**Path A — no federal exclusion requirement in the ordinary parole-revocation hearing presented.** The exclusionary rule is a remedial deterrent, not an automatic consequence of every violation. Revocation assesses compliance with conditional liberty through a supervisory proceeding, and excluding probative evidence directly obstructs that task while importing search litigation. Evans, Calandra, and Askin require attention to this additional remedial setting. The legal judgment is that the incremental deterrence from extending criminal-trial exclusion does not justify those institutional costs here, even when the searchers know parole status and are parole officers. The strongest objection is direct rather than speculative institutional incentive: these very officers can use the unlawful fruits for the supervisory consequence they administer, and criminal-trial exclusion need not reach that expected use. Path A must confront that connection rather than assert that parole officers have no enforcement interest.

**Path B — exclusion in this parole-officer/known-status setting.** Exclusion is warranted when parole officers search their supervisee in violation of the Fourth Amendment and the Board seeks the fruits to revoke that person's parole. The institutional link gives the remedy a direct deterrent target absent in Evans's non-law-enforcement-clerk situation. Without exclusion here, the normal foreseeable official use remains available despite the violation. The strongest objection is the loss of reliable evidence in a proceeding concerned with conditional release and the additional costs of resolving search disputes. Path B answers that these costs do not eliminate the ordinary constitutional boundary or justify insulating the institution's own search; it need not require full criminal-trial procedure in every respect or resolve unrelated-police and unknown-status searches.

Evans is not a general good-faith license: its court-employee and objective-reliance predicates are absent. Askin reserves its separate criminal-trial constitutional remedy and does not answer this case in either direction. Fields / Crawley confirms that violation, suppression, consent, independence, attenuation, and reliance remain distinct. It supplies no existing exception or entitlement on these new facts. The competing paths are legal extensions of the remedy, not contrary findings on whether the search was lawful.

## 3. Wisconsin Department of Corrections v. Schacht — June 22, 1998

### Review scope and supported legal alternatives

All eight Associates would reverse the whole-action removal bar and remand to the Seventh Circuit for the personal-capacity appeal. The substantial federal personal-capacity claims supply a federal-question basis independently of the claims against Wisconsin and official-capacity defendants. The sovereign's immunity from particular claims does not erase the court's power to adjudicate the otherwise removable federal claims. A court must respect the actual immunity where properly asserted; it need not treat the entire civil action as outside statutory removal jurisdiction. This determination does not review the personal-capacity merits or reinstate their summary judgment automatically.

**Path A — claim-specific immunity within a removable action.** Sections 1331 and 1441 supply the federal anchor; Hafer preserves the individual defendant/capacity distinction. The immunity of one defendant or claim is not the absence of all original jurisdiction. Section 1447(c)'s lack-of-subject-matter-jurisdiction rule therefore does not require remand of this entire action. The strongest objection is the statutory reference to a removable civil action rather than individually removable claims, coupled with the constitutional characterization of immunity. The answer is that the action actually contains federal claims the federal court may hear, and the immunity attaches at its proper defendant/claim scope instead of eliminating that independent basis.

**Path B — whole-action bar.** Treat an immune federal claim as making the civil action nonremovable in its entirety. This offers a formal reading of the action-wide removal language but fails to explain why a valid federal personal-capacity claim loses its jurisdictional character because it appears beside a claim against the State. It confuses one limit on judicial relief with absence of any statutory original jurisdiction. None of the eight selects it on this record.

**Waiver alternative.** Whether a State's own removal waives its immunity could supply another route in a properly presented case, but no general waiver-by-removal rule is necessary or adopted here. Immunity can be assumed for the specific claim while the remaining federal action survives. The actual Seminole Tribe decision lacks a controlling direct-State constitutional rationale and does not overrule Union Gas. Neither its five-vote officer holding nor its clear-statement holding becomes an Article I abrogation rule here. Chicago's federal-anchor and supplemental-jurisdiction distinctions govern at their own scope; they do not decide State immunity. Deep Sea Research's unanimous, current-term nonpossession holding likewise illustrates the need to identify the precise immunity route but supplies no rule for personal damages against Wisconsin. Gutierrez's certification-specific removal rule remains distinct.

## 4. Pearson v. Hines — June 22, 1998

### Review scope and supported legal alternatives

All eight Associates would affirm Pearson's reviewed liability judgment on the two presented legal issues and the supported sufficiency challenge. Pearson's accusation was found false by the jury, and that falsity is not challenged. The record supplies evidence of Hines's grievance activity and his stated intention that morning to complain, along with the other circumstantial evidence identified in the neutral extraction. Ordinary verdict deference, not a newly invented motive quantum or fresh credibility finding, governs. The sanctions were ten days' confinement and loss of television; damages were $100 compensatory and $1,000 punitive against Pearson. Qualified immunity is not presented. The separately set-aside hearing-officer judgment and cross-appeal are not reopened.

**Path A — affirm the distinct retaliation claim.** An official may not knowingly fabricate a disciplinary accusation in retaliation for a prisoner's protected use or threatened use of the grievance process when it produces the adverse disciplinary action established here. The prisoner must establish protected conduct, actual retaliatory causation, and injury under ordinary burdens; a disciplinary consequence is not immunized merely because it might not independently violate procedural due process. Mt. Healthy preserves the defendant's preponderance same-decision defense; a merely possible lawful reason does not establish it. A knowingly false accusation is not converted into lawful nonretaliatory action because the later board had some evidence before it. The actual confinement/privilege deprivation supplies concrete injury; the claimant need not additionally show that he abandoned grievances or lost a nonfrivolous court case. No rule that every slight or every charge without consequences is actionable is needed.

**Path B — reverse under Hill or an additional-injury rule.** Defer to the disciplinary proceeding whenever some evidence supports it, or require a deprivation independently sufficient under prison due-process or access doctrine. Its strongest justification is avoiding federal jury reexamination of ordinary discipline and preserving institutional latitude. But Hill addresses the evidentiary sufficiency of the adjudication, not an official's knowingly false retaliatory initiation. Sandin's actual substantive-constraint inquiry concerns procedural liberty, and Lewis concerns access injury; neither displaces the distinct First Amendment wrong. Applying them as complete barriers would make a prohibited retaliatory purpose lawful whenever the same sanction could permissibly be imposed for actual misconduct. All eight reject that shortcut while preserving actual same-decision proof and genuine disciplinary authority.

No earned-credit loss or extension of custody is established, so Edwards's outstanding-credit-invalidity prerequisite does not automatically bar this claim. That conclusion does not answer all possible retaliation attacks on disciplinary judgments involving credits. Crawford-El's prior unanimous holding supplies ordinary motive proof and actual causation; it does not itself establish the substantive retaliation elements or conduct-time immunity. Here the trial and findings, not that earlier remand alone, support affirmance.

## 5. Swidler & Berlin v. United States — June 25, 1998

### Review scope and supported legal alternatives

The notes record a confidential consultation for legal representation. The client died nine days later. No waiver, crime-fraud predicate, nonlegal character, or criminal defendant's constitutional entitlement has been established. The content of the withheld notes is not found. The question is whether death and substantial grand-jury investigative usefulness suffice to permit the circuit's posthumous balancing exception. Rule 501 supplies common-law development through reason and experience subject to its governing-source qualifications; its separate state-law sentence is not abrogated.

**Path A — privilege survives death without the asserted general criminal-need exception.** Predictable confidentiality protects communication at the time legal advice is sought. Death does not eliminate the living client's reason to care about later disclosure, including effects on reputation and other persons; no empirical claim about this client's subjective motives is needed. The received attorney-client relationship and its continuing protection have a different pedigree from a newly requested privilege. Upjohn supplies predictability and the distinction between communications and independently discoverable facts. Glover's estate-dispute setting serves the deceased client's testamentary intention and is not a general exception for useful evidence. Jaffee's rejection of routine evidentiary balancing supports the same predictability principle, without directly deciding this distinct privilege. The strongest objection is the loss of possibly decisive evidence in a criminal investigation after the client can no longer suffer personal criminal exposure; Rule 501 does not require preservation of a privilege whose function has materially ended.

**Path B — permit a bounded posthumous criminal-need exception.** Death can materially reduce the living-client interests protected by secrecy, and Rule 501's reason-and-experience inquiry permits reconsidering scope. A criminal investigation's substantial need can justify disclosure under the circuit's approach rather than an automatic lifetime-plus rule. The strongest objection is that a case-specific future usefulness assessment is precisely the uncertainty Jaffee identifies, and the decedent's inability to testify makes the communication uniquely valuable without making its prior confidentiality less important. The record establishes neither abandonment nor a constitutional necessity, and a judicially preferred investigation is not by itself an existing exception. A Justice taking Path B must acknowledge that Jaffee remains binding in its psychotherapy field and explain the posthumous attorney-client distinction, not overrule it silently.

The choice does not decide work product. It does not make privilege absolute, prohibit ordinary fact discovery, or resolve waiver, crime-fraud, a properly presented testamentary dispute, or a criminal defendant's competing constitutional right.
