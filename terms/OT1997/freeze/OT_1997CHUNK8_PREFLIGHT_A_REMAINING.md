## Rice v. Paladin Enterprises — May 14, 1998

**Readiness:** Ready for the confined First Amendment summary-judgment question. The packet's reference to a specific criminal project must not imply knowledge of these planned murders: the stipulation expressly denies that specific knowledge. Liability, final tort findings and damages remain open.

**Source:** The November 10, 1997 Fourth Circuit judicial opinion, 128 F.3d 233, retrieved from https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/128/128.F3d.233.96-2412.html, is saved as `terms/OT1997/research/lower/rice_v_paladin_enterprises_inc.html` and `.txt`. The operative statement of facts, legal analysis of the confined question, disposition and complete stipulation in note 2 establish the following propositions. The murder instructions need no reproduction in a handoff or public decision.

**Material corrections and qualifications:** The stipulations are solely for defendants' Rule 56 motion and may be contested in subsequent proceedings. The sole issue agreed for that motion is whether the First Amendment is a complete legal defense; all other issues of law and fact were reserved. Both *Hit Man* and the separately identified silencer manual are involved. Defendants stipulated an intent to attract and assist criminals, knowledge and intent that the publications would be used upon receipt to plan and execute murder for hire, and actual assistance to Perry in these murders. Plaintiffs also stipulated intended sales to authors, law-enforcement personnel, entertainment readers, fantasists who do not act and researchers; no finding of exclusively criminal readership is permissible. Defendants had no other known contact with Perry, no contact with Horn, and no specific knowledge of the planned crime, conspiracy or victims. The conditional state-of-mind concessions expressly preserve the legal Brandenburg argument. Intent to assist criminal readers and actual assistance are not proof of knowledge of this particular plot. No final jury finding or judgment of liability is entered by the appeal.

**Entering law and paths:** Brandenburg protects advocacy unless its own imminent-lawless-action requirements are met; NAACP v. Claiborne Hardware prevents civil responsibility based merely on association, protected advocacy or another person's violence. Giboney and Brown v. Hartlage distinguish expression that carries out participation in independently unlawful conduct. Dawson and Wisconsin v. Mitchell preserve the legally relevant connection requirement and reject punishment for abstract belief. Madsen and Schenck preserve responsible-party/actual-concert requirements without making sympathy or shared viewpoint enough. Cohen v. Cowles Media supplies no blanket press exemption from lawful generally applicable obligations, but a general-law label does not answer the constitutional question by itself.

Path A would apply Brandenburg to every public distribution of a book, requiring imminence irrespective of intentional operational assistance. Its strongest support is the danger of relabeling protected advocacy as aiding and the absence of a personal agreement or knowledge of the actual plot. Path B treats proved purposeful operational assistance to completed murder as participation, retaining specific intent and an actual substantial connection rather than mere foresight or endorsement. Its strongest objection is the possible reach to ordinary technical knowledge, journalism and fiction. The narrow stipulated combination answers that objection without deciding liability based on lesser mens rea, imposing a prior restraint, or creating a free-floating dangerous-information category. No holding treats express intent alone as sufficient without actual assistance and the causal connection required by the applicable tort. No new general intent/proof hierarchy is needed to reject the absolute defense here. State-law predicates remain to be proved in the reserved proceedings, subject to the constitutional boundary.

## United States v. Thompson — May 21, 1998

**Readiness:** Ready for the two authorized issues. Section 1259 review is supported by CAAF's actual granted marital-privacy issue and specified closed-session issue. No new hearing or supplemental facts are posited.

**Primary source repair:** The complete December 16, 1997 Court of Appeals for the Armed Forces opinion, No. 96-1412, was retrieved and read from the issuing court, https://www.armfor.uscourts.gov/opinions/1997Term/96-1412.htm, and saved as `terms/OT1997/research/lower/thompson_caaf.html` and `.txt`. It establishes the court-martial convictions and sentence, the granted/specified questions, the assault circumstances, the Article 39(b) error, and the appellate court's review of the verbatim transcript. The transcript itself is not represented as recovered. Its judicial recitation is sufficient under Engine §14 for the narrow harmlessness decision; no evidence is invented.

The wife initiated the act while trying to distract her husband during his armed assault after the loaded weapon failed to fire and he was reloading. Initiation is not a finding of voluntary intimacy. The judge and reporter entered members' closed findings deliberations without the accused or counsel. The resulting verbatim record was provided to the parties at trial. CAAF reports that members had reached their findings, asked only how to record lesser included offenses, received no substantive deliberation instruction, and heard nothing that would have been objectionable in open session. Defense counsel identified nothing requiring challenge or clarification; that absence supplements rather than replaces the Government's burden. The sentence was a bad-conduct discharge, thirty months' confinement, total forfeitures and reduction to the lowest enlisted grade; no separate sentencing error is reviewed.

**Law and alternatives:** Griswold and Eisenstadt protect decisions concerning private voluntary intimacy; neither makes marriage a defense to conduct obtained during a violent attack. No categorical rule making marital protection depend on judicial approval of the act's benefit to marriage is necessary. Existing Casey and related autonomy holdings are not blanket immunity for violence. Richenberg's distinct military-service holding and Scheffer's evidence rule supply no general military exemption from constitutional review.

Article 39(b) requires proceedings in the presence of the accused and counsel except members' deliberation/voting; the judge's entry was a violation, not a permitted deliberation. Article 59(a) tests material prejudice to substantial rights, with Allbee's rebuttable presumption against the Government for this contact. The transcript's confined administrative character and completed findings rebut it here. Sullivan's structural absence of a valid jury verdict is different from a reviewable recorded communication about entering findings already reached. Weiss preserves independent scrutiny of military justice and statutory safeguards. Rushen v. Spain and United States v. Gagnon show that an unauthorized judge-juror encounter is not automatically structural; their respective circumstances and constitutional limits remain distinct.

The stronger reversal path stresses the danger of influence from judicial entry before announcement of the verdict and inability to participate. It fails on the actual recorded encounter, not a general presumption that post-deliberation contact is harmless. The stronger privacy path stresses marriage and physical initiation; it fails because the supported assault context supplies no voluntary intimate choice. Whether Article 125 may constitutionally punish genuinely consensual private marital relations remains open. This disposition need not decide any distinct, unpresented Sixth Amendment claim or classify every closed-session contact as nonconstitutional.
