## Common propositions, explanations and limits

**I — Traditional in-rem jurisdiction.** The Eleventh Amendment does not bar a federal admiralty in-rem action concerning a wreck outside a State's possession merely because the State claims title. The Davis's actual-possession distinction supplies the sovereign-property analogy; Ex parte New York (No. 2) concerned a State-owned vessel in its possession and governmental service. The State's title claim or statutory constructive possession is not actual custody. Treasure Salvors' discussion must be read in its State-held-artifact and arrest-warrant context, not as a universal prohibition on adjudicating title to every unpossessed res. This is no general immunity exception triggered merely by an in-rem caption.

**II — Binding adjudication without a premature title award.** The district court may determine DSR's and California's competing interests in the wreck under the governing law; the resulting lawful in-rem determination may bind California's claimed interest. This permission does not establish DSR's ownership or require California to prove ASA title as a condition of avoiding immunity. No waiver is inferred from its limited appearance. No personal damages, coercive officer relief, execution removing State-held property, Article I abrogation, or rule for a State-possessed wreck is decided.

**III — Independent ASA inquiry.** Abandonment under the ASA has its maritime meaning; the district court must reconsider it independently of an assumed immunity obstacle. It may not equate age, lack of prior recovery of inaccessible property, or the State's title assertion with automatic abandonment, demand express renunciation in every case, or treat administrative convenience as proof that all insured and uninsured portions have one established status. The provisional reference to express or appropriately inferred abandonment describes that maritime inquiry; this handoff prescribes no new burden of proof or exhaustive evidentiary test.

Section 2105 asserts federal title and transfers it to the State for an abandoned wreck embedded in State submerged lands, embedded in State-protected coralline formations on those lands, or on those lands and included in or determined eligible for the National Register. Adequate public notice of location is required; the Interior Secretary, after consulting the appropriate State Historic Preservation Officer, makes the written eligibility determination under the third category. Abandoned wrecks on federal public lands belong to the United States; those on Indian lands belong to the owning tribe. Rights reserved under 43 U.S.C. §§1311, 1313, 1314 and 33 U.S.C. §§414, 415 remain unaffected. Section 2106 excludes salvage and finds for wrecks within §2105, otherwise leaves federal shipwreck law unchanged, and does not affect proceedings begun before April 28, 1988. This action began in 1991. None of these terms supplies a finding of a disputed statutory predicate.

**IV — Remedy.** Affirm jurisdiction, vacate the remainder and remand for independent ASA and title proceedings. Do not decide broad preemption of California §6313 when the ASA inquiry may eliminate any need to reach it. Insurer-derived interests, uninsured property and the United States' asserted interest remain for lawful adjudication. DSR's custodial/exclusive-salvor status establishes no final title, distribution, salvage award or right to complete recovery before the court resolves title. No surrender to California is ordered. Lawful preservation and necessary further proceedings remain with the district court.

## Substantive separate positions for assembly

**Stevens's additional function:** The distinctive history of in-rem admiralty warrants a broader account than ordinary ejectment or officer-possession litigation. His earlier Treasure Salvors reasoning assumed that the limits on an officer decree under Tindal and United States v. Lee necessarily barred a binding adjudication of the State's maritime title. On reconsideration, the early admiralty understanding described by Justices Story and Washington undermines that assumption: in-rem admiralty can adjudicate the res and bind competing interests. He supports the present common holding and regards the broader no-bar understanding as persuasive; it does not become the Court's rule for possessed property. His current Coeur d'Alene join concerns a different officer action and does not contradict this maritime distinction.

**Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer's shared additional function:** They fully join the narrow opinion while refusing to treat its discussion of Treasure Salvors as permanently embedding possession versus nonpossession in constitutional law. The historical doubts and Stevens's distinct maritime explanation leave the broader question open to reconsideration. That reservation neither decides the State-possessed case nor weakens the sufficient rule and binding adjudication authorized here. It supplies no additional majority holding.
