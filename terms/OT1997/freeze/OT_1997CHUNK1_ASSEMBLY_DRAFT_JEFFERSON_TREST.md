**Event and posture.** Jefferson v. City of Tarrant, No. 96-957. Argued November 4, 1997; decided December 9, 1997. **Action and remedy.** The writ is dismissed for want of jurisdiction. The Alabama interlocutory ruling remains in its existing posture; its federal merits are not affirmed. **Holding: This interlocutory ruling does not satisfy §1257's finality requirement.** Authority: sequential assembly to supply the selected writing and exact final joins. The substantive finality application is directly supported by the seven dismissing Associates identified above and is compatible with Stone's fixed core; Stevens disagrees with the application.

Section 1257 requires a final state-court judgment. Cox permits review of a finally resolved federal issue in bounded circumstances: proceedings are practically concluded; the issue necessarily survives every further outcome; effective later review is unavailable whatever the outcome; or reversal would preclude the relevant litigation and postponement seriously threaten federal policy. State certification and the importance of a federal issue do not themselves establish finality.

None of those routes supports review here. Unlike Richards, the same petitioners' claims and disputed liability and causation remain for substantive adjudication. Unlike Sullivan's completed conviction issue, the damages question need not survive every lawful outcome. Unlike Labron/Kilgore, effective later correction is available following an appropriate adverse final judgment. Unlike Casarotto's threatened arbitration forum, reversal here would permit damages litigation to proceed rather than protect against the relevant litigation itself.

Ritchie does not require absolute legal impossibility of a later appeal. Its note 7 recognized a possible appellate route but found renewed presentation served no useful finality purpose in its unusual setting. The Court considered disclosure injury, prosecution outcomes, completed trial and appellate review, and an inadequate contempt alternative; confidentiality alone was not dispositive. Those considerations do not create a universal checklist. Here later review can effectively correct the damages restriction, and resolving the disputed facts and remaining claims serves a substantive purpose. ### Trest — proposed public substance

**Event and posture.** Trest v. Cain, No. 96-7901. Argued November 10, 1997; decided December 9, 1997. On review of the Fifth Circuit's denial of federal habeas relief, the Court considers whether a federal appellate court must raise state procedural default when the State has not relied on it. Trest challenges prior Mississippi guilty pleas used to enhance his Louisiana sentence; the merits of that challenge are not decided.

**Action and remedy.** The Fifth Circuit's judgment is vacated, and the case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with the absence of a mandatory duty to raise procedural default on the court's own initiative. The Court grants no writ, invalidates no conviction, reduces no sentence, and orders no automatic evidentiary hearing. Whether default may lawfully be considered, and the genuinely remaining habeas issues, remain for appropriate further proceedings.

**Holding: An appellate court has no mandatory duty to raise an omitted state procedural-default defense.** Authority: sequential assembly to supply the selected writing and exact final joins. All eight Associates directly support this rule and its stated application; the scope preserves Stone's express position.

Procedural default is not a defect in federal subject-matter jurisdiction. A federal court of appeals is not required to raise it when the State has not relied on it. This holding does not decide whether a court may raise the defense, when it could properly do so, or whether its discretionary use would be permissible here.

Lambrix identifies default and nonretroactivity as distinct nonjurisdictional limits without an inflexible sequence. Its remand concerned a preserved defense, not a duty to discover an omitted one. Nonjurisdictional classification does not alone settle every question of judicial initiative, but no independent statutory or precedential command requires the initiative here. Granberry shows that comity and federalism do not themselves require automatic appellate dismissal for omitted exhaustion. That analogy distinguishes institutional interests from compulsion; exhaustion and procedural default remain different doctrines.

The Fifth Circuit treated predicted Mississippi untimeliness as compelling exclusion. The parties had not discussed the Sones ground on which it relied. Affirming on a discretionary basis the court did not express would replace its actual reasoning and assume undeveloped predicates. State finality interests may bear on permissible discretion; they do not establish the asserted mandatory obligation.

Coleman's adequate-independent-ground requirements and applicable exceptions remain intact. Neither the State's omission nor the present record establishes intentional waiver, automatic forfeiture, a valid default, cause and prejudice, or an innocence gateway. Under Lindh, the covered chapter 153 amendments do not govern this September 30, 1991 noncapital application; amended §2254(b)(3)'s exhaustion-waiver provision supplies no default rule here. Separately directed provisions retain their own scope.

The Court also reserves the predicate pleas' constitutional validity and the route for challenging expired convictions. Any lawful later consideration must address the actual ground with an appropriate opportunity to respond; the Court announces no universal constitutional requirement of additional briefing on every unargued issue.

- Granberry supplies a bounded analogy from omitted exhaustion; its discretionary framework is not extended into a present holding resolving all default-initiative questions.
