## Buchanan v. Angelone, No.96-8400

Argued November 3, 1997; decided January 21, 1998. Historical judgment: **affirmed 103 F.3d 344, 6–3**. Rehnquist wrote for O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas. Breyer dissented with Stevens and Ginsburg. No nonparticipation or partial join is reported.

- **Court, pp.275–279:** Distinguished eligibility channeling from the selection decision; the latter must permit consideration and effect of all constitutionally relevant mitigation, but does not categorically require affirmative instructions on general mitigation or specified statutory factors. Applied the *Boyde* reasonable-likelihood standard to the actual instruction and trial context, including all-evidence language, discretionary life option, two days of evidence, and both lawyers' arguments. The holding covers the general and particular instructions actually requested; it is not a rule that every omission or contradictory instruction is valid.
- **Court n.4, pp.277–278:** Independently treated the oral-argument challenge to the pattern instruction's structure as waived because Buchanan had agreed to it without objection and had not previously advanced that claim. It also rejected the proposed structural reading on its merits. Preserve this threshold/alternative merits structure; the procedural point is not a waiver of the distinct requested supplemental instructions.
- **Scalia concurrence, p.279:** Joined the Court's actual *Boyde* application in full. Separately maintained that the Eighth Amendment does not require discretionary consideration of mitigation at all; regarded *Lockett–Eddings* and *Furman* constraints as incompatible and the eligibility/selection division as unstable. This is an independent constitutional reservation, not a limited join or rejection of the Court's result.
- **Breyer dissent, pp.280–286:** Applied the same reasonable-likelihood standard but read the instruction's disjunctive syntax and sequence as likely to confine the jury to aggravation/eligibility, leaving no meaningful mitigation direction. Distinguished *Boyde*, *Johnson*, and *Franklin*; invoked *Penry*. Noted other States' explicit pattern mitigation instructions and Virginia's amended version. Held that evidence and counsels' arguments could not cure this serious judicial misinstruction. Sought rejection of the instruction's constitutional sufficiency; did not merely announce a universal right to instructions listing every mitigating factor.

**Comparator check:** Identity, date, final result, lineup, and separate-writing inventory are correct. The Scalia excerpt in the supplied comparator accidentally continues into the opening of Breyer's dissent. That cross-boundary text is an extraction defect, not part of Scalia's concurrence. The abbreviated excerpts omit Breyer's full instruction analysis and Court n.4's distinct preservation ground; use the complete sources above. The full record quotation has five paragraphs including unanimity; the Court's n.1 reproduction stops after the four substantive death/life paragraphs.

## LaChance v. Erickson / LaChance v. McManus, No.96-1395

Argued December 2, 1997; decided January 21, 1998. Historical judgment: **both Federal Circuit judgments reversed, 9–0**: 89 F.3d 1575 and the separate unpublished McManus judgment reported at 92 F.3d 1208. Rehnquist wrote for a unanimous Court; there is no separate writing or nonparticipation.

- **Statutory component, pp.265–266:** Section 7513's efficiency-of-service cause requirement and enumerated procedural rights contained no right to knowingly give false denials.
- **Constitutional component, pp.265–268:** Assuming the protected employment property interest, due process required notice and meaningful opportunity to respond but did not protect false statements about charged conduct. Relied on *Bryson* and truthful-testimony/perjury authorities, rejected the oath distinction as immaterial to an employment false-statement charge, and rejected the asserted chilling rationale.
- **Silence qualification, pp.267–268:** Recognized invocation of the Fifth Amendment where answers could expose an employee to criminal prosecution; discussed possible adverse inference in a civil/administrative investigation under *Baxter*. This is not a holding that every exercise of silence may be punished, that an unsuccessful denial proves knowing falsity, or that compelled immunized testimony has been resolved.
- **Scope/remedy:** The opinion removes the categorical legal bar to adverse action for false denials. It does not reinstate a particular named employee's penalty in its text, independently find every element of every administrative charge, or prescribe a new criminal offense. Its formal concluding disposition is “Reversed.”

**Comparator check:** Identity, date, dual-judgment result, unanimity, and absence of separate opinions are correct. The companion-case asterisk is embedded in the supplied syllabus prose, a formatting defect. The comparator correctly includes both CSRA and due process; the narrower neutral question should not obscure the statutory component. Cornell's syllabus has a visibly truncated final *Konigsberg* citation; the complete official report controls. Cornell's contemporaneous caption says “Acting Director,” while the bound report says “Director”; this does not alter identity or participation.

## Lunding v. New York Tax Appeals Tribunal, No.96-1462

Argued November 5, 1997; decided January 21, 1998. Historical judgment: **reversed and remanded 89 N.Y.2d 283, 6–3**. O'Connor wrote for Stevens, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, and Breyer. Ginsburg dissented with Rehnquist and Kennedy. No partial joins or nonparticipation are reported.

- **Court Parts I–II, pp.291–302:** Set out the nonresident tax formula and used Article IV's substantial-equality standard: substantial reason for differential treatment and substantial relation to the objective. Read *Shaffer*, *Travis*, and *Austin* as permitting justified source-linked differences but not automatic immunity for every denial of a personal deduction.
- **Part III-A/B, pp.303–311:** Found the lower court's reliance on *Goodwin* and the expense's personal character insufficient in this tax scheme. Distinguished prior summary dispositions and statutory settings, considered the history and other deductions, the apportionment mechanism including possible ratios above 100%, and alimony's lack of a fixed geographic situs. Explicitly declined to require every personal deduction or every resident tax credit for nonresidents. Majority notes 1–4 give the exact challenged paragraph, 1990 percentage instructions, prior summary-disposition cases, and statutory history.
- **Part III-C/D, pp.311–314:** Rejected income-splitting justification as insufficient for categorical denial regardless of recipient residence, and rejected the amici's proposed compensating resident-State credits as unsupported and dependent on another State's laws. Part IV, pp.314–315, held the categorical denial inadequately justified and remanded without itself constructing a replacement tax formula or computing a refund.
- **Ginsburg Part I, pp.315–320:** Treated alimony as attribution of income between former spouses rather than an ordinary expense. Assessed the taxing system's rough parity across former marital units, relied on *Travellers* and *Baldwin*, and viewed the Lundings' actual Connecticut-to-Connecticut transaction as seeking complete exemption of that income from New York tax. Her n.3's balance-of-interstate-payments premise is expressly an assumption, not record evidence. Her n.4 raises the limitation on challenging hypothetically unconstitutional applications to other taxpayers.
- **Ginsburg Part II, pp.320–326:** Alternatively accepted the expense characterization for argument and distinguished generally available personal exemptions from particular deductions. Read *Shaffer*, *Travis*, *Goodwin*, and related dispositions to permit denial of personal expenditures connected to the taxpayer's home State; located these alimony payments in Connecticut. Part III, pp.326–328, challenged the majority's income-correlation rationale as extending to other personal expenses/credits and stressed that multiple factors influence alimony. Notes 1–2 cover federal/other-state tax arrangements; n.5 elaborates *Travis*; n.6 discusses New York credits; n.7 reproduces Connecticut alimony factors. Would affirm.

**Comparator check:** Identity, date, disposition, and lineup are correct. No substantive comparator error was found. The opening-only dissent excerpt is incomplete and cannot stand in for the income-attribution argument, alternative deduction ground, scope objections, or notes. At p.316 a quotation of a secondary treatise contains bracketed descriptions reversing §§71 and 215; the preceding sentence correctly identifies §71 as recipient inclusion and §215 as payer deduction. Cornell reproduces the same anomaly. Do not propagate those reversed descriptions as statutory text.
