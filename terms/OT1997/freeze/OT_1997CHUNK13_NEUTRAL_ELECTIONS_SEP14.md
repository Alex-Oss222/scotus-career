## A. Case, record, and posture

- **Case name:** *Clifton v. Federal Election Commission*, No. 97-786.
- **Parties:** Robin Clifton, a Maine voter who reads advocacy materials, and Maine Right to Life Committee, Inc. (MRLC), petitioners; FEC, respondent. Below: *Clifton v. FEC*, 927 F. Supp. 493 (D. Me. 1996), modified, 114 F.3d 1309 (1st Cir. 1997).
- **Entry stage and event:** Petition for certiorari after the First Circuit's June 6, 1997 decision and August 8, 1997 final rehearing disposition. The petition is reviewed in February 1998, which remains within OT 1997. No Supreme Court merits grant is assumed.
- **Question presented for modeling, reconstructed:** Whether the FEC can condition corporations' dissemination of independently prepared candidate voting records and voter guides on restrictions against mere contact with candidates, a written-question/written-answer-only protocol, equal space and prominence for competing candidates, or a ban on undefined “electioneering messages”; whether any remnant restrictions survive § 441b and the First Amendment. The original petition's exact question is not available for verification.
- **Judgment under review:** District court declared challenged portions of 11 C.F.R. § 114.4(c)(4) and (5) unauthorized under FECA. The First Circuit **modified** that declaration: voting-record regulation invalid only to the extent it purported to bar mere inquiries to candidates; voter-guide regulation invalid only insofar as it required all pertinent contacts to occur in writing and compelled equal space/prominence; challenges to the “electioneering message” provisions were **remanded** for further work on content, ripeness, and severability. The court did not nullify all valid restrictions on actual coordination or express advocacy.
- **Requested relief:** Petitioners seek additional review of portions of the FEC regulation not invalidated or definitively resolved; the requested Supreme Court judgment must be fitted to the issues raised by the petition rather than assuming they appealed a loss on every issue.
- **Material record:** MRLC is a nonprofit membership corporation opposed to abortion; it publishes voter guides stating federal candidates' positions and voting records bearing on abortion questions, using general treasury funds that include donations from other corporations. Clifton receives and wishes to receive the information. Regulations took effect March 13, 1996. There is no trial finding that mere candidate inquiries actually produced a prohibited in-kind contribution or that each questioned guide was created in collaboration with a campaign.
- **Rules:** Section 114.4(c)(4) addressed corporate and union distribution of voting records and forbade coordination over their content and distribution. Section 114.4(c)(5) regulated voter guides, allowing contact about their preparation only under written questions and answers, while requiring equal space and prominence and excluding specified “electioneering messages” or scoring. Some restrictions operated even on pure questions asked of candidates about public issues.
- **Threshold and vehicle:** First Circuit left “electioneering message” meaning and severability unresolved. The appellate judgment is mixed and has a remand component; Supreme Court review of an interlocutory aspect or undecided question risks an inadequate merits record. Whether the Court may or should review a final appellate ruling notwithstanding remand is a separate certiorari concern.
- **Key dates:** District declaration May 20, 1996; First Circuit decision June 6, 1997; plaintiffs' rehearing request denied June 27, 1997; FEC rehearing/en banc request filed July 21, 1997 and denied August 8, 1997; petition considered during OT 1997 in February 1998.
- **Related cases:** *Maine Right to Life Committee v. FEC* (the separate No. 96-1818 express-advocacy regulation case), *Faucher v. FEC*, 928 F.2d 468 (1st Cir. 1991), *FEC v. Massachusetts Citizens for Life, Inc.*, 479 U.S. 238 (1986), and *Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce*, 494 U.S. 652 (1990), all relevant to the preexisting treatment of different corporate entities and expenditures.
- **Participation:** No shown Stone recusal; alternate roster must determine actual participation.

## B. Law entering the case


### Issue 1. Independent issue publications and actual coordination

- **Controlling simulated law:** Unverified.
- **Public doctrine:** FECA § 441b restricted corporate and union contributions and election expenditures, with special segregated-fund treatment. *Buckley*, 424 U.S. 1 (1976), distinguished independent expression from candidate-directed coordinated spending; *MCFL*, 479 U.S. 238 (1986), read § 441b as not reaching general issue advocacy and reserved special First Amendment protection for certain ideological nonprofits.
- **Other law:** 2 U.S.C. §§ 441a(a)(7)(B)(i), 441b; 11 C.F.R. § 114.4(c)(4)–(5).
- **Open point:** Where a candidate responds to questions about public positions, when does inquiry become cooperation that renders publishing costs a contribution made for or on behalf of that candidate?

### Issue 2. Written-only contact and equal-space condition

- **Controlling simulated law:** Unverified.
- **Public doctrine:** *Miami Herald Publishing Co. v. Tornillo*, 418 U.S. 241 (1974), rejected compulsory editorial reply space; *Hurley v. Irish-American Gay, Lesbian and Bisexual Group*, 515 U.S. 557 (1995), recognized protected editorial choices. Anti-corruption rules directed to candidate contributions serve legitimate aims but cannot necessarily prescribe the form or substance of independent association's publications.
- **Open point:** Whether *Austin*'s corporate treasury channeling permits otherwise speech-burdensome conditions for this particular nonprofit, which takes some corporate contributions, even though the agency's independent statutory authority is not thereby enlarged.

### Issue 3. “Electioneering message” and unfinished severability

- **Controlling simulated law:** Unverified.
- **Public doctrine:** First Amendment vagueness and FECA statutory construction, with a premium on the distinction between issue advocacy and express electoral direction under *Buckley* and *MCFL*. Ordinary rule severability requires examining the operative remainder and the rulemaker's likely choice.
- **Open point:** FEC did not meaningfully define the regulatory term on the appellate record. The First Circuit declined to decide whether it was constitutionally vague, what content it prohibited, or whether it could survive separately after other parts fell. That remand is unfinished at petition stage.

### Issue 4. The corporation's proper constitutional category

- **Controlling simulated law:** Unverified.
- **Public doctrine:** *MCFL* treated a voluntarily funded ideological corporation unlike a commercial treasury for independent expenditure purposes but emphasized the absence of business-corporation funding; *Austin*, 494 U.S. 652 (1990), sustained certain corporate treasury restrictions in a different organizational setting. MRLC's receipt of outside corporate donations puts its precise status between those fact patterns.
- **Open point:** The amount, conditions, and role of those corporate donations are insufficiently established to decide whether MRLC satisfies a constitutional exception. The First Circuit deliberately did not settle that question.

**Earlier same-term decisions:** Any simulated OT 1997 merits holdings up to February 1998 must be supplied by the engine. In the public docket, the separate *Maine* certiorari matter is not itself a Supreme Court merits precedent. **Unresolved same-term dependencies:** Potential changes to the alternate Court's campaign-finance doctrine and *Akins* procedural developments, if any, require separate verification.

## C. Decision-material litigation positions

**On candidate contact:** The FEC argues that regulatory prophylaxis prevents disguised in-kind contributions from corporations, including guides whose questions, rankings, and timing are coordinated with campaigns. Petitioners argue a candidate's answer to a survey or a conversation explaining a voting record does not finance or control that candidate's speech. The First Circuit held that *mere inquiry* cannot be treated as prohibited coordination but preserved the possibility that real collaboration is a contribution under lawful statutory standards.

**On written-only protocol:** The FEC says fixed written questionnaires produce a verifiable record and discourage preferential private collaboration. Petitioners answer that choosing only written communication materially restricts political dialogue, particularly with sitting lawmakers on public issues. The First Circuit invalidated the categorical contact-form rule as beyond the delegated authority in light of constitutional concerns; Judge Bownes dissented on this component, emphasizing money-in-politics concerns and the state's anticorruption interest.

**On equal space/prominence:** The FEC invokes objectively evenhanded coverage as a way to distinguish neutral voter information from a disguised candidate contribution. Petitioners assert the right to choose coverage, criticism, and editorial allocation, and invoke *Tornillo* and *Hurley*. The First Circuit invalidated compulsory equal editorial treatment, finding no identified statutory power or adequate showing that the burden was necessary to police actual contributions.

**On electioneering and corporate financing:** MRLC challenges the remaining ambiguous “electioneering message” terms. The FEC failed to commit itself in appellate briefing to a definitive narrow meaning; the appellate panel declined final judgment on meaning and severability. MRLC claims protection as ideological advocacy, but its receipt of commercial donations prevents assuming it falls within *MCFL*'s narrow special rule. The State's anti-corruption aim, the association's speaker status, and administrative authority require separate analysis.

## D. Lawful paths and uncertainty

1. **No merits review at this stage:** Leave the partial appellate disposition and lower-court remand to run; later genuine finality may clarify the regulatory remainder and the record.
2. **Affirm the narrow appellate judgment if granted:** Hold that mere inquiries are not contributions, a writing-only protocol and equal-space command lack adequate statutory foundation, and actual cooperation can remain regulable. Leave electioneering and corporate-status questions open.
3. **Further limiting construction:** If a regulatory phrase clearly and validly reaches only express advocacy or real coordination, sustain it to that extent. Such a holding requires an established interpretation and no impermissible judicial rewrite.
4. **Remand on unresolved matters:** Direct concrete analysis of “electioneering message,” ripeness, severability, and any necessary record about financing; neither validate nor condemn undefined restrictions in the abstract.

The material uncertainty is unusually procedural: the petition comes from a partly favorable, partly modified circuit judgment with a contested regulatory remainder. The FEC's claim about corporate donor influence cannot stand in for findings identifying MRLC's funding model.

## A. Case, record, and posture

- **Case name:** *Federal Election Commission v. Maine Right to Life Committee, Inc.*, No. 96-1818.
- **Parties and alias:** The Federal Election Commission (FEC), petitioner; Maine Right to Life Committee, Inc. (MRLC), and newsletter reader Hugh T. Corbett, respondents. Below: *Maine Right to Life Committee, Inc. v. FEC*, 914 F. Supp. 8 (D. Me. 1996), affirmed, 98 F.3d 1 (1st Cir. 1996).
- **Entry stage and event:** Petition for a writ of certiorari, filed May 15, 1997, carried into the Court's October 1997 opening conference period. No merits grant is assumed.
- **Question presented for modeling, reconstructed:** Whether the FEC could define prohibited or regulated “express advocacy” in 11 C.F.R. § 100.22(b) by considering the words of a communication in limited context, or whether the First Amendment and the Federal Election Campaign Act (FECA), as construed in *Buckley* and *Massachusetts Citizens for Life*, confined the FEC to a narrower category. The petition's verbatim questions are not verified from an original petition copy.
- **Court and judgment under review:** The First Circuit summarily affirmed the District of Maine for substantially its reasons. The district court declared § 100.22(b) unauthorized by FECA, refused the remaining requested injunctive and declaratory relief, dismissed the Attorney General as a defendant, and did not reach the standalone vagueness challenge.
- **Relief requested at the Supreme Court:** The FEC seeks review and reversal of the adverse declaration, with the regulatory definition restored or remanded for further analysis.
- **Material record:** MRLC is a tax-exempt nonprofit membership group with roughly 2,000 members, advocates on abortion-related issues, publishes a newsletter and candidate surveys, comments in the press, and accepts some corporate contributions into its general treasury. Corbett receives and wishes to continue receiving its communications. The regulation became effective October 5, 1995. MRLC's past and contemplated issue speech, especially near elections, risked being treated as express advocacy under subsection (b); the trial court found actual cessation of some speech after the rule took effect. No material adjudicative fact was disputed at the consolidated hearing.
- **Regulation:** Section 100.22(a) lists explicit electoral exhortations. Section 100.22(b) also covers a communication which, taken as a whole and with limited reference to external events, admits no reasonable interpretation other than an exhortation to elect or defeat a clearly identified candidate. Its context-sensitive application is contested.
- **Threshold and vehicle:** Pre-enforcement declaratory relief was granted. The FEC urged that MRLC should first request a statutory advisory opinion, but 2 U.S.C. § 437f makes that procedure permissive. The district court found facial legal invalidity reviewable without awaiting a particular enforcement action. The final declaratory judgment and summary appellate affirmance provide an identifiable reviewable ruling.
- **Key dates:** Rule effective October 5, 1995; district judgment February 1996, reconsideration denied March 8, 1996; First Circuit affirmance October 18, 1996; certiorari petition May 15, 1997; OT 1997 begins October 6, 1997.
- **Related matters:** *Faucher v. FEC*, 928 F.2d 468 (1st Cir. 1991), binds the First Circuit. The Ninth Circuit's *FEC v. Furgatch*, 807 F.2d 857 (9th Cir. 1987), supplies the contextual approach to which subsection (b) refers. *FEC v. Christian Action Network* provides another preexisting federal appellate treatment of issue advocacy. The companion OT 1997 *Clifton* petition involves different FEC rules and a related advocacy organization but is not the same lawsuit.

## B. Law entering the case


### Issue 1. Scope of “express advocacy” and agency power

- **Controlling simulated law:** Unverified. The following is the historical pre-OT 1997 public-law baseline, subject to reconciliation with the simulator's own holdings.
- **Received doctrine:** *Buckley v. Valeo*, 424 U.S. 1 (1976), narrowly construed express electoral advocacy to avoid unconstitutionally vague reach into issue speech; *FEC v. Massachusetts Citizens for Life, Inc.* (MCFL), 479 U.S. 238 (1986), likewise distinguished independent electoral advocacy from issue discussion in construing § 441b. The Supreme Court's examples do not, by themselves, resolve every imaginable functional equivalent of express words.
- **Statute and rule:** FECA, then codified at 2 U.S.C. § 441b; FEC's 11 C.F.R. § 100.22(a), (b). An agency's statute supplies the authority for its rule; the court decides the statute's legally permissible boundary.
- **Other authority:** *Faucher* rejected an FEC nonpartisanship requirement for issue-oriented guides; *Furgatch* assessed electoral exhortation partly in its communicative context. Their different emphases present a genuine circuit-level tension, not a Supreme Court resolution.
- **Open point:** Whether a no-reasonable-non-electoral-meaning formulation is inherently broader than express advocacy, or becomes unlawful only if the agency uses it to classify genuine issue discussion as an electoral command.

### Issue 2. Pre-enforcement suit and advisory-opinion procedure

- **Controlling simulated law:** Unverified; public-law reference is Article III, established pre-enforcement First Amendment litigation, and FECA § 437f.
- **Other governing law:** Advisory opinions are available by application, not stated as a mandatory exhaustion prerequisite. A genuine chilling effect may support a present controversy, but not an advisory opinion on a hypothetical dispute.
- **Open point:** Whether the association's pattern of speech and the operation of a facial rule made its threat of application sufficiently concrete.

### Issue 3. Remedy and review method

- **Controlling simulated law:** Unverified.
- **Other governing law:** Declaratory Judgment Act and ordinary appellate review; statutory grounds should be decided before unnecessary First Amendment vagueness analysis. Courts may invalidate an administrative rule outside statutory authorization without prescribing a replacement text.
- **Open point:** Whether the declaration of subsection (b)'s invalidity is broader than required to protect speech while leaving subsection (a) and valid corporate contribution restrictions untouched.

**Earlier same-term merits decisions:** None established as effective at the October 1997 conference date. **Unresolved same-term dependencies:** Any simulated opinions entered before conference require engine verification, not extrapolation from this historical record.

## C. Decision-material litigation positions

**On § 100.22(b):** The district court reasoned that even where context makes an electoral reading plausible, issue discussion can retain other reasonable meanings; the FEC cannot enlarge statutory prohibitions through an interpretation extending beyond *Buckley*, *MCFL*, and binding *Faucher*. The FEC's strongest response is that paragraph (b) demands **no other reasonable interpretation**, not mere electoral tendency, and addresses circumvention by indirect but unmistakable election-day appeals. The opposing strength is fair notice: speech may become prohibited solely through timing and external context even while its words remain unchanged. The court need not accept either a permanent list of seven words or unlimited contextual enforcement to recognize the legal disagreement.

**On justiciability:** The FEC favors advisory-opinion review and case-by-case interpretation before a facial judgment. MRLC replies that FECA does not compel consultation and its ordinary public communications cannot each wait for official clearance. The hearing record described cessation of speech, not only speculation. The district court found no triable fact requiring postponement.

**On remedy:** The FEC seeks regulatory validity, at least on a limiting construction. MRLC seeks a declaration and nonenforcement. The district court granted only a declaration against § 100.22(b), excluded the Attorney General where no enforcement threat was shown, and left unrelated FECA powers intact.

## A. Case, record, and posture

- **Case name:** *Federal Election Commission v. Williams*, No. 97-601.
- **Lower case:** *FEC v. Larry R. Williams*, 104 F.3d 237 (9th Cir. Dec. 26, 1996), arising from the Central District of California, No. CV-93-06321 ER.
- **Entry stage:** FEC petition for a writ of certiorari pending in OT 1997. No Supreme Court merits hearing is assumed.
- **Question presented for modeling, reconstructed:** Whether the five-year federal default limitation on government civil-penalty proceedings in 28 U.S.C. § 2462 applies to FECA civil assessments; whether accrual, fraudulent concealment, discovery, or required administrative complaint/conciliation procedures toll that period; and whether the same bar reaches the related requested injunction. The original petition's verbatim question has not been verified.
- **Judgment below:** The district court found Williams liable and imposed a $10,000 civil penalty plus an injunction against similar FECA violations for ten years. The Ninth Circuit reversed because the FEC's October 19, 1993 action was outside the governing five-year limitations period. Judge Fletcher dissented on equitable-tolling and mandatory-process grounds.
- **Requested Supreme Court relief:** The FEC seeks reversal or vacatur of the Ninth Circuit's time-bar holding and further proceedings on its enforcement judgment.
- **Material transactions:** During the 1987–88 presidential nomination campaign of Jack Kemp, donors giving $1,000 obtained an opportunity to buy Super Bowl tickets. Williams obtained forty tickets and placed them with friends and employees. In twenty-two instances he advanced $1,000 to a named contributor as the ticket resale price, then recovered the money through resales. The FEC alleged indirect contributions in other persons' names and violations of the individual limit, under FECA §§ 441f and 441a(a)(1)(A). The record does not establish that published reports disclosed Williams as the underlying provider of the funds.
- **Investigation timeline:** Alleged acts ended by January 31, 1988. Former employee Richard Hooton filed a complaint September 12, 1988. The FEC notified Williams and found reason to believe a violation on September 13, 1989. Following investigation, an FEC probable-cause assessment and mandatory conciliation ran May 24 through July 20, 1993. FEC sued October 19, 1993.
- **Final judgment chronology:** District court denied a limitations motion and granted partial summary judgment January 31, 1995; after a remaining count was dismissed by stipulation, final judgment entered March 7, 1995; Ninth Circuit reversed December 26, 1996.
- **Threshold:** The record contains a final civil judgment and an appellate limitations ruling. No independent claim of appellate jurisdictional defect is apparent. Whether tolling is justified may depend on actual diligence and discovery evidence, not solely the form of public reports.
- **Related:** *Cope v. Anderson*, 331 U.S. 461 (1947); *Holmberg v. Armbrecht*, 327 U.S. 392 (1946); *United States v. Walsh*, 8 F.3d 659 (9th Cir. 1993); *3M Co. v. Browner*, 17 F.3d 1453 (D.C. Cir. 1994), and *Sierra Club v. Chevron USA, Inc.*, 834 F.2d 1517 (9th Cir. 1987).
- **Participation:** Stone recusal or nonparticipation not established; check simulator's roster.

## B. Law entering the case


### Issue 1. Applicability and accrual of § 2462

- **Controlling simulated law:** Unverified.
- **Public statutory rule:** 28 U.S.C. § 2462 applies a five-year period, absent a different congressional enactment, to actions to enforce civil fines, penalties, and forfeitures. FECA had no separate express civil-penalty limitations period for the claim at issue.
- **Other governing authority:** Ninth Circuit *Walsh* treated penalty assessment as enforcement; the D.C. Circuit's *3M Co.* rejected a general discovery-of-violation accrual rule for § 2462. *FEC v. Williams* adopted that analysis.
- **Open point:** Whether “first accrued” means the prohibited transaction, the agency's later probable-cause finding, or a different legally required enforcement step, and whether a broad discovery rule is tenable in punitive public enforcement.

### Issue 2. Fraudulent concealment and diligence

- **Controlling simulated law:** Unverified.
- **Public-law source:** *Holmberg* recognized equitable relief from a limitations bar for fraud concealed from a diligent claimant. Common applications require actual concealment of operative facts and reasonable diligence, not simply an allegation that the underlying wrongdoing involved deception.
- **Question:** Can facially accurate reports that identify **nominal contributors** give legally sufficient notice of secret reimbursement by Williams, and what diligence must an agency show after a tip reveals the scheme?
- **Open point:** The Ninth Circuit majority treated the existence of listed donor names as sufficient notice, as a matter of law. Judge Fletcher disputed that conclusion because the alleged offense consisted precisely of hiding the real source behind true nominal identities.

### Issue 3. Required conciliation and limitations tolling

- **Controlling simulated law:** Unverified.
- **Other law:** Former 2 U.S.C. § 437g(a) required specified notice and conciliation steps before an FEC district-court civil action. *Sierra Club v. Chevron* addressed tolling while an agency completes mandated administrative prerequisites under a different statute.
- **Open point:** Whether compliance periods which federal law **forbids the agency from skipping** temporarily suspend a general limitation, and how long such suspension runs. The Ninth Circuit did not decide the issue because even its maximum 125-day figure was too little **if** accrual began in January 1988.

### Issue 4. Ancillary declaratory or injunctive relief

- **Controlling simulated law:** Unverified.
- **Public authority:** *Cope v. Anderson* states that equity generally refuses relief ancillary to a claim whose legal penalty action is time-barred. Distinct remedies preventing independently proven current or imminent misconduct may require their own statutory and equitable analysis.
- **Open point:** The FEC's requested ten-year forward-looking injunction was founded on the same old transactions; the Court must not silently turn a limitations period for penalties into permission to enjoin lawful future conduct.

**Earlier same-term decisions:** None identified as effective on these questions before the petition's consideration; simulator reconciliation necessary. **Administrative structure:** 28 U.S.C. § 2462, FECA's complaint, probable-cause and conciliation steps, and ordinary judicial remedial powers each keep their separate source.

## C. Decision-material litigation positions

**Section 2462:** Williams argues that a civil penalty must be asserted within five years after the violation, whether the suit initially **assesses** or later **collects** the sanction. The FEC presses a narrower reading of “enforcement” or a later accrual point. The Ninth Circuit found the text and federal appellate authority supported application from the original offense, not from an agency's discretionary internal determination. Repose, timely evidence, and notice strongly support that limit.

**Concealment:** The FEC's strongest case is that the violation was a disguised-payor scheme, meaning reports naming employees as nominal contributors did not give notice of who truly supplied the funds. Judge Fletcher rejected a categorical requirement that the FEC independently investigate every facially normal contribution. Williams responds that donors were identified in public filings, that agency investigators had means to uncover the transactions, and that concealment alone does not excuse long enforcement delay without demonstrated diligence. Distinguish what the FEC knew before September 12, 1988 from what it did after receiving Hooton's complaint.

**Administrative prerequisites:** The FEC cites mandatory notice and attempted conciliation, arguing the ordinary five-year clock should stop during federally compelled waiting periods. Williams points out that optional investigatory delay cannot enlarge a penalty deadline. The Ninth Circuit majority expressly did **not** resolve the mandatory-process rule because even assuming all 125 days, the case was untimely under its January 1988 accrual date. Fletcher reasoned that starting after September 12, 1988 left the lawsuit only 37 days past five years, so mandatory administrative tolling could change the answer.

**Injunction:** The FEC favors preventing repeat violations. Williams argues an injunction seeking enforcement of the same stale wrong cannot evade § 2462 by a change of label. The record's adjudicated past conduct does not by itself establish an independent ongoing violation or imminent repeat risk.

## D. Lawful paths and uncertainty

1. **Affirm Ninth Circuit's time bar:** Hold that § 2462 starts with the violation, no sufficient equitable concealment/diligence showing exists, and even generous compelled-process tolling is too short. Dismiss penalty and derivative injunction.
2. **Vacate/remand the tolling ruling:** Retain § 2462 and offense-date accrual as defaults but reject the premise that nominal donor disclosure legally reveals a hidden payor. Require specific findings on concealment, diligence, the point of actual discovery, and statutory mandatory waiting periods; other appellate issues may remain open.
3. **Reverse on tolling with a fully established record:** If fraudulent concealment to the September 1988 complaint and legally mandated pause of sufficient duration are undisputed or properly resolved, reinstate timeliness and remand for unresolved merits/remedy questions.
4. **Limit remedy separately:** Even if enforcement is timely, a ten-year injunction requires statutory authorization and proof sufficient for prospective equitable relief; it is not automatic from a civil fine.

Material uncertainties: the petition's exact issues, the record bearing on FEC diligence between 1988 and 1993, and whether compelled administrative intervals can be calculated conclusively without further fact finding. The case presents statutory administration and equity more directly than a speech restriction.
