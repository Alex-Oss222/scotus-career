## Proceedings, parties, and record

- Oregon voters adopted Measure 16 on November 8, 1994. Plaintiffs filed November 23, fifteen days before its scheduled effectiveness, asserting equal protection, due process, First Amendment religious and association rights, ADA, Rehabilitation Act, and RFRA claims. The district court preliminarily enjoined implementation and on August 3, 1995 granted summary judgment and a permanent injunction on equal protection alone. Its rationale was inadequate safeguards for depressed/incompetent terminally ill adults compared with protections against suicide afforded other adults. Other merits theories were not decided. Opinion ¶¶1–3.
- For the equal-protection, due-process, ADA, and Rehabilitation Act claims, the district court recognized patient plaintiffs Eric Dutson and Janice Elsner plus Maryville Nursing Home and Willows Home. Doctors Gary Lee and William Petty cross-appealed denial of their patient-representative standing; plaintiffs also cross-appealed denial of certification of a terminal-disease class. For First Amendment/RFRA claims the district court recognized Petty, the facilities, and their administrators/owners. Opinion ¶¶4–5.
- Dutson died July 2, 1995, before district-court judgment. The lower opinion says counsel apparently notified the court August 8, after judgment; this is a judicial characterization tied to CR193 and CR191/ER98–99, not independent review of those instruments. Footnote1.
- Proper defendants remaining below were Lane County District Attorney Douglas Harcleroad and eleven Oregon Board of Medical Examiners members. Oregon, its attorney general, and governor had been dismissed on Eleventh Amendment grounds; several citizens intervened as defendants under Rule24. Defendants appealed both the summary judgment and denial of their summary-judgment motion on all claims. Opinion ¶6. No separate Supreme Court docket, grant date, submission date, or later party filing is established by this source.
- Elsner had progressive muscular dystrophy, had outlived earlier expectations, and had experienced clinical depression with ambivalence about living. She had no prognosis of less than six months. The court assumed statutory terminal-disease status for analysis, partly because providers might represent actual terminally ill patients; it did not find that predicate. Opinion ¶14; footnote2.
- Her alleged harm was dying through the Act contrary to her true wishes. The appellate opinion describes the necessary contingencies: disabling depression or undue influence; oral/written request; erroneous capacity judgments by attending and consulting physicians; witnesses' failure to detect incapacity/coercion; continued impairment through renewed request after fifteen days; another erroneous informed-decision determination; and ingestion. Plaintiffs' appellee brief at49 asserted heightened depression risk, depression's link to suicide, and primary-care failures to detect suicidal depression. The court assumed arguendo the record could support those general assertions but found no individualized sufficient threat. Opinion ¶¶14–22. The assertions are not newly established empirical findings.

## Distinct judgments and reasons below

1. The court found no injury in fact for the equal-protection, due-process, ADA, and Rehabilitation Act theories. It applied *Lujan*, *Lyons*, *O'Shea*, and lower-circuit future-injury precedents to the described contingencies. It rejected difficulty finding another claimant as a reason to dispense with injury. Providers' potential representation did not cure the absence of an injured patient; an uncertified proposed class did not supply named-plaintiff standing. Opinion ¶¶7–25. Traceability and redressability were questioned but expressly not decided as additional grounds. Footnotes3–4 describe independent actors and leave redressability open; the source's then-pending Washington litigation is only a historical chronology statement, not current entering law.
2. Footnote5 separately records the argument that §4.01(1)'s good-faith immunity lowers the existing reasonable-care standard of O.R.S.§677.095. The court assumed that statutory interpretation without deciding it, and held that reduced protection alone, absent concrete actual/imminent personal injury from it, was insufficient. This separate theory should not disappear in a generic threatened-prosecution description.
3. For forced participation, plaintiffs asserted duties to transfer records (§4.01(4)), advise concerning procedures (§3.01(2)), and designate a facility witness (§2.02(4)). Assuming those duties without construing the statute, the court found no concrete injury because the challenged provisions supplied no penalty; it distinguished that requirement from imminence, which it said probably would be met if a cognizable injury existed. It separately found no present hardship sufficient for ripeness; at worst civil enforcement could be challenged when brought. It acknowledged the issues' predominantly legal fitness. Opinion ¶¶26–29.
4. For forced association, facilities and administrators asserted inability to exclude participating physicians under §4.01(2); they had not alleged any physicians at their facilities would participate. The court found no injury in fact. Opinion ¶¶30–31.
5. Footnote6 describes an additional informed-consent argument under 42U.S.C.§1395cc(f)(1)(A)(i) and O.R.S.§677.097. The court did not consider it because it challenged other informed-consent requirements, not Measure16's validity. It is not an adjudicated merits rejection.
6. The operative judgment vacated the district court's judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss the complaint for lack of ArticleIII jurisdiction. Opinion ¶¶32–33. Standing and ripeness were reviewed de novo. No substantive assisted-suicide holding was entered by this lower decision.

## Statutory instrument actually reproduced

The entire Measure16 text appears in the source appendix, ¶¶34–145. It is the enactment reproduced in the lower opinion, not independent certification that no later amendment occurred by January20,1998.

- §§1.01 and2.01: capable adult Oregon resident, medically confirmed terminal disease expected within reasonable judgment to cause death within six months, voluntary wish and written request; consulting physician verifies prognosis/capacity/voluntariness. §§2.02,3.01–3.04 prescribe witnesses, physician duties, counseling upon suspected impairment, and informed decision. §§3.06–3.08 require oral and written requests, renewed oral request after at least fifteen days, rescission opportunity, and at least48hours from written request to prescription.
- §4.01(1): “No person shall be subject to civil or criminal liability or professional disciplinary action for participating in good faith compliance with this Act.” Section4.01(2) prohibits professional organizations/associations or providers from penalizing participation or refusal in good-faith compliance. Section4.01(4) disclaims a duty to participate in provision of medication but requires, upon request when care transfers, transfer of relevant medical records. These separate clauses must not be collapsed.
- §4.02 separately criminalizes specified forgery/concealment and coercion/undue influence; it preserves civil damages for other negligence/intentional misconduct and other applicable criminal penalties. Section3.14 does not authorize lethal injection, mercy killing, or active euthanasia. Section5.01 supplies severability.
