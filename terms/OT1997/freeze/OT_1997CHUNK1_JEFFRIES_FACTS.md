## Supported lower-record facts

- Leonard Jeffries was a tenured City College/CUNY professor and chair of its Black Studies department. His Albany speech concerned public-school curriculum and historical racial oppression and included derogatory statements about Jews. The lower opinion treated its subjects as matters of public concern.
- After the speech, President Bernard Harleston and Chancellor W. Ann Reynolds arranged Board consideration of a one-year chair term, compared with the usual three years. They were not trustees and did not vote.
- Nine trustees voted for the one-year term, four voted for immediate removal, and one abstained. Trustee Blanche Bernstein, one of the four immediate-removal voters, died during trial, and Jeffries discontinued his claims against her. Fifteen defendants remained.
- The first group of jury answers found action because of the speech, no actual disruption of effective operation, and motivation by a reasonable expectation of disruption. These are distinct findings; absence of actual harm does not negate the predictive finding as a record fact.
- Later answers identified six defendants—Harleston, Reynolds, Edith Everett, Herman Badillo, Sylvia Bloom and Harold Jacobs—as acting because of the speech and as not taking the same action absent it. The jury also found malicious intent to violate rights or injure Jeffries, or callous/reckless disregard of First Amendment rights, as to those six. The verdict's alternative formulations must not be converted into a single additional finding.
- Three of those six were trustees who voted for immediate removal; one was the abstaining trustee; two were nonvoting officials. The other nine defendants were the trustees who voted for the one-year term. The published opinion states that the jury did not attribute the same retaliatory animus to those nine.
- CUNY bylaws and testimony supported the lower court's description of the chair as ministerial rather than policymaking. Jeffries retained his tenured professorship. The received sanitized facts additionally state that the off-campus event was unaffiliated with CUNY and that the speech was not assigned or controlled as an official duty; those formulations are received source recitations, not independently established from an original transcript here.
- The district court ordered two years' reinstatement as chair and entered reduced punitive awards against the six defendants after rejecting qualified immunity. The 1994 panel disposition sustained reinstatement but found the punitive interrogatories irreconcilable and required retrial on that issue.
- The **1995 judgment actually under review reversed the district-court judgment and directed judgment for defendants**. Its grounds included the reasonable-disruption prediction, its legal balancing of that predicted disruption against speech value, and a separate causation conclusion based on the nine trustees' votes. It treated those votes as an independent cause despite Harleston and Reynolds's role in presenting the proposal. These are the lower court's reasons, not new findings or governing simulated law.
- The 1995 opinion itself considered the answers concerning the six defendants irreconcilable and said such a conflict ordinarily called for retrial; it found retrial unnecessary on its independent causation ground. Any fresh packet must preserve both propositions without adopting either as the inevitable resolution.
- An amicus argued for special protection of university academic freedom. The lower court rejected that argument on the distinction between the chair appointment and retained faculty role. The supplied opinion does not support attribution of additional concessions to parties or amici.
