## Question, lower judgment, and scope

The reviewed question is whether offense-date 18 U.S.C. §1001 permits an “exculpatory no” exception for a simple false denial of wrongdoing in response to federal investigative questioning. This is an analytical formulation; the original petition and grant order are not claimed to have been recovered verbatim. Brogan seeks reversal of his false-statement conviction; the United States defends it. The separate conviction for accepting unlawful employer payments is not automatically opened by this question.

The Second Circuit affirmed. It accepted that the answers were genuine exculpatory denials under formulations recognized elsewhere, then rejected the exception categorically as unsupported by statutory language or legislative history. It reasoned that “no” conveys a statement and that a privilege to remain silent does not confer a right to lie. The panel consisted of Winter, Jacobs, and Cabranes, with Winter writing and no separate writing in the published exculpatory-no opinion. Other appellate issues were resolved by a separate summary order, which is not represented as read or reopened here.

The panel expressly reserved whether §1001 willfulness requires knowledge that the false statement is unlawful. It also recognized that surprise or lack of reflection might bear on actual criminal intent in another evidentiary record. It found sufficient evidence in this record, referring to the agents' warning and circumstances suggesting deliberation. The later warning did not precede the original answer; absence of a correction is not silently treated as a newly charged affirmative statement. The appellate reservation is not a new independent claim, an established instructional error, or an order for retrial.

## Material facts and supported party positions

While a union officer in 1987–1988, Brogan accepted cash from JRD Management Corporation, which employed union members. On **October 4, 1993**, Department of Labor and Internal Revenue Service agents made an unannounced visit to his home. They identified themselves, described the investigation, and sought cooperation. They said counsel should contact the U.S. Attorney's Office if he wished to cooperate, with appointed counsel available if necessary. He agreed to answer questions without counsel present.

Asked whether he had received cash or gifts from JRD, Brogan answered “no.” The agents already possessed company records of the payments. After the answer, they disclosed those records and said that lying to investigators was criminal. Brogan did not change his answers; the agents asked no further questions and the interview ended shortly thereafter. A jury convicted him of the false statement and unlawful employer payments. An agent's stated desire to preserve surprise establishes the interview's reported arrangement, not a judicial finding of illicit charging motive, custody, compulsion, entrapment, or manufactured evidence.

Brogan did not dispute that the answer was false or knowingly and willfully given and reportedly conceded that a literal reading defeated his proposed exception. The concession bears on the presented statutory argument; it does not prove unreported instructions or settle every hypothetical element question. He argued that §1001 protects governmental functions from perversion and that a bare denial does not produce that harm, particularly when investigators already know the contrary facts. He invoked Fifth Amendment values, the practical choice between admission, silence, and denial, possible ignorance of the right to remain silent, fear of adverse use of silence, and the risk of an added felony charge.

The Government's supported defense is the statutory coverage of knowingly false statements in federal-agency matters and the distinction between silence and deceit. The Solicitor General reportedly acknowledged that the provision could escalate otherwise innocent conduct into a felony; that litigation statement is not a concession that Brogan's payments were innocent or that this prosecution was unlawful. The reported expiration of limitations for four of five underlying payments does not establish that every charged offense was time barred or that the false-statement prosecution lacked a separate temporal basis.

The charging-date U.S. Attorneys' Manual stated that §1001 did not apply to an exculpatory no; later 1996 and September 1997 formulations described a Department policy against charging a mere denial of guilt during investigative questioning. These are authenticated policy quotations in the source handoff, not statutory amendments or established judicially enforceable defenses. Proposed congressional exceptions were not enacted. A sentencing policy against treating ordinary nonperjurious denials as obstruction does not itself alter the substantive offense.

Lanier preserves conduct-time fair warning and forbids unforeseeable criminal enlargement, while rejecting a fixed requirement of a Supreme Court factual twin. A 1997 holding cannot itself supply Brogan notice in 1993; the relevant question is what the statute and existing authority then made apparent. ## Supported alternatives and remedial limits

1. **Reject the exception under the enacted coverage.** A deliberate negative answer can be a false statement in an authorized investigative matter, and the statutory text contains no express oral-denial carveout. Bryson and the distinction between silence and lying support that construction. The strongest counterargument is that statutory setting, history, Fifth Amendment concerns, and the longstanding lower-court exception may support a narrower meaning even without an express exemption. The answer must address those sources rather than treat charging discretion as a statutory element or a cure for ambiguity.
2. **Recognize a bounded statutory exception for a bare investigative denial.** The narrow construction could distinguish a reactive oral denial from volunteered deception, affirmative misdirection, claims for governmental benefits, or regulatory submissions. Bryson's affidavit setting and the agency-function rationale allow that argument to be considered without creating a constitutional license to lie. Its central difficulty is fitting the limitation to the actual “any false” language, the statutory disjunctive clauses, and the competing lower formulations. A judgment on this route must state the exception's actual conditions and why this record meets them; it may not import an entire circuit checklist without analysis.
3. **Reject a categorical exception while preserving distinct fault, materiality, or notice questions at their actual procedural scope.** The Court can confine its holding to the presented construction and leave unpresented applications open. A claim-specific remand requires a genuinely available ground and material predicate; the lower court's reservations and a research gap alone do not establish one. The conceded knowing/willful character and the record warning sequence must be respected, with no invented contrary jury finding or preserved instructional objection.

An affirmance would decide the presented false-statement issue without validating every imaginable investigative tactic. Reversal based on statutory exclusion would require count-specific implementation and would not itself disturb the employer-payment conviction or prescribe an unverified sentence. No path supplies a right to remain silent under every circumstance, a new warning mandate, automatic acquittal on other counts, or immunity from prosecution based solely on internal executive policy.
