## Supported transition dispute

Hohn's section 2255 motion was pending at AEDPA's April 24, 1996 enactment; his notice of appeal followed enactment. In his merits brief, Part V, printed pages 40–44, Hohn expressly advances a separate route to section 1254 jurisdiction: he argues Lindh's chapter-153 reasoning means that a federal prisoner whose district-court motion was filed before enactment needs no certificate at all, and hence his ordinary appeal entered the court of appeals. He seeks vacatur and a remand directing consideration of that appeal on this alternative. He distinguishes his pending district-court case from the later appellate stage and cites a split among the circuits over the certificate provision. This is an affirmative alternative argument, not an assumed concession or an argument invented during preparation.

In the United States' reply brief, printed pages 3–4, the Government objects that this alternative was not raised below or in the petition and is outside the review question. It separately opposes the argument on the merits: Hohn had not filed his notice of appeal when AEDPA became law; the certificate condition governs appellate procedure and operates prospectively when imposed before the appeal. It invokes jurisdictional/procedural and prospective-relief distinctions in Landgraf. The Government does not concede that Lindh removes the certificate requirement.

Hohn's reply, Part IV, printed pages 17–18 and notes 15–17, disputes forfeiture and scope. He says the Government itself identified Lindh in its response to the petition and he relied on it in his certiorari reply, argues the transition point responds directly to the jurisdictional question and may be addressed as jurisdictional, and relies on intervening law as an additional reason to consider it. He argues statutory text and Lindh's negative implication control before default retroactivity analysis. These competing descriptions of preservation are party submissions, not findings by this extraction.

The separate statutory-review path remains presented: Hohn argues a denied certificate proceeding is itself judicial business reviewable under section 1254(1). The Government's merits submission accepts review but favors the historical common-law-writ route and statutory stare decisis. Neither agreement on review nor agreement on remand creates jurisdiction. No proposed rule, certificate issuance, collateral relief, or choice between the general statutory-review question and the transition alternative is adopted here.
