## Historical comparison and changed legal premises

Historically the Court affirmed, 5–4: O'Connor wrote for Rehnquist, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer; Stevens dissented alone; Scalia dissented with Souter and Ginsburg. Every Associate's historical judgment vote agrees with the provisional judgment commitment. That agreement does not make the historical reasoning usable without adjustment.

The historical majority, 524 U.S. at 728–729, treated the constitutional element argument as foreclosed by historical Almendarez-Torres and treated its recidivism holding as permitting the increased maximum. Neither proposition is current law. The actual March 24 decision holds, as a statutory matter, that §1326(b)(2)'s prior conviction is an aggravated-offense element. Its constitutional-avoidance discussion has four joins and reserves the ultimate constitutional issue. The separate definitive constitutional position is not a majority rule. The decision establishes neither a general constitutional prior-conviction exception nor a general constitutional maximum-increase rule.

This changes the majority supporters' available authority, not necessarily their judgment. O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer themselves maintained the recidivism construction and its bounded constitutional permission in the actual Almendarez-Torres dissent. They must now defend the California characterization on that own-position basis and the surviving offense/sentencing distinction, without claiming that controlling Almendarez-Torres has settled it. Their historical result remains strongly supported after this removal of an unavailable premise.

The same change removes the historical dissenters' need to reject a controlling constitutional recidivism exception. Scalia, Souter and Ginsburg's historical Monge opinion expressly declined to extend Bullington to genuine noncapital sentencing, 524 U.S. at 737. Their objection was that this proceeding did the work of adjudicating an offense element, notwithstanding its label. Their earlier constitutional doubt could no longer be avoided through federal statutory construction because California had authoritatively classified its own statute. That reasoning supports the element-function ground already present in the independent handoff; it does not support merging these three Justices into Stevens's distinct extension of acquittal-like finality to noncapital sentencing.

The state's conceded failure to prove the personal-conduct qualification is a shared historical and current premise. It is not a changed fact explaining a departure. The validated neutral packet distinguishes that qualification from the fact of conviction itself; the latter was established, the former was not. No new finding about the old assault is made. Similarly, the separate prior-prison-term enhancement and the drug convictions are not placed in issue merely because the historical dissent discussed the aggregate eleven-year term.

## Proposition and compatibility controls for assembly

1. **Reach.** All eight Associates retain the direct-review merits commitment. The historical majority objected that the element theory had not been expressly presented below or in Monge's brief, then also rejected it on the merits. Scalia's footnote 2 treated it as a legal theory within the preserved double-jeopardy claim. This is a historical preservation disagreement, not evidence of a new filing. The present element characterization may answer the attachment issue inherent in the preserved claim; it does not create a separate jury, indictment or due-process claim. No new waiver or procedural default is supplied.

2. **Attachment and sufficient ground.** Stevens supports finality for this failed trial-like aggravating adjudication without requiring a capital penalty or a universal element test. Scalia, Souter and Ginsburg support the constitutional element classification and ensuing offense-acquittal finality; they reject extending Bullington to a proceeding that truly remains ordinary noncapital sentencing. O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer reject attachment on the bounded noncapital-recidivism characterization. No common judgment vote silently adopts a different group's sufficient ground.

3. **Possible common reversal proposition.** All four reversal supporters can join the confined conclusion that the personal-conduct qualification not established by the prior conviction, and required here to authorize the greater maximum, receives offense-element finality after the State's completed unsuccessful proof attempt. Stevens need not abandon his additional ground to join that conclusion. A proposed writing must actually adopt this characterization to obtain the three element-ground joins. A writing that calls the proceeding genuine sentencing throughout and simply extends Bullington does not obtain them. Actual final joins remain an assembly task; no common opinion is assigned or deemed joined here.

4. **Breadth.** The historical Scalia opinion and its Souter/Ginsburg joins supply affirmative evidence for their constitutional objection to treating a fact necessary to authorize a greater maximum as merely sentencing, including their rejection of an automatic recidivism exemption. Their independent acceptance of a case-confined operative holding is not a refusal of that underlying principle. Nevertheless, neither the prior statutory Almendarez-Torres holding nor a shared disposition in Monge establishes a Court rule for every possible maximum-increasing fact. No comprehensive holding on mandatory minima, unchanged-range findings, all administrative prior-conviction determinations, collateral attacks, or a distinct jury remedy is supplied by this handoff.

5. **Insufficiency.** Every Associate accepts the State's concession. Once constitutional acquittal protection attaches, Burks and Hinds bar another opportunity to supply missing prosecution proof. No Justice proposes a trial-error characterization or a proof-repair exception to that rule. The four affirmance positions depend on nonattachment, not on forgiveness of insufficiency. Watts's different-burden evidentiary-use distinction supplies no permission to retry a protected allegation under the same criminal burden.

6. **Precedent.** Caspari leaves direct-review merits open. Witte and Watts remain bounded to their actual conviction-authorized sentencing uses and reservations. McMillan's unchanged-maximum holding does not resolve this increase. Bullington and Rumsey remain effective for their established capital adjudications; Stevens may extend their finality principle, whereas the three element-ground supporters distinguish this constitutional offense adjudication from ordinary noncapital sentencing. DiFrancesco retains the ordinary sentencing/acquittal distinction but does not decide the disputed classification by label alone. Dixon retains Grady; neither its retention nor a historical dissent's elements discussion overrules it. Hinds and Burks retain their mandatory consequence after actual insufficiency in a constitutionally protected adjudication.

No Marks aggregation is proposed. A numerical overlap in remedy cannot combine Stevens's independent extension ground with an express refusal of that extension, or create a general rule out of constitutionally incompatible grounds.
