## What the historical decision actually establishes

The historical judgment affirmed the Seventh Circuit, 7–2. Kennedy's opinion had six full supporters: Rehnquist, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer. Ginsburg concurred in the judgment and expressly endorsed the operative employer-liability rule and the rejection of dispositive harassment labels; she did not join the remainder of Kennedy's opinion. Thomas dissented, joined by Scalia. Thus the operative historical rule had seven express supporters, not merely six, and Ginsburg cannot be counted historically as reserving the defense.

The majority distinguished statutory discrimination from employer attribution. Numerous unfulfilled threats were reviewed as hostile-environment allegations; the Court accepted the lower court's severe-or-pervasive treatment for the case and left the sufficiency of a single unfulfilled threat open. Agency aid was distinct from ordinary employment scope and, ordinarily, from apparent authority. Employment proximity alone was inadequate. The opinion then adopted general vicarious liability for an actionable hostile environment created by an immediate or successively higher supervisor, coupled with a defense where no tangible employment action occurred. The employer had to prove by a preponderance both reasonable care to prevent and correct promptly and the employee's unreasonable failure to use preventive or corrective opportunities or otherwise avoid harm. A written policy was not invariably necessary; unreasonable nonuse of a complaint process normally satisfied the second element but was not its exclusive proof. Harassment culminating in a tangible employment action excluded the defense. Pages 754–765 explain this package; a significant employment-status or benefit change was not limited to lost pay.

The historical remand allowed the employee to prove her claim and the employer to prove the newly recognized defense. It did not find either proved. The district court could consider pleading amendments or discovery supplementation; neither was mandatory. Ginsburg's short writing adopts the operative package without all the majority's agency discussion. Thomas and Scalia would instead impose employer negligence for hostile environments and attribute actual discriminatory company employment actions without that requirement. They treated the known policy and noncomplaint as defeating negligence in this record. Their dissent also objected to status-driven liability, apparent-authority confusion, different treatment of race and sex harassment, and the new defense's uncertainty. Footnote 3 accepted that unfulfilled threats could create a hostile environment; footnote 4 tied its restrictive agency understanding to ordinary business/apparent authority. None of these objections may be reduced to a generic preference for defendants.

## Actual changed premises and the defense fork

The operative entering rule is **Employer attribution of supervisor-created harassment**: actual-authority aid from Andrade, February 26, 1997; express preponderance burden and the separate apparent-authority mechanics from Harrison, September 15, 1997; current wording clarified in Faragher, September 8, 1997. The plaintiff must prove actual delegated supervisory power materially aided creation or maintenance of the actionable environment. Rank, proximity and employment are insufficient. Final hiring/firing power, a fulfilled threat, economic loss and higher-management notice are not invariably necessary. Personal purpose or violation of a prohibition alone does not negate actual aid. Apparent authority separately requires an employer-attributable manifestation and reasonable reliance; it is not a condition of actual-power attribution.

All eight Associates themselves joined that route in Andrade and Harrison and the conclusive actual-aid application in Faragher Parts II–IV. Those are substantive prior positions, including a supported liability judgment without any completed personnel-action requirement. They are stronger evidence than a generic assertion that precedent differs. Faragher and Harrison also preserve a complete-defense question; they neither decide it negatively nor require its indefinite postponement. Their express reservation alone is not a reason to retain P3.

The strongest historical alternative remains legally available: preserve plaintiff-proved actual aid but add a separately justified defense for effective prevention/correction and unreasonable employee nonuse, specify burdens and treatment of completed actions, and permit proof on remand. The present known-policy record makes that question more salient than Faragher's uncommunicated policy. Missing originals would not prohibit announcing a legal defense and leaving its application below; the historical Court itself did not find the defense proved. Accordingly, lack of policy details is not the sufficient explanation for a departure.

The substantive reason for retaining the narrower result is that the historical opinion introduced the defense as part of resolving an otherwise unsettled and malleable attribution standard. Its final rule made immediate or successively higher supervisory status the trigger for general vicarious liability. Current law instead already assigns the plaintiff a concrete actual-power/material-aid burden and leaves ordinary non-attribution available when that connection fails. A new complete defense is therefore not needed to supply the limit on automatic supervisory imputation that historical pages 763–765 sought to secure. Prevention incentives retain force, but do not alone dictate extinguishing all already attributable harm, a tangible-action dividing line, or the historical two-element burden. Each Associate's prior position and particular reason for retaining that distinction appear below. This is a selected bounded resolution, not a holding that the historical defense is unlawful or that no future case can adopt it.

The majority's historical desire to give broad guidance does not compel a new formula once Andrade, Faragher and Harrison already provide the governing answer. P1–P2 reject the submission/completed-retaliation and notice-only barriers while preserving the proof burden; P3 rejects only a policy/nonuse shortcut and leaves the complete-defense question open; P4 supplies the resulting ordinary remand. The employee's opportunity to prove actual aid does not become a judicial finding of agency or liability. No universal tangible-action definition or single-threat sufficiency rule is necessary.

## Reconciled operative propositions and remedy


**P1 — No submission or completed-retaliation prerequisite.** An employee who rejects sexual advances and suffers no completed tangible retaliation may still pursue a Title VII hostile-environment claim. The ordinary sex-causation, objective/subjective environment and employer-attribution requirements remain. The Court decides no independently preserved quid-pro-quo claim by relabeling this one.

**P2 — Existing attribution rule applied.** The plaintiff retains the preponderance burden on actual delegated power and material aid. Slowik's supported role in hiring/promotion, though subject to superior approval, and the alleged use of work-related authority cannot be excluded merely because the threats were unfulfilled, his purpose was personal, he was not the alter ego or higher management lacked notice. These features permit the supported actual-authority inquiry on ordinary summary-judgment principles; they establish neither liability nor every element as a matter of law. A supervisor's claim of power alone would require the distinct apparent-authority analysis if relied on instead.

**P3 — Limited policy disposition and continuing reservation.** Ellerth's knowledge that a policy existed and failure to report during employment do not alone establish a complete defense or eliminate earlier material aid. The Court does not find this policy effective or ineffective, the reporting routes safe or unsafe, or Ellerth's conduct reasonable or unreasonable. The general complete-defense question remains open on the precise terms stated in B. The Court neither adopts a special tangible-action exception nor a new attribution burden shift.

**P4 — Formal judgment and ordinary remand.** Affirm the Seventh Circuit's judgment insofar as it reversed summary judgment on the appealed Title VII employer-attribution claim, and remand for further proceedings consistent with P1–P3. The judgment does not endorse every rationale in the en banc opinions. The lower courts may resolve supported, genuinely preserved independent grounds through ordinary procedures. No mandatory trial, second agency factfinding proceeding, liability finding, award or discovery order follows. The separately dismissed constructive-discharge claim remains outside this disposition absent established preservation; no new resignation-based claim is created.

**Support:** Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer each support P1–P4 at this stated generality and the same formal judgment. This is an eight-Associate commitment coalition, not a final nine-Justice tally. Their separate recoverability positions below are not added to P3.

## Reconciled separate remedial position — three Associates only


Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer support a separately expressed Title VII avoidable-consequences proposal. It is an application of their own June 22 Gebser published remedial position to the employment setting, not a claim that Gebser decided Title VII or that this proposal already controls.

Under their proposed rule, after the plaintiff establishes the underlying claim and otherwise recoverable harm, the employer bears the burden of establishing by a preponderance that internal remedies were adequately publicized and likely to provide redress without undue risk, effort or expense; that the employee unreasonably failed to use those measures; and that the particular loss sought consequently could have been avoided. Recovery would be reduced or barred only to that proved extent. The explicit preponderance term is part of this modeled civil remedial proposal, not an assertion that Gebser announced a new standard of proof. A paper policy, nonreporting alone, an unsafe or ineffective route, eventual correction or the possibility of a complaint does not establish the defense. Harm incurred before a reasonable opportunity to obtain protection, or harm that those measures could not have prevented, is not eliminated by later nonuse.

This proposal creates no automatic complete employer immunity and does not shift Ellerth's existing attribution burden. It creates no special immunity conditioned on the absence of a tangible employment action and no categorical exception triggered by the presence of one: those questions belong to the distinct unresolved complete-defense issue. If all otherwise compensable loss were proved avoidable, the causal proposal could eliminate that loss recovery; it would not erase the discrimination, establish that every form of relief was unavailable or decide fees. No facts here establish its application. Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas do not join adoption of these proposed terms. The three proponents may join P1–P4 while explaining this additional, noncontrolling position; no current author or assignment is designated.
