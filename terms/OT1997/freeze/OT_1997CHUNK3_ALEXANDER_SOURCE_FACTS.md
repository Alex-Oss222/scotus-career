## Factual and procedural propositions established by the opinion

- The case is an appeal from a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal, with appellate plenary review accepting factual allegations. It is not a finding of malpractice or a trial on medical causation. McKee authored the court opinion; Greenberg and Alito sat; Alito wrote separately, agreeing with the essential disposition and much of the reasoning.
- Karen Alexander was eight and one-half months pregnant when admitted July 15, 1992, for delivery. Recorded fetal vital signs fourteen minutes before cesarean delivery appeared normal; the child was stillborn. The opinion describes the certificate's stated cardiovascular-collapse cause, an autopsy, and the name Kaylyn Elissa Alexander. Footnote 2 distinguishes the plaintiffs’ statement of death in utero/before actual birth from hospital records’ “interpartum demise.” The precise instant and medical mechanism are not independently established.
- In July 1994 Alexander filed a state malpractice action individually and in proposed estate capacities. She sought emotional-distress and mental-suffering recovery individually and wrongful-death/survival relief in representative capacities. The surrogate denied letters of administration ad prosequendum on October 18 and 31, 1994, because the child was stillborn.
- Alexander, lawyer Dennis Drazin and Drazin & Warshaw filed a federal §1983 action October 28, 1994, against the Governor, Attorney General and all twenty-one county surrogates. Alexander asserted individual claims, claims on behalf of her child, and proposed mother/child classes. The Drazin plaintiffs asserted their own and proposed lawyer/law-firm class claims. The federal complaint originally requested declarations, injunctions, letters, and money damages.
- **Footnote 6:** The appeal did **not** challenge dismissal of Count V money damages. Declaratory and injunctive claims in Counts I–IV were the appealed components. The original request for money does not establish that damages remained on appeal.
- Fifteen surrogates entered consent orders agreeing not to oppose further factual/legal contentions and to be bound by future orders (fn5). This was not an appellate merits holding for plaintiffs, a statewide default, or an agreement by every defendant. Other defendants sought dismissal. The motions for class certification and summary judgment were denied consequentially after dismissal; the appellate court did not independently resolve their propriety because it affirmed dismissal (fn7).
- The opinion identifies substituted Attorney General Verniero and an October 25, 1995 caption amendment. No separate Supreme Court filing dates are established.

## State-law provisions and source qualifications

The court quotes N.J.S.A. 2A:31-1: liability when the death of a person is caused by a wrongful act, neglect or default that would have supported that person's damages action if death had not ensued. It describes wrongful-death damages as survivors' pecuniary loss, including the pecuniary value of specified support, services and advice; emotional loss is not itself that statutory recovery. Section 2A:31-5 also permits medical/hospital/funeral expenses.

The court quotes N.J.S.A. 2A:15-3: executors/administrators may sue for a trespass to the deceased's person or property, recovering damages the deceased would have had if living, with funeral/burial expenses in qualifying death cases. It describes conscious predeath pain/suffering and loss-of-enjoyment damages in the relevant New Jersey precedents. Wrongful death and survival are separate causes with different beneficiaries and measures.

Giardina v. Bennett, 111 N.J. 412, 545 A.2d 139 (1988), is the authoritative New Jersey construction excluding stillbirth from wrongful death and recognizing parents’ direct tort injuries. **The court expressly states Giardina did not decide survival.** The federal district court inferred a comparable survival limitation; neither party disputed that construction on appeal, so the circuit assumed it. That assumption is not a recovered state-high-court survival holding.

The parents’ Giardina remedy is not unlimited. Footnote 17 quotes Carey v. Lovett, 132 N.J. 44, 622 A.2d 1279, 1288 (1993): emotional distress must be so severe as to cause physical manifestations or destroy basic emotional security; ordinary worry/stress or upset is insufficient. The opinion does not find Alexander actually satisfied that standard, succeeded in the state action, or received damages. Footnote 15 expressly disclaims a holding that the Fourteenth Amendment requires a tort remedy for prenatal injury.

## Reported lower arguments and rulings, without fresh target framing

1. The child-representative claim asserted unequal treatment of fetuses injured prenatally and born alive before dying versus those stillborn. The circuit relied on original Roe's federal-personhood holding and distinguished constitutional status from allegations of biological humanity. It accepted the well-pleaded factual allegations for Rule 12 purposes but treated the legal category as distinct. The opinion’s references to **historical post-divergence Casey** are not governing simulated-law extracts; the separate exact-law handoff supplies the actual rules.
2. Footnote 8 says the circuit **assumed** Alexander had standing to assert the child's claim. The opinion did not establish all representative-standing predicates. The individual mother separately asserted her own due-process/equality rights, so those routes are not collapsed in this extraction.
3. The mother asserted a fundamental relationship with the fetus and challenged the denial of the additional tort routes. The court did not decide whether the prenatal relationship itself is fundamental; it held these remedial classifications did not interfere with that relationship or protected reproductive decisions and applied rational-basis review. It invoked legitimate limits on tort recovery and proof difficulties, while acknowledging that New Jersey could provide broader remedies.
4. The court discussed Levy, Glona, Weber and Parham and distinguished the legitimacy classifications there. It did not decide that every family-related statutory classification is immune from federal review. Its citations to historical Beach/Heller must be checked against the actual simulated sections supplied separately.
5. The Drazin plaintiffs' own-right and third-party theories failed in the circuit. It found no constitutional attorney right to bring these suits, relied on the mother's ability to assert her own interests, and discussed relationship/obstacle/conflict requirements. It did not establish Article III injury by lawyers as a factual finding; its third-party discussion used an assumed injury premise.
6. Alito's concurrence agreed that the Supreme Court had held a fetus outside Fourteenth Amendment “person,” but criticized language potentially suggesting biological human beings are constitutional nonpersons when taken out of context. He independently emphasized the absence of a recognized stillbirth-injury recovery at adoption of the Fourteenth Amendment and for years afterward. He is a lower-court source, not a member of the simulated Supreme Court roster.
