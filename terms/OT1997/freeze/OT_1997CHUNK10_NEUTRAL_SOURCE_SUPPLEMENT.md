## Geissal: applicable statutory qualification

Official source: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-524/pdf/USREPORTS-524-74.pdf, printed page 81 and note 7. The applicable section 1162(2)(D) text is copied mechanically to `OT_1997CHUNK10_GEISSAL_STATUTE_ONLY.txt` beside this handoff. The later acquired group plan must not contain an exclusion or limitation with respect to any preexisting condition of the beneficiary. That condition-specific rule is distinct from comparative inadequacy or deductible differences between plans. The magistrate found no evidence that Aetna's plan excluded or limited coverage for Geissal's condition; that is a lower-court finding, not the statutory command.

Footnote 7 separately records the 1996 HIPAA addition concerning an exclusion or limitation that does not apply to, or is satisfied by, the beneficiary under the named federal insurance-portability provisions; the opinion states that amendment was not effective when this dispute arose. Do not substitute the later text for the applicable version.

The Rule 54(b) judgment concerned Counts I (COBRA) and II (estoppel); Counts III (waiver) and IV (documents) were outside it. The record did not establish full Aetna reimbursement; counsel represented unpaid Greek Islands trip bills. These are not findings awarding damages or resolving inter-plan reimbursement.

## New Mexico ex rel. Ortiz v. Reed: objective extradition prerequisites

Official source: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-524/pdf/USREPORTS-524-151.pdf, pages 151-153, objective narrative only. Reed received a 25-year sentence for armed robbery and theft of drugs, was paroled from Ohio in 1992, and left Ohio in 1993 before a scheduled parole-officer meeting after officials said they planned revocation. Ohio sought extradition; New Mexico's Governor issued the warrant; Reed was arrested in October 1994. New Mexico's trial court granted habeas relief in January 1995 and its Supreme Court affirmed in September 1997.

The source expressly reports that New Mexico's Supreme Court agreed the first three Doran prerequisites were satisfied: facially sufficient documents, a charge in Ohio, and Reed's identity as the requested person. The dispute concerned fugitive status based on duress, not an unresolved identity, charge, or document defect. The courts credited his fear of revocation without due process and bodily injury on return; the testimony was unrebutted. Ohio was not a party at that hearing. Do not turn this into an Ohio concession or an independent federal factual finding. New Mexico also relied on its constitutional right of seeking and obtaining safety. These facts support the legal question's resolution without inventing a warrant defect or remedial fact.

## McBryde: action, statutory distinctions, and review route

Primary lower judicial text: https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/117/117.F3d.208.95-11082.html, paragraphs 47-63, 70-88, 124-136; local `mcbryde.txt`. The case is In re John H. McBryde, No. 95-11082, decided July 2, 1997. It concerns two pending cases, United States v. Satz and Torres v. Trinity Industries, not a prospective general bar on new assignments.

On April 27, 1995, district Chief Judge Buchmeyer vacated McBryde's April 25 Torres order and reassigned Torres to himself. On May 1 he vacated McBryde's contempt-related Satz findings and reassigned Satz. On May 3 McBryde declared the Satz reassignment void and signed a sentence; May 4 Buchmeyer declared that action void and directed the clerk to accept only his filings. Subsequent July/August memoranda explained the transfers and displaced McBryde's factual findings. The disputed facts concerned alleged false testimony/withheld sentencing material in Satz and the clerk's failure to invest a minor's funds and her response in Torres. Do not assume either faction's account was conclusively established.

After McBryde requested assistance and DOJ also wrote, a special committee heard witnesses October 19, 1995. It heard McBryde but refused his request to attend others' testimony. On October 20 the Judicial Council denied his request and, expressly invoking section 332, reassigned Satz and Torres retroactively effective May 1 and April 27. It adopted contrary factual conclusions and criticized his conduct. He did not acquiesce. No separate council enforcement proceeding occurred; existing district orders implemented the transfers. He sought section 1651(a) mandamus. The appellate judgment reviewed the district chief judge's implementing acts and returned the cases; it did not decide the separate Fifth Amendment claim. District phases had ended, but possible contempt matters remained, so the opinion did not regard completion as a mootness bar.

Then-effective statutory text is quoted in the contemporaneous lower opinion. Section 137 provides assignment by district rules/orders, a chief judge's assignment authority insofar as those do not otherwise prescribe, and council orders if the district judges cannot agree. Special Order 3-130 required random assignment with 44 percent of Fort Worth cases assigned to McBryde. Section 332(d)(1) authorizes necessary and appropriate orders for effective and expeditious administration of justice. Section 332(d)(2) requires officers/employees to carry out council orders and permits a district-court contempt proceeding for failure to comply with an order or section 372(c) subpoena. Section 372(c)(6)(B)(iv) authorizes, after its prescribed process, suspension of further assignments temporarily for a time certain: text about new cases, not a grant to transfer pending cases. Section 372(c)(3) addresses complaints directly related to merits or procedural rulings; (c)(6)(B) separately authorizes reprimands/censures; (c)(10) supplies internal review and finality with judicial-review preclusion except as specified. Whether these distinctions permit the transfers is for adjudication.
