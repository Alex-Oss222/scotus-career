**Habeas and evidence.** Exact registered standards included in the slice are **Crime innocence as a gateway to otherwise barred habeas claims** (*Schlup*, January 23, 1995), **Collective Brady materiality and investigating-team responsibility** (*Kyles*, April 19, 1995), **Supreme Court original habeas after an authorization-review bar** (*Felker*, June 28, 1996), **Federal-prisoner limitation and successive-motion certification**, **Second or successive state-custody habeas applications**, **Temporal reach of ordinary AEDPA habeas amendments** (*Lindh*, June 23, 1997), **No delay-based substitute for statutory habeas dismissal**, **Interim protection necessary for required habeas process**, and **Equitable delay independently of a habeas procedural bar** (*Lonchar*, April 1, 1996). The slice contains each complete trigger, qualification, and authority rather than a name-only list.

*Schlup* requires reliable new evidence assessed with the whole probative record and a showing that, more likely than not, no reasonable properly instructed juror would find guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. It requires more than doubt or ordinary prejudice, but is a gateway, not an affirmative adjudication of innocence. Evidence is not categorically excluded because it is impeachment, comes from a prisoner, or would be inadmissible at trial; reliability and the full record remain essential. Stevens wrote the controlling gateway holding, joined by O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer among the Associates. Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas preferred a stricter quantum; that objection is a prior position, not current law.

*Herrera*, January 25, 1993, establishes that its particular showing was insufficient; it establishes neither a general freestanding innocence threshold nor a controlling categorical denial of a constitutional right. O'Connor and Kennedy explained why execution of an established innocent person would be intolerable. Stevens and Souter joined Blackmun's legal position supporting a probable-innocence inquiry and remand. Scalia and Thomas's categorical objection did not command the Court.

*Kyles* assesses the cumulative lawful uses of favorable suppressed evidence for a reasonable probability of a different result. It does not require proof of innocence or item-by-item materiality. The prosecution must learn of favorable evidence known to police acting on its case team; it has no categorical duty to search every government office. The preserved distinction for evidence independently known to the defense cannot be erased. Once material Brady suppression is established, a second harmless-error screen cannot defeat relief. Souter's controlling opinion was joined by O'Connor, Stevens, Ginsburg, and Breyer among the Associates. Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas accepted cumulative materiality but disputed its application. The registered **Preserved protected-silence and personal-intent errors on federal habeas review** has its own scope; historical broad descriptions of *Brecht* cannot displace the actual simulated rule or *Kyles*'s claim-specific consequence.

*Felker* preserves original habeas despite the authorization-review bar but does not permit relabeling barred appellate review. Rule 20 requires exceptional circumstances, no adequate relief elsewhere, relevant exhaustion, and an explanation for not applying to the district court. Section 2244(b)'s restrictions inform original-writ discretion; their direct binding force in that channel remains reserved. Original jurisdiction alone supplies neither a writ nor a stay. *Triestman*, March 12, 1998, unanimously holds that § 2255's certification cross-reference incorporates § 2244(b)(3)(E)'s ordinary-certiorari bar; a petition presenting only refusal of certification cannot be converted into a different proceeding. It leaves the savings clause, substantive retroactivity, carry liability, and factual inquiry unresolved.

*Lindh*'s registered holding concerns pending noncapital chapter 153 applications and does not decide every Title I transition or automatically settle chapter 154 in a capital case. *Trest*, December 9, 1997, holds that courts need not raise an omitted nonjurisdictional default sua sponte; it does not hold that an asserted default is waived, or decide the full range of permissible sua-sponte practice.
