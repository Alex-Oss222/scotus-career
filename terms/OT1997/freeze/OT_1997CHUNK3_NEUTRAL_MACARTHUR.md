**Question and scope:** Whether the Privacy Act permits continued retention of lawfully collected records describing First Amendment activity after their original law-enforcement pertinence has ended, and whether the plaintiffs have standing for their constitutional challenge. Lindblom's statutory claim requires its own Article III, statutory civil-action and remedial analysis before a contested retention holding. The Foundation asserts only a constitutional claim. ## Full lower record, including separate writing and footnote

Judge Douglas H. Ginsburg wrote for himself and Randolph; Tatel concurred in the constitutional-standing result on a narrower rationale and dissented on the Privacy Act construction. Douglas H. Ginsburg is not Justice Ruth Bader Ginsburg. The complete opinion, Tatel writing and majority footnote were read. The court affirmed summary judgment for the FBI.

The Foundation makes grants to organizations addressing political, social and economic issues. Its then-president Lance Lindblom sometimes met foreign leaders and political dissidents. After learning of FBI interest, plaintiffs requested their records under FOIA. The FBI reported a twenty-three-page Lindblom file and five pages mentioning the Foundation in other files, with no separate Foundation file. Some released redacted pages describe Lindblom's associational activities. Other information was withheld under FOIA Exemptions 1, 2, 7(C) and 7(D). The court's casual interchange of “file” and “record” for this case does not displace the Act's separate record/system definitions for a genuinely disputed remedial question.

The action sought expungement of associational records and an injunction against maintaining such records in the future. Lindblom invoked the Privacy Act and the First Amendment; the Foundation relied on the First Amendment. The district court examined withheld documents in camera and found they concerned investigations of other persons; plaintiffs appeared through incidental, apparently innocent contacts. It granted the FBI summary judgment and denied further discovery. On appeal, plaintiffs abandoned FOIA and Fifth/Ninth Amendment claims. Lindblom did not challenge the original collection, so the panel assumed that the records were pertinent to and within the scope of an authorized law-enforcement activity when collected. That premise does not establish that every disputed record is currently irrelevant, that its retention causes no statutory adverse effect, or that a particular Privacy Act remedial exemption applies.

The majority read §552a(e)(7)'s pertinence qualification as describing the record, rather than imposing continuing necessity for retention. It emphasized that an authorized law-enforcement “activity” is broader than an open investigation: older information may remain useful to connected inquiries, subsequent events or a reopened case. It held that lawful pertinence at collection permits retention without a showing of pertinence to a currently authorized activity. The majority distinguished retention from dissemination, which §552a(b) separately restricts, and rejected a categorical objection to maintaining an individual's information under that individual's name. It also sustained denial of discovery under the district court's discretion, citing classification and the in camera review. That ruling does not decide what limited further inquiry would be necessary if the governing legal or threshold premise changes.

Tatel read the statutory definition of “maintain,” which includes both collection and maintenance, to require pertinence at the time of keeping as well as gathering. Congress used “compiled for” and “collect” elsewhere but used the broader defined term here. He argued that the Act separately addresses unnecessary retention and dissemination; disclosure restrictions therefore do not eliminate the retention protection. His interpretation requires a continuing authorized law-enforcement connection, not automatic destruction upon closure of a particular investigation. Neither opinion determined all record-specific consequences under the dissent's interpretation.

The Foundation submitted affidavits from Valerie Lies and Anne Hallett describing general reputational stigma, possible deterrence of grant applicants and possible harm to employees' future employment. The majority found no identified employee with diminished prospects, no sufficiently described grantee response, and no concrete and imminent organizational injury. It also found no mechanism by which prospective grantees would learn of references in FBI records other than plaintiffs' own publicity, making the asserted injury appear self-inflicted. Its footnote expressly reserved a stronger case involving evidence of FOIA requests and resulting avoidance of a foundation.

Tatel agreed that the Foundation lacked standing because its affidavits did not connect FBI retention to third-party knowledge. He rejected the majority's broader assessment that such reputational injury was inherently too speculative. Evidence that grant applicants customarily check FBI files could supply a causal mechanism, and Meese v. Keene shows that supported reputational consequences need not be proved by identifying each lost supporter. His suggested evidence was a hypothetical illustration, not a fact found in this record.

Lindblom left the Foundation in 1994 and worked at the Ford Foundation by the appeal. He supplied no separate constitutional-standing argument or affidavits, did not claim impaired employment prospects of his own, and relied derivatively on the Foundation's asserted harm. The panel therefore rejected his constitutional standing. It did not separately decide whether actual continued retention of his own protected information supplied injury for his Privacy Act claim, or whether he satisfied §552a(g)(1)(D)'s adverse-effect requirement. The published opinion identifies requested equitable relief; it does not establish a preserved damages demand, an administrative amendment-request history, a particular (g) route, or a finding of no statutory adverse effect.

## Governing law and threshold routes

Read [the selected exact-law slice](../entering-law/OT_1997CHUNK3_MACARTHUR.md): Lujan, Arizonans, the already effective June 2, 1997 Steel Co. decision, Department of Defense v. FLRA, Reich and Meyer; relevant Standards; exact 1997 §552a definitions, duties, remedies and exemptions; exact §702; and the 1997 FBI exemption rule. Date-eligible Doe v. FBI, Nagel and Clarkson identify the remedial issue without deciding this case. There is no imported later historical Steel Co., Doe v. Chao, or Cooper doctrine.

**1. Statutory claim's own Article III controversy.** Lujan requires concrete, particularized actual or imminent injury, traceability and likely redressability, supported by specific facts at summary judgment. A statutory label or demand that the FBI comply with public law does not itself suffice. Lindblom can distinguish the rejected Foundation theory: the FBI admittedly retains records of his own associations, and Congress may recognize a concrete personal privacy interest in that continuing retention. If the asserted invasion of that interest is adequately established and a lawful order could end it, neither speculative third-party knowledge nor impaired future employment is necessarily required. The contrary argument is that a prohibited-record allegation, without a supported personal invasion of the protected interest or available redress, still presents only abstract illegality or subjective chill. The court must identify the actual protected interest and record evidence rather than treat the constitutional-standing ruling as automatically dispositive, or treat every asserted statutory violation as sufficient. The legal merits of the claimed prohibition may be contested without automatically eliminating standing.

Steel Co. forbids assuming Article III power merely to reject the disputed retention construction on its merits. Arizonans permits corrective disposition without merits jurisdiction, but does not authorize a retention holding on that basis. A supported threshold dismissal, or a limited remand to address an unresolved threshold necessary to the statutory judgment, remains available. Not every statutory element becomes jurisdictional simply because §552a(g)(1) includes a jurisdictional grant; Article III injury, statutory entitlement, sovereign consent and relief must remain analytically distinct.

**2. Actual civil-action route and adverse effect.** Section (e)(7) is a substantive prohibition with three alternatives: express statutory authorization, individual authorization, or pertinence to and scope of an authorized law-enforcement activity. Section (g)(1)(D) authorizes suit for other violations only when the noncompliance has an adverse effect on an individual. The question whether the particular continuing privacy invasion supplies that adverse effect must be answered under the text and actual evidence; neither the bare violation nor the Foundation's failed reputational theory resolves it by itself. “Adverse effect” under (D) is not the distinct “adverse determination” required by (g)(1)(C).

Doe v. FBI rejected an attempted direct (e)(7) expungement claim that bypassed (g)(1)(D), relying on an unchallenged no-adverse-effect finding. No corresponding finding is reported here. Doe is a date-eligible circuit authority on the actual statutory mechanism, not a new Supreme Court holding or proof that Lindblom loses. Conversely, invoking Nagel or Clarkson cannot erase the later articulated statutory prerequisites. Nagel distinguishes ordinary amendment exhaustion from an (e)(7) claim; Clarkson links possible expungement to the amendment provisions, states the limited statutory injunction categories, and reserves actual jurisdictional facts. Their contexts and routes must be reconciled rather than converted into an unconditional right to expungement.

**3. The remedy actually sought.** Section (g)(1)(A), with (d)(2)–(3) and (g)(2), provides a route to judicial amendment after a qualifying agency refusal/review failure. The request, review and applicable exemption predicates are not supplied by the reported merits discussion. Section (g)(1)(B)/(g)(3) concerns access, and the abandoned FOIA dispute is not a continuing expungement cause. For (g)(1)(C)/(D), (g)(4) expressly provides damages upon intentional or willful conduct, actual damages resulting from the violation, and its stated minimum/costs provisions. The fact that an (e)(7) duty exists does not itself give a court unlimited injunctive power. This case seeks prospective relief; a hypothetical new damages claim cannot manufacture redressability or preserve an otherwise unavailable action. No later Supreme Court interpretation of the damages language is assumed.

The FBI's 1997 §16.96 rule lists Central Records System exemptions, including (d) and (g), to the extent statutory (j)/(k) authorizes them. Neither statutory exemption provision permits suspension of (e)(7)'s substantive duty. Doe's treatment of nonexemptible duties prevents a blanket assumption that a civil-remedy exemption defeats their enforcement. At the same time, lawful exemption from (d) can matter to a derivative amendment claim. The agency must establish the applicable category and legal scope; the general rule alone does not prove that these particular records satisfy it. The FOIA exemptions used to withhold documents do not automatically decide the Privacy Act remedial exemption.

Reich and the exact 1997 §702 permit consideration of an independently available prospective equitable route against unlawful official action. Section 702's nonmoney waiver does not by itself establish a cause, dispense with the relevant review predicates, or override a statute that expressly or impliedly forbids the requested relief. The court must assess whether this presented action supports such a route and whether the Privacy Act's remedial scheme displaces it; no amended pleading, named officer or preserved theory is invented. A mandatory decree must identify the responsible officer as §702 requires. Meyer separately bars creating a direct agency Bivens damages action merely from a waiver; it does not decide this statutory prospective claim.

These uncertainties permit supported threshold alternatives without declaring facts against a party because the original papers were not recovered. A lawful narrow disposition may leave retention merits unresolved. If a necessary remedial predicate is genuinely disputed and dispositive, the appropriate next inquiry concerns that predicate, not unrestricted discovery of classified files or an invented concession.

## Statutory merits and constitutional claim, if lawfully reached

**Retention construction A.** The FBI's strongest reading is that pertinence of the record when lawfully gathered suffices, and that Congress's broader “activity” encompasses use of historical information beyond one open case. Separate disclosure controls reduce some risks; Congress did not write “currently necessary.” This supports affirmance of the statutory construction only after the threshold route is sound. Its difficulty is explaining the independent meaning of “maintain” in (a)(3) and (e)(7), and why the substantive condition would cease to matter during continued retention when the Act elsewhere expressly uses collection-based wording.

**Retention construction B.** Lindblom's strongest reading applies the pertinence/scope limitation whenever the agency maintains the protected record, including continued keeping. Collection and retention are textually distinct, and preventing unnecessary inventories is a different protection from limiting public disclosure. The objection is administrability and the legitimate continuing relevance of old facts to broad authorized activities. This construction need not require immediate destruction whenever an investigation closes, perpetual demonstrations of investigative necessity, or public exposure of classified reasons. A sufficiently articulated continuing connection may satisfy the text. A legal correction would ordinarily require record-specific application rather than an order destroying every file. Any distinct statutory authorization, individual authorization or concrete preservation obligation must be established, not presumed from general agency practice.

**Constitutional standing.** The existing affidavits support a narrow failure-of-traceability conclusion because they do not establish how prospective grant applicants would learn of the FBI references. The majority's additional injury/imminence reasoning is available but must track Lujan's actual requirements and favorable summary-judgment inferences. Meese rejects a categorical proposition that reputational injury requires proof of a completed loss or testimony from every deterred person; it involved concrete affidavits and a public governmental label. No similar causal evidence is reported here. Laird rejects subjective chill alone, not every personal statutory privacy interest. Lindblom's separately unsupported constitutional theory cannot borrow a Foundation injury that has not been established. The constitutional merits should remain undecided if Article III is absent.

**Disposition and further proceedings.** Distinguish the statutory and constitutional judgments, the two plaintiffs, and each available remedy. Possible supported paths include threshold dismissal of an unavailable statutory action with constitutional dismissal; limited corrective remand for unresolved jurisdiction/remedy predicates; or, if the route is established, affirmance or correction of the retention construction with appropriately limited further proceedings. Correcting a legal premise does not establish current irrelevance, intentional wrongdoing, damages, unrestricted discovery or entitlement to purge all FBI records. Existing in camera practice can protect confidential material while addressing a genuinely material unresolved issue.
