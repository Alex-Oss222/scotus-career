## The specific obstacle to reaching retention merits

**Article III redressability for Lindblom's statutory claim is not established on the supported record.** His identified retained associational records provide a real candidate personal privacy injury, distinct from the Foundation's prediction of other people's reactions. The merits of whether that retention is prohibited must not be smuggled into injury analysis. But a judicial order must be legally available through an actual surviving action to supply likely redress; its physical usefulness alone is insufficient under the adopted Steel Co. holding. The available source does not resolve any of the following possible routes sufficiently to establish that necessary link:

1. **Amendment/expungement:** Sections 552a(d)(2)–(3), (g)(1)(A) and (g)(2) authorize amendment on their particular request/refusal/review predicates, subject to a lawful applicable exemption. The report establishes neither satisfaction nor failure of that route and does not establish the relevant record-specific exemption. It cannot be treated as proved unavailable, but it cannot simply be assumed satisfied.
2. **An independently available equitable action:** Reich and §702 preserve a genuine possible route, while separating waiver from cause and retaining statutory displacement and other legal/equitable limits. This record does not establish the presented cause and review predicates necessary to use that route or settle whether the Privacy Act displaces it. No new officer defendant or pleading may be supplied. The separate requirement to identify a responsible officer in an eventual mandatory decree does not itself decide jurisdiction.
3. **Statutory money relief:** The original suit did request monetary damages. The report does not allocate that request to a surviving Privacy Act claim or establish its appellate preservation or abandonment. Sections (g)(1)(D) and (g)(4) therefore cannot be treated as a verified surviving redress route or as a proved abandoned one. Nor may the Court create a new damages count. No later Supreme Court construction of the damages language is used.

This is an unresolved **availability-of-lawful-redress** question, not a finding that Lindblom necessarily lacks standing or that every requested remedy is unavailable. The particular statutory cause, (g)(1)(D)'s adverse effect, sovereign consent, and relief limits remain distinct inquiries. The Court does not declare every statutory prerequisite constitutional jurisdiction, require determination of all damages elements before every merits ruling, or prescribe an exhaustive sequence among nonmerits grounds. A supported dispositive ground may end the case without resolving other questions.

The separate constitutional claims fail on different grounds: the Foundation has not supported the causal mechanism connecting retention to outsiders' knowledge and asserted response, and Lindblom's presented derivative theory supplies no distinct supported personal injury. Their dismissal does not establish the failure of his statutory personal-information theory. All nine support leaving those constitutional dismissals undisturbed on the narrow grounds, without reaching constitutional retention merits.

**Strongest immediate-merits alternative, reconsidered under the full instruction:** Treat ongoing possession of Lindblom's actual records as concrete personal injury, then treat Nagel or Reich as supplying redress. The personal-interest argument is substantial. Nagel's full no-universal-exhaustion and remedial discussion cannot be disregarded, particularly for Scalia, who joined it. But Nagel does not establish this action's adverse effect and actual surviving relief; Doe and Clarkson expose, rather than resolve, the distinct civil-action and exemption issues. Reich does not establish this action's cause or absence of statutory displacement. Step 4 of Stone's test expressly recognizes that relief must be authorized; that recognition does not establish an actual available route. The exact proposal therefore does not answer the recorded objection that presently prevents merits reach. Conversely, dismissal of the entire statutory action for lack of redress would improperly assume every unresolved route fails, including the unresolved original money demand.
