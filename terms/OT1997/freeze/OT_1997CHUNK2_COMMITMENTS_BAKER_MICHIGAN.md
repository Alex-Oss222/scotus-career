## 1. Justice Kennedy

**Judgment and remedy.** Reverse and remand on the reviewed full-faith-and-credit ground. Section 1738 does not require exclusion of Elwell's testimony in the Bakers' action merely because the Michigan decree prohibits him from assisting litigation against GM. The remand permits consideration of properly remaining appellate and evidentiary questions. It neither reinstates the entire $11.3 million judgment nor orders a new trial, compulsory admission of every answer, a Michigan modification proceeding, or any relief against the Michigan decree.

**Sufficient ground and operative proposition.** Begin with Michigan's judgment effect. On this supported record, its ordinary preclusion rules and reported nonparty-injunction applications do not give the GM–Elwell decree controlling effect against the Bakers in their independent action. Section 1738 requires the rendering State's lawful effect; it does not supply a missing basis to bind them. Consequently the decree supplies no mandatory exclusion rule against their use of the forum's evidence process on the recognition ground presented here. This is a bounded state-effect determination, not a conclusion that all effects of every personal injunction disappear whenever another litigant is a nonparty.

Kennedy's joins in Matsushita Parts I–III support both steps: consent judgments remain within §1738, and their actual rendering-state effect must be respected without enlargement. His join in Part IV-A and Richards preserves the independent requirement of lawful authority to bind an absent person. Those constitutional commitments prevent treating a common interest or notice as a substitute for representation; they do not themselves establish a universal constitutional right to obtain a particular witness's evidence. His join in Matsushita IV-B also leaves the complete collateral-review question reserved. Neither a public-policy objection nor an allegation of Michigan legal error is needed to decide the present state-effect issue.

**Competing paths and counterarguments.** The strongest objection to the selected ground is that GM seeks compliance with a personal command to Elwell, who was a Michigan party, rather than preclusion of the Bakers' tort claim. A lawful injunction can have practical consequences for strangers without adjudicating their claims. Kennedy accepts that distinction. The answer is correspondingly confined: the proposed federal-court exclusion must have its own recognition basis; personal obligation alone does not establish that Michigan would give the decree controlling effect in the Bakers' separate proceeding. Bacon and Detroit Railway make nonparty effect a concrete state-law inquiry, not merely an abstract constitutional concern. No established binding relationship or applicable state-law exception supplies the requested effect here. Preserving possible personal consequences for Elwell does not require the Missouri court to treat the decree as conclusive against the Bakers.

The competing path would sustain exclusion until the entering court grants relief, relying on Rule 2.613(B) and Celotex. Its strongest support is the continuing obligation to use orderly review of a valid applicable injunction, which Kennedy joined in Celotex. It fails as a basis for this disposition because neither the rule's reported modification direction nor Celotex establishes that the Bakers are seeking modification or are bound to obtain it. Celotex involved actual bankruptcy relatedness, sufficient interim authority, an applicable restraint on the proposed execution and concrete relief/review mechanisms. Its qualified rule cannot supply the missing Michigan effect by analogy. Kennedy makes no finding that the Michigan court lacked jurisdiction over GM and Elwell or that its decree was transparently invalid.

A broader route could independently protect a separate court's authority over evidence from an external decree. The strongest objection is that an unqualified forum-power rule could defeat judgments whose actual state-law effects §1738 protects and could obscure lawful personal obligations. Kennedy does not reach that broader boundary because the supported Michigan inquiry resolves the asserted mandatory exclusion. Likewise, he does not use the separate settlement agreement as a sufficient construction ground: its reported protection for court-ordered testimony matters, but its unquoted terms do not establish merger into, modification of, or an unrestricted exception within the injunction.

**Exact joins and reservations.** Kennedy will join an opinion's rendering-state-first rule, the bounded Michigan application, rejection of a free-standing public-policy escape from a valid judgment, preservation of lawful interparty effect, distinction between recognition and adjudication of a separate enforcement claim, and the limited reversal/remand. He will join a restatement of Richards's actual nonparty-binding minimum, but not its transformation into an independent holding that loss of access to evidence always amounts to unconstitutional preclusion. He withholds assent from a categorical exclusion of injunctions from §1738, a general rule that foreign evidence orders always prevail, an unrestricted forum exception, or a holding that Elwell is immune from Michigan enforcement. Those propositions are unnecessary here, not resolved adversely in every future application. No broader constitutional holding is an independently sufficient ground in this commitment.

**Source-dependent limits.** The represented absence of particular privilege objections is not a universal waiver or a finding that all testimony was nonprivileged. The decree's Georgia exception and the agreement's court-ordered-testimony protection remain distinct. No omitted Michigan exception, unproved relationship, state-court modification, breach claim or contempt issue is adjudicated. Additional retrieval is not indispensable to this bounded ground; adopting any of those further propositions would require its own source support.

## 2. Justice O'Connor

**Judgment and remedy.** Reverse and remand, rejecting the Eighth Circuit's use of full faith and credit to require exclusion on the supported record. Leave other properly remaining grounds for ordinary appellate adjudication. No automatic restoration of the damages verdict, mandatory admission, new hearing, modification of the Michigan decree, or adjudication of Elwell's liability accompanies the judgment.

**Sufficient ground and operative proposition.** Michigan law supplies the first and sufficient inquiry. Its reported ordinary preclusion conditions and nonparty-injunction decisions do not establish controlling effect against these nonparty plaintiffs. GM's invocation of the decree must satisfy the ordinary state requirements, including its burden of persuasion; the fact that it obtained a valid consent injunction against Elwell does not itself establish those requirements against the Bakers. Section 1738 therefore does not compel their forum to exclude the testimony on that basis. The decree retains its lawful force among persons it binds.

This conclusion follows the actual rule of O'Connor's Matsushita Parts I and II, not a generic preference for a short opinion. Part I brings the consent judgment within §1738 and requires rendering-state effect. Part II gives the incorporated Delaware release its supported ordinary effect while preserving its actual limits; it does not require recognizing every effect requested by a judgment creditor. Marrese supplies that inquiry, not an inflexible first-instance remand, so the available Michigan rules and established nonparty status permit decision now. Matsushita Part III's nonrepeal holding supplies no automatic approval of coercive interference with a different proceeding. Her Part IV-A and join in Richards preserve constitutional binding limits independently of notice, and her IV-B reservation remains intact. The state-law disposition does not resolve the broader collateral-review question.

**Competing paths and counterarguments.** The strongest challenge to this state-law ground is that a preclusion analysis may mistake the legal operation: Elwell is directly enjoined, and enforcement of his personal promise or obligation need not extinguish the Bakers' claim. O'Connor accepts that a judgment's consequences are not exhausted by claim preclusion. But recognizing the existing personal command is different from establishing that this separate court must enforce it by excluding testimony against litigants the decree has not been shown to bind. Michigan's reported injunction cases supply actual support for refusing the proposed controlling effect against a stranger. The present judgment can reject that effect without declaring the command void or deciding a separate action against Elwell. The source record supplies no applicable exception that changes this application; the result does not rest on unavailability of original filings.

The contrary orderly-review path would require the Bakers or Elwell to obtain Michigan relief before testimony could be admitted. Rule 2.613(B) provides a real channel for a party seeking modification, and O'Connor's Celotex joins give operative orders and meaningful review their proper force. The decisive objection is that this does not establish that an outsider's evidence request is itself a modification request. Celotex's actual relatedness, sufficient interim authority, coverage and relief/review predicates were resolved there. They are not presumptions about the Michigan decree's reach here. O'Connor declines to affirm on an unestablished duty to proceed in Michigan, while leaving a genuine modification request subject to the applicable Michigan procedure.

The broader alternative would establish a federal limit on exporting a decree into another court's evidentiary process independently of state-law effect. Its strongest support is the distinction between preserving a judgment and controlling another proceeding. Its strongest objection is that the boundary must account for judgments and relationships that lawfully have wider effects. O'Connor's Matsushita approach permits a dispositive state-law answer here, making a general federal enforcement boundary unnecessary. She neither adopts the District Court's broad public-policy rationale nor declares that modifiability alone defeats recognition. A still narrower instrument-only route is also unavailable as a conclusive ground: the court-ordered-testimony protection is reported in the separate agreement, not as a general exception in the injunction itself.

**Exact joins and reservations.** O'Connor will join the §1738 state-effect requirement, the reported Michigan-rule application to the Bakers, preservation of the decree between those lawfully bound, rejection of public policy and mere modifiability as categorical recognition exceptions, and the bounded judgment. She will join the statement that recognition does not itself enlarge the judgment's reach or decide separate personal enforcement. She does not join an independently sufficient constitutional holding about all nonparty access to witnesses, a categorical distinction denying injunctions judgment status, or a broad evidentiary-sovereignty rule that bypasses rendering-state effect. A separate writing would serve only to state this sufficient ground and withheld breadth if the available principal rationale exceeded it; no writer or other Justice's join is assigned.

**Source-dependent limits.** The allocation of persuasion is not a factual finding of forfeiture, absent filings or concession. Michigan's possible exceptions, genuine privity or control, and separate personal-obligation consequences remain subject to actual proof and law when properly presented. No exploratory remand to create a new binding theory is ordered merely because all originals were not recovered. Actual privilege, ordinary admissibility, agreement enforcement and other appellate grounds remain separate; the record establishes neither a universal privilege waiver nor entitlement to the complete verdict.

## 3. Justice Thomas

**Judgment and remedy.** Reverse and remand as to the reviewed mandatory-exclusion ruling. Preserve adjudication of properly remaining appellate questions without reinstating the entire verdict or directing a new trial. Do not dissolve, revise or declare unenforceable the Michigan decree between GM and Elwell; impose no contempt, breach, privilege or testimony ruling beyond the recognition question presented.

**Sufficient ground and operative proposition.** The statutory recognition obligation extends to the effect Michigan actually gives this judgment. The reported Michigan rules do not establish that the decree controls the Bakers' separate litigation: they were not parties, the ordinary same-party and mutuality conditions are not supplied, and no legally sufficient exception or binding relationship is established. The reported injunction applications directly support that limited conclusion. Giving the decree its interparty effect does not supply the missing state-law authority to require exclusion against these plaintiffs. Accordingly §1738 does not sustain the Eighth Circuit's exclusion ground on this record.

Thomas's joins in Matsushita Parts I–III require honoring actual state effect notwithstanding consent or objections to state-law correctness; they also preserve the express limit that recognition does not approve every coercive interference with another action. His joins in Part IV-A and Richards preserve the constitutional prerequisite to binding nonparties, while IV-B leaves the full collateral-review boundary open. This commitment applies the anterior Michigan-law inquiry rather than expanding that constitutional minimum into a general evidence entitlement.

His Celotex opinion supplies a particularly important limit on the rationale. It resolved actual subject-matter relatedness, sufficient statutory interim authority, coverage of the proposed bond execution and the qualified duty to obtain relief through concrete authorized channels. It did not hold that all judicial orders can be disregarded whenever outsiders are affected. Nor did it hold that an issuing court's personal jurisdiction over one witness establishes controlling effect in every later proceeding involving that witness. Thomas preserves that difference as part of the sufficient ground.

**Competing paths and counterarguments.** The strongest case for affirmance is that Elwell, unlike the Bakers, was a party, so an order admitting his testimony could facilitate conduct expressly forbidden by an operative injunction. Personal restraints can remain consequential across state lines; Celotex forbids a second court from defeating an applicable order merely because it prefers another outcome. Thomas accepts those propositions at their lawful scope. The obstacle to affirmance is the missing legal bridge from Elwell's personal duty to a compulsory evidence ruling against the Bakers. Michigan's nonparty-injunction decisions and ordinary conditions do not supply it. Neither Rule 2.613(B)'s direction for modification requests nor the abstract existence of a review mechanism makes the Bakers' use of evidence a request to modify the Michigan judgment. The Missouri court's recognition decision does not adjudicate that Elwell may ignore every personal obligation with impunity.

The strongest objection to the selected ground is that nonparty preclusion rules alone do not describe every enforcement consequence of a valid personal injunction. Thomas therefore confines the holding to the asserted compulsory exclusion in this litigation and expressly reserves a genuine, properly presented enforcement claim against Elwell. He does not conclude that nonparty status universally defeats injunction effects, that the Michigan court lacked jurisdiction, or that a substantial-jurisdiction or transparent-invalidity exception has been proved. The selected ground rests on the supported state-law effect, not a new exception to Celotex's orderly-review requirement.

A broader federal route could distinguish recognition from the receiving forum's enforcement or evidence machinery. The strongest support is that a valid personal order need not become an independent evidentiary command to another court. The strongest objection is that a categorical formulation could undermine a legally applicable restraint, as the competing execution authorization did in Celotex. Thomas accepts the limited distinction needed here but withholds a general rule that evidence process always overrides a foreign injunction or that enforcement consequences are wholly outside §1738. He also declines to rest reversal solely on construction of the settlement: its reported testimony protection cannot be silently incorporated into the decree, and it establishes no adjudicated Michigan modification.

**Exact joins and reservations.** Thomas will join the rendering-state-effect rule, the bounded Michigan nonparty application, preservation of valid interparty obligations and lawful modification procedures, rejection of a general public-policy exception, and the limited reversal/remand. He will join Richards's existing constitutional binding rule as background but not a new independent holding treating every restriction on evidence as binding a nonparty's claim. He will not join language licensing collateral defiance of an applicable injunction, eliminating Celotex's orderly-review requirements, declaring every interstate testimony restraint unenforceable, or deciding hypothetical contempt or contract liability. No universal exception based on the forum's preference or the injunction's modifiability is accepted.

**Source-dependent limits.** No actual privity, participation, control, representative authority, preclusion exception, unquoted contract term, Michigan modification or particular waived privilege is invented. The ordinary Michigan burden permits adjudicating the effect asserted on this supported record without assuming a new theory or requiring a research-driven factual hearing. The full state opinions and historical rule text would become necessary only for a ground depending on an omitted exception or definitive modification-power proposition; this commitment adopts neither. The absence of originals creates no litigant default.
