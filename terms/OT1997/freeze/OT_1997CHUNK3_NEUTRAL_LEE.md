The questions are:

1. Whether a patient, a patient representative, or a proposed class has supported concrete, actual or imminent injury from the challenged patient-protection provisions, including the separate reduced-standard-of-care theory.
2. Whether the claimed duties imposed on objecting providers and facilities present a concrete injury and a ripe controversy; and whether the separate claimed association restriction is supported by threatened application.

## Record and judgments below

The complete verified lower-opinion recitations, their status and source links are preserved in [the clean source-facts handoff](OT_1997CHUNK3_SOURCE_FACTS_LEE.md). That handoff and the judicial text of the linked lower opinion support the following facts; unavailable original declarations and filings are not treated as absent from the judicial record.

Oregon voters adopted Measure 16 on November 8, 1994. Plaintiffs filed November 23, fifteen days before scheduled effectiveness. They asserted equal protection, due process, the First Amendment's religious and association protections, the ADA, the Rehabilitation Act and RFRA. The District Court first enjoined implementation, then granted summary judgment and a permanent injunction on August 3, 1995 on equal protection alone. It considered safeguards for depressed or incompetent terminally ill adults inadequate compared with suicide protections for other adults. It did not adjudicate the other merits theories. The Ninth Circuit reviewed standing and ripeness de novo, vacated the District Court judgment, and directed dismissal of the complaint for lack of Article III jurisdiction. Brunetti wrote for Goodwin, Brunetti and district judge Samuel P. King; there was no separate writing.

The properly named defendants remaining below were Lane County District Attorney Douglas Harcleroad and eleven Oregon Board of Medical Examiners members. Oregon, its governor and attorney general had been dismissed on Eleventh Amendment grounds. Several citizens intervened as defendants. Their intervention does not itself establish an independent Article III injury or expand the proper defendants. The reported appeals concern both plaintiffs' summary judgment and denial of defendants' requested summary judgment on all claims; the cross-appeals concern patient-representative standing and refused class certification.

### Patient injury, representation and proposed class

- The District Court recognized Eric Dutson, Janice Elsner, Maryville Nursing Home and Willows Home for the equal-protection, due-process, ADA and Rehabilitation Act challenges. Doctors Gary Lee and William Petty challenged denial of their standing to represent patients. The proposed terminal-disease class was never certified.
- Dutson died July 2, 1995, before the August 3 judgment. The opinion's statement that counsel apparently gave notice August 8 is a judicial characterization, not independent verification of the original instruments. No surviving personal damages claim, substitution, or certified class is established by these sources.
- Elsner had progressive muscular dystrophy, had outlived earlier expectations and had experienced clinical depression and ambivalence about living. She had no prognosis of death within six months. The Ninth Circuit assumed her statutory terminal-disease qualification for its analysis, partly because represented patients might actually qualify. That assumption is not a finding of a six-month prognosis.
- Her asserted injury was death through Measure 16 contrary to her true wishes. The reported chain requires disabling depression or undue influence, oral and written requests, erroneous judgments of capability and voluntariness by two physicians and witnesses, continuing impairment through the renewed request after fifteen days, another erroneous informed-decision judgment, and ingestion.
- Plaintiffs' appellee brief at page 49 asserted higher depression risk among terminally ill people, depression's connection with suicide, and primary-care failures to identify suicidal depression. The Ninth Circuit assumed arguendo the record could support those general assertions but found no sufficient individualized threatened injury. Those assertions are neither independently verified medical findings nor proof that safeguards never fail.
- The lower court concluded that providers could not cure the absence of injury to the represented patients and that an uncertified proposed class could not replace named-plaintiff standing. It questioned causation and redressability but did not decide either as an additional ground. Footnote 3 identifies independent choices of the patient, physicians, witnesses and possible outside influencers; footnote 4 expressly leaves redressability unresolved.
- Footnote 5 preserves a distinct argument: §4.01(1)'s good-faith immunity allegedly reduces the ordinary reasonable-care protection under O.R.S. §677.095. The lower court assumed that construction without deciding it, but held that a reduction alone, without concrete actual or imminent personal injury from it, did not suffice. This theory is not a threatened-prosecution claim.

### Provider duties and facility association

The District Court recognized Dr. Petty, the two facilities and their administrators or owners for the First Amendment and RFRA claims. Their asserted direct injuries must remain distinct from their representation of patients.

The claimed compelled acts were transfer of relevant medical records when care transfers (§4.01(4)), advice concerning Measure 16 procedures (§3.01(2)), and designation of a witness for a facility resident (§2.02(4)). The Ninth Circuit assumed those asserted duties without construing the Act. It held that the challenged provisions imposed no penalty, criminal or otherwise, and therefore found no concrete injury. It expressly distinguished this conclusion from imminence, which it said probably would be satisfied if a cognizable injury existed. Its separate ripeness analysis accepted predominantly legal fitness but found no present hardship; at worst, civil enforcement could be challenged when brought. Neither a specific enforcement threat nor a present request triggering the disputed duties is independently established.

The facilities and administrators also claimed §4.01(2) prevented exclusion of participating physicians. The lower court found no allegation that any physician at their facilities intended to participate. This is a separate application problem, not the same penalty-based reason used for compelled participation.

Footnote 6 records a further advice theory based on 42 U.S.C. §1395cc(f)(1)(A)(i) and O.R.S. §677.097. The court did not consider it because it challenged those other informed-consent laws, rather than Measure 16's validity. It is not an adjudicated merits rejection, an invitation to add claims, or proof of preservation of a different challenge in the authorized review.

### Actual statutory instrument and construction limits

The lower opinion's appendix reproduces the entire Measure 16 enactment. It supports the text reviewed below, not an independent certification of every amendment or external development through January 20, 1998. No later alteration or new patient event is assumed.

The Act requires a capable adult Oregon resident, a medically confirmed incurable and irreversible disease expected within reasonable medical judgment to cause death within six months, a voluntary wish and a written request. It requires attending and consulting physician determinations, witnesses, counseling when either physician suspects impairment, an informed decision, renewed oral request after at least fifteen days, rescission opportunities and at least forty-eight hours from written request to prescription. These are actual prescribed safeguards, not findings about their efficacy.

The provisions most material to the distinct claims read:

> §4.01(1): “No person shall be subject to civil or criminal liability or professional disciplinary action for participating in good faith compliance with this Act.”

> §4.01(2): “No professional organization or association, or health care provider, may subject a person to censure, discipline, suspension, loss of license, loss of privileges, loss of membership or other penalty for participating or refusing to participate in good faith compliance with this Act.”

> §4.01(4): “No health care provider shall be under any duty, whether by contract, by statute or by any other legal requirement to participate in the provision to a qualified patient of medication to end his or her life in a humane and dignified manner. If a health care provider is unable or unwilling to carry out a patient's request under this Act, and the patient transfers his or her care to a new health care provider, the prior health care provider shall transfer, upon request, a copy of the patient's relevant medical records to the new health care provider.”

Section 2.02(4) requires a designated qualified witness when the requester is a long-term-care-facility patient. Section 3.01(2), within the attending physician's prescribed responsibilities, lists diagnosis, prognosis, medication risks, probable result and feasible alternatives including comfort care, hospice and pain control. Its text does not itself settle plaintiffs' broader claim that every objecting physician must recommend Measure 16. Section 4.02 separately criminalizes specified forgery, concealment and coercion, preserves further civil liability for other negligent conduct or intentional misconduct, and retains other applicable criminal penalties. Section 3.14 excludes lethal injection, mercy killing and active euthanasia; §5.01 supplies severability. These clauses cannot be collapsed into a general duty to prescribe or a universal immunity from negligence.

### Patient claims and the medical-care protection theory

**Jurisdictional dismissal path.** Apply *Lujan*'s summary-judgment standard and *Lyons*'s personal-exposure rule to find that the recited general risks, prior depression and chain of contingent choices and errors do not establish this plaintiff's actual or imminent injury. Even assuming statutory qualification, an unsupported future harm cannot be supplied by the importance of death, difficulty identifying another plaintiff, proposed class allegations or provider representation. A bare change in a legal standard similarly cannot substitute for concrete personal harm. This path can affirm dismissal of these claims without deciding statutory immunity construction, traceability, redressability or assisted-death merits.

Its strongest objection is that the threats are connected to an existing progressive illness, prior depression and an enacted system governing the patient's future care, rather than an unrelated hypothetical return. *Lujan* requires reasonable inferences and adopts no particular-document prerequisite. Nor may a court demand that death occur before recognizing a supported threat. The answer must address the reported individualized facts and the assumed/generalized status of the empirical propositions; simply counting contingencies or quoting the circuit's probability formulation is insufficient.

**Concrete-risk or present-protection path.** Evaluate whether the reported personal illness and depression evidence, together with the challenged existing care protection, establish a personal threatened harm or present impairment of a concrete interest under the governing standard. Distinguish actual exposure to a lower care protection from the public's abstract preference for stricter regulation. If the appellate court demanded an unsupported formal or excessive certainty requirement, a claim-specific vacatur for application of the correct summary-judgment standard is available without finding the ultimate statutory or constitutional violation.

Its strongest objections are Elsner's unestablished six-month qualification, the absence of an individualized impending request or equivalent application, and the required independent choices and mistakes. The record supplies no fact that she is already receiving a different level of care. The immunity construction is assumed, not decided, and §4.02 preserves other negligence liability. A ruling recognizing sufficient injury must still address the actual defendants, traceability and likely redress; those elements were not resolved below. This route cannot be supported merely by treating a claimed right to proper safeguards as self-proving injury. The reported record supports testing the route, not inventing missing exposure.

**Representation and class limits.** Provider representation may be analyzed under the *Powers* conditions if an injured patient and the representative's own required stake exist. It does not create the missing substantive injury. The denied certification provides no extant certified class with an independent continuing controversy. Dutson's death requires its own continuing-controversy analysis and does not erase viable claims of others. No substitution, damages residue or relation-back fact is invented.

### Compelled provider participation and ripeness

**Dismissal on the supported application path.** The Court may uphold dismissal if the asserted statutory duties have no concrete application, cognizable present burden or supported enforcement consequence for these plaintiffs, with predominantly legal fitness insufficient to supply hardship. *Toilet Goods* and *Abbott Laboratories* permit attention to actual application and review opportunity. Section 4.01(4)'s express refusal protection and the conditional nature of record transfer can matter without deciding the entire state-law construction. This route must distinguish absence of an identified injury from a universal rule that only a criminal or express statutory penalty can injure.

The strongest contrary argument is that a legally imposed obligation to act against religious conviction may itself burden present conduct; criminal punishment is not the only injury *Lujan* recognizes. *Babbitt* rejects forcing a properly situated plaintiff to expose itself to prosecution, and the lower court itself considered imminence probably sufficient on its assumed construction. Its acknowledgment of possible civil enforcement also requires care in reasoning from “no penalty” to “no injury.” A sufficient statutory duty plus concrete present application could support both injury and hardship even before a penalty is imposed.

**Correct the penalty-based rule and remand path.** The Court may reject absence of an express penalty as categorically dispositive, vacate the participation-claim dismissal and return for the claim-specific statutory application, concrete burden, proper-defendant relation and ripeness analysis. The text's refusal protection, record-transfer condition, witness provision and attending-physician context must be separately examined. A narrower decision can leave the patient and association dismissals undisturbed. Its strongest objection is that rejecting the legal rationale does not supply a triggering request, actual coercive obligation, or existing hardship; the reported facts may independently sustain dismissal. The mandate should therefore track the ground actually found erroneous, without finding an unreported duty, injury, enforcement event, RFRA cause of action or substantive violation.

Neither route decides whether a later merits claim succeeds under the First Amendment or RFRA. If a later decision requires RFRA's effective substantive reach or another unprovided merits rule, that issue needs its own entering-law preparation; the present threshold packet does not certify it by listing the pleaded claim.

### Facility association and remedies

The reported absence of any participating physician at the facilities supports a distinct prospective-injury dismissal. The strongest contrary position would require concrete threatened operation of the exclusion restriction or a present operational burden; neither can be invented from general opposition to the Act. The same text protects refusal to participate, and the precise reach of its anti-penalty rule remains a construction question. A remand on participation alone need not reopen this separate deficiency.

If all claims lack jurisdiction, affirming the appellate judgment leaves its direction to vacate the District Court's declaration and injunction and dismiss the action operative. The Court may explain jurisdictional correction without purporting to uphold Measure 16 on the merits. If only some threshold rulings are erroneous, a component-specific vacatur/remand is available; standing for a provider's distinct claim does not automatically support the patient-based equal-protection injunction. If the District Court lacked jurisdiction over its decided equal-protection claim, revival of an undecided provider claim supplies no automatic merits restoration. *Arizonans* supports limited corrective authority while requiring the actual procedural and equitable setting to govern judgment clearing. No universal retrospective invalidation, automatic renewed injunction, merits judgment, mandatory hearing, or additional filing order follows.
