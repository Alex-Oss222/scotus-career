# Ringuette: supplemental neutral record facts

Source: *Ringuette v. City of Fall River*, 146 F.3d 1 (1st Cir. June 4, 1998), full opinion retrieved from https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/146/146.F3d.1.96-1107.html. Saved primary judicial text: `../research/chunk10_comparator/ringuette.txt`. This extract supplies objective record/procedural facts and the judgment under review; it supplies no Supreme Court model or comparator outcome. Source limitation: the appellate opinion reports the testimony and district-court findings; original trial testimony has not been recovered here.

## Event and defendant-specific conduct

- July 27, 1992, about 7 p.m.: officers found Ringuette slumped over a car, unsteady, with bloodshot eyes and an odor of alcohol. He repeatedly requested transport to his brother without giving an address. They believed he was intoxicated. Prescription-pill overdose was established later; the officers did not know it at any relevant time. Police booked him at 7:19 p.m. into protective custody. The cell was locked, unmonitored by video, and not directly observable by the booking officer.
- Massachusetts General Laws ch. 111B, section 8, allowed taking an incapacitated person, with or without consent, to his residence, a treatment facility, or the police station; at the station the officer in charge was to transfer him to the nearest available treatment facility. No call was made, but subsequent evidence showed no available bed; the center had accepted no short-notice police transfer in seven years. The statutory command, distinct from those facts, prohibited detention against the person's will except that, if suitable treatment was unavailable, an incapacitated person could be held until no longer incapacitated or twelve hours, whichever was shorter. The statute did not provide for a second twelve-hour form.
- An overnight officer monitored at fifteen-minute intervals. He testified that at approximately 5 a.m. Ringuette responded to an offer of release with profanity; at 6:20 a.m. he said he was not ready to leave. The officer said the next shift would release him anyway.
- Officer Raymond Paradis became booking officer at 6:50 a.m. July 28 and learned of the previous conversation. Knowing Ringuette from prior protective custody, Paradis testified that he soon asked about leaving; Ringuette replied in slurred speech that he had nowhere to go and was still partly intoxicated. Supervising Sergeant Richard Levesque was told this and authorized a second protective-custody form. The booking officer had immediate responsibility for booking, cell checks, and feeding and reported to the shift sergeant.
- Paradis apparently checked only a couple of times between 7 a.m. and 3 p.m. No food or water had been given despite a handbook requiring food after five hours. The next officer found Ringuette sitting and then lying on the floor; he refused food and mumbled incoherently. At about 6 p.m., moaning and gurgling prompted Levesque to call a technician, who arrived almost immediately. Ringuette was motionless in vomit and in shock. Hospital findings included no measurable blood pressure, severe dehydration, prescription-pill overdose, and unexplained burns. Compartmental syndrome impaired his left arm and leg. Expert testimony placed its likely onset three to six hours before the technician arrived. Later departmental proceedings found derelictions by both Paradis and Levesque.

## Preserved issues and procedural standard

- Complaint filed June 2, 1993. Summary judgment dismissed Chief McDonald and several claims; that decision was not directly challenged in this appeal. The surviving claims were city negligence and, against Paradis and Levesque, unreasonable seizure and deliberate indifference to serious medical needs. The underlying conduct date for clearly established law is July 27-28, 1992, not 1998.
- At the October-November 1995 trial, after evidence and before submission to the jury, the judge granted judgment as a matter of law to both officers on the seizure claim on qualified-immunity grounds. This is a post-evidence ruling, not summary judgment. The jury rejected deliberate indifference as to each officer. It found city negligence and damages of $1,780,360, reduced under the state statutory cap to $100,000. The appealed federal issue is seizure immunity; the jury's medical-care verdict and city negligence recovery are distinct.
- Levesque pleaded qualified immunity; Paradis did not. Plaintiff nevertheless litigated immunity against both without raising the pleading objection at the dispositive motion or reconsideration. The First Circuit rejected the later waiver objection because the issue was presented and decided without objection or prejudice, citing Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(b).
- The offers of release and refusals were not wholly undisputed. Expert medical testimony could be read to imply incapacity to speak during some relevant time. The district judge found that Ringuette expressed a desire to remain or answered in a way the officers believed indicated consent. That finding rested on two officers, including a nondefendant. The First Circuit reported that neither the finding itself nor the judge's procedure for resolving the disputed immunity facts was directly challenged on appeal. Do not convert this account into a jury finding, undisputed consent, waiver of seizure, or authority to extend the statutory limit.
- The district judge treated the renewed form and locked cell as a seizure; the release offers and apparent willingness to remain affected reasonableness and immunity. The First Circuit affirmed the officers' immunity. It distinguished a person demanding release from the reported offers/refusals and apparent continuing incapacity and did not treat every breach of a state detention statute as necessarily a federal violation. Whether these preserved facts support a constitutional rule or remedy here is for independent modeling.
