## Richenberg v. Cohen — December 8, 1997

### Identity, channel, and question

Scheduled review of the Eighth Circuit's October 3, 1996 final civil judgment in Richenberg v. Perry, 97 F.3d 256, **No. 95-4181**. The ordinary review channel is 28 U.S.C. §1254(1), not §1257 or an original application. The supplied authority places merits review on December 8, 1997; no Supreme Court docket number or separate grant date is invented. No case-specific nonparticipation is established in the neutral institutional record.

The analytical granted question concerns the legality of discharging a service member through an identity-statement presumption without genuinely available individualized rebuttal. It does not establish that rebuttal was unavailable in this case. Richenberg requests reversal and reinstatement or a lawful new separation proceeding, with prospective relief. His lower-court due-process, speech, attainder, and APA claims were broader than the supplied review question; their existence does not automatically enlarge the current grant.

### Verified lower-court record and dispute

Source: [published Eighth Circuit opinion, 97 F.3d 256](https://static.case.law/f3d/97/html/0256-01.html), with court/date/docket identification and separate lower-court writing; local [source text](../research/preflight-c/richenberg-lower.txt). The official [1994 statutory text](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1994-title10/html/USCODE-1994-title10-subtitleA-partII-chap37-sec654.htm) independently establishes the enacted rules. Statements of fact below remain judicial recitations rather than a claim that the complete administrative record was retrieved.

- An initial December 1993 Board hearing was followed by reconsideration under the new policy and a second hearing in June 1994. The Secretary ordered honorable discharge on August 28, 1995. The District Court granted the Government summary judgment; the Eighth Circuit affirmed. No absent hearing or exclusion of offered evidence is established.
- Richenberg acknowledged homosexual identity and denied intending prohibited conduct. He supplied strong service, character, integrity, and discipline evidence. On cross-examination, he acknowledged attraction to men and gave equivocal responses about possible future sexual activity and marriage if lawful. Those responses are not admissions of a completed act, actual marriage, or a statutory marriage violation.
- The appellate majority read the Board's decision as a credibility assessment of his stated lack of intent or propensity in light of those responses. It considered the Secretary's decision supported by substantial evidence despite the possibility of competing inferences.
- Chief Judge Arnold's dissent identified a distinct problem: the Board of Inquiry's stated finding gave no explanation distinguishing likelihood or intent from orientation; the Board of Review relied on attraction and discounted character witnesses as not specifically asked about propensity. The dissent disputed that treatment of the testimony and thought either relief on the existing record or a remand for an explanation warranted. This is a disagreement over the existing record and agency reasons, not an established finding that rebuttal was fictitious.
- The lower opinion quotes the Government's position that the presumption is rebuttable and its reliance on customary military deference. That supports the limited attribution; no concession on constitutional invalidity, an administrative proof standard, reinstatement, or appellate remedy is inferred.

### Issue snapshots and operative limits

**Equality and the inference.** Thomasson, effective June 9, 1997, supplies ordinary rationality, a conduct-related inference, the likelihood definition, and genuine rebuttal. Walmer concerned admitted conduct at a preliminary stage and does not decide this application. Romer requires a rational relationship to the actual disadvantage while permitting conceivable legitimate grounds, reasonable predictions, and imperfect fit. The Government has no newly adopted actual-purpose or individualized-impairment burden. The challenger does not automatically prevail from service excellence or absence of completed acts. Orientation or attraction cannot be made conclusively disqualifying by relabeling it propensity.

**Statutory implementation.** Section 654(b)(2) requires separation on an approved statement finding unless a further approved finding establishes the member's demonstration that he does not engage in, attempt, have a propensity toward, or intend homosexual acts. The directive requires notice of the presumption and an opportunity to present rebuttal; its propensity definition requires likelihood beyond abstract desire. Section 654(e) permits, rather than commands, omission of processing only on both avoidance/termination-intent and military-best-interest determinations. Neither predicate is established here. Section (b)(1)'s separate retention exception requires all five findings: departure from usual behavior, unlikely recurrence, absence of force/coercion/intimidation, continued presence consistent with discipline/order/morale, and no propensity or intent. Those findings and (b)(3)'s marriage/attempted marriage route are not substitutes for the (b)(2) rebuttal inquiry. The map links the exact statutory text and §654(f)'s bodily-contact definitions.

**Speech and actual grounds.** Thomasson permits relevant statements as evidence of a lawful conduct-related predicate. Dawson and Mitchell preserve the difference between relevant proof and punishment of irrelevant belief. Waters does not create a universal discharge-investigation checklist. Whether the actual decision rested on a permissible likelihood inference or a conclusive orientation premise remains a record/legal characterization question; this handoff resolves neither.

**Review and remedy.** The Eighth Circuit assumed APA reviewability and expressly left it undecided in footnote 8 because the Government did not contest it. This is not a statutory bar to §1254 review of the constitutional controversy and not a holding that every military discharge is APA-reviewable. Thomasson reserved independent APA and due-process questions. A remedy depending on §706 must identify the lawful review route and standard rather than treat the assumption as settled Supreme Court doctrine. Preserve the distinction between deference to military policy, review of legal construction, and supported agency factfinding. The directive passages retrieved do not establish a separate numerical administrative burden of proof.

### Supported decisional paths for testing

1. Sustain the reviewed discharge if the existing reasons and record support an actual likelihood/intent finding after meaningful rebuttal under Thomasson. The principal objection is that the agency's expressed reasons may not perform that distinguishing work; an appellate credibility explanation cannot simply be substituted for a missing agency reason.
2. Set aside or remand the reviewed application if the decision treated orientation or attraction as conclusive, denied meaningful effect to rebuttal, or otherwise failed the governing legal requirement. Any ruling must identify the defect actually established and distinguish strong character evidence from an automatic defense. A bounded agency-explanation or lawful-reconsideration remand is distinct from a judicial finding that all facts require retention.
3. If a broader constitutional holding is necessary and within review, evaluate it against Thomasson's exact conduct-related application and Romer's ordinary-review limits. No proposed change in scrutiny or invalidation of all statutory routes follows automatically from this record.

A remand need not decide ultimate reinstatement, backpay, unrelated service qualifications, or entitlement to a new evidentiary hearing. Relief must identify what judgment or agency action is displaced and what remains open; a new hearing, final reinstatement, and mere additional explanation have different predicates.

## Trest v. Cain — December 9, 1997

### Identity, channel, and question

Supreme Court No. 96-7901; scheduled merits review of the Fifth Circuit's September 17, 1996 judgment in Trest v. Whitley, 94 F.3d 1005, **No. 94-40515**, under §1254(1). The supplied question asks whether a federal appellate court must raise state procedural default on its own when the State has not relied on it. The formulation is analytical, not a quotation from a recovered petition. No case-specific new nonparticipation is established.

### Verified lower-court record

Source: [published Fifth Circuit opinion, 94 F.3d 1005](https://static.case.law/f3d/94/html/1005-01.html), local [source](../research/preflight-c/trest-lower.txt). The narrow question and Supreme Court docket/date are supplied neutral metadata; this preflight has not opened the same matter's Supreme Court disposition.

- Trest's Louisiana armed-robbery sentence was enhanced using earlier Mississippi burglary convictions. He challenges the constitutional sufficiency of the earlier guilty pleas. The federal noncapital habeas petition was filed September 30, 1991. No constitutional defect in those convictions is found by this handoff.
- The State did not specifically respond to this ground; the magistrate's report did not directly address it. Trest rearticulated it in objections and supplied a plea transcript. The District Court denied relief.
- The Fifth Circuit relied on predicted Mississippi untimeliness under its postconviction statute and Sones. The opinion's footnote 2 says neither party cited or discussed Sones in the briefs, argument, or memoranda. It treated the resulting default as preventing consideration, observed no cause/prejudice or miscarriage showing, and left the separate predicate-conviction review question unresolved.
- Judge Parker dissented that Louisiana had not relied on the Mississippi time bar and Trest lacked an opportunity to respond to the unasserted defense. His legal characterization is a competing lower-court view, not an established Supreme Court waiver finding. Likewise, the majority's prediction of a state bar is not an actual state-court judgment dismissing these claims.

### Issue snapshots

**Classification and obligation.** Lambrix directly establishes that default and Teague are nonjurisdictional, with no inflexible sequencing rule. Its actual remand concerned a preserved default defense. A nonjurisdictional classification does not alone resolve every court-initiative or forfeiture question. The institutional argument for obligatory consideration must identify a legal duty consistent with Lambrix rather than label default subject-matter jurisdiction.

**Power, discretion, and fair opportunity.** Granberry addresses omitted exhaustion, not default; it rejects both automatic waiver and an absolute appellate dismissal command in its setting. Comity, federalism, efficiency, and the actual record inform that discretion. Whether analogous discretion exists for procedural default, and any limits where the State intentionally relinquishes a defense or the prisoner lacks notice, are separate from whether action is mandatory. The present record does not establish intentional State relinquishment merely from omission.

**Underlying predicates and relief.** Coleman requires an adequate independent state rule and applicable exceptions, including the supported anticipated-default setting; it does not make every possible time bar jurisdictional. Adequacy, actual application, cause/prejudice, the proper innocence gateway, and reviewability of attacks on expired predicate convictions remain separate. The present narrow review supplies no basis to award a writ, invalidate a conviction, shorten a sentence, or find gateway innocence. Lower reliance on historical Gray does not import that different Supreme Court decision into governing simulated law; see the map's explicit Gray qualification.

**Temporal regime.** Lindh governs this pre-April 24, 1996 noncapital application. Covered chapter 153 amendments do not supply a new express-waiver rule here. Exhaustion and default must remain distinct; separately directed AEDPA provisions retain their own scope.

### Supported paths and counterarguments

1. Resolve only whether appellate consideration is compulsory, with a bounded remand if the judgment used an incorrect compulsory premise. The competing concern is avoiding unnecessary remand if the lower judgment actually rested on a lawful discretionary ground; the Court must assess the judgment's stated reasons and available adversarial process rather than invent that ground.
2. Address the permissibility and limits of discretionary consideration only insofar as necessary and within review. Granberry supplies an analogy, while different default consequences, party presentation, and the absence of a developed response supply limits. A ruling that consideration is not required would not itself forbid it; a ruling recognizing discretion would not validate every exercise.
3. If the supplied record supports an independent preserved ground within review, that ground would require its own legal predicates. Neither the anticipated state bar nor the unresolved predicate-conviction merits question becomes established simply to preserve the judgment.

The remedy may leave the lower courts to determine any lawfully available defense with an appropriate opportunity to address it. Such a remand does not direct denial or grant of habeas relief and does not compel a new evidentiary hearing without a separate basis.
