## Questions, scope, and relief

The petition's question, reproduced in the authenticated source, is:

> Whether the National Labor Relations Board erred in holding that a successor employer cannot conduct a poll to determine whether a majority of its employees support a union unless it already has obtained so much evidence of no majority support as to render the poll meaningless.

Allentown challenged both the facial rationality of the threshold and its application below. Its reported merits submission also argued that the Board had substituted a strict count of repudiating employees for its announced reasonable-doubt standard. Whether application and substantial-evidence review are fairly included in the actual question remains a scope determination, rather than a separately certified grant of every factual issue. The Court can distinguish a supporting argument about the actual standard from an independent request to reweigh evidence. Hubbard's public governing-law holding preserves that distinction and rejects enlargement from merits briefing alone.

Allentown seeks reversal and denial of enforcement; the Board defends enforcement of its unfair-labor-practice and bargaining order. A correction to the polling rule may require reconsideration under the correct standard. A supported conclusion that the existing rule was misapplied may have a different mandate. Rejecting enforcement on one ground does not decide every possible representation proceeding or establish current employee preferences.

The lower court separately rejected an objection to the bargaining order's explanation and decertification bar because it was not urged before the Board and no §10(e) extraordinary circumstance excused the omission. That is a documented preservation ruling, distinct from research availability and from the two preserved polling challenges. The unchallenged successor finding and appropriate bargaining unit are not reopened.

## Record and judgment below

Mack sold its branch assets to the managers' new company in December 1990. Allentown rehired 32 of the former 45 unit employees to do similar work. Local Lodge 724 had represented the service and parts employees for 17 years. The union sought recognition on January 2, 1991. Allentown's January 25 letter refused recognition, asserted a good-faith doubt of majority support, and announced an independent secret-ballot poll. On February 8 a Roman Catholic priest supervised the vote: 19 against representation, 13 for. The later result is not evidence management possessed when it decided to poll.

The ALJ found successorship and a rebuttable presumption of continued majority support; those findings were adopted and not challenged on appeal. The ALJ found compliance with Struksnes polling safeguards. The disputed prerequisite was objective good-faith reasonable doubt of the incumbent's majority. The Board adopted the adverse findings and ordered recognition and bargaining. Describe the poll's §8(a)(1) issue and refusal-to-bargain consequences separately; the general introductory account's reference to §§8(a)(1)/(5) does not establish that each was pleaded as an independent prohibition of the poll itself.

The employee evidence must be assessed cumulatively, without converting reports about groups into an arithmetic tally of distinct employees:

- The ALJ accepted six individual statements and assumed a seventh supported doubt, yielding seven of 32. Hoffman said he did not want to work in a union shop and would seek other work if required to do so. Other excluded statements involved workers not in the unit at the relevant time, remoteness, or equivocal terms. The appellate court assessed January 25, when management announced the poll, and excluded later hires and workers no longer employed.
- Marsh complained at his reemployment interview that he was not being represented for his $35 dues. The ALJ distinguished dissatisfaction with representation from opposition to representation and considered the job-interview context. Ridgick's statement that no union was needed if the company treated workers well followed an indication that the new company would be nonunion; his 1986 decertification inquiry was considered remote.
- Bloch reported that the entire night shift, five or six workers, did not want the union. He did not testify to his basis; the other workers supplied neither independent statements to management nor testimony. Whether those shortcomings justify the weight assigned, rather than a categorical evidentiary exclusion, is disputed.
- Mohr, a service shop steward and bargaining-committee member, predicted a union loss with the new company and said employees did not want a union. He personally could work with or without it. The ALJ recognized his possible access to views but noted uncertainty about source, size of the reported majority, department coverage, and rehired composition. The ALJ found the statement insufficient even in combination with the other evidence; the Board separately said management could not legitimately rely on a statement concerning the old workforce. Those are different stated reasons and must not be collapsed into a single automatic credibility finding.

The D.C. Circuit majority accepted the Board's policy judgment that polls can unsettle employees and bargaining relationships. It reasoned that polling can confirm uncertainty before withdrawal, that allowing informal polls more readily than Board elections poses a regulatory anomaly, and that imperfections in the agency scheme do not authorize a judicial substitute. It sustained the evidence assessment and enforced the order. Sentelle dissented: he favored the lower threshold used in the Fifth, Sixth, and Ninth Circuits, emphasized safeguarded polling and the cumulative employee reports, and regarded the Board's application as unsupported. His characterization of the poll as confirming minority status is a dissenting argument, not a substitute for the pre-poll record.

## Entering law and analytical alternatives

The [entering-law slice](../entering-law/OT_1997CHUNK3_ALLENTOWN.md) contains the exact Auciello and procedural Hubbard case sections, the operative Standards module and its June 3, 1996 authority, roster, statutory texts, and tightly linked predivergence sources. Opening law through September 15, 1997 is supplemented by the [current-term public projection](OT_1997CHUNK3_OPENING_NEUTRAL.md) through January 15, 1998; the runtime claim that no same-term cases have completed is obsolete.

Auciello unanimously permits an explained timing rule barring repudiation after acceptance on previously known doubt. It distinguishes actual minority, objectively supported doubt, presumptions, and representation routes; the presumption is not proof of employee preference. It does not decide this poll threshold. Fall River preserves rational statutory policy and supported factual application as separate inquiries. Gissel recognizes protected noncoercive views and the employment setting, acknowledges safeguarded inquiry, and leaves whether its allowances are mandatory undecided. Neither Gissel nor Struksnes itself establishes an unlimited right to poll an incumbent unit whenever procedural safeguards are met. Universal Camera requires whole-record review while preserving choices between fairly conflicting inferences.

Supported paths for independent assessment include:

1. **Sustain the threshold and its application.** Explained bargaining-stability and employee-protection concerns may justify a reasonable-doubt prerequisite even where safeguards are met; the Board may assign limited probative weight to equivocal, contextual, or unverified reports. The contrary argument is that a purported doubt standard cannot demand proof of minority or disregard credible circumstantial uncertainty. Whole-record review must evaluate the actual collective assessment and the Board's stated reasons.
2. **Sustain the announced threshold but reject or remand its application, if within review scope.** A legal mismatch between the announced rule and required proof, or an unsupportable treatment of the whole record, could warrant relief without setting a new polling threshold. The contrary argument is that this would relabel permissible evidentiary weighting as a hidden rule or substitute the reviewing court's inferences for the agency's. The precise remand depends on whether the defect requires fresh agency application or admits only one lawful enforcement result.
3. **Reject the threshold as inconsistent with the Act or inadequately justified.** Safeguarded polling can inform genuine uncertainty rather than require evidence already sufficient to withdraw recognition; §§7 and 8(c) and the distinct consequences of polls and elections support the challenge. The contrary argument is that neither statutory silence nor marginal utility establishes a right to poll, and the agency's stability rationale may remain permissible. Invalidating the threshold does not automatically prescribe the Fifth/Sixth/Ninth Circuit substitute or resolve all representation law.
4. **Resolve the facial issue while limiting further application review.** The actual question, lower record, and governing-law distinction may support reaching the rule's operation while declining an independently framed evidence issue. A scope restriction must be reasoned from the question and applicable doctrine, not inferred from missing research files.

No path treats the later 19–13 poll as retroactive authorization, assumes actual minority from employer doubt, creates a numerical doubt threshold, or silently resolves the separately unpreserved bargaining-remedy objection.
