## 2. Identity, participants, live dispute, and limits of review

The Yankton Sioux Tribe sued on its own behalf and that of its members; Chairman Darrell E. Drapeau also appeared individually. Several counties formed the Southern Missouri Recycling and Waste Management District in February 1992 to build a municipal solid-waste facility. The Waste District acquired the site in fee from a non-Indian. It lies within the original 1858 treaty boundaries and, on the published record, is **unallotted land ceded under the 1894 Act**. A 1904 Homestead Act deed to Lars K. Langeland matches the complaint's parcel description. At that time the individual allotments remained under their twenty-five-year trust restrictions; authorization for early fee patents under the Burke Act came in 1906. This affirmative parcel evidence, rather than fee ownership alone, identifies the category presented.

The Tribe opposed a compacted-clay liner during state permitting proceedings and sought a synthetic composite liner. After a December 1993 hearing, the State Board granted the permit and concluded that state rules did not require the composite liner. The Sixth Judicial Circuit affirmed, and no state supreme court appeal followed. The federal action began in September 1994, seeking an injunction and a declaration concerning federal EPA composite-liner requirements, 40 C.F.R. §258.40(b) (1997). The Waste District brought South Dakota into the action to defend state permitting jurisdiction.

After a five-day bench trial, the District Court rejected the Tribe's asserted regulatory authority over this non-Indian activity on fee land and found no demonstrated threat sufficient for the asserted inherent-sovereignty exception. It declined to enjoin construction. **The Tribe did not appeal that adverse injunction and regulatory-authority determination.** The District Court separately declared that the 1894 Act had not changed the reservation's exterior boundaries and that the site consequently lay in an Indian reservation where the federal environmental rules applied. South Dakota appealed that declaration. The divided Eighth Circuit affirmed; Judge Murphy wrote the majority and Judge Magill dissented. Their competing statutory constructions are positions to evaluate, not established historical facts.

The EPA subsequently waived the composite-liner requirement and permitted construction with compacted clay. The Waste District did not appeal because construction could proceed, but supported the State in review because continued claims of tribal jurisdiction could affect this or similar activity. The Tribe and the United States, appearing as amicus, supported continued reservation status. The State asserted primary jurisdiction over the ceded land.

The present dispute therefore concerns the adverse declaration of reservation status and its jurisdictional consequences. It is not accurately framed as an extant request to compel this landfill's composite liner. The waiver alone does not establish that the State's challenge to the declaration has ceased to matter. Nor does a prospective concern about similar projects alone establish Article III jurisdiction. A model must identify the continuing legal effect of the challenged declaration if addressing mootness; the published posture supports that inquiry without inventing a new pending enforcement action. The opening law's requirement to establish Article III jurisdiction applies. There is no basis to revive an unappealed injunction ruling or to equate reservation status with general tribal regulatory authority over every nonmember activity.

All nine current Justices are seated: Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer. No case-specific nonparticipation is established. The normal Court quorum and voting rules apply; O'Connor's Eighth Circuit allotment does not make this a single-Justice disposition. ## 3. Facts and their evidentiary status

The 1858 treaty reserved a described 400,000-acre tract in southeastern Charles Mix County; the survey measured 430,405 acres. It promised peaceful possession, restricted white settlement, and provided substantial continuing federal payments and assistance. Allotment legislation subsequently placed individual 160-acre allotments in trust for twenty-five years. The clean published facts report 167,325 acres apportioned by 1890, approximately 95,000 further acres allotted under the 1891 Act, and approximately 168,000 unallotted acres remaining after reserved governmental and religious uses. The lower opinion acknowledges conflicting acreage evidence and quotes an older Court of Claims estimate of roughly 230,000 allotted and 200,000 unallotted acres. Those approximate aggregates should retain their provenance. None displaces the specific waste-site classification or justifies inventing an exact map of all surviving lands.

Commissioners began negotiations in October 1892. Recorded concerns included the price, treaty annuities, and outstanding federal obligations. The supplied account reports no express discussion establishing what the future boundaries would be. By March 1893, 255 of 458 eligible adult male members had signed. Investigation of alleged signature fraud delayed congressional consideration; the record supplied here does not establish an adjudication invalidating the ratified agreement. Congress ratified on August 15, 1894, appropriating $600,000 plus separately specified payments. The fixed consideration was about $3.60 per acre; settlers were required to pay $3.75 per acre under the implementation provision. The Court of Claims later found the original consideration unconscionably inadequate and awarded the difference from its $1,337,381.50 valuation. That compensation determination does not itself undo the transaction or resolve reservation boundaries.

The presidential proclamation opened the ceded land on May 21, 1895. The lower opinion's May 16 proclamation date and the effective opening date are not interchangeable events. Approximately ninety percent of unallotted tracts were settled by the turn of the century. Most individual allotments also eventually passed in fee to non-Indians. The contemporary published account reports approximately 30,000 acres of Indian allotted land and 6,000 acres of tribal land; less than ten percent of the original area was then Indian-owned. These later title changes must not be treated as congressional diminishment by themselves.

The negotiations and subsequent evidence support competing inferences rather than a stipulated common intention:

- Commissioner Cole warned of catastrophic consequences if federal rations, clothes, supplies, and school support stopped. The historian described the Tribe's fear of losing annuities. That evidence explains the importance of guaranteed federal obligations. It does not establish that each signatory understood the agreement as extinguishing jurisdiction or as preserving it.
- The commissioners' report called the surplus land the last common-property bond and described tribal interests as a thing of the past. A March 1894 letter by several chiefs and members urged ratification and recognition of state and federal law. Those are affirmative contemporary indications relevant to the State's reading. Statements envisioning possible county government subject to state law and an eventual assimilated future do not automatically answer when, over which land, and by what enactment particular jurisdiction changed.
- References to restoring lands to the public domain appear in legislative discussion and administrative accounts. **No operative public-domain restoration command appears in this Act.** Some congressional remarks discussed several agreements together, with different operative terms. Their weight depends on specificity and context.
- The 1896 statute called the settlement area the “Yankton Indian Reservation,” while some contemporary administrative descriptions used “former.” Later congressional, executive, judicial, and cartographic usage also varied. A 1941 Interior opinion concluded the boundaries persisted; a 1969 memorandum described the Act as eliminating boundaries and terminating territorial authority. The former opinion had not been disavowed. These conflicting labels cannot substitute for construction of the 1894 enactment.
- South Dakota consistently exercised extensive civil, criminal, and regulatory authority over opened land. The lower record reports no evidence of comparable tribal jurisdiction over nontrust land until recently, and the 1962 amended tribal constitution referred to original lands “now owned” by the Tribe. These are evidence of administration and understandings, not forfeiture, estoppel, or a freestanding power to redraw boundaries.
- The contemporary published account describes non-Indians as more than two-thirds of inhabitants. The lower opinion separately discusses census estimates and the Tribe's higher membership-based estimate, producing an approximate Indian-population range of thirty-two to forty-four percent. It flags uncertainty in nineteenth-century census geography and population categories. Tribal employment, population, and acquisitions had increased after the casino opened in 1991. No precise population threshold determines the statutory question, and a later demographic change neither diminishes nor restores a reservation on its own.

## 4. Exact statutory problem

The controlling instrument is the Act of August 15, 1894, ch. 290, §12, 28 Stat. 286, 314–319, ratifying the incorporated 1892 agreement. The preamble refers to negotiations for “surrender of portions of their respective reservations” and to the Yanktons' willingness to dispose of a portion of reserved land. That framing must be read with the specific operative provisions.

**Article I:** “The Yankton tribe of Dakota or Sioux Indians hereby cede, sell, relinquish, and convey to the United States all their claim, right, title, and interest in and to all the unallotted lands within the limits of the reservation set apart to said Indians as aforesaid.”

**Article II:** “In consideration for the lands ceded, sold, relinquished, and conveyed to the United States as aforesaid, the United States stipulates and agrees to pay to the said Yankton tribe of Sioux Indians the sum of six hundred thousand dollars ($600,000), as hereinbefore provided for.” Articles III–VI prescribe the distribution and use of that consideration: $100,000 for initial per-capita payment and $500,000 credited to a Treasury fund, with five-percent interest and provisions governing principal, assistance, schools, courts, other institutions, and later per-capita distribution. Congressional ratification appropriates the consideration. This is a federal purchase commitment, not merely a promise to remit whatever resale proceeds the Government happens to obtain.

**Article VIII:** “Such part of the surplus lands hereby ceded and sold to the United States as may now be occupied by the United States for agency, schools, and other purposes, shall be reserved from sale to settlers until they are no longer required for such purposes. But all other lands included in this sale shall, immediately after the ratification of this agreement by Congress, be offered for sale through the proper land office, to be disposed of under the existing land laws of the United States, to actual bona fide settlers only.” The language calls the agency and school parcels ceded but withholds them from settler sale. It requires separate attention to their status; neither side may silently treat their status as identical to an ordinary sold homestead tract.

**Article XVII:** “No intoxicating liquors nor other intoxicants shall ever be sold or given away upon any of the lands by this agreement ceded and sold to the United States, nor upon any other lands within or comprising the reservations of the Yankton Sioux or Dakota Indians as described in the treaty between the said Indians and the United States, dated April 19th, 1858, and as afterwards surveyed and set off to the said Indians. The penalty for the violation of this provision shall be such as Congress may prescribe in the act ratifying this agreement.” Ratification supplies the penalty. The express distinction between ceded land and other land is relevant, as is the provision's perpetual operation. It is not itself a complete boundary definition.

**Article XVIII:** “Nothing in this agreement shall be construed to abrogate the treaty of April 19th, 1858, between the Yankton tribe of Sioux Indians and the United States. And after the signing of this agreement, and its ratification by Congress, all provisions of the said treaty of April 19th, 1858, shall be in full force and effect, the same as though this agreement had not been made, and the said Yankton Indians shall continue to receive their annuities under the said treaty of April 19th, 1858.” The first sentence and the general preservation clause are real statutory language, alongside the annuity clause. They cannot be deleted merely because annuities were important in negotiation. Conversely, a construction preserving every earlier exclusion and possession provision literally would conflict with the authorized sale and settlement. The question is which reconciliation respects the enacted whole.

Other provisions matter to scope. Article IX regulates leases of trust allotments; Article XIII safeguards recognized members' peaceful possession and tribal rights; Article XIV confirms specified allotments and protects allotted lands from later congressional alienation; Article XI addresses an allottee's death without heirs during the trust period. Article X provides a purchase opportunity for religious organizations occupying land. Article V funds continuing institutions and contemplates subsequent changes following title and citizenship. These provisions show that individual and institutional arrangements continue; their precise effect on the exterior boundary requires analysis rather than an automatic inference in either direction. The scouts' payments, prior-claim protection, and Pipestone referral do not decide this parcel's reservation status.

The ratifying text opens ceded lands on presidential proclamation under the homestead and townsite laws, with the exception of the sixteenth and thirty-sixth sections, which “shall be reserved for common-school purposes and be subject to the laws of the State of South Dakota.” The 1889 enabling Act's school-grant restriction supplies context, as quoted in the clean facts and lower dissent: reservation land was excepted until extinguishment and restoration to the public domain. The State can argue that the later school disposition presupposes withdrawal. The Tribe can argue that the specific later enactment supplies a limited school disposition, and that making those sections expressly subject to state law need not settle every other tract. The later specific grant, possible redundancy, and background rule should be confronted; none is a conclusive substitute for Articles I, II, VIII, and XVIII.

## 5. Governing law and proposition-level authority

### Actual simulated Hagen

Hagen v. Utah, decided February 23, 1994, has a direct six-Justice Court rationale: O'Connor, joined by Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, and Ginsburg. Congress diminished the Uintah Reservation as to unallotted lands covered by the opening program because the **1902 operative direction restoring those lands to the public domain survived the 1903–1905 amendments and implementation**. Myton was in the opened area on that record, permitting the territorial criminal jurisdiction asserted there. Opening or fee ownership alone was insufficient. The $70,064.48 payment was not treated as purchase of the surplus land. Later population and state administration did not independently alter boundaries.

Hagen applies Solem's unequal weighting of statutory text, contemporaneous context, and subsequent treatment. It preserves Rosebud's statutory-continuity analysis and the absence of mandatory formula or fixed payment; Seymour's separation of fee title from retained reservation boundaries; Mattz's requirement of congressional diminishment beyond sale and settlement; and DeCoteau's recognition that clear cession can suffice without prescribing its exact words or payment structure for all cases. Its treatment of Bourland separates exclusion-derived regulation and residual tribal authority from the boundary question. Its treatment of Sac and Fox preserves distinct §1151 categories; Negonsott provides no general territorial authorization outside its Kansas-specific context.

Hagen therefore supplies a method and limits, not an answer determined by the shared word “opening.” The difference between its operative restoration command and Yankton's cession, price, and savings clause is material. An argument that the absence of Hagen's precise public-domain words necessarily defeats diminishment also conflicts with Hagen's recognition of other sufficient expressions of congressional intent. Its judgment did not settle every trust parcel, private title, sovereign power, or offense; the same discipline of scope is required here.

### Surviving predivergence law

**Solem, 465 U.S. 463, particularly 468–472 and its footnotes.** Only Congress can diminish a reservation; title transfers and settlement do not suffice. The statutory language is the most probative evidence. Explicit cession or other present and total surrender of tribal interests strongly suggests withdrawal of unallotted opened land. When coupled with an unconditional congressional compensation commitment, it creates an “almost insurmountable presumption” of diminishment. That phrase is strongly weighted rather than conclusive; it cannot be converted into an unstated mandatory evidentiary burden. Conversely, neither cession language nor fixed compensation is a necessary form for every diminishment statute. An unequivocal contemporaneous understanding shown by negotiations and legislative reports can establish congressional intent in the appropriate setting.

Solem places later governmental treatment and population below the Act and contemporary context. Demographics are an additional clue to what Congress expected, not a boundary-changing event; footnote 13 warns about their practical limitations. When both statutory text and legislative history fail to supply substantial and compelling evidence of congressional intent, the traditional solicitude for tribes requires the old boundaries to remain. The 1908 Cheyenne River Act at issue there authorized sale and disposition for the Tribe's benefit without purchasing the land outright, and the remainder of that scheme supported continued reservation status. Agency and school reservations, further allotments, and other retained interests mattered in context. Isolated “diminished” or nonoperative public-domain language did not settle the whole instrument. Its footnote 18 treats liquor and school provisions as secondary rather than self-executing proof of withdrawal. Contradictory later labels provided little assistance.

Solem's discussion of 18 U.S.C. §1151 preserves distinct categories: reservation land notwithstanding a patent, dependent Indian communities, and Indian allotments with unextinguished Indian titles. Its footnote 8 recognizes that retained allotments or other protected categories can remain Indian country despite diminishment. A holding about ceded land does not decide all of those categories.

**DeCoteau, controlling opinion, 420 U.S. 425–449.** The Court found that the Lake Traverse transaction terminated reservation status over the unallotted land because the enacted outright cession, fixed compensation, and unusually clear negotiation history established the intended surrender. The instrument was not merely a mechanism to sell land and hold proceeds for the Tribe. The tribal construction canon operates on legal ambiguity and does not authorize disregarding clear tribal and congressional expressions. Allotments retained distinct Indian-country status; the opinion did not dispose of every issue created by movement between parcels. Its school-section discussion, including footnote 33, recognized competing readings of the language making school lands subject to state law. Its holding is binding at its stated scope, not a rule that the word “cede” mechanically controls every later enactment regardless of other provisions.

The lower dissent invokes a DeCoteau-related savings clause as an analogy. The complete appended earlier instruments were not read here, so this packet does not independently verify that additional clause comparison or rely on an assertion that DeCoteau considered and rejected a clause identical to Article XVIII. The controlling cession holding and Solem's express account of its significance are fully available without that auxiliary analogy. The actual Yankton clause is fully verified.

**Dion, 476 U.S. 734.** Congressional treaty abrogation requires clear and plain intent. Express language is preferable but not an absolute prerequisite. The essential inquiry is whether Congress actually considered the conflict with the treaty right and chose to abrogate it. The eagle-protection statute's narrow religious-use permit and its specific legislative history supplied that evidence concerning eagle hunting. That holding did not extinguish every treaty right. Dion's references to the Yankton Reservation do not decide the effect of the 1894 Act on its boundaries; the location and membership assumptions in footnote 1 are not a boundary adjudication. Dion supports identifying the particular right and Congress's actual action, while Solem and simulated Hagen supply the more specific diminishment framework.

No separate reservation-diminishment module appears in the opening Standards and Tests. An unrelated Indian-law module must not replace these sources. The State's general regulatory powers, the Tribe's authority over nonmembers, ownership, reservation boundaries, and the §1151 categories are related but distinct questions.

## 6. Strongest supported paths and their burdens of explanation

### A. Remove the ceded, unallotted parcel from the reservation

Articles I and II together have the precise features Solem gives exceptional weight: a present and comprehensive cession of all tribal interests in the unallotted land and an unconditional federal purchase commitment. The Government assumed the fixed purchase obligation; the Tribe did not simply retain beneficial ownership while awaiting resale receipts. An analysis cannot discount that combination by considering each article in isolation or by requiring the negotiations to repeat an otherwise clear statutory choice.

On this reading, Article XVIII preserves continuing obligations, especially the promised annuities, while permitting the specific land transaction to operate. Its preservation language cannot literally retain treaty provisions forbidding the very dispossession and non-Indian settlement the Act authorizes. The State must nevertheless explain why the necessary accommodation extends to reservation status, and why general treaty preservation is not compatible with the transfer of title alone. Calling the clause “annuities only” is a conclusion requiring justification from its entire text and context, not a literal quotation of what the first two clauses say.

Contemporary statements concerning the end of common property and anticipated state government can support the cession inference. School-section dispositions and the permanent liquor rule can corroborate a jurisdictional change if their alternative readings are addressed. State administration and settlement can confirm the interpretation grounded in the enactment; they cannot supply congressional intent missing from the law. The absence of operative public-domain language is a distinction from Hagen, but not a mandatory-word barrier under Hagen or Solem.

The corresponding judgment could reject the declaration insofar as it treats this ceded, unallotted waste-site tract as reservation land, and remand for disposition consistent with that conclusion and the actual remaining regulatory controversy. It should not announce total disestablishment, adjudicate all allotted or agency parcels, cancel trust status, restore an unappealed tribal injunction claim, or establish unlimited state jurisdiction over every person or activity. Even a broader interpretation offered by a party does not make a broader judgment necessary.

### B. Preserve the original reservation boundaries while recognizing the land sale

Article XVIII is unusually broad on its face: it preserves “all provisions” of the 1858 treaty “the same as though this agreement had not been made,” and then separately promises continued annuities. The Tribe can reconcile it with Articles I and II by recognizing the transfer of proprietary interests and settlement rights while retaining the jurisdictional boundary. Congress could sell unallotted land for a fixed price without withdrawing that land from a reservation. Under this reading, the particular savings clause supplies the reason not to apply the general cession inference conclusively. The explanation must acknowledge the force of the combined cession-and-price language; merely reciting that a sale alone does not diminish a reservation would be insufficient.

Article VIII's continuing agency and school use, the allotment protections and institutional provisions, the absence of express boundary discussion during negotiation, and the statute's actual preservation language support this construction. Later references to the reservation can be corroborative, but inconsistent later terminology cannot supply a missing congressional choice. The liquor rule's perpetual reach gives it possible independent work even if the reservation remained; school sections may have been given a specific status without determining the whole region. These readings require attention to the State's enabling-Act argument and the stronger features distinguishing this transaction from Solem's sales-agency arrangement.

The corresponding judgment could affirm the reservation-status declaration at the scope actually necessary to this parcel and the continuing jurisdictional dispute. Such affirmance would not establish the Tribe's regulatory authority over the Waste District, which was rejected below and not appealed. It would not itself nullify the EPA waiver or dictate a composite liner. Any necessary clarification of the declaration's consequences must respect those limits.

If an actual Article III problem emerges from the continuing effect of the declaration, it must be resolved before a merits ruling under the entering jurisdictional law. The packet does not manufacture mootness from the EPA waiver or invent a live enforcement dispute to avoid examining it. The Court can decide whether this category was removed while reserving the status of allotments, agency lands, and subsequently acquired trust property. Reserving those questions is different from presuming that the entire reservation ceased to exist, and different from holding that every remaining parcel necessarily retains reservation status. Section 1151 categories must be determined on their own legal and factual predicates when actually presented.
