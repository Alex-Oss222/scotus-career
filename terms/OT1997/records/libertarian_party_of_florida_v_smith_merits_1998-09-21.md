## Decision kernel

The question is whether Florida Statutes §99.103(1) (1993) violates federal political-association and equality protections by distributing approximately half of qualifying parties' candidates' filing fees to those parties' executive committees for party expenses, while excluding parties below five percent of the State's registered voters. No finding establishes that minor-party fees were specially exacted for, or directly transferred to, rival parties. The Florida Supreme Court treated the relevant state constitutional protection as coextensive with the federal protection in this case.

## Standards and Tests selection: Write-in voting within the complete ballot-access system

### Write-in voting within the complete ballot-access system

**Governing question:** Whether a ban on write-in voting violates electoral-choice or independent expressive rights.

**Current rule:** Under the existing Anderson inquiry, the court assesses the character and magnitude of the actual injury against the State's precise interests and the extent to which those interests require the burden. Severe burdens require narrow tailoring to a compelling interest; reasonable, nondiscriminatory burdens ordinarily may be justified by important regulatory interests. When the complete access system imposes only reasonable burdens, a write-in prohibition is presumptively valid and may be sustained by proportionate interests, including primary finality and prevention of evasive candidacies or party raiding. The ballot need not provide an official count and publication of votes for fictional figures or arbitrary protest messages as an independent expressive entitlement.

**Authority by component:**
- Governing inherited framework: Burdick v. Takushi, “1. Review of election regulations follows the actual burden”, June 8, 1992; White, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Thomas (five), applying Anderson and the earlier simulated Norman rule.
- Limited-burden write-in presumption: Burdick, “2. Hawaii's complete write-in prohibition is valid in its reasonably accessible system,” June 8, 1992; same five Justices.
- Arbitrary-message claim: Burdick, “3. The ballot need not serve as an official tally of arbitrary political messages,” June 8, 1992; same controlling five-Justice opinion, with all nine agreeing in rejection of that component.

**Present operation effective:** Burdick v. Takushi, June 8, 1992, for the write-in presumption and expressive boundary; the underlying Anderson framework is applied unchanged.

**Exceptions and limits:** No absolute validation of all write-in bans follows. Materially more restrictive access systems may impose severe burdens. The rule does not replace strict scrutiny of content-based campaign restrictions on public sidewalks.

**Holdings navigation:** Burdick v. Takushi, 504 U.S. 428 (1992) — Ballot access and political parties.


## Standards and Tests selection: Authorized party expansion and independently qualified slate components

### Authorized party expansion and independently qualified slate components

**Governing question:** Whether a State may deny ballot access through a geographic party-name barrier or failure of candidates in a different electorate to qualify.

**Current rule:** A State may require meaningful support in each separate electorate. It may not categorically bar authorized candidates from expanding an existing party under its name, or disqualify otherwise qualified countywide and city candidates solely because candidates in a different district lack sufficient signatures. Protection against unauthorized appropriation of a party's name does not justify a geographic barrier that also prohibits authorized expansion; support requirements for one electorate do not justify invalidating candidates who qualified in their own constituencies.

**Authority by component:**

- Authorized name expansion, independent qualification, and separate-electorate support: Norman v. Reed — Ballot-access holding and operative rule, January 14, 1992; Souter's opinion joined by Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, and Kennedy.

**Present operation effective:** *Norman*, January 14, 1992.

**Open question:** Other district structures, cumulative burdens, and actual unauthorized name use remain outside this holding.

**Holdings navigation:** Norman v. Reed, 502 U.S. 279 (1992) — Ballot access and political parties.

### 2. This objective support criterion is constitutional at the demonstrated burden

**Holding and operative rule:** A State may allocate this limited party-expense benefit according to an objective five-percent registered-support criterion where lawfully collected fees, continued candidate access, unrestricted private political spending and the demonstrated record establish no severe associational burden, and the measure reasonably serves stable public participation. The holding permits demonstrated-support financing; it does not permit preserving named incumbents or suppressing political viewpoints as independent objectives.

**Authority:** Stevens's Part II is joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; eight direct rationale votes, including the stated limitations.

**Controlling explanation:** Anderson v. Celebrezze requires comparing the character and magnitude of actual injury with the State's precise interests and their need for the burden. Burdick v. Takushi applies that inquiry to the complete electoral setting: severe burdens demand narrowly fitted compelling justification; reasonable, nondiscriminatory burdens ordinarily may rest on important regulatory interests. A funding label does not excuse examination of the classification, but a comparative benefit alone does not establish severe restriction.

Buckley v. Valeo, Part III, permits demonstrated-support conditions on election funding and distinguishes withholding a subsidy from excluding a candidacy. Here the objective, potentially changing registration threshold reasonably measures stable party support. Supporting established public participation without subsidizing every splinter organization is a legitimate allocation interest. Wilson's individual ballot qualification does not establish an automatic entitlement to party-wide financial assistance. Norman v. Reed protects independently qualified candidacies; this provision does not remove one from the ballot.

The strongest contrary argument is that selective aid magnifies existing parties' advantages after rivals qualify. Such an allocation remains reviewable for substantial suppression, viewpoint favoritism or discriminatory administration. This record establishes no such burden beyond the limited benefit's existence. Florida's registration measure differs from Buckley's vote criteria, expenditure ceilings and post-election payments; those features are not imported here. The Court does not adopt unqualified rhetoric about discouraging minor parties, a general rational-basis exemption for funding, or validity for every five-percent threshold.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Anderson applies the actual-burden and precise-interest inquiry to this financial classification.
- Burdick preserves burden-sensitive scrutiny; its write-in presumption is not transferred to all subsidies.
- Buckley supplies demonstrated-support funding authority while retaining constitutional limits and political fluidity; the schemes are not identical.
- Norman preserves qualified-component ballot protection without creating automatic party-expense funding.

- National Endowment for the Arts v. Finley supplies no universal subsidy immunity; its arts-program holding and divided spending rationales retain their actual scope.

- Anderson applies the actual-burden and precise-interest inquiry to this financial classification.
- Burdick preserves burden-sensitive scrutiny; its write-in presumption is not transferred to all subsidies.
- Buckley supplies demonstrated-support funding authority while retaining constitutional limits and political fluidity; the schemes are not identical.
- Norman preserves qualified-component ballot protection without creating automatic party-expense funding.
- Arkansas Educational Television Commission v. Forbes preserves review of genuine support criteria and viewpoint discrimination; its forum classification is not an election-finance rule.
- National Endowment for the Arts v. Finley supplies no universal subsidy immunity; its arts-program holding and divided spending rationales retain their actual scope.

### 3. Affirmance supplies no new payment or allocation entitlement

**Holding and operative rule:** Because the presented federal challenge fails, the judgment sustaining §99.103(1)'s limited allocation is affirmed without a rebate, refund or judicially prescribed formula. The lawful collection of candidate fees, access to the ballot and a materially different source-of-funds exaction remain outside the adjudicated challenge.

**Authority:** Stevens's Part III is joined by all seven other Associates; eight direct rationale votes.

**Controlling explanation:** The challengers sought relief against discriminatory operation of the allocation, while conceding lawful fee collection. The Court's bounded constitutional conclusion supplies no basis for enjoining this scheme or directing new payments. No state-law refund claim, expenditure ceiling or future financing system is decided.

**Precedent treatment:** Buckley remains a source of lawful financing distinctions, not a command to adopt its formula or award money in this case.

- Anderson v. Celebrezze: applies the actual injury, precise interest and necessary-burden inquiry.
- Burdick v. Takushi: retains scrutiny according to actual burden; no universal funding exemption or write-in presumption for subsidies is created.

## Law After Decision

Florida's limited, objective party-expense allocation survives this federal challenge on the demonstrated record. Public election assistance may distinguish demonstrated party support without automatically following every candidate's ballot qualification. Actual burdens, viewpoint manipulation and discriminatory administration remain reviewable; neither established-party preservation for its own sake nor the funding label ends constitutional scrutiny. No monetary entitlement or general validity for five-percent thresholds results.

## Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, dissenting.** Stone-Zsela would reverse because this mandatory candidate-fee allocation gives an institutional benefit to established parties while withholding the same form of support from a recognized smaller party whose candidate has already qualified and paid the lawful fee. Anderson and Burdick require examination of that injury and Florida's actual interest. The five-percent statewide-registration threshold measures accumulated party strength rather than this candidate's qualified race or an identified administrative cost of returning the fee. For him, preserving established organizations and avoiding factional alternatives do not adequately justify this particular exclusion; institutional advantage cannot be its own constitutional end.

Buckley permits genuine support-sensitive financing and is not overruled by that position. Norman reinforces that unrelated party-organization restrictions cannot nullify valid candidate qualification, though Stone-Zsela does not claim the challenged rule literally removes Wilson from the ballot. The party asserts the participation interests of its members and voters, not personal rights for every legal entity. Collection and distribution of fees are government action; total ballot exclusion is unnecessary for constitutional review. No finding that minor-party payments were directly transferred to rivals is needed for his objection to the different benefits generated by the same type of lawful fee.

He would enjoin further distribution under the invalid classification after orderly implementation on remand. Florida could end the rebate, extend it evenhandedly to recognized parties with qualified candidates, or adopt a genuine support-sensitive measure justified by an independent constitutional interest. He would require no repayment of completed distributions and create no unrestricted entitlement to public money, guarantee of every minor party's survival, or obligation to finance every prospective candidate. His proposed invalidation does not command a majority.

## Procedure After Action

The Florida Supreme Court judgment stands on the federal question. No new funding, refund, ballot placement, allocation formula or administrative timetable is ordered. The Court leaves unpresented state-law entitlements and materially different future applications undecided.
