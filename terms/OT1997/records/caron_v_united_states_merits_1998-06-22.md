## Law of the decision

### A continuing state firearms restriction retains the conviction's federal effect

**Holding and operative rule:** When a State restores civil rights but expressly retains the substantial firearm disability presented here—prohibiting handgun possession outside the home or business—the restoration does not remove the conviction under 18 U.S.C. §921(a)(20). The retained conviction has the federal consequences prescribed by the applicable firearms statutes, including for rifles and shotguns the State permits the person to possess; those statutes' independent offense and enhancement requirements still must be met.

**Authority:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court is joined in full by Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer. These six directly adopt the statutory rule, its explanation, application, limits and affirmance.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 921(a)(20) excludes a restored conviction unless the restoration expressly retains the identified firearms prohibition. The conviction is the object classified, not a sequence of licenses for individual weapon transactions. Beecham directs the Court to the convicting jurisdiction's law to identify restoration. The federal statute then determines its consequences. Massachusetts's automatic restoration and permission to possess rifles and shotguns are accepted here, but its significant remaining handgun restriction prevents the conviction from being disregarded under the federal unless clause.

The contrary reading correctly emphasizes the actual state permission. It fails, however, to explain why Congress's qualification would disregard a continuing state firearms disability whenever some other possession remained lawful. The State need not prohibit every firearm in every place before the federal qualification has work to do. Congress borrowed state restoration status while prescribing its own broader consequence. That structure resolves the competing constructions; under Beecham and Chapman, lenity does not displace a meaning established by the ordinary interpretive inquiry. The separate statutes still determine which convictions qualify, what conduct and commerce connection are prohibited, and which enhancement applies.

The conclusion rests on the actual Massachusetts restriction, not a finding that Caron is individually dangerous. Love recognizes the personal and incorporated arms guarantee but does not resolve every convicted-person restriction. Neither that constitutional holding nor Staples's offense-specific factual-knowledge rule rewrites this restoration clause. Bryan addresses knowing unlawful dealing under a different provision. No distinct constitutional or mental-state issue is decided.

**Application and limits:** The Massachusetts convictions remain countable against the restoration objection, and the First Circuit's statutory judgment is affirmed. This is not a complete Massachusetts ban on handguns: possession in the home or business remained permitted. The Court accepts the parties' restoration premise without deciding every automatic restoration mechanism. It does not reconsider original violent-felony classifications, separately adjudicate the California predicate, pronounce every firearms disability constitutional, impose a new sentence or resolve an independently cognizable issue outside the presented restoration question. Section 922(g)(1)'s prohibition and commerce predicates remain distinct from §924(e)(1)'s three qualifying convictions, separate-occasion condition and minimum fifteen-year sentence.

They choose the defendant's side of the all-or-nothing inquiry: because the restoration does not contain the required complete possession prohibition, these restored convictions are excluded. Federal protective purpose cannot replace Congress's chosen express condition.

Independently, the textually natural restoration reading remains at least sufficiently unresolved after context and purpose are examined to require lenity. Chapman and Granderson reserve that principle for ambiguity surviving ordinary interpretation; the dissent relies on the actual statutory difficulty, not merely the existence of two proposed readings. It would remove this unless-clause basis for counting the Massachusetts convictions and return the reviewed sentencing consequences for lawful disposition. It orders neither acquittal on all counts nor exclusion of every other predicate, and it decides no constitutional challenge. Both the textual ground and residual-lenity ground have the assent of all three dissenters.

## Procedure After Action

The First Circuit's statutory judgment remains effective. Any further sentencing proceedings must treat the Massachusetts convictions as retained against this §921(a)(20) objection while respecting the independently applicable offense, enhancement and procedural requirements. This Court orders no new sentence or separate constitutional disposition.
