## Controlling law of the decision

### Section 504(c) supplies no independent statutory jury entitlement

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 504(c)'s authorization of statutory damages does not itself confer a right to a jury determination. That statutory conclusion does not settle the separate Seventh Amendment allocation.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: eight direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** The provision supplies a damages election, ranges and specific conditions for adjustment. Its references to what the court considers just, the court's findings and its discretion contain no independent jury command. Reading those references in the context of the whole subsection yields no statutory entitlement to jury assessment. The contrary institutional reading of court cannot displace that conclusion merely to avoid the constitutional issue. The Court must therefore determine whether the Constitution preserves the jury's role despite the statute's allocation; statutory silence about a jury does not eliminate constitutional protection.

### The Seventh Amendment protects the initial determination of statutory-damages facts and amount

**Holding and operative rule:** On a timely jury demand in this private copyright statutory-damages action, the Seventh Amendment assigns disputed factual issues pertinent to the award, including willfulness and the amount within the lawful statutory range, to the jury. The court continues to interpret the statute, identify the lawful range, decide legal sufficiency and conduct constitutionally permissible review; the ruling does not assign injunctions, fees, costs, equitable accountings or historically judicial questions to juries.

**Authority:** The same eight Justices join the Court's constitutional holding and damages remand. Scalia supports the judgment on the separate statutory ground.

**Controlling explanation:** Copyright damages have a historical legal analogue, and the monetary remedy seeks compensation or punishment of the kind traditionally assessed by a jury. A range requiring judgment does not turn that task into equitable relief. Markman's historical and functional inquiry distinguishes a legal action's jury protection from the assignment of a particular legal-meaning function; its judicial patent-construction rule does not transfer disputed damages valuation to a judge.

Tull's allocation of the amount of a governmental civil penalty does not control this private copyright-damages function. Congress may identify the lawful range without removing the jury's task of deciding the pertinent facts and selecting the amount within it. Gasperini and Hetzel concern lawful review and the new-trial election after an award; they do not authorize initial judicial substitution for the demanded jury. Their applicable review limits remain intact.

Feltner demanded a jury, but the judge decided willfulness and the statutory amount. Those determinations cannot stand on that procedure. The remand changes the decisionmaker for the protected work, not the statute's ranges, burdens, per-work unit or exceptions. It does not resolve every challenge to the count of works, reopen unreviewed liability or guarantee any particular award.

**Operative statutory qualifications:** The owner may elect statutory damages before final judgment instead of actual damages and profits. The ordinary range is $500–$20,000 for all infringements of any one work for which one infringer is individually liable or two or more infringers are jointly and severally liable; all parts of a compilation or derivative work are one work. Owner-proved willfulness permits, but does not require, an increase to $100,000. Infringer-proved lack of awareness and of reason to believe the acts infringed permits, but does not require, reduction to no less than $200.

Mandatory remission is a separate rule. It requires belief, with reasonable grounds, that the use was fair use under §107, and either (1) reproduction in copies or phonorecords by an employee or agent of a nonprofit educational institution, library or archives acting within employment, or by that institution, library or archives itself; or (2) performance of a published nondramatic literary work, or reproduction of a transmission program embodying its performance, by a public broadcasting entity as defined in §118(g), or by a person as a regular part of that entity's nonprofit activities. Innocence or broadcaster status alone does not satisfy this rule. The Court finds no exception established here.

## Event

Feltner v. Columbia Pictures Television, Inc., No. 96-1768. Argued January 21, 1998; decided March 31, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, reviewing 106 F.3d 284. The question is whether §504(c) of the Copyright Act or the Seventh Amendment entitles Feltner to a jury determination of statutory damages.

After license termination, the stations continued broadcasting Columbia's programs. The District Court granted summary judgment on infringement liability, denied Feltner's jury demand and, after a bench trial, found willfulness and 440 infringements. It selected $20,000 for each, totaling $8.8 million. The Ninth Circuit affirmed the relevant damages rulings but separately vacated the costs-and-fees award for explanation. Only Columbia's copyright claims against Feltner remained.
