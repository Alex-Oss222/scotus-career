## Holdings

### Article IV does not permit this inadequately justified categorical exclusion

**Holding and operative rule:** When a State taxes another State's citizens on income earned within its borders, a discriminatory deduction rule must rest on a substantial reason beyond noncitizenship, and the discrimination must bear a close relationship to that reason. Section 631(b)(6)'s categorical exclusion of qualifying alimony from deductions against New York-source income fails that inquiry on the source, personal-expense, initial-computation and family-income justifications offered here; genuine source allocation remains permissible, and identical resident and nonresident tax codes are not required.

**Authority:** The seven-Justice Opinion of the Court supplies direct majority authority for this rule, its application and the qualifications below.

**Controlling explanation:** Toomer protects substantial equality in earning a livelihood across state lines while allowing considerable state latitude. Shaffer permits taxation of local income and corresponding source-related deduction limits; Travis nevertheless rejects unjustified unequal personal allowances. Those decisions require examination of the particular deduction and tax, not ordinary rationality alone or mechanical equality.

New York taxes residents' worldwide income and nonresidents' local income. That real difference does not by itself justify excluding all proportionate recognition of alimony. The payment adjusts income available to the payer and lacks an exclusively Connecticut source merely because the former spouses and divorce decree are there. The lower court's Goodwin analogy involved geographically connected home-State property and medical expenses; it does not resolve this alimony provision. The Court adopts no rule requiring apportionment of every personal expense.

The whole calculation also matters. Qualifying alimony reduces hypothetical resident liability and the federal-adjusted-gross-income denominator, but § 631(b)(6) denies the deduction in the New York-source numerator. The initial benefit therefore does not establish substantial equality in the final burden. The ratio was uncapped; no particular actual ratio or total effective rate is found.

The asserted allocation of taxable family income likewise fails to match the enacted line. Payer residence determines the deduction without conditioning it on whether New York taxes the recipient. Examining that mismatch addresses the classification imposing the Lundings' own assessment, without finding how often different family arrangements occur. Neither a hypothetical recipient-based rule nor its constitutionality is decided.

Austin prevents another State's credit, deduction or fiscal choice from curing established discrimination. Connecticut's absence of earned-income taxation in this year supplies no cure. Fulton and Oregon Waste reinforce scrutiny of actual operation and unsupported offsets only by analogy; their Commerce Clause tests do not govern Article IV. States retain justified source limits and permissible cooperative tax arrangements.

### Relief follows the established tax-remedy predicates

**Holding and operative rule:** The unconstitutional treatment of the challenged assessment requires reversal and remand for lawful state allocation, severability and constitutionally adequate relief. When the State compelled payment before meaningful review, it must provide adequate backward-looking correction; independently, a clearly held-out postpayment remedy cannot be withdrawn after payment, including voluntary payment, subject to ordinary proof and lawful previously announced conditions.

**Authority:** The same seven Justices join the Opinion of the Court's remedial holding in full.

**Controlling explanation:** McKesson requires meaningful correction when payment preceded an adequate opportunity to challenge; Harper distinguishes application of the governing federal rule from the lawful remedy. Fulton preserves state severability and remedial choices, including lawful refunds, constitutionally permissible nondiscriminatory retrospective equalization, or a lawful combination when backward-looking relief is required. Reich separately prevents a State from withdrawing a clear postpayment route after the taxpayer paid and substituting an unpursued prepayment route. Adequate prospective procedures remain permissible with fair notice.

The reported $3,724 additional assessment plus interest does not establish payment or an entitlement to that sum as a refund. The State must apply the constitutional rule through the procedures and payment facts actually established. The Court neither selects the taxpayers' proposed deduction formula as the exclusive remedy nor automatically restores every aspect of the Appellate Division's order. It orders no interest award, new assessment against others, or reopening of unrelated final tax years.

## Law After Decision

A State's narrower nonresident tax base and the personal character of a deduction do not automatically justify denying it. Courts must examine the challenged distinction in the complete tax scheme, require a substantial independent reason closely related to it, and preserve justified source allocation. Section 631(b)(6)'s categorical alimony exclusion cannot be sustained on the reasons offered here.

This decision adds that Article IV application without requiring identical deductions, strict numerical tax equality, the Commerce Clause's compensatory-tax elements or a particular replacement formula. It decides no Equal Protection or Commerce Clause claim, no dispute about federal alimony qualification, and no hypothetical recipient-based alternative. The existing conditional rules governing unconstitutional-tax relief remain in force.

## Separate Writings

Ginsburg, joined by Kennedy, would affirm on two independent grounds.

First, the payer deduction and recipient inclusion allocate taxable income between former spouses. When both are New York residents, the State taxes the recipient and permits the payer's deduction; when both are Connecticut residents, denying the deduction preserves taxation of New York-source income that would otherwise pass outside the State's resident tax base. In their view, that source-tax and income-attribution relationship supplies a substantial justification in this case. Article IV requires substantial rather than mechanically precise equality. Imperfect treatment of other, mixed-residence couples does not establish these taxpayers' entitlement to remove the income from New York taxation. This position does not depend on a finding of aggregate interstate-payment balance or on Connecticut's imposing a tax.

Second, even treating alimony as an expense, the Connecticut payer, recipient and decree locate this particular marital obligation in the home State; it is not an expense of producing New York professional income. Shaffer and Travis permit the corresponding source limitation, and Goodwin's home-State expense analysis supports leaving that personal expense to Connecticut's law and policy. Travis's general personal exemption is different from a deduction for a particular nonlocal obligation. They also object that correlation with a payer's income does not distinguish alimony from many other personal expenditures; the Court's disclaimer of a universal deduction rule does not, in their view, resolve that distinction.

Their objection to relying on other former couples is a merits objection, not a vote to dismiss for lack of standing. They accept Austin's rule against curing established discrimination through another State's tax choices but find this allocation justified in the first place. They join neither the invalidity holding nor the correction mandate; their grounds are noncontrolling.
