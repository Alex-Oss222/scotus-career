## Law of the decision

### The pleaded duties cannot be adjudicated without choosing military training policy

**Holding and operative rule:** A military setting alone does not make a damages action nonjusticiable. These claims nevertheless cannot proceed because determining the alleged duties concerning drill notification, command terminology, live arming and questioning orders would require choosing among military training, obedience and safety policies without an independently manageable legal standard on the supplied record.

**Authority:** Kennedy's opinion of the Court, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas and Breyer; five of nine participating Justices directly adopt the rule, application and limits.

**Controlling explanation:** Baker requires examination of the particular claim, not rejection because its subject is military. Nixon preserves that issue-specific inquiry. Here ordinary negligence terminology does not identify a duty independent of the disputed training choices. Deciding which personnel should know that an attack is simulated, what live-weapons commands should mean, and when operators should question an apparent firing instruction would select the balance between realistic preparation, command obedience and safety that the pleaded allegations challenge.

The contrary argument has force: this was an unintended live discharge during an intended simulation, and compensation after an accident differs from continuing supervision. Gilligan expressly left specific-injury controversies distinct from the ongoing supervision it rejected. But that reservation neither makes every damages action justiciable nor supplies a manageable standard for these proposed duties. Retrospective relief can still depend on judicial selection of training policy. No independently fixed operational command is established in the supplied record that separates the alleged breach from that selection; this is not a finding that no such command exists or that plaintiffs withheld evidence.

Weiss and Loving preserve enforcement of actual constitutional and enacted limits. Dalton concerns a particular statutory review scheme and supplies no general executive immunity. Richenberg likewise combines substantial military weight with an enforceable legal standard. None supplies the independent duty missing from this claim-specific inquiry. NATO participation, possible embarrassment and military status alone would be insufficient. The Court affirms the political-question judgment without finding that the firing was lawful, deciding negligence or damages, or resolving statutory coverage, sovereign immunity or Feres. A materially different claim enforcing an independent legal duty remains outside this holding.

## Separate Writings

**Stevens, joined by Stone-Zsela, Souter and Ginsburg, dissenting.** The dissent would vacate and remand for separate examination of the claims. The intended simulation and accidental live firing supply a concrete reason to distinguish execution from design; listing training and command purposes does not establish that every allegation requires choosing policy. Gilligan's reservation of specific-injury cases and Nixon's claim-specific limitation oppose the blanket effect of the judgment. Weiss and Loving preserve enforceable legal constraints despite military subject matter, and Dalton does not supply general executive insulation.

The dissent accepts that a court cannot invent a safer training rule, call it operational, and thereby evade the political-question doctrine. The remand would require the Eleventh Circuit to identify which allegations actually require selection of constitutionally committed military policy or lack manageable standards, without guaranteeing a surviving negligence claim, discovery, liability or compensation. No particular written protocol is found or presumed violated. Statutory coverage, sovereign immunity, Feres, causation, damages and the justiciability of any specifically identified high-level policy challenge remain unresolved. The dissent's proposed examination is not the Court's mandate.
