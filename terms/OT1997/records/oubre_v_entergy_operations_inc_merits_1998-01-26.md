## Holdings

### A deficient ADEA waiver cannot be enforced through retention or prior tender

**Holding and operative rule:** A release failing the applicable minimum requirements of 29 U.S.C. §626(f) cannot bar an ADEA action merely because the employee retains its consideration, whether the asserted bar is called ratification, prior tender or estoppel resting on that same retention. The party asserting waiver validity retains the statutory burden of proving a knowing and voluntary waiver under the applicable paragraph. The ruling neither invalidates a compliant waiver nor decides a distinct fraud, contract or equitable claim.

**Authority:** The seven Justices joining the Opinion of the Court adopt this entire statutory rule, its application and its bounded remedy. The concurrence's additional voidability explanation is not part of that holding.

**Controlling explanation:** Congress made minimum safeguards conditions of an effective ADEA waiver. This individual agreement omitted a specific reference to ADEA rights, allowed fourteen rather than at least twenty-one days for consideration, and supplied no seven-day postexecution revocation opportunity. Signature, actual attorney consultation and later awareness do not supply those missing protections. No group program triggering the forty-five-day period and disclosures, or settlement of an already filed charge or action under paragraph (2), is established. The latter setting has its own requirements: paragraph (1)(A)–(E) and a reasonable consideration period.

Entergy's strongest argument distinguishes initial execution from later informed affirmation and invokes the ordinary obligation to restore a bargain's benefits before repudiating it. But the asserted consequence would make this deficient waiver extinguish the very claim Congress protected. Congress need not name each contract doctrine that could otherwise circumvent its mandatory conditions. A tender requirement accomplishing the same bar cannot stand. The Court need not accept Entergy's description of common law in every setting to resolve its asserted effect here.

Mezzanatto permits its specified knowing-and-voluntary evidentiary waiver while preserving right-specific statutory restrictions; it supplies no exception to these express safeguards or burden. McKennon distinguishes an ADEA claim from legally supported remedial limitations; it does not establish a severance-repayment right. Likewise, §626(f)(4)'s protection of EEOC enforcement and employee participation remains distinct from private monetary relief.

Oubre's ability to repay is unknown and unnecessary to the holding. Lawful restitution, recoupment, setoff and the release's effect on other rights remain undecided. Preserving those questions creates no recovery entitlement or automatic $6,258 deduction. The employee must still prove her discrimination claim.

## Law After Decision

An employer cannot obtain the effect of an ADEA waiver that fails Congress's minimum conditions by relying on retention of the payment or making its return a prerequisite to the action. This construction adds the statutory ratification-and-tender rule while leaving ordinary discrimination burdens and independently lawful remedies intact. It declares neither the whole contract absolutely void nor every payment immune from recovery. Other claims, genuinely distinct equitable conduct, and a later compliant agreement require their own grounds. No automatic repayment or generalized employer accounting right follows.

## Separate Writings

Breyer, joined by O'Connor, fully agrees with the Court but explains why the deficient arrangement should be treated as voidable at the worker's election rather than absolutely void. The statute protects the employee; absolute nullity could let the employer use its own omission to cancel reciprocal benefits even when the employee does not sue. Filing the ADEA action rejects the promise not to sue. An employer may then seek whatever restitution, relief from reciprocal obligations or damages credit the law and agreement independently permit. Their concurrence awards none of those remedies and preserves the Court's prohibition on prior tender. This additional explanation is noncontrolling.

Scalia would affirm for failure to tender back. In his view, restoration before repudiating an exchange is a surviving common-law condition distinct from original waiver validity. He nevertheless rejects ratification on this record: discovering that required statutory safeguards were omitted does not remove that impediment, unlike discovering a concealed fraud or mistake that can be knowingly accepted afterward. He therefore does not join Thomas's ratification ground, and makes no finding that Oubre could repay or that a monetary judgment is warranted.

Thomas would affirm on later knowing ratification and separately preserves the tender condition. He distinguishes Congress's minimum conditions for original execution from common-law affirmation of a voidable bargain after discovery of its defect. His background-common-law analysis, reflected in Astoria, Isbrandtsen and Norfolk Redevelopment, requires a sufficient statutory basis for displacing those distinct doctrines; he finds none here. The reported knowing retention therefore supports affirmance in his view. He does not find the original agreement compliant, shift §626(f)(3)'s original-validity burden or award restitution. His ground and Scalia's uncured-impediment objection remain different.
