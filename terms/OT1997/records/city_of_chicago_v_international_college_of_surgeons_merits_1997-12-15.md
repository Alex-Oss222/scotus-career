## Holdings

**1. These mixed complaints support federal jurisdiction.** Substantial federal constitutional claims on the face of the complaints supply original jurisdiction under §1331 and permit removal of the qualifying civil actions under §1441(a). Section 1367(a) supplies supplemental jurisdiction over the related state constitutional and administrative-review claims because they form part of the same Article III case. The state claims do not fall outside that grant merely because their review is confined to an administrative record. Thomas, Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer directly adopt Part I.

The complaints challenge the same property designations, permit denials and administrative procedures under federal and state law. Their common facts, injury and requested relief meet the enacted same-case relation, informed by Gibbs's common-nucleus analysis. Congress did not require each supplemental claim to supply independent original jurisdiction; that would defeat the function of supplemental jurisdiction. Illinois's restriction on new evidence and presumption that agency factual findings are correct describe the task the reviewing court performs, not a categorical exception to §1367. The cases concern local administrative decisions, not appellate review of a state-court judgment.

The structural objection that a federal trial court cannot perform state administrative review does not overcome the actual statutory grant in these mixed cases. Recognition of power leaves the governing state review standards intact and leaves restraint to its proper source. The Court decides neither an action containing only a state administrative appeal nor a diversity basis for such an action.

**2. Jurisdiction does not compel retention.** Section 1367(c) separately permits declining supplemental jurisdiction where the claim raises novel or complex state-law issues, substantially predominates, remains after all original-jurisdiction claims have been dismissed, or presents exceptional circumstances with other compelling reasons. Established abstention remains available on its own predicates. Part I's seven Justices adopt this distinction and its limits.

Economy, convenience, fairness and comity inform the lawful exercise; they do not replace statutory conditions or erase jurisdiction. Quackenbush distinguishes power from abstention and confines outright Burford surrender to equitable or otherwise discretionary relief; an independent legal-damages claim may be stayed on a justified showing but cannot simply be relinquished on that ground. Burford's actual requirements, including timely and adequate state review and either difficult state-law questions of transcendent public importance or disruption of coherent state policy on a matter of substantial public concern, must be met if invoked. No such application is decided here. The Seventh Circuit did not reach the alternative retention arguments, so Part II's six Justices return the case for their ordinary consideration. No constitutional merits or permit entitlement follows from recognizing jurisdiction.

## Law After Decision

A district court with original jurisdiction over these substantial federal claims also has supplemental power over closely related state administrative-review claims, notwithstanding their record-confined character. Power remains separate from §1367(c), abstention and the limits of the requested remedy. The ruling establishes no categorical administrative-appeal jurisdiction, diversity route, exception to §1447(d), mandatory retention rule or alteration of state review standards.

## Separate Writings

Justice Ginsburg, joined by Justice Stevens, dissents. The general jurisdictional grants do not sufficiently authorize transfer of the appellate function Illinois assigns to its courts: review confined to a local agency record, with its facts presumptively correct. An independent federal-rights suit is different from the particular statutory review proceeding brought here. A common factual nucleus and federal issue therefore do not answer the antecedent power question, and later discretion to abstain cannot supply missing authority. They would affirm the jurisdictional remand; their objection is to statutory power, not its discretionary exercise.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela joins Part I and reversal. He would direct the District Court to recognize supplemental jurisdiction and then decide, under §1367(c), abstention principles and ordinary remedial law, whether to retain, stay or remand the state claims. His additional district-court instruction does not command the Court; he neither requires retention nor determines that abstention or remand is warranted.
