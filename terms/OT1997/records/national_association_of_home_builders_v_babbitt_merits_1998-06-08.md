### I. The enacted taking class is not made economic by the projects' commercial features

**Holding and operative rule.** For this challenge to section 9(a)(1)(B), the regulated class is the taking of listed wildlife, including habitat-caused injury, without a commercial element. The economic character of the reviewed development projects does not by itself supply a constitutionally sufficient connection fitted to that enacted class; the asserted mechanism for these applications must be identified independently of those commercial features.

**Authority.** Part I, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas: five Justices. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer do not join this part, although they join Parts II-A, III and IV and the judgment; Ginsburg and Breyer also join Part II-B.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the relevant class is the prohibition Congress enacted. Lopez requires a constitutionally sufficient connection fitted to that class before aggregation measures magnitude. Section 9 makes injury to listed wildlife its predicate whether the actor undertakes an exchange, construction or private noneconomic conduct. Sweet Home's habitat construction identifies an injury-causing act but supplies no commercial element. A court cannot treat the actual expenditures or development function of these projects as though Congress had defined an economic land-conversion class.

The contrary as-applied argument has force because the construction itself alters the habitat, rather than merely financing some unrelated act. But that factual identity does not answer the legal fit question. Hinds requires the enacted predicate to perform its own work; an owner's economic relations do not substitute for it. Here the injury predicate likewise remains distinct from an economic-development classification. Wickard and Perez preserve aggregation of properly defined economic classes, and Hodel preserves regulation of economic activity for environmental ends. Those authorities do not establish that this taking class acquires the necessary commerce connection from the commercial features of particular projects. The Court adds no statutory element, makes no categorical rule against regulation of intrastate species, and leaves supported alternative interstate mechanisms to the following parts.

### II. Necessary inclusion in a valid interstate scheme sustains the taking prohibition; an independent externality route remains available

**Holding and operative rule.** Congress may regulate intrastate conduct that is neither itself interstate commerce nor independently shown to have substantial interstate effects when inclusion of the regulated class is a reasonably adapted and integral means of executing an otherwise valid federal regulation of interstate commerce. The Court identifies the independently valid interstate regulation, the intrastate class actually covered, and a genuine functional relationship between the two; Congress may not establish that relationship merely by declaring a national objective or assembling unrelated provisions into one statute. Where Congress has established an integrated system regulating interstate exploitation, movement and commercial disposition of protected wildlife, it may include the unauthorized taking of listed wildlife as a means of preserving the populations and enforceability of that system. The Constitution does not require separate proof that every species crosses state boundaries, participates in interstate commerce, or would independently produce a substantial interstate commercial effect if left unprotected. A defendant may show that a purported application is outside the relevant statutory class, that the supposed interstate regime is not independently valid, or that the asserted integral relationship is merely nominal or pretextual.

The section 9(a)(1)(B) taking prohibition, as applied to the listed Delhi Sands flower-loving fly within the Act's integrated system, falls within Congress's Commerce and Necessary and Proper powers. This constitutional conclusion neither establishes an actual taking nor eliminates the statutory conditions for liability, permits or relief.

Congress may also regulate noneconomic intrastate conduct when it reasonably identifies, on a materially adequate legislative or adjudicative basis, a substantial interstate externality generated by the regulated class that individual States cannot reasonably confine or internalize, and the federal prohibition reasonably fits that demonstrated spillover. That route is an independent alternative. It need not be satisfied where necessary inclusion establishes congressional power, and it is not applied to this fly.

The second route requires identification of the regulated class; a concrete cross-border ecological, commercial or comparable interstate effect; why that effect is substantial rather than speculative or merely nationally interesting; why the affected States cannot reasonably internalize or control it through ordinary territorial authority; and the reasonable fit of the federal prohibition to that effect. Biodiversity's importance, speculative scientific or future economic value, tourism, development pressure, the statute's comprehensiveness, and the occurrence of similar problems in many States are insufficient by themselves. The rule requires no fixed number of States, incidents, dollars or species, market percentage or scientific threshold.

**Authority.** Part II-A's necessary-inclusion rule and its application are joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: seven Justices. Part II-B's independent externality rule and its complete limits are joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer: five Justices. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer join Part II-A while preserving their disagreement with Part I.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that local conduct is not beyond congressional power merely because it is noneconomic, but national importance does not establish an interstate mechanism. The Commerce Clause, with the Necessary and Proper Clause, lets Congress select means reasonably adapted to the effective execution of a valid regulation of commerce among the States; it does not require every included intrastate act to be independently regulable. McCulloch states that relationship, and Wickard, Perez and Hodel examine a valid regulatory class and the practical relation of included conduct to the federal scheme. Lopez recognized inclusion genuinely necessary and proper to prevent evasion or material impairment of a valid interstate regime and reserved its fuller definition for a case presenting an actual integrated regime. An included class that preserves the regulated resource and the enforceability of the scheme is such a means. Hale, applied in Rybar and Kirk, sustained possession integrated with restrictions on prohibited machinegun stock; that is a different example, and no machinegun-specific premise is imported into conservation law.

The unit of analysis is the regulated class within the federal scheme, not each species or each local controversy. The Act regulates the importation, exportation, interstate transport, sale and commercial handling of listed wildlife, the possession and shipment of unlawfully taken wildlife, the taking of listed wildlife, and permissible incidental taking. Its conservation provisions protect the populations whose exploitation and interstate commercial movement the Act also regulates; those provisions are not a collection of unrelated prohibitions. Congress can reasonably determine that protecting listed populations against unauthorized depletion is a necessary component of that system. The question is whether the intrastate prohibition performs a reasonable, identifiable function within an independently valid scheme, not whether removing protection from one local species would independently produce a substantial interstate economic consequence. The builders' challenge does not show that these applications fall outside section 9's class, that the Act's interstate regulation is invalid, or that the relationship is nominal or pretextual. The fly's absence from trade does not defeat an application whose relationship to commerce lies in the scheme it serves.

The dissent correctly insists that calling a statute comprehensive does not establish congressional power and that Congress cannot turn every local activity into interstate commerce by enacting a national statute that includes it. The rule keeps that limit. A law criminalizing ordinary local misconduct solely because the misconduct has long-term economic consequences would not qualify; neither would a statute whose interstate provision is a decorative addition to an otherwise general police regulation. But requiring a separate species-specific interstate economic relationship would make the evidentiary demands of the externality route govern necessary inclusion as well, and the Necessary and Proper Clause is not a least-restrictive-means provision. The constitutional source and the identified relationship do the decisive work: this is neither balancing of biodiversity against private economic interests nor a categorical exemption for environmental legislation.

The second route addresses a different connection left open by Lopez. A concrete burden crossing jurisdictional lines, or operating through an inherently interstate system beyond reasonable territorial control, can itself connect the regulated class to commerce among the States. Substantiality, territorial limitation and statutory fit distinguish that mechanism from generalized downstream effects through health, productivity or consumption. No balancing of national desirability against local preference replaces those requirements. The same problem occurring in several States does not show that its effects cross their authority.

The dissent also correctly demands an actual mechanism but would deny independent constitutional force to a demonstrated interstate externality. The Court concludes that requiring an independently established market-regime relationship in every such case is too narrow: a regulated class may itself produce a concrete interstate burden beyond reasonable territorial control, supplying a distinct connection. The Court requires the Government to support each part of that mechanism and its fit; regulatory need alone does not establish it. Because Part II-A establishes power here, the route is not applied to this fly. The Court makes neither every ecosystem national nor every species a commodity. Actual interstate sale, transport, trafficking, migratory movement and another independently sufficient Commerce mechanism remain distinct.

### III. The Government identifies the regime, the covered class and a genuine functional relationship; courts decide those boundaries independently

**Holding and operative rule.** The Government must identify the independently valid interstate regulation, the intrastate class actually covered, and a genuine functional relationship between the two. Courts determine those legal boundaries independently while respecting reasonable congressional judgments about regulatory necessity and practical enforcement. The Necessary and Proper Clause is not a least-restrictive-means provision: the Government need not prove that every conceivable exemption would cause a quantitatively demonstrated failure of enforcement. A court may not invent a regime or relationship that the statute and its supporting legislative or adjudicative basis do not supply.

**Authority.** Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: seven Justices.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that a constitutional label is not proof of the connection it names. Lopez separates the judicial identification of an eligible mechanism from legislative judgment within the resulting power. The first task is not discharged by accumulating national benefits. The second does not require judges to redo environmental science or choose the best policy. A reasoned legislative or adjudicative basis may support predictive conclusions even though scientific uncertainty remains; deference does not turn an unsupported assertion into a demonstrated interstate relationship.

For necessary inclusion, the relationship appears from the statute's operation and any supporting legislative or adjudicative basis. Here the Act's text and structure identify each element: its valid regulation of interstate trade in listed wildlife, the taking class section 9(a)(1)(B) actually covers, and that class's function in preserving the populations the trade provisions regulate and keeping those provisions enforceable. General claims that endangered species are valuable, that people travel to see wildlife, or that research might produce future commercial uses do not perform that work by themselves. No universal formal-legislative-findings requirement, defendant-specific congressional-findings requirement, clear-and-convincing burden or numerical benchmark is imposed. The obligation does not presume that every individual act must cross a state boundary or produce a separately substantial effect.

### IV. The judgment sustaining the challenged applications is affirmed

**Holding and operative rule.** The D.C. Circuit's judgment sustaining application of section 9(a)(1)(B) to the challenged habitat modification against this Commerce Clause objection is affirmed. Part II-A resolves the constitutional question presented, so no Commerce Clause evidentiary remand follows. The builders receive no constitutional exemption from section 9 and no injunction against its enforcement on the Commerce Clause theory; the Government may enforce the statute subject to its actual elements and exceptions.

**Authority.** Part IV, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: seven Justices.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the existing judgment is sustained by the necessary-inclusion ground, without substituting the projects' economic importance, biodiversity value or the breadth of the ESA for the required relationship. The published record establishes development-related habitat modification and the absence of trade in this fly. Neither fact defeats the application, because the relationship that sustains it lies in the taking prohibition's function within the Act's valid interstate regulation. A further hearing on the local fly would leave the constitutional question unsettled without bearing on that relationship.

The disposition does not grant or deny the particular section 10 permit, resolve an independent property claim, prescribe the final intersection design or authorize damages; whether the proposed intersection construction will cause a prohibited taking remains governed by the statutory limits below. Any independently pending statutory or permitting issues retain their ordinary procedural posture. Sweet Home's statutory construction remains binding: a permissible reading of the harm prohibition does not itself settle Congress's constitutional power in every case.

**Statutory and remedial limits.** Sweet Home requires significant habitat modification or degradation that actually kills or injures protected wildlife by significantly impairing essential behavior, including breeding, feeding or sheltering. Proximate causation and ordinary foreseeability remain required; speculative future harm or bare but-for linkage does not suffice. An omission requires an independently existing legal duty, and this decision creates no general habitat-maintenance duty. No actual injury, liability or permit entitlement is newly found. Otherwise lawful development alone is not an exemption. Section 10(a)(1)(B) permission remains confined to taking incidental to, rather than the purpose of, otherwise lawful activity. A conservation plan must state likely impacts, minimization and mitigation measures and funding, alternatives considered and reasons rejected, and other necessary appropriate measures. After public comment the Secretary must find incidental taking, maximum-practicable minimization and mitigation, adequate funding, no appreciable reduction in survival-and-recovery likelihood in the wild, fulfillment of other required measures and receipt of assurances. Those findings require issuance with necessary or appropriate conditions and reporting; noncompliance requires revocation. The substation's existing permit decides no entitlement concerning the separate intersection/corridor controversy.

The distinct threatened-species protections, section 7 agency and incidental-take provisions, scientific permissions, experimental-population provisions and enforcement channels receive no new application here. Sweet Home's reserved section 6(g)(2) question remains reserved; deciding its terms or application would require the operative official text and applicable State-agreement record. The Court inserts no commercial element into section 9 and announces no categorical immunity for traditional State subjects.

## Precedent Treatment

- **United States v. Lopez, April 26, 1995:** Applied and clarified. Its connection-before-magnitude sequence, enacted-class fit, independent judicial eligibility inquiry, no-insertion rule and rule against manufacturing a commerce connection from aggregate downstream consequences remain controlling. The Court gives fuller definition to the necessary-inclusion route Lopez recognized and reserved, and resolves its open question concerning a materially supported substantial interstate externality; generalized downstream effects remain insufficient.
- **United States v. Hale, February 12, 1996:** Preserved. Its machinegun-regulation holding supplies a different example of including local possession within a valid interstate transfer-and-stock regime; no machinegun-specific premise is imported into conservation law. The anti-evasion and material-impairment relationship it applied is assessed for the regulated class within the federal scheme, not species by species, and statutory comprehensiveness alone does not establish it.
- **United States v. Rybar, November 24, 1997:** Preserved as an application of Hale to the demonstrated relationship between possession and prohibited machinegun stock; that relationship is not inferred for the ESA.
- **United States v. Kirk, February 14, 1998:** Preserved for the same fitted regime relationship and the absence of any judicially added interstate element; no machinegun premise is transferred to species protection without support.
- **McCulloch v. Maryland:** Applied: Congress may select means reasonably adapted to the effective execution of an enumerated power, and the Necessary and Proper Clause does not require every included intrastate act to be independently regulable under the Commerce Clause.
- **Wickard v. Filburn:** Applied for examination of a valid regulatory class and the practical relation of included conduct to the federal scheme, with the Necessary and Proper Clause supplying the decisive relationship. Aggregation measures effects after a sufficient connection; it does not manufacture the connection missing from a noneconomic taking class.
- **Perez v. United States:** Applied for class-wide assessment of conduct included in a valid federal regulatory class without requiring every act independently to cross a state boundary.
- **Hodel v. Virginia Surface Mining & Reclamation Association:** Applied for regulation of an integrated class for environmental purposes within a valid interstate scheme; distinguished from treating economic features of particular projects as the enacted taking class.
- **Babbitt v. Sweet Home Chapter of Communities for a Great Oregon, June 29, 1995:** Applied and left intact as statutory law governing habitat-caused actual injury, ordinary causation and conditional permissions. It decided no universal Commerce authority over intrastate takings.
- **American Life League v. Reno, November 13, 1995:** Preserved for intentional conduct whose object and operation directly interfere with an actual interstate service market; that distinct mechanism does not establish all environmental applications.
- **United States v. Robinson, March 23, 1998:** Preserved for direct interference with operating commercial assets; the relevant market relationship remains necessary, rather than generalized consequences of local conduct.
- **United States v. Hinds, April 6, 1998:** Applied for the independent work of the enacted predicate and the insufficiency of an owner's economic associations by themselves. Its property-use construction does not insert a use element into the ESA.
- **United States v. Bird, April 13, 1998:** Preserved for direct interference with an actual service market. Its conduct-and-purpose mechanism remains distinct from the two noneconomic-conduct routes addressed here.

## Law After Decision

Congress may include intrastate conduct that is neither itself interstate commerce nor independently shown to have substantial interstate effects when inclusion of the regulated class is a reasonably adapted and integral means of executing an otherwise valid federal regulation of interstate commerce. The Government identifies the valid interstate regulation, the covered intrastate class and a genuine functional relationship between them; courts determine those legal boundaries independently while respecting reasonable congressional judgments, without a least-restrictive-means test, species-by-species or crossing proof, a numerical threshold or a universal formal-findings requirement. A defendant may show that an application is outside the statutory class, that the interstate regime is not independently valid, or that the relationship is nominal or pretextual. Noneconomic intrastate conduct may also fall within federal Commerce authority through a materially supported substantial interstate externality that individual States cannot reasonably confine or internalize, an independent route that need not be satisfied where necessary inclusion establishes power. The enacted class comes first; economic features of these projects, national biodiversity value and comprehensiveness alone do not establish the required connection.

This decision gives fuller definition to the necessary-inclusion route Lopez reserved, develops the externality question Lopez left open and preserves Hale's regime holding. ESA section 9(a)(1)(B)'s taking prohibition, as applied to the listed fly within the Act's integrated system, is within Congress's Commerce and Necessary and Proper powers. Sweet Home's statutory injury, causation and permission requirements remain intact.

## Separate Writings

**Kennedy, concurring, joined by O'Connor.** They join both routes, the identification-and-fit rule and the affirmance in full. Lopez requires a meaningful distinction between commerce among the States and a general police power. Hale demonstrates one way a local act may be necessary to interstate regulation; American Life League, Robinson and Bird identify other concrete interstate relationships from a prohibition's actual operation. No one mechanism is an exhaustive taxonomy. The necessary-inclusion rule keeps that distinction because a court independently identifies a valid interstate regulation and the actual function the local prohibition performs within it; a declared national objective or a decorative interstate provision does not suffice. The Act's regulation of interstate trade in listed wildlife and its protection of the populations those provisions govern supply that function from the statute's own operation, not from a hospital label, construction expenditure or the national importance of biodiversity, so no further record about this fly is needed. A substantial burden that escapes ordinary territorial control may also have the interstate character the Constitution requires even though the source activity is local and noneconomic.

The territorial limit does real work. A problem's existence in many States, uneven State policy or the desirability of national uniformity does not alone show that States cannot reasonably contain or internalize the asserted effect. The Court must identify the effect that crosses their authority and test its fit to the regulated class. Because Part II-A establishes power here, that route is not applied to this fly. They do not demand categorical invalidation or an added commercial-transaction element.

**Breyer, concurring in Parts II, III and IV and the judgment, and dissenting from Part I, joined by Ginsburg.** They would regard the actual habitat-altering construction and conversion as a sufficient economic-application ground under Sweet Home, Hinds, Wickard, Perez and Hodel. They therefore do not join Part I's rejection of that ground. The construction is the injury-causing activity, not simply the owner's collateral purchases.

Part I nevertheless has five votes independently of theirs and supplies the Court's governing classification. Part II-A sustains these applications on that classification as well, and they join it, its application, the identification-and-fit rule in Part III and the affirmance in Part IV. The ESA's integrated regulation of interstate trade in listed wildlife and its protection of the populations that trade concerns form one practical regulatory system, and Congress may reasonably judge that protecting those populations against unauthorized depletion is a necessary part of it. Their judgment vote therefore rests on two sufficient grounds: their own economic-application view and the Court's necessary-inclusion rule.

The two routes recognize practical interstate relationships without categorically immunizing noneconomic local conduct. The Government must identify a genuine relationship, but that showing does not require de novo judicial environmental science, universal formal findings, a fixed quantity of interstate effects, clear-and-convincing proof or proof that every exemption would defeat the regime. Congress retains substantial latitude over regulatory necessity and practical enforcement. They join Part II-B's independent externality route as an available alternative that this case does not require. Their economic-application view remains a disagreement with Part I, not a competing holding.

**Stevens, concurring in Parts II-A, III and IV and the judgment, and dissenting from Part I, joined by Souter.** They join the Court's necessary-inclusion rule, its application to the fly, the identification-and-fit rule and the affirmance. They would also affirm on a further ground. The habitat-altering construction and conversion are themselves economic activities operating in actual land, construction, financing, labor and materials markets. Sweet Home identifies that modification as the injury-causing conduct, while Hinds directs attention to actual function rather than an owner's unrelated economic relationships. Their as-applied inquiry therefore identifies the activity section 9 actually reaches here; it does not insert a new element or declare every taking economic. Wickard and Perez permit class-wide aggregation after this economic connection, and Hodel confirms that environmental purpose does not disqualify regulation of economic activity.

The Court's enacted-class approach, in their view, treats the breadth of the taking prohibition as a reason to disregard the narrower economic application before the Court. Lopez's separate payment fact and Hinds's owner purchases differ from the physical development that itself causes the habitat injury here. They need no speculative biodiversity benefits. The necessary-inclusion ground independently sustains the judgment, because the taking prohibition protects the populations whose interstate exploitation the Act regulates and requires no showing that this fly is traded. Because Part II-A suffices, they do not reach the independent externality route, reserving it for a case that requires it.

**Scalia, concurring in Part I, and dissenting from Parts II-A, II-B, III and IV and the judgment, joined by Thomas.** They would reverse and remand for relief confined to the challenged applications. They join the Court's ruling that the taking class cannot be reconstructed from these projects' economic attributes. Lopez's no-insertion rule and the Hale line require a connection grounded in the enacted prohibition, not the actor's expenditures, motive or the national importance of the subject. They accept necessary inclusion of local conduct when a demonstrated relationship to a valid interstate regime exists, as in the machinegun cases, where excluding possession would have defeated the transfer-and-stock restriction. They find no such relationship here. Calling a statute comprehensive does not establish congressional power, and the record shows no trade in this fly and no evasion or material impairment of the Act's trade provisions from its local taking. A functional relationship identified at the level of the whole statute, without that showing, makes the national statute that includes a local prohibition its own constitutional justification.

They also reject the independent externality route. A substantial ecological burden that States cannot reasonably confine does not, for them, establish commerce or necessary protection of an existing commerce regime. The territorial-control inquiry risks substituting the need for national regulation for the enumerated power to undertake it. The Court's evidentiary requirements narrow that concern but do not resolve it. They would grant confined relief against the reviewed applications on the grounds offered. They obey Sweet Home's statutory construction, do not overrule the entering Lopez/Hale architecture, and propose no facial invalidation of the ESA, categorical intrastate-species immunity or blanket development permit.

## Procedure After Action

The D.C. Circuit's judgment is affirmed, and its judgment sustaining the challenged applications against the Commerce Clause objection remains in effect. Any independently pending statutory or permitting issues retain their ordinary procedural posture. Supreme Court merits review of this event is complete.
