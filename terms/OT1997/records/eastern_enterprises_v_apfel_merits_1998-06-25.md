## Event, reach and entering law

Argued March 4, 1998; decided June 25, 1998. On certiorari to the First Circuit, 110 F.3d 150, which affirmed summary judgment enforcing the Coal Act against Eastern. The constitutional retroactivity questions, not a renewed statutory-assignment claim, are before the Court. The district-court action requested declaratory and injunctive protection, not Treasury compensation. Eastern's actual assessment supplies injury; the federal respondent's abandoned jurisdictional objection does not confer jurisdiction.

Eastern directly mined coal through 1965 and signed the 1947–1964 wage agreements. The early funds reserved trustee revision of benefits; later agreements added express retiree and lifetime guarantees. Eastern retained subsidiary ownership until 1987 and received substantial dividends. Those facts do not prove either total economic withdrawal in 1965 or an enforceable lifetime promise. Over 1,000 retirees employed before 1966 were assigned under 26 U.S.C. §9706(a)(3). Annual premiums exceeded $5 million; competing cumulative estimates of about $51 million and $100 million are not an adjudicated award.

The June 25 frozen entering-law slice supplies Concrete Pipe's actual contextual takings rejection and rational pooled-allocation holding; Carlton's rational review of retrospective operation, without a one-year limit or reliance veto; Landgraf's priority for express temporal direction subject to constitutional review; and Phillips's ownership-only holding concerning actual interest. No historical version displaces those decisions. Unrecovered indemnity terms, actual corporate control and evidence of an implied lifetime promise are not resolved. These limits permit the bounded constitutional judgment.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate. Nine participate; five votes constitute a majority. Review proceeds through the supplied lawful appellate channel. There is no identified participation change between submission and decision.

## Controlling holdings

### 1. The judgment invalidates these assessments without a controlling affirmative rationale

**Judgment and operative consequence:** Eastern's actual tier-three assessments may not be enforced; the case returns for declaratory and injunctive relief confined to that application. The judgment establishes no general constitutional deadline for retroactive legislation and no rule invalidating all Coal Act obligations.

**Authority:** O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas support the contextual takings ground in the opinion announcing the judgment. Kennedy and Stone-Zsela support a different due-process ground. Those five support the judgment, but neither rationale is a logical subset of the other. No controlling rationale follows under Marks.

**Explanation of authority:** The three takings Justices assess the obligation's economic impact, expectations and character. The two due-process Justices instead examine whether the exceptional retrospective operation bears the required rational relationship to Eastern's prior undertaking. A takings conclusion does not entail their due-process conclusion, and the due-process conclusion does not entail a taking. The Court therefore preserves the distinct writings rather than creating a hybrid constitutional test. The party-specific judgment is binding; neither affirmative rationale is Court law.

### 2. This obligation against general assets does not take identified property

**Holding and operative rule:** A statutory obligation to pay money from unspecified general assets, without appropriating a particular fund, parcel, accrued interest or other identified property interest, does not constitute the asserted taking of property. Eastern's Coal Act obligation has that character and fails at this antecedent Takings Clause requirement, without resolving whether due process permits its retrospective operation.

**Authority:** Kennedy's property section and Breyer's dissent, joined by Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg, expressly adopt this same proposition. These five direct votes establish the negative Takings rule despite their disagreement about the judgment. Stone-Zsela supplies no additional join; no Marks combination is used.

**Controlling explanation:** The Act requires payment but selects no particular asset for transfer. Money can be property, yet that fact does not make every tax or financial liability an appropriation of an identified property interest. Phillips concerns ownership of actual interest generated by an identified principal fund and expressly leaves taking and compensation separate; it does not supply the missing property predicate here. Concrete Pipe rejected a pension takings challenge through contextual analysis. That rejection does not require a taking to be found whenever legislation imposes a monetary burden, and its actual holding remains intact. The severity and retrospective reach of a general obligation can present a due-process question; they do not themselves identify property taken. The five Justices do not decide entitlement to Treasury compensation for a taking that this proposition rejects.

**Precedent treatment:** Phillips's actual-interest ownership rule is distinguished; Concrete Pipe's contextual rejection of its particular pension claim is preserved; Marks supplies no common affirmative rationale for the present reversal.

## Published noncontrolling positions

**O'Connor's opinion announcing the judgment, joined by Scalia, Thomas.** These three Justices would treat the exceptional burden as a regulatory taking under the combined economic-impact, reasonable-expectations and governmental-character factors of Connolly and Concrete Pipe, which supply contextual review of pension obligations. Eastern faces more than $5 million annually for workers it directly employed before 1966, with competing lifetime estimates around $51 million and $100 million. Dollar size alone is insufficient. The decades of retrospective reach and mismatch between the early undertaking and much later lifetime guarantees together distinguish the earlier pooled-liability decisions. Subsidiary ownership and dividends are acknowledged; they do not establish an assumption of every later guarantee. The opinion creates neither a per se rule for monetary liabilities nor a fixed temporal limit.

The three also permit equitable relief in this particular scheme. Payments go to the privately operated Combined Fund; a corresponding Treasury reimbursement would not sensibly preserve Congress's intended allocation. That statutory structure, rather than the Government's abandoned objection, supports this remedy. The ground does not generally authorize injunctions whenever compensation is available for a lawful taking. O'Connor's concern about due process supplies no independent invalidity holding; Scalia and Thomas do not accept substantive economic due process as an alternative ground.

**Kennedy, joined by Stone-Zsela in the due-process section, concurring in the judgment.** Carlton requires rational justification for the retrospective operation itself. These two Justices find the particular combination of remoteness, magnitude and disproportion insufficiently connected to Eastern's pre-1966 undertaking. The original arrangements allowed alteration of benefits; materially later guarantees cannot be assigned backward without constitutional limit merely because the same workers once worked for Eastern. Retained ownership and dividends do not by themselves establish assumption of that later undertaking. Concrete Pipe concerned a different pooled relationship, and Carlton a prompt correction of a limited tax interval. The proposed rule is confined to this exceptional application and requires no fixed lookback deadline, conclusive reliance defense or adjudicated binding lifetime contract. Kennedy alone adds the negative property analysis; Stone-Zsela does not join it.

**Breyer, joined by Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg, dissenting.** These four reject the taking claim because no particular property interest is appropriated, but would sustain the obligation under due process. Turner Elkhorn, R. A. Gray, Concrete Pipe and Carlton permit rational adjustments of economic obligations, including pooled costs not precisely matched to an employer's own contributions. Congress could connect the cost of protecting these retirees to Eastern's actual employment and participation in the benefit undertaking, informed by the industry's benefit expectations and the later ownership relationship. A legally enforceable lifetime promise is not required for that rational legislative judgment; the dissent does not find one. The real retrospective severity does not make this relationship arbitrary, though mere former employment would not validate every imaginable future charge. A rough legislative allocation may be rational without being the fairest possible formula. These Justices would leave the enforcement judgment intact without deciding indemnity or unrelated assignments.
