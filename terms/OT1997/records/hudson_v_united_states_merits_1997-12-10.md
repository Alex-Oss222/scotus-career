## Holdings

**1. Civil intent remains rebuttable through examination of actual punitive operation.** Congress's civil designation of these banking monetary sanctions and the regulatory character of the occupational restriction establish the intended civil framework. That designation may be overcome by the clearest proof that the sanctions' purpose or effect is criminal; their supported design and operation must be assessed together, without treating deterrence, severity, occupational burden or overlap with crime as individually conclusive. Part I controls through Breyer, Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg.

Ward supplies the intent-and-effect inquiry. Ursery preserves both its demanding override requirement and the distinct field of personal civil penalties; Hendricks requires meaningful examination of actual operation. The monetary provisions expressly impose civil assessments through administrative procedures. Their good-faith and gravity considerations affect amount and do not establish a universal willfulness element. The applicable original monetary authorization cannot be replaced by the increased tiers enacted after the February 1989 notice. Mendoza-Martinez informs punitive-effect examination without requiring a new exhaustive checklist. Civil wording is relevant, but it cannot make actual punishment immune from constitutional review.

**2. These monetary assessments survive Halper's retained personal-penalty inquiry.** A personal civil penalty remains subject to Halper's requirement of a rational remedial relationship, including reasonable approximation; that rule is not overruled or replaced by a universal statutory-face inquiry. On the supported record, the actual $44,000 in total monetary assessments, compared with the District Court's finding of $72,000 in government costs pursuing these defendants, permits a remedial characterization and requires no further accounting. Part II controls through Breyer, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg.

The Government may recover legitimate pursuit costs without establishing that deterrence played no part in the regulatory response. The objective relation answers the present claim; the agency's failure initially to calculate its costs does not itself establish punishment. The initial $200,000 demand is not the obligation ultimately imposed. Nor are alleged banking losses substituted for proved government costs. This application creates no automatic monetary ratio, immunity for every penalty below an asserted cost figure, or universal compensation ceiling for other sanctions.

**3. The combined sanctions do not bar this prosecution.** The banking restriction, which permits participation with written regulatory authorization, protects positions of trust in a regulated industry. Considered with the actual financial assessments, its supported operation does not provide the clearest proof of criminal punishment; the subsequent prosecution therefore is not barred on the presented multiple-punishment theory. Part III controls through Breyer, Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg.

The restriction's substantial occupational effect does not erase its protective function, and the Court considers that effect together with the monetary burden. Kurth Ranch involved an extraordinary arrest-linked assessment on confiscated contraband whose combined operation made it a second punishment; these sanctions have no comparable defining operation. Hendricks concerned prisonlike custody and the actual failure of a civil treatment structure, facts absent here. Those distinctions preserve both precedents. A different banking scheme or materially different operation could produce a different classification. Because criminal punishment is not established, the Court does not decide Blockburger or retained Grady offense identity, attachment in punitive civil proceedings, the Excessive Fines Clause, or independent due-process objections.

## Separate Writings

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela concurs in Parts I and III and the judgment. He applies Ursery and Hendricks without alteration: the combined design and actual operation of these monetary and occupational measures do not establish the clearest proof of criminal punishment. Their accountability and protective functions differ from Kurth Ranch's extraordinary exaction and Hendricks's prisonlike application. He does not join the separate independent Halper-accounting analysis and does not reject Halper's continuing force. His ground leaves different operational facts, excessiveness and offense identity open.

Justice Scalia, joined by Justice Thomas, concurs in the judgment. Their account requires successive criminal prosecutions, and the genuinely civil banking proceedings do not supply the first such prosecution. They would reject Halper's freestanding civil multiple-punishment method, while allowing inquiry into whether a nominally civil proceeding is actually criminal. That theory does not command a majority and does not overrule Halper.
