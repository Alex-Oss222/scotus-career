## Decision kernel

The warrant’s validity is undisputed. A reliable informant reported a person believed to be Alan Shelby, a dangerous escaped prisoner, at Ramirez’s house; an agent saw a similar-looking man there. Officers also received information about weapons in the garage. Approximately 45 officers executed the warrant early in the morning. A loudspeaker announcement accompanied the breaking of one garage window and the pointing of a firearm through it to prevent access to the reported weapons. Ramirez believed burglars were entering, fired into the garage ceiling, and surrendered after police identification and returned fire. He waived Miranda rights and admitted firearm possession and felon status; a later warrant recovered the guns. Shelby was absent. The District Court found insufficient exigency for destructive entry under both the Constitution and §3109; the Ninth Circuit affirmed on its heightened standard. ### I. Property damage does not raise the announcement threshold

**Holding and operative rule:** Damage during warrant execution does not categorically require a greater exigency than particularized reasonable suspicion that ordinary announcement would be dangerous or futile, or would inhibit effective investigation, including through destruction of evidence. The facts supporting that exception must be known when the challenged force begins, and the means and extent of any destruction remain independently subject to Fourth Amendment reasonableness.

**Controlling explanation:** Wilson makes the common-law announcement protection part of constitutional reasonableness while preserving supported exceptions. Richards requires particular facts and rational inferences rather than an offense category, unsupported hunch or a no-knock label on a warrant. Breaking property does not change that announcement inquiry into a separate higher tier. The contrary rule correctly recognizes that destruction aggravates an intrusion, but addresses that concern in the wrong place. Whether the particular destructive act is reasonable remains a separate question; justified surprise does not authorize needless or disproportionate damage. The Court adopts no universal waiting period and revisits no disputed standard-of-review rule.

### II. The contemporaneous danger and limited damage justified this execution

**Holding and operative rule:** The reliable report of a dangerous escapee, corroborating observation and location-specific information about garage weapons supplied reasonable suspicion that ordinary announcement would create danger. Breaking one garage window to control access to those weapons, with a firearm directed through the opening for that purpose, was reasonable on this record; the justification permits neither gratuitous destruction nor any amount of damage merely because announcement may be excused.

**Controlling explanation:** Richards requires circumstances known before force. The informant’s report was partly corroborated by the agent’s observation, and the reported weapons gave the officers a concrete reason to prevent a rush into the garage. The limited window break addressed that identified danger. The number of officers outside did not eliminate a possible armed person’s access to weapons inside. Their security purpose is supported by those facts; purpose alone would not suffice. Ramirez’s later shot supplies no retrospective justification, and the later failure to find Shelby does not erase the information reasonably available beforehand. The Court decides the challenged entry and window damage, not every subsequent act. Gratuitous, punitive, disproportionate or objective-unrelated destruction receives no approval, and the remedy for materially different damage remains open.

### III. Section 3109 preserves a supported common-law danger exception

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 3109 ordinarily permits breaking to execute a search warrant after notice of authority and purpose and refused admission, and separately permits breaking when necessary to liberate the officer or someone assisting execution. Its codification of the common-law announcement rule also preserves a danger exception satisfied by particularized reasonable suspicion that following the ordinary process would expose officers or occupants to danger; the pre-entry escapee and garage-weapons information satisfies that exception here.

**Controlling explanation:** Simultaneous announcement and breaking do not establish prior refused admission. Nor does this record establish an officer or assistant needing liberation. The entry instead rests on the incorporated common-law danger qualification. Miller identifies the common-law source of the statutory protection but reserved a general exigency question. Sabbath treats opening an unlocked door as breaking and, in its discussion of common-law exceptions, supplies direct interpretive support without having held that every asserted danger suffices. Indeed, its unsupported danger claim failed. The Court now adopts the danger construction for the concrete supported circumstances here; it does not attribute that holding to those earlier reservations.

The statutory protection carries its traditional safety qualification, rather than automatically acquiring every constitutional exception. Wilson’s common-law account and Richards’s particularized standard guide this bounded construction. The express liberation alternative concerns freeing an impeded officer or helper and remains distinct. Because breaking already includes entry without physical damage, the statute creates no greater announcement threshold merely because property is damaged. No statutory rule for every possible futility, escape or evidence-destruction situation is necessary. Both asserted grounds for suppression fail, so the Court need not decide attenuation, independent source, inevitable discovery or the exclusionary consequence of an established violation.

## Event

United States v. Ramirez, No. 96-1469. Plenary merits decision, March 4, 1998; October Term 1997, chunk 5. Argued January 13, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit. The United States challenges the affirmance of an order suppressing firearms evidence under the Fourth Amendment and 18 U.S.C. §3109 because officers broke a garage window while executing a no-knock warrant. The questions concern the announcement standard when property is damaged, the reasonableness of that damage and the separate statutory ground.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate. No Justice is recused.

## Public Action

The judgment of the Ninth Circuit is reversed, and the case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with the Court’s opinion.

## Judgment & Remedy

Reversed and remanded, 9–0. Supporting: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. The suppression judgment cannot stand on a heightened announcement standard triggered by damage, or on the challenged entry and single-window damage under the standards applied here. This decision orders no acquittal, conviction or sentence and establishes no automatic exclusionary exception for later statements, a later warrant or recovered evidence.
