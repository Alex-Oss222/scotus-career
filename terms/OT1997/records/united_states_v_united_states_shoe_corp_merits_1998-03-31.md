## Controlling law of the decision

### I. The Court of International Trade has exclusive residual jurisdiction over this levy challenge

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1581(i)(4), read with §1581(i)(1) and 26 U.S.C. §4462(f)(2), gives the Court of International Trade exclusive jurisdiction over this challenge to administration and enforcement of the Harbor Maintenance Tax. The challenge attacks the legislatively fixed exaction rather than a protestable Customs decision under §1514, so §1581(a)'s protest-denial channel is not the necessary route.

**Authority:** Ginsburg's Opinion of the Court, joined by all eight other Justices: nine direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** Congress directs that the levy be treated as a customs duty for jurisdictional purposes. The statute provides revenue from imports as well as imposing other applications of the same levy; §1581(i)(4) covers its administration and enforcement. That direction supplies the appropriate exclusive forum before the constitutional question is reached.

Section 1581(a) concerns denial of a protest against a Customs decision of the kind governed by §1514. Here Customs passively collected a charge fixed by Congress; its form response did not supply a discretionary valuation, classification or other decision that determines this constitutional challenge. A document called a protest does not change the action's nature. Residual jurisdiction therefore does not excuse a missed protest deadline or circumvent an otherwise governing specific channel.

Section 1581(i)'s exclusion for specified antidumping or countervailing determinations reviewable by the Court of International Trade or the designated binational panels, and §1581(j)'s exclusion of actions under Tariff Act §305, do not encompass this levy claim. Section 4462(f)(1)'s regulation-qualified application of customs administration and its penalty rule, and (f)(3)'s exclusion of internal-revenue administration, remain distinct from (f)(2)'s jurisdictional direction. Calling the levy a customs duty for jurisdiction does not decide its constitutional character.

### II. The value-based charge on export loading is an export tax, not a compensatory service fee

**Holding and operative rule:** The Export Clause prohibits this Harbor Maintenance Tax as applied to exports because its ad valorem assessment does not fairly measure services or facilities used by the exporter. A genuine compensatory charge may use reasonable administrative approximations, but dedicated revenue and a general connection to useful harbor work do not convert a cargo-value export tax into a permissible fee.

**Authority:** The same nine Justices join the Court's merits holding and affirmance of the export-refund judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** IBM establishes that nondiscrimination cannot save a federal tax on exports. The Government accordingly defends this levy as a user fee. Pace permits compensation for actual governmental services and safeguards, allowing workable approximation rather than exact individualized billing. The distinction turns on the charge's relationship to the service, not its name.

The levy imposes 0.125 percent of cargo value at export loading. Its receipts enter a dedicated harbor-maintenance fund, but cargo value does not fairly measure the exporter's use of the relevant facilities or services. The fund's useful purpose supplies no missing relationship between the tax base and that use. The Court need not invent a benefit ratio or find each particular payment excessive to identify this structural defect.

Northwest Airlines concerns a different statutory airport-fee regime and does not replace the Export Clause with a general reasonableness or nondiscrimination test. IBM's service reservations likewise do not exempt every charge related to shipping. This decision concerns the uncontested goods in export transit and the export payments claimed. It reaches neither import or domestic applications, nor a differently measured service fee, nor general taxes on legally independent events. Reasonable inspection, storage, wharfage and facility-use charges are not prohibited by this holding.

## Event

United States v. United States Shoe Corp., No. 97-372. Argued March 4, 1998; decided March 31, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the Federal Circuit, reviewing 114 F.3d 1564. The questions are whether the Court of International Trade has jurisdiction over this Harbor Maintenance Tax refund action and whether the levy on exports is a tax prohibited by the Export Clause or a permissible service charge.

U.S. Shoe paid the levy on exports during April–June 1994, submitted a protest to Customs and received a form response defending the statutory assessment. Its complaint invoked §1581(a) or alternatively §1581(i). The Court of International Trade sustained jurisdiction under §1581(i) and entered an export-refund judgment; the Federal Circuit affirmed. The Government does not contest that the goods were in export transit.

## Law After Decision

The Court of International Trade is the exclusive forum for this administration-and-enforcement refund claim under §1581(i). The existing value-based Harbor Maintenance Tax cannot be applied to exports on the theory that dedicated harbor spending makes it a user fee. The decision preserves genuine service charges, nonexport applications and legally independent taxable transactions. No other case is automatically transferred or adjudicated.
