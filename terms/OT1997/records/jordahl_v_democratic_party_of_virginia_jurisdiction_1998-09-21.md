## Controlling holdings

### 1. Past restraints do not alone establish a present controversy under changed law

**Holding and operative rule:** A prospective challenge requires identified intended conduct facing a realistic current enforcement threat; ended injunctions under replaced or materially amended provisions do not alone supply it. Count One must be dismissed on this record, without requiring an actual prosecution or deciding whether any publication or disclosure rule is constitutional.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Part I, joined by all nine.

**Controlling explanation:** Pic-A-State Pennsylvania, Inc. v. Reno and Virginia v. American Booksellers Association permit review before enforcement where the threatened constraint is concrete. Arizonans for Official English v. Arizona requires a continuing personal controversy. Here all injunctions against these plaintiffs have ended, the statutes have materially changed, and no identified intended activity faces a supported current threat. The Party's failure to disavow every future suit does not fill that gap. Caldwell's authoritative exclusion of solely issue advocacy from the current Virginia provisions further weakens a threat resting on issue-only speech, without treating the express vote-against pamphlet as issue-only or deciding any past order. Amendment does not automatically moot every claim, and the importance of timely political speech cannot replace the absent current controversy. No rule limits proper defendants universally to the Commonwealth, bars every suit against a private enforcer, or disposes of accrued damages merely because prospective relief ended.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Pic-A-State applies the concrete-threat requirement and preserves review without prosecution.
- American Booksellers preserves credible pre-enforcement review; no such threat is established here.
- Arizonans applies the continuing-controversy requirement without extending its unavailable-damages holding to these private damages defendants.
- Caldwell supplies current Virginia construction only; it does not adjudicate the past injunctions.

### 2. These pleaded damages counts seek appellate reassessment of particular state judgments

**Holding and operative rule:** A federal district court lacks authority under Rooker and Feldman to adjudicate these counts insofar as their asserted federal wrong is the legal error of the particular state injunctions and their requested damages compensate those orders' direct effects. The rule is confined to that pleaded theory; it does not bar a claim merely because state litigation caused, preceded or bears on an independently actionable injury.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Part II, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; seven direct votes.

**Controlling explanation:** Rooker v. Fidelity Trust Co. and District of Columbia Court of Appeals v. Feldman reserve appellate reassessment of state judicial decisions to the proper appellate channel. The orders here applied law after an opportunity to object; prospective terms did not make those adjudications legislative acts. The published descriptions of the four counts demand a determination that the specific restraints were legally erroneous, without identifying a separate procurement wrong. Their later expiration or dissolution and the demand for damages against the procuring party do not change that pleaded wrong. Dennis v. Sparks preserves independent corrupt-procurement claims, and Johnson v. De Grandy preserves original federal enforcement without appellate review of a judgment against the claimant. Neither supplies a missing independent allegation here. The Court rejects a universal rule covering every consequence of a state order and a simple but-for causation test. It makes no finding that the Party's conduct was lawful and decides no preclusion, state action, immunity, limitations, personal responsibility or damages question.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Rooker applies the lower-federal appellate boundary to the actual wrong and relief asserted.
- Feldman applies the original-action versus individual-judgment distinction; prospective relief can remain judicial adjudication.
- Dennis preserves genuinely independent procurement claims; judicial immunity does not immunize private corrupt conspirators.
- De Grandy preserves its independent-enforcement holding and is distinguished from these pleaded attacks on judgments against the claimants.
- Lugar v. Edmondson Oil Co. remains the separate state-attribution inquiry; merely winning or filing private litigation does not establish it.

### 3. Heck supplies no universal civil-injunction jurisdiction bar

**Holding and operative rule:** Heck's favorable-invalidation requirement concerns present §1983 cognizability in its bounded custody setting, not lower-federal appellate jurisdiction. It supplies no universal rule requiring favorable termination of every civil injunction before an independent damages action may proceed.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Part III, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; seven direct votes on this explanation. The separate writings likewise reject a categorical bar but do not add joins to the Court's opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** Heck v. Humphrey coordinates §1983 with a real lawful opportunity to challenge an outstanding conviction in custody, while preserving independently cognizable injuries and its unresolved populations. Edwards v. Balisok applies that rule to invalidity-dependent disciplinary-credit claims. Spencer v. Kemna keeps Article III injury, statutory access and possible civil relief distinct and preserves the reservations for former prisoners and those without a realistic habeas opportunity. Those decisions neither convert cognizability into subject-matter jurisdiction nor authorize appellate reassessment of these orders. The lower court's assimilation of Heck to Rooker and Feldman is rejected. Affirmance rests on the actual claim-specific jurisdictional analysis, not a new civil-injunction favorable-termination rule.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Heck retains its bounded statutory prerequisite and independent-injury limits; it is not a Rooker-Feldman decision.
- Edwards retains the invalidity-dependent disciplinary-credit extension, without governing these civil orders.
- Spencer preserves the distinct inquiries and unresolved populations; no exemption or universal bar is created here.

### 4. The state claims lack the required supplemental-jurisdiction foundation

**Holding and operative rule:** Where the pleaded federal claims supply no original jurisdiction, §1367(a) supplies no supplemental jurisdiction over their related state claims. Counts Four, Five, Six and Nine therefore remain dismissed without prejudice, without an adjudication of tort merits or a discretionary retention decision under §1367(c)(3).

**Authority:** Kennedy's Part IV, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; seven direct votes.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 1367(a) requires a civil action within the district court's original jurisdiction before its supplemental grant operates. Count One lacks a current controversy and the pleaded damages counts demand appellate authority the district court does not possess. The district court's actual no-original-jurisdiction ground therefore supports dismissal. Section 1367(c)(3), which permits declining supplemental jurisdiction after claims within original jurisdiction have been dismissed, is not an interchangeable description of that defect. Nothing here requires a court possessing original jurisdiction to retain every related state claim. State tort elements, timeliness and recovery remain for a competent forum.

**Precedent treatment:** The Court corrects the Fourth Circuit's discretionary description while preserving the district court's §1367(a) ground; no earlier Supreme Court decision is displaced.

## Published noncontrolling and procedural continuity

**Stevens, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** Stevens joins the prospective dismissal but would remand all four damages counts. His Feldman dissent distinguishes an original collateral constitutional claim from an appeal, separately from its objection to characterizing the licensing proceeding as judicial. The injunctions have ended, and damages are sought from their private procurer rather than an order undoing operative state relief. De Grandy and Heck preserve original jurisdiction, preclusion and cognizability as distinct questions; Dennis shows that a judicial source of injury does not alone settle jurisdiction. The absence of an identified independent fraud and the need to assess legal error are substantial contrary considerations, but do not turn these original damages actions into appeals for him. He finds no fraud, state action, exoneration or liability and would reconsider, not require retention of, the state claims.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** Stone-Zsela agrees that changed statutes and the absent concrete future threat defeat Count One. He would vacate the categorical treatment of damages and remand to separate independent coercive-process injuries from claims requiring rejection of the state judgments. Voter guides, including nonendorsing comparisons, concern protected political expression; expiration alone cannot make an interruption harmless. McIntyre's protection against unjustified identification burdens informs that concern without prohibiting all campaign-finance reporting, narrowly tailored fraud rules or valid disclosure for materially different organizational conduct. The express vote-against 1989 pamphlet is not recast as issue-only, and Caldwell does not decide its past restraint.

Rooker and Feldman still forbid district-court appellate review. A properly pleaded independent misuse claim may proceed only if it does not depend on declaring the state judgment void and satisfies Lugar's separate state-action requirement, protected injury and a valid cause of action. Merely filing an adverse suit is not enough. Stone-Zsela would require examination of the actual allegations, not find those elements now. The right to petition remains; an unsuccessful suit or dissolved order does not by itself establish abuse of process, malicious prosecution or §1983 liability. He would reconsider supplemental jurisdiction after sorting the federal claims, award no damages now and leave the ended orders intact. His proposed remand does not command a majority.

The judgment is affirmed on the stated jurisdictional grounds. The state claims remain dismissed without prejudice. No federal merits trial, damages assessment, renewed injunction or Supreme Court-ordered rewriting of the pleadings follows; the Court decides no unpresented claim or future controversy.

## Event

Jordahl v. Democratic Party of Virginia, No. 97-859. Signed jurisdictional decision after plenary submission, September 21, 1998; October Term 1997, chunk 13. Review under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) of the Fourth Circuit's July 28, 1997 judgment, 122 F.3d 192, affirming dismissal of federal and related state claims. No exact oral-argument date in this Court is established.

The questions are whether a present controversy supports prospective relief against use of Virginia injunctions to enforce election-disclosure laws, whether these federal damages counts seek prohibited appellate reassessment of state judgments, and whether supplemental jurisdiction supports the state counts. The Court does not reach the alleged First Amendment violations.

The three injunction histories differ. The November 1989 Richmond order expired after the election or compliance; that action was dismissed for failure to prosecute in October 1994. The 1989 Fairfax order became permanent after a hearing and was dissolved as to Virginia Leadership Council and Jordahl by stipulation on September 30, 1994, surviving as to others until March 21, 1996. The Virginia Supreme Court dissolved the 1993 Fairfax order against LaRock and others on November 1, 1993, without an opinion. Dissolution establishes no constitutional rationale or tort liability. One 1989 pamphlet expressly urged a vote against Linda Toddy Puller if readers disagreed with her positions; other guides were described as nonendorsing.

Count One seeks prospective restraint of the Party's use of Virginia Code §8.01-620 injunctions to enforce §§24.2-908, -910 and -1014. The old §24.1-277 authorship provision, its 1993 replacement and the July 1, 1996 amendments differ. Virginia Society for Human Life v. Caldwell, decided June 5, 1998, construes the current election-influence language in §§24.2-901, -910 and -1014 as express advocacy of election or defeat of an identified candidate, excluding solely issue advocacy; §901's committee definition carries that construction into §908. Section 908 requires an organization statement from a political committee anticipating more than $200 in annual contributions or expenditures. Section 910(B)'s independent-expenditure reports and §1014's authorship requirement are separate. Section 901(B)'s treatment of qualifying §501(c)(3) organizations does not make §501(c)(4) issue-only groups covered. Caldwell does not characterize every plaintiff's speech or decide the earlier injunctions.

Counts Two and Three seek damages for the 1989 orders; Seven and Eight concern the 1993 order. The published courts characterize them as requiring the injunctions to be declared erroneously issued; no independent fraud, deception or corrupt judicial conspiracy is identified. Hearings afforded opportunities to object, and the 1993 order noted constitutional objections. State Counts Four and Five allege abuse of process, Six malicious prosecution and Nine trespass to chattels.

## Holdings

### 1. Past restraints do not alone establish a present controversy under changed law

**Holding and operative rule:** A prospective challenge requires identified intended conduct facing a realistic current enforcement threat; ended injunctions under replaced or materially amended provisions do not alone supply it. Count One must be dismissed on this record, without requiring an actual prosecution or deciding whether any publication or disclosure rule is constitutional.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Part I, joined by all nine.

**Controlling explanation:** Pic-A-State Pennsylvania, Inc. v. Reno and Virginia v. American Booksellers Association permit review before enforcement where the threatened constraint is concrete. Arizonans for Official English v. Arizona requires a continuing personal controversy. Here all injunctions against these plaintiffs have ended, the statutes have materially changed, and no identified intended activity faces a supported current threat. The Party's failure to disavow every future suit does not fill that gap. Caldwell's authoritative exclusion of solely issue advocacy from the current Virginia provisions further weakens a threat resting on issue-only speech, without treating the express vote-against pamphlet as issue-only or deciding any past order. Amendment does not automatically moot every claim, and the importance of timely political speech cannot replace the absent current controversy. No rule limits proper defendants universally to the Commonwealth, bars every suit against a private enforcer, or disposes of accrued damages merely because prospective relief ended.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Pic-A-State applies the concrete-threat requirement and preserves review without prosecution.
- American Booksellers preserves credible pre-enforcement review; no such threat is established here.
- Arizonans applies the continuing-controversy requirement without extending its unavailable-damages holding to these private damages defendants.
- Caldwell supplies current Virginia construction only; it does not adjudicate the past injunctions.

### 2. These pleaded damages counts seek appellate reassessment of particular state judgments

**Holding and operative rule:** A federal district court lacks authority under Rooker and Feldman to adjudicate these counts insofar as their asserted federal wrong is the legal error of the particular state injunctions and their requested damages compensate those orders' direct effects. The rule is confined to that pleaded theory; it does not bar a claim merely because state litigation caused, preceded or bears on an independently actionable injury.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Part II, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; seven direct votes.

**Controlling explanation:** Rooker v. Fidelity Trust Co. and District of Columbia Court of Appeals v. Feldman reserve appellate reassessment of state judicial decisions to the proper appellate channel. The orders here applied law after an opportunity to object; prospective terms did not make those adjudications legislative acts. The published descriptions of the four counts demand a determination that the specific restraints were legally erroneous, without identifying a separate procurement wrong. Their later expiration or dissolution and the demand for damages against the procuring party do not change that pleaded wrong. Dennis v. Sparks preserves independent corrupt-procurement claims, and Johnson v. De Grandy preserves original federal enforcement without appellate review of a judgment against the claimant. Neither supplies a missing independent allegation here. The Court rejects a universal rule covering every consequence of a state order and a simple but-for causation test. It makes no finding that the Party's conduct was lawful and decides no preclusion, state action, immunity, limitations, personal responsibility or damages question.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Rooker applies the lower-federal appellate boundary to the actual wrong and relief asserted.
- Feldman applies the original-action versus individual-judgment distinction; prospective relief can remain judicial adjudication.
- Dennis preserves genuinely independent procurement claims; judicial immunity does not immunize private corrupt conspirators.
- De Grandy preserves its independent-enforcement holding and is distinguished from these pleaded attacks on judgments against the claimants.
- Lugar v. Edmondson Oil Co. remains the separate state-attribution inquiry; merely winning or filing private litigation does not establish it.

### 3. Heck supplies no universal civil-injunction jurisdiction bar

**Holding and operative rule:** Heck's favorable-invalidation requirement concerns present §1983 cognizability in its bounded custody setting, not lower-federal appellate jurisdiction. It supplies no universal rule requiring favorable termination of every civil injunction before an independent damages action may proceed.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Part III, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; seven direct votes on this explanation. The separate writings likewise reject a categorical bar but do not add joins to the Court's opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** Heck v. Humphrey coordinates §1983 with a real lawful opportunity to challenge an outstanding conviction in custody, while preserving independently cognizable injuries and its unresolved populations. Edwards v. Balisok applies that rule to invalidity-dependent disciplinary-credit claims. Spencer v. Kemna keeps Article III injury, statutory access and possible civil relief distinct and preserves the reservations for former prisoners and those without a realistic habeas opportunity. Those decisions neither convert cognizability into subject-matter jurisdiction nor authorize appellate reassessment of these orders. The lower court's assimilation of Heck to Rooker and Feldman is rejected. Affirmance rests on the actual claim-specific jurisdictional analysis, not a new civil-injunction favorable-termination rule.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Heck retains its bounded statutory prerequisite and independent-injury limits; it is not a Rooker-Feldman decision.
- Edwards retains the invalidity-dependent disciplinary-credit extension, without governing these civil orders.
- Spencer preserves the distinct inquiries and unresolved populations; no exemption or universal bar is created here.

### 4. The state claims lack the required supplemental-jurisdiction foundation

**Holding and operative rule:** Where the pleaded federal claims supply no original jurisdiction, §1367(a) supplies no supplemental jurisdiction over their related state claims. Counts Four, Five, Six and Nine therefore remain dismissed without prejudice, without an adjudication of tort merits or a discretionary retention decision under §1367(c)(3).

**Authority:** Kennedy's Part IV, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; seven direct votes.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 1367(a) requires a civil action within the district court's original jurisdiction before its supplemental grant operates. Count One lacks a current controversy and the pleaded damages counts demand appellate authority the district court does not possess. The district court's actual no-original-jurisdiction ground therefore supports dismissal. Section 1367(c)(3), which permits declining supplemental jurisdiction after claims within original jurisdiction have been dismissed, is not an interchangeable description of that defect. Nothing here requires a court possessing original jurisdiction to retain every related state claim. State tort elements, timeliness and recovery remain for a competent forum.

**Precedent treatment:** The Court corrects the Fourth Circuit's discretionary description while preserving the district court's §1367(a) ground; no earlier Supreme Court decision is displaced.

## Law After Decision

Prospective jurisdiction depends on a concrete present threat under current law. Damages claims do not become moot merely because an injunction ends, but these pleaded claims cannot obtain lower-federal appellate reassessment of the state orders. Independent procurement claims remain possible subject to ordinary jurisdiction, pleading, attribution and merits requirements. With no original federal jurisdiction here, the related state claims are dismissed without prejudice under §1367(a). No new speech, tort-liability, limitations or favorable-termination rule results.

## Separate Writings

**Stevens, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** Stevens joins the prospective dismissal but would remand all four damages counts. His Feldman dissent distinguishes an original collateral constitutional claim from an appeal, separately from its objection to characterizing the licensing proceeding as judicial. The injunctions have ended, and damages are sought from their private procurer rather than an order undoing operative state relief. De Grandy and Heck preserve original jurisdiction, preclusion and cognizability as distinct questions; Dennis shows that a judicial source of injury does not alone settle jurisdiction. The absence of an identified independent fraud and the need to assess legal error are substantial contrary considerations, but do not turn these original damages actions into appeals for him. He finds no fraud, state action, exoneration or liability and would reconsider, not require retention of, the state claims.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** Stone-Zsela agrees that changed statutes and the absent concrete future threat defeat Count One. He would vacate the categorical treatment of damages and remand to separate independent coercive-process injuries from claims requiring rejection of the state judgments. Voter guides, including nonendorsing comparisons, concern protected political expression; expiration alone cannot make an interruption harmless. McIntyre's protection against unjustified identification burdens informs that concern without prohibiting all campaign-finance reporting, narrowly tailored fraud rules or valid disclosure for materially different organizational conduct. The express vote-against 1989 pamphlet is not recast as issue-only, and Caldwell does not decide its past restraint.

Rooker and Feldman still forbid district-court appellate review. A properly pleaded independent misuse claim may proceed only if it does not depend on declaring the state judgment void and satisfies Lugar's separate state-action requirement, protected injury and a valid cause of action. Merely filing an adverse suit is not enough. Stone-Zsela would require examination of the actual allegations, not find those elements now. The right to petition remains; an unsuccessful suit or dissolved order does not by itself establish abuse of process, malicious prosecution or §1983 liability. He would reconsider supplemental jurisdiction after sorting the federal claims, award no damages now and leave the ended orders intact. His proposed remand does not command a majority.
