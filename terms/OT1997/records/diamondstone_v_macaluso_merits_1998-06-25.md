On these circumstances, prior nonproduction, intervening time, changed vehicles, valid inspection stickers and absence of fresh evidence did not supply reasonable suspicion for stops 3–10 or 12; anticipated refusal after a renewed stop cannot itself authorize making it.

The State's strongest argument is that repeated nonproduction suggests continued noncompliance. Here that inference lacked a sufficient current connection to actual noncoverage, given the elapsed time, changing inspected cars and lack of new evidence. An unlawful stop does not itself establish the absence of every prosecution ground, institution of proceedings, malice, favorable termination or damages.

**Authority:** The eight Associates join the consequential-remedy rationale. Stone-Zsela supports this component's disposition only.

**Controlling explanation:** The rejected instruction could have influenced the jury's assessment of the insurance prosecutions, so the existing retrial remedy remains appropriate. The other Vermont elements retain independent force. This conclusion creates no federal malicious-prosecution rule and reopens neither the force verdict nor the zero-damages defamation determination. Ricci's distinct station-custody holding does not expand this review to adjudicate the later restraint anew. The specific state retrial is not an automatic federal trial or damages award.

**Precedent treatment:** Ricci preserves the distinction between initial authority and additional custody without deciding an unreviewed custody claim here; no new federal tort doctrine is adopted.

The nine identified initial stops are unlawful under the existing objective seizure rule. Harlow/Anderson remains the governing defense, applied separately by stop and official. Judicial notice can matter without preclusion or automatic liability; superior encouragement supplies no independent authority. The civil-insurance privilege rejection supplies no stop power. ## Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and in the judgment.** Applicable judicial rulings rejecting the same premise for materially similar stops can remove any automatic claim of reasonable mistake. An official cannot simply invoke general uncertainty about traffic enforcement after receiving that correction, and supervisory encouragement cannot authorize unconstitutional action. His agreement extends to the identified unlawful initial stops, genuinely new-violation reservation and individual remand limits, without adopting every rationale in the Court's opinion.

He would replace the retained immunity framework with a contextual defense under which the official bears the justificatory burden. Pertinent law, actual judicial correction, each official's information and a genuine reasonable misunderstanding must be examined, rather than supplying a duplicative excuse for deliberate violations. This is a proposed change; the Court retains Harlow/Anderson. He specifies no new quantum of proof, exhaustive factor list or compulsory procedure. A fresh violation or genuinely different factual premise may justify a later stop, and individual knowledge cannot be presumed from rank. Even failure of the defense proves neither every constitutional element nor causation, supervisory liability, damages or a right to trial. His agreement with the privilege disposition and state retrial adds no separate ground on those components.
