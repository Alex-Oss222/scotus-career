## Event, posture and authority

Phillips v. Washington Legal Foundation, No. 96-1578. Argued January 13, 1998; decided June 15, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the Fifth Circuit. The question is whether interest actually generated by client funds in a mandatory IOLTA account is the private property of the principal’s owner for Takings Clause purposes.

Texas requires lawyers to place client funds in IOLTA when they cannot reasonably be expected to earn interest for the client, or when expected interest would not cover the costs of establishing and maintaining the account, service charges, accounting and tax reporting. The interest goes to the Texas Equal Access to Justice Foundation to finance legal services for persons with low incomes. The District Court granted summary judgment on the ground that clients had no property interest in the proceeds. The Fifth Circuit reversed that determination and left the remaining takings questions for further proceedings.

Nine qualified Justices participate at both milestones; the quorum is satisfied and five votes control each judgment and proposition. No nonparticipation is established. This event uses the law effective before June 15, 1998. Other uncoordinated decisions of that date, including those processed earlier for assignment, are not substantive entering authority.

## Entering law and reach

## Controlling law

### Actually generated interest follows the client’s principal

**Holding and operative rule:** Interest actually generated by client funds in an IOLTA account is the private property of the principal’s owner unless a valid background property rule assigns it elsewhere. The inability to earn a positive net return outside IOLTA does not itself eliminate ownership of the interest once generated; this holding determines title, not whether a taking occurred or compensation is owed.

**Authority:** Scalia’s Opinion of the Court is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Kennedy and Thomas. These five directly adopt the ownership rule, its explanation, limits and affirmance.

**Controlling explanation:** Texas recognizes the clients’ ownership of their principal. Its traditional rule assigns the interest earned on that principal to the same owner. Roth directs attention to those existing rules and understandings; Webb’s forbids turning the resulting private earnings into public property by a new governmental declaration. Lucas preserves the distinction between a genuine antecedent limit on title and a newly imposed public burden. Its separate categorical land-use rule does not decide this case.

The pooled client funds generated the interest. Pooling and the public purpose of the proceeds do not sever that relationship. The absence of a profitable alternative return may matter greatly to the later taking and compensation questions, but value and ownership are not identical. An owner’s interests in possession, control and disposition are not extinguished merely because separately earning the increment would cost more than it produces.

Federal permission for NOW accounts and tax nonattribution do not independently assign title under the background property rule. Individuals are a permitted NOW category; for-profit corporations and partnerships face a distinct restriction, and the Federal Reserve’s charitable-interest interpretation addresses banking eligibility. The Court decides neither the validity of that interpretation nor banking legality by resolving ownership. The IRS’s treatment where clients control neither placement nor recipient likewise does not resolve title.

The Court does not decide whether Texas has taken the interest, whether compensation exceeds zero, public use, standing, First Amendment claims or entitlement to declaratory, injunctive or monetary relief. Nor does it prohibit reasonable charges for actual services or characterize this diversion as such a fee. No finding establishes that the particular funds could earn net client interest, individually or through feasible attorney pooling. Anticipated profits and improperly placed funds raise different questions.

## Continuity and next stage

**Justice Souter, dissenting, joined by Justices Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer.** The dissent would vacate the limited judgment and remand for the lower courts to consider property, taking and compensation together. The legal and economic arrangement matters to whether an isolated title declaration has any consequence under the Takings Clause. Those matters could defeat a takings claim, but the dissent would have the lower courts decide them rather than declare no taking or zero compensation here.

The dissent accepts that property identification and valuation are distinct, but regards their isolated treatment in this case as liable to distort the full constitutional inquiry. Webb’s involved an independently productive fund; its protection against public relabeling does not settle ownership of this otherwise unavailable return. Lucas protects established entitlements without deciding how this claimed benefit must be identified.

This argument does not assume that every individual is legally barred from a NOW account. Funds capable of earning net client interest belong outside IOLTA, and diversion of such interest or principal presents a different question. The dissent fixes no compensation amount, accepts no public-purpose exemption from the Takings Clause and does not direct final judgment against every claim.

The Fifth Circuit’s judgment and existing remand remain in place. Further proceedings must respect client ownership of the generated interest and decide the remaining live questions under their own governing law. No injunction, damages award, particular compensation measure or ultimate constitutional disposition is ordered.

### Issue and two supported paths

The only merits question resolved here is ownership of interest actually generated by client principal in Texas IOLTA accounts. Both paths preserve principal ownership and the program's eligibility limit: client funds belong outside IOLTA when they can reasonably yield net interest for the client, including through feasible attorney pooling. No finding establishes that the particular respondent funds can do so. The NOW statute's eligible beneficial-interest categories, the Federal Reserve's charitable-exclusive-interest interpretation, and the IRS's nonattribution rule are distinct from one another and from the Texas ownership inquiry. Individuals are an eligible NOW category in their own right; the federal restriction on for-profit corporations and partnerships is not imputed to every client. The common-law Texas interest principle is supported by Webb's published discussion of Sellers v. Harris County, 483 S.W.2d 242; Sellers itself was not independently read here.

**Path P — affirm the property determination and remand the remaining questions.** Actually generated interest follows the private principal. The inability to realize a profitable individual return does not by itself extinguish ownership of interest once generated. Webb's rejects government conversion of private earnings merely by asserting that the legal arrangement created them; Lucas preserves the difference between an antecedent title limit and a new public assignment. The net-yield objection may affect a taking, economic loss and compensation, but does not alone transfer title. The federal permission to use NOW accounts and the tax treatment do not themselves adjudicate constitutional ownership. This path does not decide whether a taking occurred or any compensation is owed.

**Path N — reverse the property determination for funds properly subject to this program.** The asserted interest entitlement must be defined through the whole legal and economic arrangement, not an abstract incident detached from its conditions. The no-net-return limit, even after feasible pooling, leaves no preexisting economically realizable client benefit; for ineligible corporate funds, the charitable entitlement additionally permits a return under the stated federal arrangement. On this account the program has not appropriated a preexisting beneficial entitlement to the resulting charitable return. Webb's involved an independently valuable interpleader fund and expressly bounded its decision; it does not make every gross return a client entitlement regardless of the conditions producing it. Lucas governs destruction of an existing entitlement, not identification of this one. Improper placement of funds able to earn net client interest would be a different case.

The strongest objection to P is that recognizing a client beneficial interest appears inconsistent with the federal arrangement permitting charitable interest for otherwise ineligible funds and ignores the no-net-yield premise. P answers by separating statutory banking eligibility and economic value from the ownership question; it does not declare the arrangement unlawful. The strongest objection to N is that a government could eliminate property simply by assigning its fruits to someone else, and that an individual's unprofitable interest may still be owned. N therefore depends on treating the available beneficial entitlement as part of property identification, rather than using economic value solely to measure compensation. A new public-purpose declaration alone is insufficient. Neither path converts the worthy legal-services use into a dispositive constitutional exemption.

## Separate Writings

**Justice Souter, dissenting, joined by Justices Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer.** The dissent would vacate the limited judgment and remand for the lower courts to consider property, taking and compensation together. The legal and economic arrangement matters to whether an isolated title declaration has any consequence under the Takings Clause. Roth’s reference to existing entitlements does not require examining one incident of ownership apart from the rules making that interest available. Penn Central directs attention to economic impact, reasonable investment-backed expectations and the character of governmental action, while compensation ordinarily concerns the owner’s loss rather than the recipient’s gain. Those matters could defeat a takings claim, but the dissent would have the lower courts decide them rather than declare no taking or zero compensation here.

The dissent accepts that property identification and valuation are distinct, but regards their isolated treatment in this case as liable to distort the full constitutional inquiry. It does not approve confiscation of the principal, which unquestionably belongs to the client. A claim concerning compulsory use of that principal remains available for lawful consideration even under the dissenters’ alternative rejection of the asserted interest entitlement. The four also join Breyer’s alternative analysis if the Court decides ownership alone.

**Justice Breyer, separately dissenting, joined by Justices Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg.** He joins Souter’s proposed integrated remand. If ownership is reached in isolation, he would reject the claimed entitlement for funds properly placed under the no-net-return program rule. The operative arrangement makes a charitable return possible where a client otherwise has no legally and economically realizable net benefit. Webb’s involved an independently productive fund; its protection against public relabeling does not settle ownership of this otherwise unavailable return. Lucas protects established entitlements without deciding how this claimed benefit must be identified.

This argument does not assume that every individual is legally barred from a NOW account. Individual eligibility, restrictions on corporate and partnership funds, administrative and reporting costs, and the distinction between gross and net yield must remain separate. The Federal Reserve interpretation is part of the banking setting, not a holding that its validity has been adjudicated. The reported Texas concession allows assessment of net client earnings through feasible attorney pooling; it establishes no positive alternative yield for these particular funds. Funds capable of earning net client interest belong outside IOLTA, and diversion of such interest or principal presents a different question. The dissent fixes no compensation amount, accepts no public-purpose exemption from the Takings Clause and does not direct final judgment against every claim.

## Procedure After Action

The Fifth Circuit’s judgment and existing remand remain in place. Further proceedings must respect client ownership of the generated interest and decide the remaining live questions under their own governing law. No injunction, damages award, particular compensation measure or ultimate constitutional disposition is ordered.
