## Event, posture and authority

Pennsylvania Department of Corrections v. Yeskey, No. 97-634. Argued April 28, 1998; decided June 15, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the Third Circuit. The question is whether Title II of the Americans with Disabilities Act covers state prisons and their programs.

Yeskey, sentenced to 18 to 36 months, was recommended for a motivational boot camp whose successful completion could permit parole after six months. He alleges that admission was refused because of his history of hypertension. The District Court dismissed on the ground that the ADA did not apply to state inmates; the Third Circuit reversed that categorical ruling.

Nine qualified Justices participate at both milestones; the quorum is satisfied and five votes control each judgment and proposition. No nonparticipation is established. ## Controlling law

### Title II covers state prisons and their programs

**Holding and operative rule:** A state correctional department is a public entity under 42 U.S.C. §12131, and its prison services, programs and activities fall within §12132. Custody supplies no categorical exception: a qualified individual may not, by reason of disability, be excluded from participation, denied the benefits of those services, programs or activities, or otherwise subjected to discrimination by the public entity.

**Authority:** O’Connor’s Opinion of the Court is joined in full by all eight other Justices. The complete statutory rule and its limits command nine direct votes.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 12131 includes state and local governments and their departments, agencies, special-purpose districts and other instrumentalities. A state correctional department falls within that definition. A prison’s boot-camp opportunity is a program offering a benefit; its custodial setting does not make that description inapplicable. Section 12132 contains no exception for involuntary custody.

Even assuming Gregory’s clear-statement principle applies, the enacted definition and program language supply the required clarity. Gregory addressed an ambiguous express employee exception, not a rule that Congress must list each covered institution by name. Neither the importance of prison administration nor an assertion that Congress failed to anticipate this application authorizes adding an exception to comprehensive text.

Coverage does not establish liability. A qualified individual must meet essential eligibility requirements, with or without reasonable modifications to rules, policies or practices, removal of architectural, communication or transportation barriers, or auxiliary aids and services. The Court decides neither that Yeskey has a statutory disability nor that he meets those requirements. It requires no particular modification or fundamental alteration and displaces no legitimate security or direct-threat defense. The question whether a cause of action or particular relief is available, the constitutional scope of congressional power, abrogation, sovereign immunity and damages are not decided. Those distinct questions cannot be resolved by treating the institution as outside the statutory definition.

### Common disposition, rule and limits

Affirm the Third Circuit's rejection of a categorical prison exclusion and remand for remaining proceedings. Title II includes state prisons within the statutory public-entity definition. Its protection of a qualified individual against disability-based exclusion from services, programs or activities, denial of their benefits, or other discrimination by such an entity is not limited to voluntary custody. A prison-operated boot-camp opportunity potentially shortening confinement is within that coverage; the statutory merits predicates remain to be established.

The definition includes State and local governments and their departments, agencies, special-purpose districts and other instrumentalities. A qualified individual must meet essential eligibility requirements with or without the modifications, barrier removal or auxiliary assistance the statute identifies. The Court does not hold that every prisoner is disabled or qualified, that hypertension establishes disability in every case, or that every requested modification is reasonable. Security defenses and actual program eligibility remain distinct from a categorical exclusion of the institution.

Even assuming Gregory's clear-statement principle applies, the enacted public-entity and program language supplies the required clarity. Gregory involved a materially ambiguous express employee exception; it does not demand that Congress list every covered state institution. Ordinary meaning and statutory structure are sufficient. No supposed failure of Congress specifically to anticipate prisons overrides that text.
