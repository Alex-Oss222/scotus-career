## Controlling holdings

### Effective consideration of mitigation under this complete charge

**Holding and operative rule:** The Eighth and Fourteenth Amendments require an effective sentencing route for the material independent culpability significance of supported mitigation; a reasonable likelihood that the charge, evidence and trial as a whole prevent that use requires correction. This charge supplies that route, so the Constitution did not require the requested additional general mitigation definition or four factor-specific instructions.

**Authority:** Part I of O'Connor's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, controls with six direct votes. Scalia joins the application of governing law while separately reserving his disagreement with its constitutional foundation.

**Controlling explanation:** Lockett and Eddings protect meaningful mitigating effect, and Graham and Johnson require a lawful means of expressing independent reduced culpability even when aggravating facts are true. Boyde asks whether the complete charge and trial create a reasonable likelihood of prohibited operation, not whether a restrictive reading is merely possible. This jury was first told to choose death or life. The Commonwealth then had to prove statutory vileness beyond a reasonable doubt. Even after that finding, paragraph three permitted death rather than requiring it and directed life if all the evidence made death unjustified. Failure to prove vileness separately required life; death also required unanimous agreement. The latter directions did not convert the preceding discretionary choice into a mandatory death sentence. Background and emotional-disturbance evidence could therefore support life without a false answer about an aggravating fact.

The contrary reading treats the disjunctive wording and later failure-of-aggravation paragraph as confining life to absence of vileness. Read together with the initial choice and paragraph three's express post-aggravator alternative, they do not create the required reasonable likelihood. Two days of testimony, both sides' explanation of mitigation, the prosecutor's acknowledgment that vileness did not require death, and the verdict form's reference to mitigation confirm the judicially authorized route. Counsel and admitted evidence do not cure an otherwise restrictive instruction. Delo permits an evidentiary predicate for a particular-factor instruction; Tuilaepa permits individualized selection without fixed labels or weights. Neither makes admissibility alone sufficient. The Court finds no instruction error, no actual juror mental state, and no evidentiary predicate for every requested factor. It prescribes no universal wording or favorable weight and does not decide an unpresented mitigation-unanimity challenge.

### The distinct statutory-factor due-process contention

**Holding and operative rule:** Virginia's identified mitigation factors and provision permitting relevant mitigation evidence do not by themselves create a federal due-process entitlement to each requested instructional formulation. No independent state-law entitlement to specified wording or separately preserved arbitrary refusal to honor such an entitlement is established here.

**Authority:** Part II of O'Connor's opinion, joined by Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, controls with eight direct votes. Stone-Zsela agrees in rejection of this distinct claim only.

**Controlling explanation:** The statutory allowance of offense circumstances, history, background and other mitigation preserves a broad evidentiary field; its list is not exclusive. That permission does not require the judge to give the requested formulations for prior criminal history, emotional disturbance, impaired capacity and age. The constitutional effective-route inquiry remains separate and is answered in Part I. The Court neither determines every factor's factual support nor decides a different claim that Virginia arbitrarily disregarded an established instruction entitlement.

## Separate Writings

Breyer, joined by Stevens and Ginsburg, agrees with Part II's narrow statutory conclusion but finds a reasonable likelihood that the charge prevented independent mitigating use. Its disjunctive wording, followed by the direction to impose life if vileness was not proved, could lead ordinary jurors to understand life as dependent on failure of aggravation. The court supplied no explanation clarifying how background and emotional disturbance could independently justify life. Lockett, Penry, Graham and Johnson require that route; Boyde requires assessing the charge as jurors would understand it. Taylor and Carter reinforce that advocacy does not substitute for necessary judicial guidance. Two days of evidence and the verdict form's mitigation reference do not establish a cure. They would vacate only the reviewed instruction denial and remand under the error determination, preserving applicable collateral restrictions and any necessary correction or harmlessness inquiry. They require neither every requested factor instruction nor an unconditional writ, guilt vacatur or immediate resentencing.

Scalia joins the Court's application of governing law but repeats his constitutional objection to the mitigation doctrine. In his view, Lockett and Eddings' demand for discretionary mitigating consideration is difficult to reconcile with Furman's restraint on arbitrary sentencing discretion, and the eligibility/selection distinction does not answer that tension. His reservation changes neither his no-error join nor the Court's controlling rule.
