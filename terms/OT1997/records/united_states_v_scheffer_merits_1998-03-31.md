## Controlling law of the decision

### Supported reliability concerns justify this categorical polygraph rule against the presented constitutional challenge

**Holding and operative rule:** The constitutional right to present a defense permits evidentiary restrictions justified by legitimate trial interests and proportionate to their purposes. Serious, supported concerns about polygraph reliability justify Rule 707's categorical exclusion in this setting, where the accused can present his own factual account and underlying evidence; the Constitution does not require individualized admissibility proceedings for this credibility proffer.

**Authority:** Thomas's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: eight direct joins. The credibility-role and collateral-litigation rationales are separate, three-Justice grounds and do not become holdings.

**Controlling explanation:** Chambers and Rock protect a meaningful defense while allowing sufficiently justified evidentiary limits. Egelhoff applies that protection to rebuttal of the mental-state element actually retained by law and expressly preserves reliability restrictions. Here the rulemakers could respond to substantial disagreement about the reliability of polygraph conclusions with a uniform rule. An examiner's scientific certification that the accused is truthful differs from the accused's own account of the events. Scheffer testified to lack of knowing ingestion; Rule 707 did not remove that factual defense or deem the disputed element established.

The Government's administration of this examination and the importance of credibility do not establish that the Constitution requires an individualized foundation hearing. Daubert and Joiner govern the general Federal Rules' scientific-evidence inquiry; they do not constitutionalize that particular screening arrangement for every military evidence rule. The categorical form alone therefore does not make this restriction arbitrary or disproportionate.

The Court does not find that every polygraph is unreliable, determine an accuracy percentage, or resolve this test's truthfulness. Nor does permission to testify alone necessarily establish a complete defense in every case. The holding concerns this justified restriction and this proffer, leaving ordinary evidence rules and actual remaining claims intact. A separate Article 36(a) statutory-validity question is not decided.

**Rule's scope and exception:** Rule 707(a) excludes polygraph results, examiner opinions, and references to offering, failing to take or taking an examination. Rule 707(b) preserves statements made during an examination when they are otherwise admissible; it does not make every examination statement admissible.

## Public Projection

## Event

United States v. Scheffer, No. 96-1133. Argued November 3, 1997; decided March 31, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Armed Forces under 28 U.S.C. §1259, reviewing 44 M.J. 442. The question is whether Military Rule of Evidence 707's categorical exclusion of polygraph evidence violates the accused's constitutional right to present a defense.

An Air Force investigator administered the examination before the urinalysis result was known. The examiner considered Scheffer's answers to show no deception. At his court-martial, Scheffer denied knowing drug ingestion and sought the examiner's testimony to support his credibility after impeachment. The judge excluded it under Rule 707 without an individualized reliability hearing. The military appellate court removed that categorical obstacle on constitutional grounds; it did not hold this proffer necessarily admissible.

## Participation

All nine Justices participate at argument and decision: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the military appellate judgment on the polygraph-exclusion question and returns the case for consistent further proceedings.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 8–1.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Stevens.

The constitutional ruling invalidating the categorical exclusion is set aside. The decision neither automatically reinstates all convictions and the sentence nor resolves actual remaining claims. It orders no acquittal, compulsory admission, new trial or individualized reliability hearing.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Chambers v. Mississippi:** Preserved for meaningful presentation of a defense subject to justified evidentiary limits.
- **Rock v. Arkansas:** Distinguished at the sufficient reliability justification and nature of this credibility proffer; no absolute scientific-evidence entitlement is inferred.
- **Montana v. Egelhoff:** Applied at its protection for rebuttal of retained elements and express reservation for justified reliability rules; the substantive element is not redefined here.
- **Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals:** Preserved as construction of the original general Federal Rules, with flexible reliability-and-fit screening; its architecture is not imposed as a constitutional requirement.
- **General Electric Co. v. Joiner:** Preserved for inferential fit and abuse-of-discretion review under those Rules; it does not invalidate Rule 707's separate categorical scheme.

## Law After Decision

Rule 707 survives this constitutional challenge. A meaningful defense remains protected, but the Constitution permits this supported reliability restriction without a mandatory case-specific polygraph hearing. No constitutional jury-monopoly rule, general bar on credibility-related expertise or general authority to exclude inconvenient defense evidence follows from the three-Justice concurrence. Statutory validity under Article 36(a) remains undecided.

## Separate Writings

**Scalia, joined by Souter and Thomas, concurring.** In addition to reliability, two features independently justify this category-specific rule: preserving the court members' responsibility to judge credibility against a scientific certification of the accused's truthfulness, and avoiding recurring collateral litigation over examiner qualifications, question design, countermeasures and interpretation. These grounds permit this polygraph rule; they do not reject every expert opinion bearing on credibility or every defense proffer requiring time. All three fully join the Court's reliability ground. Their additional grounds have three votes and are not controlling.

**Kennedy, joined by O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer, concurring.** Reasonable disagreement about polygraph reliability permits the present rule, but constitutional permission does not establish that a categorical ban is the best evidentiary policy. These Justices decline to make protection of the factfinder from ultimate-issue assistance or collateral-litigation concerns independently sufficient grounds here. Their qualification preserves the possible significance of a stronger future proffer without finding this one admissible, requiring a new hearing or altering the present judgment.

**Stevens, dissenting.** A total prohibition prevents an accused from showing that an important defense proffer has a sufficient foundation. The Government administered this examination, and its results bear on the credibility of the denial of knowing ingestion. Egelhoff's meaningful opportunity to rebut the actual element and Rock's treatment of unsupported categorical exclusion favor lawful foundation review. Allowing Scheffer to speak does not by itself answer the exclusion of corroborative support. Stevens would sustain removal of the per se obstacle without deciding ultimate admission, acquittal or entitlement to a new trial before that inquiry. He preserves a concern under Article 36(a), which directs general federal criminal evidence principles so far as the President considers practicable and bars inconsistency with the military code, but does not rest his disposition on that unbriefed statutory question.
