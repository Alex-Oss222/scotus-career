* **Material Record:** Before enactment of the Tax Reform Act of 1986, the Internal Revenue Code gave property and casualty (PC) insurers a full deduction for “loss reserves”: estimated amounts of losses reported but not yet paid, losses incurred but not yet reported, and administrative costs of resolving claims. In each taxable year, not only losses paid, but the full amount of the loss reserves, reduced by the amount of the loss reserves claimed for the prior taxable year, were treated as a business expense. Section 1023 of the 1986 Act required PC insurers, beginning with the 1987 taxable year, to discount unpaid losses to present value when claiming them as a deduction. Requiring insurers to subtract undiscounted year-end 1986 reserves from discounted year-end 1987 reserves in computing 1987 losses would produce artificially low deductions, so the Act included a transitional rule requiring insurers to discount 1986 reserves as well. This rule changed the “method of accounting” for computing taxable income. To avoid requiring PC insurers to recognize as income the difference between undiscounted and discounted year-end 1986 loss reserves, the Act afforded them a “fresh start,” to wit, an exclusion from taxable income of the difference between undiscounted and discounted year-end 1986 loss reserves. § 1023(e)(3)(A). It foreclosed the possibility that they would inflate reserves to manipulate the “fresh start” by excepting “reserve strengthening” from the exclusion. § 1023(e)(3)(B). Treasury Regulation § 1.846-3(c)(3)(ii) defines “reserve strengthening” to include any net additions to reserves. Respondent Commissioner determined that petitioner, Atlantic Mutual Insurance Co., and its subsidiary, a PC insurer, made net additions to loss reserves in 1986, reducing the “fresh start” entitlement and resulting in a tax deficiency. The Tax Court disagreed, holding that “reserve strengthening” refers to only those increases that result from changes in computation methods or assumptions. In reversing, the Third Circuit concluded that the Treasury Regulation's definition of “reserve strengthening” is based on a permissible statutory construction. ## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Officially designated moment of silence

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the merits of the officially designated moment-of-silence question. During moments of silence at the May and August 1993 commencements, audience members recited the Lord's Prayer aloud. The May recitation was spontaneous by all accounts; TSU officials said they had no advance knowledge of it and denied complicity in the August recitation; and Chaudhuri conceded that there is no evidence of university complicity in either. On that record the recitations were private religious acts of audience members, outside the grant, and the judgment stands as to them; Stone states the official/private line below only to fix the limit of the holding.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Under the Lemon test as updated in Agostini, the inquiry identifies the governmental act and the function it performs; purpose is evidence of that function, not a freestanding prong. A genuine moment of silence is the university's own act, but the act is silence: it does not organize worship, prescribe religious profession or observance, or direct participants toward prayer, and each participant remains free to reflect, pray or do neither. Adoption of the moment as an alternative to spoken prayer does not by itself make it a devotional exercise. Audience members who spontaneously pray during the silence, without university direction, engage in their own private religious exercise; that prayer is not the university's and does not establish religion.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** A public university does not violate the Establishment Clause by designating a genuine moment of silence at an official function, including one adopted as an alternative to spoken prayer, when the university neither prescribes nor directs prayer or other religious exercise during it.
- **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Section I shows that the university replaced spoken prayer with moments of silence in 1993: first at the May commencement, when President Hefner announced there would be no verbal prayers and that a moment of silence would be acceptable "to solemnize the occasion," and then at all university functions where prayers had been offered. The record shows speakers asking the audience to rise or stand for silence, not any official instruction to pray during it, including at the August commencement, where the speaker who called for silence was identified in the program as a local pastor. Silence alone communicates no prescribed religious content, so its validity turns on actual design and administration. That the 1988 counsel letter described a moment of silence as an "appropriate vehicle for allowing the tradition of prayer without running afoul of the Constitution," and that the university adopted one as a replacement during this litigation, does not by itself make the silence a devotional exercise: the letter was the system general counsel's advice, the purpose President Hefner stated was to solemnize the occasion, and Section I shows no official direction to pray during the silence. Tanford stated that a moment of silence is not prohibited by its holding but did not decide that it is permissible; Stone would decide that question here for a genuine moment of silence. The official-worship prohibition applied in Tanford reaches the university's own worship, not private prayer by individuals who choose to pray, and Chaudhuri concedes no university complicity in the audience recitations.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** A nominal moment of silence used as a direction to pray, an officially led or prompted recitation, or university complicity in audience prayer would present the university's own religious exercise and a different case. Stone makes no merits ruling on the 1993 audience recitations, which lie outside the grant.

### Issue 2: Officially arranged university invocation

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the merits where university officials arranged an invocation, benediction or other spoken prayer as part of an official university function. Stone reads the first granted question's "officially arranged, nonsectarian invocations" to include the benedictions that formed part of the same former prayer practice: Section I shows that TSU formerly included invocations and benedictions at certain university events and that the Sixth Circuit upheld the former nonsectarian prayers as a single practice. The university discontinued the spoken prayers in 1993. The damages claim against the named administrators supplies the live controversy, as stated in Part B; Stone does not decide whether a prospective claim against the spoken prayers remains live, which the Sixth Circuit said appeared moot and Judge Jones would have held not moot.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Under the Lemon test as updated in Agostini, government may not organize worship as its own activity, and that specific prohibition controls when it resolves the conduct. A public university that selects or invites a speaker to lead an official audience in prayer and places the prayer in its program organizes worship as a university activity. The constitutional wrong is the university's sponsorship of worship, not whether listeners are persuaded, pressured or offended. A secular purpose of solemnizing the event cannot justify the religious means the university selected. Nonsectarian wording, the audience's adulthood and formally voluntary attendance do not cure that sponsorship. The violation therefore does not depend on Chaudhuri's alleged practical employment pressure to attend, which Stone need not resolve: Section I records his assertion that faculty were required to attend and that his evaluations included a "university service" component, and the defendants' contrary showing, including the dean's affidavit. That dispute remains relevant to the separate Lee and Ingebretsen pressure ground, which Stone does not reach. The Sixth Circuit, deciding before the Court's December 22, 1997 reversal in Tanford, cited the Seventh Circuit's Tanford decision for the prayers' secular purposes, the de minimis character of the religious gesture and of any entanglement, and the absence of coercion to participate.
