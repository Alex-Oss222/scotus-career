## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Prior aggravated-felony conviction as an offense element

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the statutory classification and the constitutional allocation question. Almendarez-Torres's admission of the prior convictions establishes their factual existence but does not cure the indictment's omission of a fact necessary to authorize punishment above § 1326(a)'s two-year maximum.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Stone would hold that § 1326(b)(2) defines an aggravated form of unlawful reentry rather than a mere sentencing factor. A fact that changes the maximum punishment legally authorized by the charged offense from two years to twenty years performs the work of an element and must be charged in the indictment and either admitted in a plea to that charged offense or proved to a jury beyond a reasonable doubt. The legislature's placement or label cannot transfer that maximum-defining fact entirely to the sentencing judge. Prior convictions may be established efficiently through judicial records, admissions, or stipulations, as *Old Chief* illustrates, but evidentiary convenience does not alter element status. *Custis* concerns whether an existing conviction may be collaterally attacked at sentencing and therefore does not answer the charging question. *Watts* reinforces the boundary because judicial factfinding remains permissible only within the punishment already authorized by the conviction.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** When § 1326(b)(2)'s prior aggravated-felony conviction is necessary to authorize punishment above § 1326(a)'s two-year maximum, that prior conviction is an element of the aggravated unlawful-reentry offense. It must be alleged in the federal indictment and, unless validly admitted as an element of the charged offense, proved to a jury beyond a reasonable doubt. Because this indictment omitted the aggravated-felony element, Almendarez-Torres may not be sentenced above § 1326(a)'s maximum on the existing conviction.
- **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Section 1326(a) supplies a complete unlawful-reentry offense and fixes a two-year ceiling. Subsection (b)(2) does not merely guide selection of a sentence within that ceiling; it authorizes a tenfold increase to twenty years based on an additional historical fact. The constitutional distinction between elements and ordinary sentencing facts turns on legal effect, not drafting placement. *Gaudin* protects the jury's authority over elements, while *Watts* preserves judicial sentencing factfinding only inside the conviction-authorized range. Treating the aggravated-felony fact as a sentencing factor would allow a plea to the two-year offense to support punishment Congress reserved for a materially aggravated offense that the indictment never charged. Almendarez-Torres admitted the prior convictions, so their existence is not disputed, but he did not plead guilty to an indictment containing that maximum-defining element. The eighty-five-month sentence therefore exceeds the punishment authorized by the conviction before the Court.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** This holding does not prevent courts from considering prior convictions when selecting a sentence within an independently authorized range, permit collateral attack beyond *Custis*, require prejudicial details of a prior conviction to be placed before a jury when an admission or stipulation establishes the element, or decide sentencing facts that do not increase the statutory maximum.

> Held: Subsection (b)(2) is a penalty provision, which simply authorizes an enhanced sentence. Since it does not create a separate crime, the Government is not required to charge the fact of an earlier conviction in the indictment. Pp. 228-248. (a) An indictment must set forth each element of the crime that it charges, Hamling v. United States, 418 U. S. 87, 117, but it need not set forth factors relevant only to the sentencing of an offender found guilty of the charged crime. Within limits, see McMillan v. Pennsylvania, 477 U. S. 79, 84-91, the question of which factors are which is normally a matter for Congress. See Staples v. United States, 511 U. S. 600, 604. Pp. 228-229. (b) That Congress intended subsection (b)(2) to set forth a sentencing factor is reasonably clear from a number of considerations. Its subject matter is a typical sentencing factor, and the lower courts have almost uniformly interpreted statutes that authorize higher sentences for recidivists as setting forth sentencing factors, not as creating separate crimes. In addition, the words “subject to subsection (b)” at the beginning of subsection (a) and “[n]otwithstanding subsection (a)” at
> 
> the beginning of subsection (b) have a meaning that is neither obscure nor pointless if subsection (b) is interpreted to provide additional penalties, but not if it is intended to set forth substantive crimes. Moreover, the circumstances of subsection (b)'s adoption support this reading of the statutory text. The title of the 1988 amendment—“Criminal penalties for reentry of certain deported aliens,” 102 Stat. 4471 (emphasis added)—also signals a provision that deals with penalties for a substantive crime, and it is reinforced by a legislative history that speaks only about the creation of new penalties. Finally, interpreting the subsection to create a separate offense risks unfairness, for the introduction at trial of evidence of a defendant's prior crimes risks significant prejudice. See, e. g., Spencer v. Texas, 385 U. S. 554, 560. Pp. 229-235. (c) Additional arguments supporting a contrary interpretation—that the magnitude of the increase in the maximum authorized sentence shows a congressional intent to create a separate crime, that statutory language added after petitioner's conviction offers courts guidance on how to interpret subsection (b)(2), and that the doctrine of constitutional doubt requires this Court to interpret the subsection as setting forth a separate crime—are rejected. Pp. 235-239. (d) There is not sufficient support, in this Court's precedents or elsewhere, for petitioner's claim that the Constitution requires Congress to treat recidivism as an element of the offense irrespective of Congress' contrary intent. At most, In re Winship, 397 U. S. 358, 364; Mullaney v. Wilbur, 421 U. S. 684, 704; Patterson v. New York, 432 U. S. 197; and Specht v. Patterson, 386 U. S. 605, taken together, yield the broad proposition that sometimes the Constitution does require (though sometimes it does not require) the State to treat a sentencing factor as an element of the crime, but they offer no more support than that for petitioner's position. And a legislature's decision to treat recidivism, in particular, as a sentencing factor rather than an element of the crime does not exceed constitutional limits on the legislature's power to define the elements of an offense. McMillan v. Pennsylvania, supra, distinguished. Petitioner's additional arguments—that courts have a tradition of treating recidivism as an element of the related crime, and that this Court should simply adopt a rule that any significant increase in a statutory maximum sentence would trigger a constitutional “elements” requirement—are rejected. Pp. 239-247. (e) The Court expresses no view on whether some heightened standard of proof might apply to sentencing determinations bearing significantly on the severity of sentence. Cf. United States v. Watts, 519 U. S. 148, 156, and n. 2 (per curiam). Pp. 247-248. 113 F. 3d 515, affirmed.

> Held: The judgment is reversed. 44 M. J. 442, reversed. Justice Thomas delivered the opinion of the Court with respect to Parts I, II-A, and II-D, concluding that Military Rule of Evidence 707 does not unconstitutionally abridge the right of accused members of the military to present a defense. Pp. 308-312, 315-317. (a) A defendant's right to present relevant evidence is subject to reasonable restrictions to accommodate other legitimate interests in the criminal trial process. See, e. g., Rock v. Arkansas, 483 U. S. 44, 55. State and federal rulemakers therefore have broad latitude under the Constitution to establish rules excluding evidence. Such rules do not abridge an accused's right to present a defense so long as they are not “arbitrary” or “disproportionate to the purposes they are designed to serve.” E. g., id., at 56. This Court has found the exclusion of evidence to be unconstitutionally arbitrary or disproportionate only where it has infringed upon a weighty interest of the accused. See, e. g., id., at 58. Rule 707 serves the legitimate interest of ensuring that only reliable evidence is introduced. There is simply no consensus that polygraph evidence is reliable: The scientific community and the state and federal courts are extremely polarized on the matter. Pp. 308-312.
> 
> (b) Rule 707 does not implicate a sufficiently weighty interest of the accused to raise a constitutional concern under this Court's precedents. The three cases principally relied upon by the Court of Appeals, Rock, supra, at 57, Washington v. Texas, 388 U. S. 14, 23, and Chambers v. Mississippi, 410 U. S. 284, 302-303, do not support a right to introduce polygraph evidence, even in very narrow circumstances. The exclusions of evidence there declared unconstitutional significantly undermined fundamental elements of the accused's defense. Such is not the case here, where the court members heard all the relevant details of the charged offense from respondent's perspective, and Rule 707 did not preclude him from introducing any factual evidence, but merely barred him from introducing expert opinion testimony to bolster his own credibility. Moreover, in contrast to the rule at issue in Rock, supra, at 52, Rule 707 did not prohibit respondent from testifying on his own behalf; he freely exercised his choice to convey his version of the facts at trial. > (a) The Seventh Amendment applies to both common-law causes of action and to statutory actions more analogous to cases tried in 18thcentury courts of law than to suits customarily tried in courts of equity or admiralty. Before the adoption of the Seventh Amendment, the common law and statutes in England and this country granted copyright owners causes of action for infringement. Here there is no similar analogy, and there is clear and direct historical evidence that juries, both as a general matter and in copyright cases, set the amount of damages awarded to a successful plaintiff. ## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Commercial use requirement for federal arson

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone decides the first Question Granted, whether the church was “used in” interstate commerce or in an activity affecting interstate commerce under § 844(i), before any constitutional question, because a fair reading of the enacted words “used in” can resolve the case. Hinds stipulated every other element of the offense and contested this one: the district court heard evidence on it before denying his motion to dismiss, and the Fourth Circuit reviewed the sufficiency of the evidence on it. The commerce element alone is therefore at issue and properly before the Court. Because the statutory element fails on this record, he does not reach the second Question Granted, which arises only if § 844(i) covers the church. He does not hold § 844(i) unconstitutional, and he does not develop the Commerce Clause.
* **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether a church building devoted to worship is actively used in interstate commerce or commerce-affecting activity merely because its operation involves purchases, utilities, insurance, employees, donations, mail or national religious affiliation.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Section 844(i) protects property used in interstate commerce or in an activity affecting interstate commerce. Stone reads that requirement as referring to the property’s actual, active use in commercial activity, not to every economic relationship its owner has, and he applies it narrowly enough to preserve the distinction between active commercial use and the ordinary incidents of owning or operating property. The church’s purchase and use of utilities, insurance, religious materials and other goods and services, the out-of-state insurance in its full-time employee’s benefits package, its employment of staff, and the funds it sent to a national organization of churches are interstate connections of the congregation. By themselves they do not show that the church building was used in commerce or in a commercial activity affecting commerce. Treating every utility customer, insured employer or donor as covered would erase the statutory word “used” and leave the use requirement no independent work. It would also let generalized economic connections do the work that Lopez refused to give a chain of downstream effects. Stone’s reversal rests on the scope of § 844(i), not on a finding that religious institutions have no economic connections. The Government’s strongest contention is that the church actively participated in a funded interstate network: as a member of a national organization of churches, it sent funds each year that the organization passed on as aid to missionary work, seminaries, institutions of higher education and other programs. Stone rejects that as inadequate to transform this building’s use for worship into the commercial use Congress specified. This is his own construction of the statute in OT1997; no later decision supplies the rule. The reversal does not depend on the Establishment Clause or on the Lemon test as updated in Agostini.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** A worship building is not “used in” interstate commerce, or in an activity affecting interstate commerce, within § 844(i) merely because maintaining or operating the property entails ordinary interstate purchases, utilities, insurance, employment, donations, mail or national organizational affiliation. “Used” requires active employment of the property itself in the relevant commercial activity. A church building used for worship is therefore not brought within § 844(i) merely because its congregation buys utilities, insurance, religious materials and other goods and services, provides an employee insurance procured from out-of-state companies, employs staff, or sends funds to a national organization of churches, even if some of those goods or materials reach it through interstate channels. The Government’s proof rested on those connections and did not establish the statutory element. The § 844(i) conviction must therefore be reversed and a judgment of acquittal entered on that count, without deciding Congress’s Commerce Clause authority.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Section 844(i) concerns the actual use of the burned property in commerce or an activity affecting commerce. The object of the statute is the function of the building itself. A rental property or business facility may be actively employed in a commercial market. A church devoted to worship does not become commercial property because its modern operation inevitably involves goods, services, insurance, utilities, money and interstate communications. The property here was the Pleasant Ridge United Church of Christ in Greensboro, North Carolina: a church, not a private residence or a commercial building. The Government relied on the church’s annual transmission to a national organization of churches of funds raised from its members and other sources, which the organization passed on as aid to missionary work, seminaries, institutions of higher education and other programs; its one full-time and two part-time employees; insurance procured through companies outside North Carolina for the full-time employee’s benefits package; and the purchase and use of utilities, insurance, various religious materials and other goods and services in its day-to-day operations. Those facts establish interstate connections, and Stone does not doubt them. They do not establish that the building itself was actively used for commercial purposes. They describe what the congregation bought, paid and gave in support of noncommercial worship, not a commercial use of the property. The Fourth Circuit held that the church’s day-to-day operations affected interstate commerce to the degree necessary to support the conviction. That answers whether the congregation’s activities affected commerce, not whether the building was used in commerce or in a commercial activity affecting commerce. Stone’s statutory distinction is active commercial use of the property versus incidental purchases supporting noncommercial worship. Russell v. United States, 471 U.S. 858 (1985), is consistent with that distinction: the building there was itself rented to tenants, and renting real estate is a commercial activity affecting commerce. Reading those ordinary incidents as sufficient would erase the statutory word “used” and reproduce the same kind of remote-economy reasoning that Lopez rejected at the constitutional level. Lopez supplies constitutional-avoidance context here, not the rule of decision. Its Opinion of the Court permits a fair narrowing construction of enacted language and forbids inserting an element Congress did not enact; Stone gives the enacted word “used” its meaning and adds nothing. Its connection-before-magnitude sequence shows why the broader reading would raise a serious constitutional question: read to reach every building whose owner buys, insures, employs or gives across State lines, § 844(i) would rest federal coverage on a chain from destruction of the building to the owner’s purchases, insurance, payroll and gifts, and from them to interstate markets. Lopez held that generalized downstream consequences through insurance, consumption or public expenditure cannot themselves supply the missing connection, so whether such a chain could support coverage is a serious constitutional question, not one Stone answers. The active-use reading instead ties coverage to the property’s own employment in commercial activity, as with the rental building in Russell. The Court therefore need not decide whether Congress could constitutionally reach every building with such contacts. Hale’s commerce holding does not dispense with the separately enacted property-use element.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The construction turns on how the property is used, not on the religious identity of its owner, and it creates no religious exemption from § 844(i) and no constitutional immunity for churches. The holding does not exclude religious property actually used for a commercial enterprise, rental activity, substantial commercial leasing or materially mixed commercial use. Stone does not decide whether a particular church building, or part of one, that is itself actively used in commercial activity falls within the statute; the Government’s showing here identifies no such use. Stone also leaves open four further questions: Congress’s constitutional authority to reach this arson, including the Commerce Clause ceiling that the second Question Granted presents; Hinds’s contention on appeal that, after Lopez, the Government had to prove the church substantially affected interstate commerce; the validity or reach of any other federal statute protecting places of worship, including the FACE worship branches left unchallenged in American Life League; and the reach of differently worded statutes, such as the Hobbs Act, that reach conduct affecting commerce rather than property used in commerce. The reversal does not mean the church was outside ordinary criminal-law protection. It means the federal government did not establish jurisdiction under this particular statute.

### Issue 2: Establishment Clause objection to the federal arson prosecution

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Hinds argued on appeal that his prosecution offended the Establishment Clause, and the Fourth Circuit rejected the argument; Section I’s material record states both. The objection is not a separately stated Question Granted. Stone addresses it for two reasons and only as far as they require. First, it bears on the ground of Issue 1: an Establishment Clause doubt about applying § 844(i) to churches, if serious, could be offered as a further reason to read the statute narrowly for religious property, and the statutory line would then turn on the owner’s religion rather than the building’s use. Stone finds no such doubt, so the Establishment Clause plays no part in the construction. Second, Hinds pressed it as an alternative ground for the same reversal, and Stone declines that ground so that the reversal rests on the statute alone. He resolves the objection at the established-permissible-relationship route of the Lemon test as updated in Agostini and goes no further: he does not reach the residual attribution-and-function route, any governmental regulation of a church’s own conduct, or any question of Congress’s power. Rejecting it is therefore not the broader constitutional holding that Section I’s threshold entry says Stone avoids. If the Court treats the objection as outside the grant, his rejection remains his stated reason for not relying on it and for not letting it inform the construction.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Stone rejects the objection. Lemon remains the governing Establishment Clause test as updated in Agostini v. Felton (June 23, 1997), under which “a specific prohibition or distinct doctrine controls when it resolves the conduct; an established permissible relationship defeats the claim on that ground; otherwise a legally sufficient unresolved allegation is decided through attribution and function under ordinary procedure.” The second route resolves this objection. The governmental act is a federal prosecution, under a general arson statute, of the person charged with burning a church. Its function is protecting property through ordinary criminal law. Everson v. Board of Education, 330 U.S. 1 (1947), sustained reimbursement of bus fares for pupils attending parochial schools as part of a general public-welfare program, and its reasoning treated that benefit like other general public services, such as ordinary police and fire protection, from which the Establishment Clause does not require government to cut religious institutions off. Neutral enforcement of a valid arson law belongs to that established permissible relationship. Such a prosecution does not organize worship, adopt a creed, or finance devotional instruction. None of Agostini’s prohibitions is implicated: government “may not organize worship as its own activity, prescribe religious profession or observance, adopt doctrine as governmental truth, solicit religious adherence through public authority, govern theology or confer civil authority on a religious basis.” Nor does the prosecution involve “devotional financing.” The church’s religious identity does not by itself decide the objection, because under Agostini religious identity is among the considerations that “are relevant in context but are not individually decisive.”
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** A neutral federal prosecution for arson of a church does not violate the Establishment Clause, provided the underlying criminal statute validly applies. Protecting a church’s property through general criminal law falls within the established permissible relationship of general public-safety services on which Everson’s bus-fare holding rested. That relationship defeats the objection under the Lemon test as updated in Agostini.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** As the Fourth Circuit described it, Hinds’s argument is that if a church can affect interstate commerce it can be regulated, and that government regulation of churches would violate the Establishment Clause. The argument fails at both steps. This prosecution regulates the arsonist’s conduct, not the church’s worship, doctrine or governance; protecting a church building from destruction does not regulate the church. And asking whether § 844(i)’s commerce element is proved about a church’s property is an ordinary inquiry into a statutory element; it neither governs theology nor confers civil authority on a religious basis. On this question Stone reaches the Fourth Circuit’s result, that prosecution under a facially neutral statute applied in a neutral manner does not offend the First Amendment, and he rests that result on the established-permissible-relationship route. The contrast is the university’s own organized commencement worship in Tanford v. Brand (December 22, 1997), which the specific-prohibition route resolved; here the Government performs no religious function. Stone’s statutory reading in Issue 1 is neither compelled by nor based on the Establishment Clause. Congress’s protection of churches under a valid power would raise no establishment problem, and the reversal rests solely on the enacted use element.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** This position decides no accommodation, funding, legislative-prayer or ceremonial question. It creates no rule that every governmental benefit to a religious institution is permissible, and it does not decide Congress’s authority to protect places of worship under any enumerated power. Free exercise and institutional religious autonomy retain their distinct sources and burdens.

### Decision order and interaction of the two questions

* **Decision order:** Stone takes the questions in this order. (1) He construes § 844(i)’s use element first, because the statutory question can resolve the case without any constitutional holding. (2) In construing it, he identifies which constitutional concern may properly inform the reading: Lopez’s connection-before-magnitude sequence supplies avoidance context against the broad reading, while Hinds’s Establishment Clause objection supplies none, because it fails at the established-permissible-relationship route (Issue 2). (3) The use element fails on this record, so the judgment is reversal on Issue 1, and the second Question Granted, which arises only if § 844(i) covers the church, is not reached. (4) He declines the Establishment Clause objection as an alternative ground, deciding it only at the route that resolves it. (5) The remedy, a judgment of acquittal on the § 844(i) count, follows from Issue 1 alone.
* **Independence of the two answers:** Neither answer rests on the other. The statutory reading rests on the enacted word “used” and the building’s use, not on the Establishment Clause, and it applies in the same way to property of any owner, religious or not, that is not itself employed in commercial activity; because the objection fails at the established-permissible-relationship route, it supplies no further reason to narrow that reading for religious property. The Establishment Clause answer is the same whatever the answer to the statutory question, because the governmental act it examines, a prosecution of the person charged with burning the building under a general property-protection statute, performs the same function whether or not the commerce element is proved. The Issue 2 holding is conditioned on the statute’s valid application: it removes one objection and never supplies statutory coverage or commerce power.
* **Why neither question yields a religious exemption:** The statutory line turns on the building’s use, not the owner’s religion. A worship building falls outside § 844(i) for the same reason any property not actively employed in commercial activity would, and religious property actually used for a commercial enterprise, rental activity, substantial commercial leasing or materially mixed commercial use is not excluded. The Establishment Clause answer runs the same way. Neutral protection of property through general criminal law is an established permissible relationship under the Lemon test as updated in Agostini, so the Clause neither forbids extending that protection to a church nor requires excluding a church from it, and religious identity is relevant in context but not individually decisive. The Commerce question, which Stone does not reach, would not supply a religious line either. Lopez directs that question to the regulated statutory class and the interstate mechanism connecting it to commerce among the several States, not to the national importance or subject matter of the problem, and it holds that the Constitution reserves no permanent substantive subject areas exclusively to the States, that a valid commerce power does not disappear because a State also has police authority over the subject, and that Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority remains undisturbed. Stone draws from those holdings no conclusion about Congress’s power over any building.
* **What Lopez contributes to the avoidance reasoning:** Lopez’s Opinion of the Court makes connection before magnitude the controlling sequence, and that places the constitutional concern Stone avoids at the connection stage. The concern is not whether this church’s activities, or churches’ activities in the aggregate, substantially affect interstate commerce. It is whether a reading that covers any building whose owner buys, insures, employs or gives across State lines would rest the statute’s reach on generalized economic consequences that Lopez holds cannot themselves supply the missing connection. Four further features of Lopez shape the avoidance. First, it permits a fair narrowing construction of enacted language and forbids inserting an element, so avoidance operates here only because § 844(i) contains an enacted use phrase to construe, and Stone construes it without adding anything. Second, it adopts no rule that each individual regulated act must independently have a substantial effect, so the avoidance reasoning does not depend on Hinds’s contention that the Government had to prove the church substantially affected commerce, which Stone leaves open. Third, it determines no particular form of jurisdictional element to be automatically sufficient, so the jurisdictional element that the Fourth Circuit held § 844(i) contains does not by itself settle the constitutional footing of a reading that drains the element of content; the question is what connection the element, as read, requires. Fourth, it preserves Wickard and Perez for genuinely economic classes, and the active-use reading, which ties coverage to the property’s own employment in commercial activity, as with the rental building in Russell, points the statute toward that kind of connection without deciding Congress’s power over any particular building. The avoidance context confirms the textual reading; it does not replace it.
* **What the Lemon test as updated in Agostini contributes:** Under Lemon’s former independent purpose, primary-effect and excessive-entanglement prongs, Hinds’s objection would have been tested prong by prong: whether protecting a church building serves a secular purpose, whether doing so has a primary effect of advancing religion, and whether a court’s examination of the congregation’s funds, staff and purchases to decide the commerce element entangles government with religion. Agostini keeps Lemon as the governing test but makes purpose, effects and entanglement evidence within one inquiry with three routes, and the former independent prongs no longer operate as a separate or fallback method. Stone therefore identifies the governmental act, a federal prosecution of the person charged with burning the church, and its function, protecting property through general criminal law. No specific prohibition or distinct doctrine resolves the conduct: the prosecution organizes no worship, prescribes no religious profession or observance, adopts no doctrine, solicits no religious adherence, governs no theology, confers no civil authority on a religious basis and finances no devotional activity, and the general arson statute draws no denominational classification. The established-permissible-relationship route then defeats the claim on that ground, through Everson’s treatment of general public services such as ordinary police and fire protection, so the residual attribution-and-function route is not reached. The protection the church receives is not measured against a freestanding primary-effect prong; effects are evidence of the governmental function. Examining the congregation’s operations as record facts on a statutory element is not the civil control of doctrine or religious control of public authority that the updated test forbids. Facial neutrality, on which the Fourth Circuit rested, is likewise evidence of the governmental function rather than the ground of decision; the ground is the established permissible relationship, and on it Stone reaches the Fourth Circuit’s result.
