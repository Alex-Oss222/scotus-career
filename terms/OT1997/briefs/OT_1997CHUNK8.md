> Because the termination provision does not violate separation of powers principles, equal protection, or due process, we affirm the district court's finding that the termination provision survives the constitutional challenges brought by the plaintiffs. Our analysis in reaching that holding largely tracks that of the other courts that have declared the termination provision constitutional. Cf., e.g., Gavin, 122 F.3d 1081; Plyler, 100 F.3d at 370-75; Benjamin, 935 F.Supp. at 344-57; Suffolk, 952 F.Supp. at 875-83. Our agreement with two of those decisions--Plyler and the determination of the court below--however, ends there. These two decisions followed their holdings that the termination provision was constitutional with vacaturs of the consent decrees.17 But like the Suffolk court, we believe, instead, that this is by no means the consequence of upholding the constitutionality of the termination provision. To the contrary, we find that the provision's constitutionality is only comfortably preserved by assuming that, while the PLRA limits the jurisdiction of the federal courts to enforce consent decrees, it does not destroy the underlying agreements themselves.

> We are aware, however, that "[c]onsent decrees are entered into by parties to a case after careful negotiation has produced agreement on their precise terms." United States v. Armour & Co., 402 U.S. 673, 681, 91 S.Ct. 1752, 1757, 29 L.Ed.2d 256 (1971). Hence, it may be that the plaintiffs would prefer to seek enforcement of the Decrees in their entirety in state court rather than pursuing what might in the end give rise to partial federal court relief under section 3626(b)(3) and partial state court relief based on local contract law principles. But the choice is up to them, and should they choose to remain in federal court, we hold that they are entitled to an evidentiary hearing on their allegations of current and ongoing violations of federal rights.

> The judgment of the court below is AFFIRMED insofar as it upheld the constitutionality of the termination provision of the PLRA, and is REVERSED insofar as it vacated the Consent Decrees. The stay issued by this court is modified, but remains in effect.

The record facts below are from the Second Circuit’s opinion, 127 F.3d 207 (2d Cir. 1997); bracketed pin cites are to the numbered paragraphs of the Public.Resource.Org text linked in Section I.E.
  - **Parties and school:** The Bronx Household of Faith, an evangelical Christian church, and its pastors, Jack Roberts and Robert Hall, sued Community School District No. 10, its board president and the Board of Education of the City of New York over the refusal to permit the church to use the gymnasium-auditorium of M.S. 206B, the Anne Cross Mersereau Middle School, for weekly religious worship services. [¶1] M.S. 206B serves students aged 9 through 12 in grades 5 through 7. [¶12]
  - **Prior rentals:** The church had previously rented the M.S. 206B building twice, in 1994 and 1995, for programs of sports and games, and once in 1995 for a banquet. [¶13]
  - **The request:** Pastor Hall asked to rent the gymnasium each Sunday “for the purpose of conducting church worship services.” He wrote that the services would include “hymn singing, communion, Bible reading, Bible preaching and teaching,” and he cited overcrowding at the church’s present meeting location. [¶13]
  - **The denial:** A District 10 official denied the request under the SOP provision barring after-school use of school facilities by outside organizations for religious services or instruction. The application was denied again after the pastor asked the superintendent to reconsider. [¶13]
  - **New York Education Law § 414:** Section 414 does not list religious worship among the uses of school property it authorizes. It allows districts to permit uses including educational instruction; social, civic and recreational meetings and entertainments, and other community-welfare uses, that are “non-exclusive and open to the general public”; civic forums and community centers; and graduation exercises of nonprofit schools “provided that no religious service is performed.” [¶3]
  - **SOPs 5.6.1 and 5.6.2:** The New York City Board of Education’s Standard Operating Procedures give primary use of school premises to Board activities and next preference to community, youth and adult group activities. They permit use “[f]or the purpose of instruction in any branch of education, learning or the arts” (SOP 5.6.1) and for “social, civic and recreational meetings and entertainments, and other uses pertaining to the welfare of the community” (SOP 5.6.2). [¶¶4-6]
  - **SOP 5.9:** SOP 5.9, a Board of Education policy that governs District 10, provides: “No outside organization or group may be allowed to conduct religious services or religious instruction on school premises after school. However, the use of school premises by outside organizations or groups after school for the purposes of discussing religious material or material which contains a religious viewpoint or for distributing such material is permissible.” [¶¶4, 11, 12]
  - **Uses in 1994-95:** The parties agreed that during the 1994-95 school year District 10 facilities were used for tutoring programs; Boy Scout training, which Judge Cabranes’s opinion describes as including “ethical education”; Girl Scout training “in skills and character”; Pius XII Beacon programs including after-school tutoring and “character training, with no religious content”; a Grace Lutheran Church program on Black History Month; Greek language and history classes for young people offered by the Greek Orthodox Community of Riverdale; concerts; workshops on drugs and AIDS; and adult GED classes. [¶12; Cabranes, J., ¶57 & n.1]
  - **Stipulation:** The parties stipulated that “Community School District 10 has consistently followed Policy 5.9 by never renting school facilities to non-school groups or organizations, including Bronx Household of Faith, for the purpose of conducting religious worship services and religious instruction on school premises.” [¶15]
  - **Scope of the record:** The record covered school uses during a single school year. It disclosed no request to use the premises for discussing religious material or secular matters from a religious viewpoint. [¶32; Cabranes, J., ¶49]
  - **District court:** The district court (Preska, J.) granted summary judgment to the defendants on cross-motions. It found a limited public forum and found restrictions reasonably related to preserving access “primarily for educational purposes,” and it dismissed the remaining claims without analysis. [¶2]
  - **Second Circuit majority:** Judge Miner, writing for the court on a panel with Judges Van Graafeiland and Cabranes, held M.S. 206B a limited rather than open public forum and held SOP 5.9’s prohibition of religious services and instruction reasonable and viewpoint neutral. The court reasoned that “it is reasonable for state legislators and school authorities to avoid the identification of a middle school with a particular church” and to consider “the effect upon the minds of middle school children of designating their school as a church.” It found the non-exclusive-use provisions of no significance. [¶¶23, 28-33]
  - **Establishment Clause and other claims below:** The court stated that “there is no need for the Board of Education to rely on the Establishment Clause in limiting the use of the public school forum.” It rejected the Establishment Clause, Free Exercise and Equal Protection claims, held the RFRA claim moot after City of Boerne v. Flores, and affirmed. The affirmance covered both the services and the instruction parts of SOP 5.9 as applied to the church. [¶¶28, 35, 39, 40, 42, 43, 63]
  - **Judge Cabranes:** Judge Cabranes concurred in part and dissented in part. Stating that he was constrained by circuit precedent and the parties’ factual stipulations, he agreed that the forum was limited, and he concurred in upholding the ban on religious services as reasonable and viewpoint neutral, finding “no real secular analogue” to services and describing that ban as a form of content discrimination. He dissented from “that part of the judgment upholding the ban on religious instruction.” He reasoned that the District allowed “secular instruction related to ‘ethics’ and ‘character,’ paradigmatic contexts for religious instruction,” and that the instruction ban discriminates on the basis of viewpoint under Lamb’s Chapel and Rosenberger. He noted that the parties had stipulated that the church sought the gymnasium for “religious worship services,” so any difficulty in separating services and instruction from other religious speech was not presented. [¶¶45-46, 56-57, 61-63]

Under the Lemon test as updated in Agostini, letting a private church use public school space for its own worship would not, by itself, establish religion: the school administers access to its property, and the worship remains the church’s activity rather than a service the school organizes. The district therefore cannot defend its exclusion as compelled by the Establishment Clause or by a claim that the Constitution automatically forbids worship in a public building, and avoiding an Establishment Clause violation supplies no sufficient reason for excluding the church’s private worship. For the same reason, the Second Circuit’s rationale that the school may avoid identification with a particular church cannot support the exclusion absent facts establishing genuine governmental adoption. That removes the Establishment Clause objection to private worship; it does not create a right to use every public building. The free-speech claim turns on the forum the district actually opened. A limited public forum may be confined by reasonable, viewpoint-neutral limits on the categories of activity it admits, and the district is not necessarily required to open such a forum to regular worship services. On the stipulated record, excluding regular worship services as a category of use outside the forum actually opened is a reasonable, viewpoint-neutral subject-matter limit; the secular community uses the district admitted are not comparable worship services. The category must be defined by the activity—a worship service as such—not by religious content, identity or perspective. Once the district admits instruction on a particular subject, it cannot exclude an otherwise eligible speaker simply for teaching that subject from a religious perspective, so the affirmance does not approve the religious-instruction exclusion. If the record established that the forum was actually open to comparable activities, Stone would reverse the church’s exclusion.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Permitting a private church to use public school facilities for its own worship does not by itself establish religion under the Lemon test as updated in Agostini, so a school district cannot justify excluding the church’s worship as required by the Establishment Clause, or by an interest in avoiding the school’s identification with the church, absent facts showing genuine governmental adoption. A district that has created a limited public forum may nonetheless exclude regular worship services as a category of use through a reasonable, viewpoint-neutral limit it has consistently applied, where the category is defined by the activity of a worship service as such and the forum has not been opened to comparable activities; but once the district admits instruction on a subject, it may not exclude an otherwise eligible speaker for teaching that subject from a religious perspective.
- **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** On the Establishment Clause question, Agostini directs the inquiry to the governmental act and the function it performs: government may not organize worship as its own activity, but private-access authorization, religious identity and physical location are relevant in context and not individually decisive. Here the governmental act would be renting school property to an outside congregation under the district’s facility-use rules, as the district had already done for the church’s sports programs and banquet; the services—hymn singing, communion, Bible reading, Bible preaching and teaching—would be the church’s own, sought by its pastor for its congregation each Sunday. Nothing in the record shows official selection, control, adoption, preference or obscured sponsorship. Tanford v. Brand marks the contrast: there the university selected a religious leader and incorporated prayer into its own commencement, and Tanford itself keeps neutral access to a genuine forum distinct from official worship. Lamb’s Chapel, Good News and Church on the Rock hold that equal private access on terms of real private sponsorship does not establish religion; Chabad holds that location alone does not make clearly private expression governmental; and Pinette supplies no automatic attribution from location. Because those holdings reserved standalone worship, the conclusion here rests on the attribution-and-function inquiry of the Lemon test as updated in Agostini, which they support. The Second Circuit did not rest on an Establishment Clause defense—it said there was “no need” for the Board to rely on the Clause—but it counted toward the exclusion’s reasonableness an interest in avoiding “the identification of a middle school with a particular church” and in the effect on middle-school children of designating their school as a church. Stone rejects that rationale: it makes location and religious identity decisive, which the updated Lemon test does not permit, and it treats private worship as the school’s without any fact of genuine governmental adoption. Good News held that an 11–15-year-old audience did not, on its record, convert a private after-school program into school speech; the ages of M.S. 206B’s students do not make an outside congregation’s Sunday rental the school’s worship. On the free-speech question, the exclusion survives instead through independent forum doctrine. Lamb’s Chapel does not require an unlimited forum or invalidate neutral restrictions on subjects and activities outside the actual forum; Good News holds that schools need not open every facility to every religious activity; Church on the Rock preserves standalone worship reservations; and Good News, applying Perry and Cornelius, permits genuine, reasonable and viewpoint-neutral management of a limited forum. Section 414 and SOPs 5.6.1 and 5.6.2 define this forum by instruction in any branch of education, learning or the arts and by social, civic and recreational meetings and entertainments and other community-welfare uses; § 414 does not list worship; and the parties stipulated that District 10 has consistently never rented school facilities for religious worship services. The uses actually admitted in 1994-95—tutoring, Scout and Beacon character training, Greek language and history classes, a Black History Month program, concerts, drug and AIDS workshops and GED classes—include no worship service or comparable activity. Grace Lutheran Church and the church itself used the facilities for admitted purposes, and SOP 5.9 expressly permits outside groups to discuss religious material and material containing a religious viewpoint. On that stipulated record, a congregation’s regular worship service is a category of use the district never admitted, not a religious viewpoint on an admitted subject, and confining the forum to its designated instructional, civic, social, recreational and community-welfare uses is a reasonable, viewpoint-neutral subject-matter limit without reliance on the identification rationale. That category is defined by the activity as a worship service as such, not by its religious content: Good News treats prayer and Bible instruction within an admitted moral-development program as a religious viewpoint, and Church on the Rock treats teaching a faith or inviting conversion or prayer within an admitted program the same way. Question 1’s reference to comparable secular community activities does not change the result on this record, because none of the admitted uses is a comparable worship service. The religious-instruction clause stands differently. The forum admits instruction in any branch of education, and the district admitted Girl Scout and Beacon character training and, as Judge Cabranes’s opinion records, Boy Scout ethical education. Under Lamb’s Chapel, Good News, Church on the Rock and Rosenberger, once a subject is admitted, an otherwise eligible speaker cannot be excluded for teaching it from a religious perspective; a clause permitting secular instruction on character and ethics while barring religious instruction on the same subjects excludes the religious perspective. Judge Cabranes dissented below on that ground; his separate opinion is recorded as the lower-court view, not as authority. Because the church’s application sought the gymnasium for worship services, Stone states that rule as a limit on the affirmance rather than as a separate judgment. The narrow result therefore does not depend on Lemon’s former independent prongs; it survives through independent public-forum doctrine, and its reasoning respects the Lemon test as updated in Agostini.

If the record established that the forum was actually open to comparable activities, the rule of Lamb’s Chapel, Good News and Chabad against excluding an otherwise eligible religious viewpoint from access actually opened to comparable expression would control, and Stone would reverse the church’s exclusion. The position does not approve SOP 5.9’s religious-instruction exclusion as applied to subjects the forum admits, and it does not adopt the Second Circuit’s identification-with-a-church rationale. Genuine governmental adoption—official selection, control, adoption, preference or obscured sponsorship—could change the Establishment Clause answer, and government organization of worship as its own activity remains prohibited.
