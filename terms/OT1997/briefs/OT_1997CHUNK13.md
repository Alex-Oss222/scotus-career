## A. Case, record, and posture

- **Case name:** *Clifton v. Federal Election Commission*, No. 97-786.
- **Parties:** Robin Clifton, a Maine voter who reads advocacy materials, and Maine Right to Life Committee, Inc. (MRLC), petitioners; FEC, respondent. Below: *Clifton v. FEC*, 927 F. Supp. 493 (D. Me. 1996), modified, 114 F.3d 1309 (1st Cir. 1997).

- **Question presented for modeling, reconstructed:** Whether the FEC can condition corporations' dissemination of independently prepared candidate voting records and voter guides on restrictions against mere contact with candidates, a written-question/written-answer-only protocol, equal space and prominence for competing candidates, or a ban on undefined “electioneering messages”; whether any remnant restrictions survive § 441b and the First Amendment. The original petition's exact question is not available for verification.
- **Judgment under review:** District court declared challenged portions of 11 C.F.R. § 114.4(c)(4) and (5) unauthorized under FECA. The First Circuit **modified** that declaration: voting-record regulation invalid only to the extent it purported to bar mere inquiries to candidates; voter-guide regulation invalid only insofar as it required all pertinent contacts to occur in writing and compelled equal space/prominence; challenges to the “electioneering message” provisions were **remanded** for further work on content, ripeness, and severability. The court did not nullify all valid restrictions on actual coordination or express advocacy.

- **Material record:** MRLC is a nonprofit membership corporation opposed to abortion; it publishes voter guides stating federal candidates' positions and voting records bearing on abortion questions, using general treasury funds that include donations from other corporations. Clifton receives and wishes to receive the information. Regulations took effect March 13, 1996. There is no trial finding that mere candidate inquiries actually produced a prohibited in-kind contribution or that each questioned guide was created in collaboration with a campaign.
- **Rules:** Section 114.4(c)(4) addressed corporate and union distribution of voting records and forbade coordination over their content and distribution. Section 114.4(c)(5) regulated voter guides, allowing contact about their preparation only under written questions and answers, while requiring equal space and prominence and excluding specified “electioneering messages” or scoring. Some restrictions operated even on pure questions asked of candidates about public issues.

The appellate judgment is mixed and has a remand component; Supreme Court review of an interlocutory aspect or undecided question risks an inadequate merits record. Whether the Court may or should review a final appellate ruling notwithstanding remand is a separate certiorari concern.

### Issue 1. Independent issue publications and actual coordination

- **Controlling simulated law:** Unverified.
- **Public doctrine:** FECA § 441b restricted corporate and union contributions and election expenditures, with special segregated-fund treatment. *Buckley*, 424 U.S. 1 (1976), distinguished independent expression from candidate-directed coordinated spending; *MCFL*, 479 U.S. 238 (1986), read § 441b as not reaching general issue advocacy and reserved special First Amendment protection for certain ideological nonprofits.

- **Open point:** Where a candidate responds to questions about public positions, when does inquiry become cooperation that renders publishing costs a contribution made for or on behalf of that candidate?

Anti-corruption rules directed to candidate contributions serve legitimate aims but cannot necessarily prescribe the form or substance of independent association's publications.

## C. Decision-material litigation positions

**On candidate contact:** The FEC argues that regulatory prophylaxis prevents disguised in-kind contributions from corporations, including guides whose questions, rankings, and timing are coordinated with campaigns. Petitioners argue a candidate's answer to a survey or a conversation explaining a voting record does not finance or control that candidate's speech. The First Circuit held that *mere inquiry* cannot be treated as prohibited coordination but preserved the possibility that real collaboration is a contribution under lawful statutory standards.

**On written-only protocol:** The FEC says fixed written questionnaires produce a verifiable record and discourage preferential private collaboration. Petitioners answer that choosing only written communication materially restricts political dialogue, particularly with sitting lawmakers on public issues. The First Circuit invalidated the categorical contact-form rule as beyond the delegated authority in light of constitutional concerns; Judge Bownes dissented on this component, emphasizing money-in-politics concerns and the state's anticorruption interest.

**On equal space/prominence:** The FEC invokes objectively evenhanded coverage as a way to distinguish neutral voter information from a disguised candidate contribution. Petitioners assert the right to choose coverage, criticism, and editorial allocation, and invoke *Tornillo* and *Hurley*. The First Circuit invalidated compulsory equal editorial treatment, finding no identified statutory power or adequate showing that the burden was necessary to police actual contributions.

**On electioneering and corporate financing:** MRLC challenges the remaining ambiguous “electioneering message” terms. The FEC failed to commit itself in appellate briefing to a definitive narrow meaning; the appellate panel declined final judgment on meaning and severability. MRLC claims protection as ideological advocacy, but its receipt of commercial donations prevents assuming it falls within *MCFL*'s narrow special rule. The State's anti-corruption aim, the association's speaker status, and administrative authority require separate analysis.

## D. Lawful paths and uncertainty

1. **No merits review at this stage:** Leave the partial appellate disposition and lower-court remand to run; later genuine finality may clarify the regulatory remainder and the record.
2. **Affirm the narrow appellate judgment if granted:** Hold that mere inquiries are not contributions, a writing-only protocol and equal-space command lack adequate statutory foundation, and actual cooperation can remain regulable. Leave electioneering and corporate-status questions open.
3. **Further limiting construction:** If a regulatory phrase clearly and validly reaches only express advocacy or real coordination, sustain it to that extent. Such a holding requires an established interpretation and no impermissible judicial rewrite.

The material uncertainty is unusually procedural: the petition comes from a partly favorable, partly modified circuit judgment with a contested regulatory remainder. The FEC's claim about corporate donor influence cannot stand in for findings identifying MRLC's funding model.

## C. Issue positions

### Issue 1. Candidate inquiry versus coordination

- **Reach:** Properly before the Court to the extent necessary to review the modified regulation.
- **Stone's position:** Apply the actual expenditure/contribution distinction and the principle that agency power comes from enacted law. A speaker's conversation with a candidate about policy or votes is not, without more, spending undertaken on the candidate's behalf.
- **Proposed holding:** Prohibiting **mere inquiry** under § 114.4(c)(4) exceeds § 441b's permissible treatment of contributions. Actual candidate direction of content, collaboration on distribution, or an agreed service to the campaign may create a legally different transaction.
- **Application:** MRLC's attempt to obtain the meaning of recorded votes is information gathering. There is no factual finding of collaboration making the resulting guide a candidate expense. An agency can investigate the latter without adopting a categorical presumption that the former equals it.
- **Boundary:** The holding is not a right to launder a paid political ad through a staged interview; true coordination remains subject to the statute.

### Issue 2. The written-only contacts condition

- **Reach:** Expressly decided by the First Circuit.
- **Stone's position:** The First Amendment protects political association and communication in the act of gathering and discussing issues, even when the publication is corporate-financed. The FEC may not add a limitation on oral dialogue merely because an audit trail would be convenient.
- **Proposed holding:** In the absence of actual coordination or a congressionally authorized narrow prophylactic rule, § 114.4(c)(5)'s mandate of written questions and written replies is invalid.
- **Reasoning:** Written answers may be good administrative evidence but the Commission has not shown why face-to-face discussion necessarily effects a transfer to a candidate. The agency cannot replace the needed factual distinction with an indiscriminate method-of-contact ban. Commercial treasury restrictions may be permissible under other sources; they do not enlarge this rule's authority.
- **Boundary:** Fraudulent or coordinated services and sham questions intended to supply campaign work can still be regulated on their proved elements.

### Issue 3. Editorial space and prominence

- **Reach:** Expressly decided by the First Circuit.
- **Stone's position:** Apply *Tornillo* and *Hurley* under the source-bound election doctrine. A privately composed issue guide does not owe equal column space to every candidate as a constitutional or default statutory requirement.
- **Proposed holding:** The compulsory equal-space-and-prominence clause is outside the FEC's delegation and burdens editorial control, even when framed as an evenhanded election safeguard.
- **Application:** The FEC may require accurate disclosure where authorized, prohibit a real in-kind corporate contribution, and enforce false reporting rules; it cannot force a private ideological publisher to devote specified pages to a candidate whose positions it criticizes. The distribution of source funds is a legally separate question from forcing a particular text or layout.
- **Boundary:** A government-authored neutral voter guide or voluntary condition on use of a properly defined public resource would raise different sources and is not resolved.

### Issue 4. The unfinished “electioneering message” clauses

- **Reach:** **Not reached on the merits** in this posture. The appellate judgment expressly remanded content, ripeness, and severability.
- **Stone's position:** Enforce the limits of the preserved record. The failure of the FEC to supply a clear operative interpretation is reason for careful lower-court analysis, not a license to infer every possible unlawful application and decide them all.
- **Proposed holding:** No new merits holding. Leave the First Circuit's remand for consideration of whether the clauses can survive independently and what validly promulgated meaning, if any, they bear.
- **Boundary:** The agency may not enforce an undefined broadened category of issue advocacy through mere labels. If later review yields a ripe, supported challenge, the court must resolve it on its own lawful record.

### Issue 5. MRLC's corporate donor funding and *MCFL*

- **Reach:** Not necessary to the controlling statutory judgment; expressly reserved.
- **Stone's position:** Stone's stable source-of-money approach distinguishes voluntary ideological associations from commercial corporate treasuries. But *MCFL*'s actual historical criteria, and MRLC's receipt of some business-corporation funds, prevent pretending the exception is automatically satisfied.
- **Proposed holding:** None as to MRLC's ultimate status. The case resolves the FEC's challenged regulatory means without extending the narrow corporate ideological exception, nullifying *Austin*, or importing later campaign-finance law.
- **Boundary:** If a later case squarely requires corporate categorization, examine the organization's funding sources, voluntary natural-person support, relevant segregation, and the legal test then controlling. Do not invent missing donor proportions here.

**Stone canon note:** This disposition follows the order: delegation, preserved claims, record, remedy. It honors protection of political issue speech while leaving lawful regulation of commercial treasury influence and actual candidate coordination available. It makes no unsupported merits holding from an unfinished remand.

## C. Decision-material litigation positions

**Section 2462:** Williams argues that a civil penalty must be asserted within five years after the violation, whether the suit initially **assesses** or later **collects** the sanction. **Concealment:** The FEC's strongest case is that the violation was a disguised-payor scheme, meaning reports naming employees as nominal contributors did not give notice of who truly supplied the funds. ### Issue 1. A five-year public-penalty deadline is a real deadline

### Issue 2. Concealed source cannot be proven disclosed by a nominal-donor list

The State's own rationale emphasizes preserving established parties and avoiding factional alternatives, which does not answer why recognized smaller parties must be categorically excluded from this particular benefit. The constitutional defect is the operation and purpose of this allocation, not the mere existence of unequal subsidies.

Florida's collection and reallocation of candidate fees is governmental action. When that action assists entrenched party structures and excludes ballot-qualified smaller parties, constitutional review is warranted even without proof of total ballot exclusion.

## B. Law entering the case

The governing distinction in this Court's decisions is between state and local racial preferences under *City of Richmond v. J.A. Croson Co.* and the separate federal remedial arrangement in *Adarand Constructors, Inc. v. Peña* (1995). The latter was decided under the retained *Metro Broadcasting* federal important-objective and substantial-relationship standard, not a new federal strict-scrutiny rule. The *Northeastern Florida Chapter of Associated General Contractors v. Jacksonville* (1993) decision recognizes unequal competitive opportunity as a constitutional injury without proving the contract would otherwise have been awarded.

### Issue 1: Racial and ethnic contracting preferences

- **Governing law:** *City of Richmond v. J.A. Croson Co.*, 488 U.S. 469 (1989), subjects local race-conscious contracting to strict scrutiny, requiring identified remedial injury and narrow fit. In this Court's *Adarand Constructors, Inc. v. Peña* (1995), a separate federal optional subcontracting incentive was sustained under *Metro Broadcasting*'s important-government-objective and substantial-relationship standard. *Adarand* expressly retained the distinct *Croson* rule for state and local measures; it did not extend federal strict scrutiny to every remedial classification. Local Dade County remains governed by *Croson* unless that rule is expressly reconsidered.
- **Open point:** Statistical evidence both reflects the operation of an existing long-running BBE program and can be distorted by firm-size composition; whether available studies demonstrate sufficient discrimination in the relevant procurement market is contested.

### Issue 2: Sex-conscious contracting preferences

- **Governing law:** *United States v. Virginia*, 518 U.S. 515 (1996), requires an exceedingly persuasive justification for sex classifications within the intermediate-scrutiny framework. The Eleventh Circuit treated the WBE program under intermediate scrutiny.
- **Open point:** Whether mixed industry data and acknowledged gaps establish an important remedial objective substantially related to the preference actually deployed.

### Issue 3: Remedy

- **Governing law:** 42 U.S.C. § 1983 where properly invoked, the Fourteenth Amendment, traditional equitable requirements, and standards of review for factual findings following trial.
- **Open point:** Whether different outcomes for BBE, HBE, and WBE measures require correspondingly separate injunctive boundaries.

## C. Decision-material litigation positions

- **Below:** No strong evidentiary foundation for the race and ethnic programs. Bidder-to-award rates did not consistently disfavor covered firms; many contract-dollar disparities diminished when firm size was accounted for. Women's program also lacked sufficiently persuasive substantiation. The permanent injunction stood.
- **Petitioners:** Statistical comparisons, income disparities, contracting history, and testimonial evidence show barriers that naive bidder comparisons miss, particularly because preferences already in effect could make remaining disparities appear small. County government can correct exclusion in its own procurement market.
- **Respondents:** The government must prove more than historical unfairness or national disparity; size-adjusted data do not show general County discrimination sufficient to give legally consequential preferences to every favored contractor. Individual nonbeneficiaries lose contract opportunities.
- **Limit:** A numerical goal may operate as a real set-aside, and a neutral-sounding label does not determine burden. Conversely, reducing observed discrimination through years of a corrective program can affect what current raw statistics show.

## D. Lawful paths and uncertainty

1. Affirm all programs' invalidation under *Croson*, *Adarand*, and intermediate sex scrutiny, accepting trial findings absent clear error.
2. Reconsider *Croson*'s rule for a properly supported local remedial classification, openly stating and defending any doctrinal change; if a modified rule is adopted, vacate racial components and remand for actual evidence, corrective fit and nonbeneficiaries' opportunities. This would not follow automatically from the distinct federal *Adarand* holding.
3. Any remand must avoid presuming findings absent from the record, automatically reinstating the County's entire scheme, or treating evidence sufficient for one target group as sufficient for all.

### Issue 2: Bird-take prohibition and federal agencies
