## Forney v. Apfel — No. 97-5737

### Common disposition, rule and limits

Reverse the Ninth Circuit's refusal to entertain the appeal and remand for appellate consideration. A sentence-four judgment under §405(g) ends the district-court civil action and is final for §1291 review. A claimant who sought an immediate benefits award but received only renewed agency proceedings remains adversely affected by the denial of that larger relief and may appeal. Prevailing-party status for fees and entitlement to appeal answer different questions.

Schaefer supplies sentence-four termination; Finkelstein supplies the statutory final-judgment structure while expressly reserving the claimant's broader-relief situation; the ordinary adverse-stake principle supplies the remaining step. The remand's practical benefit does not give Forney everything she sought. Newport News distinguishes a general administrative interest from an actual party's injury; it supplies no contrary rule for this denied benefits request.

The result establishes neither disability nor entitlement to an immediate award, and does not instruct the circuit to substitute its own factfinding. Sentence-six proceedings retain their separate conditions: the Secretary's good-cause motion before answering, or new material evidence and good cause for earlier omission. This decision does not decide the appealability of those remands, every agency remand, a judgment granting complete relief, or an appeal seeking only a different explanation of wholly favorable relief.

## Phillips v. Washington Legal Foundation — No. 96-1578

### Issue and two supported paths

The only merits question resolved here is ownership of interest actually generated by client principal in Texas IOLTA accounts. Both paths preserve principal ownership and the program's eligibility limit: client funds belong outside IOLTA when they can reasonably yield net interest for the client, including through feasible attorney pooling. No finding establishes that the particular respondent funds can do so. The NOW statute's eligible beneficial-interest categories, the Federal Reserve's charitable-exclusive-interest interpretation, and the IRS's nonattribution rule are distinct from one another and from the Texas ownership inquiry. Individuals are an eligible NOW category in their own right; the federal restriction on for-profit corporations and partnerships is not imputed to every client. The common-law Texas interest principle is supported by Webb's published discussion of Sellers v. Harris County, 483 S.W.2d 242; Sellers itself was not independently read here.

**Path P — affirm the property determination and remand the remaining questions.** Actually generated interest follows the private principal. The inability to realize a profitable individual return does not by itself extinguish ownership of interest once generated. Webb's rejects government conversion of private earnings merely by asserting that the legal arrangement created them; Lucas preserves the difference between an antecedent title limit and a new public assignment. The net-yield objection may affect a taking, economic loss and compensation, but does not alone transfer title. The federal permission to use NOW accounts and the tax treatment do not themselves adjudicate constitutional ownership. This path does not decide whether a taking occurred or any compensation is owed.

**Path N — reverse the property determination for funds properly subject to this program.** The asserted interest entitlement must be defined through the whole legal and economic arrangement, not an abstract incident detached from its conditions. The no-net-return limit, even after feasible pooling, leaves no preexisting economically realizable client benefit; for ineligible corporate funds, the charitable entitlement additionally permits a return under the stated federal arrangement. On this account the program has not appropriated a preexisting beneficial entitlement to the resulting charitable return. Webb's involved an independently valuable interpleader fund and expressly bounded its decision; it does not make every gross return a client entitlement regardless of the conditions producing it. Lucas governs destruction of an existing entitlement, not identification of this one. Improper placement of funds able to earn net client interest would be a different case.

The strongest objection to P is that recognizing a client beneficial interest appears inconsistent with the federal arrangement permitting charitable interest for otherwise ineligible funds and ignores the no-net-yield premise. P answers by separating statutory banking eligibility and economic value from the ownership question; it does not declare the arrangement unlawful. The strongest objection to N is that a government could eliminate property simply by assigning its fruits to someone else, and that an individual's unprofitable interest may still be owned. N therefore depends on treating the available beneficial entitlement as part of property identification, rather than using economic value solely to measure compensation. A new public-purpose declaration alone is insufficient. Neither path converts the worthy legal-services use into a dispositive constitutional exemption.

## Pennsylvania Department of Corrections v. Yeskey — No. 97-634

### Common disposition, rule and limits

Affirm the Third Circuit's rejection of a categorical prison exclusion and remand for remaining proceedings. Title II includes state prisons within the statutory public-entity definition. Its protection of a qualified individual against disability-based exclusion from services, programs or activities, denial of their benefits, or other discrimination by such an entity is not limited to voluntary custody. A prison-operated boot-camp opportunity potentially shortening confinement is within that coverage; the statutory merits predicates remain to be established.

The definition includes State and local governments and their departments, agencies, special-purpose districts and other instrumentalities. A qualified individual must meet essential eligibility requirements with or without the modifications, barrier removal or auxiliary assistance the statute identifies. The Court does not hold that every prisoner is disabled or qualified, that hypertension establishes disability in every case, or that every requested modification is reasonable. Security defenses and actual program eligibility remain distinct from a categorical exclusion of the institution.

Even assuming Gregory's clear-statement principle applies, the enacted public-entity and program language supplies the required clarity. Gregory involved a materially ambiguous express employee exception; it does not demand that Congress list every covered state institution. Ordinary meaning and statutory structure are sufficient. No supposed failure of Congress specifically to anticipate prisons overrides that text.

## Hohn v. United States — No. 96-8986

### Component definitions and operative paths

**H1 — statutory review.** Six Justices support jurisdiction under §1254(1) over the actual appellate certificate denial. A request for permission to appeal presents a judicial controversy over the applicant's asserted entitlement. The panel's resolution is a case in the court of appeals even though denial prevents the underlying merits appeal from proceeding. Section 2253(c)'s condition on taking an appeal does not eliminate the threshold judicial proceeding used to determine that condition. Whether the panel correctly required a certificate is distinct from the existence of its adjudication. The statutory before-or-after-judgment language accommodates review of a threshold decision.

These six would expressly overrule House v. Mayo only insofar as it denies statutory certiorari jurisdiction over such a certificate refusal. House's contrary identification of the case solely with an authorized underlying appeal does not adequately account for the separate judicial request. The original-writ discussion and all other holdings are not overruled or enlarged. Statutory stare decisis is a substantial objection; the answer is the mistaken case-identification premise and its procedural jurisdictional setting, not a policy preference for habeas relief or a claim that House has already disappeared. No claim of later historical overruling or post-divergence practice is used.

**H2 — certificate applicability and temporal law.** Hohn affirmatively argues that Lindh removes the certificate requirement from his pre-enactment §2255 motion. The Government disputes preservation and question scope, and argues that the post-enactment July 29, 1996 appeal is the relevant proceeding. Hohn disputes both objections. These are conflicting submissions, not established forfeiture or agreement. Lindh's application-date rule and express limits must be honored; Felker's post-enactment authorization/review holding must also be preserved.

For the six supporting H1, H2 need not be decided to establish jurisdiction over the actual judicial denial. They reserve both preservation and applicability for lawful consideration on remand. This is not an assumption of jurisdiction: H1 independently establishes it whether the panel required a certificate correctly or erroneously. They do not hold that Lindh requires a certificate, that every appeal is a new case for all AEDPA provisions, or that Hohn forfeited his pending-case argument. The narrower no-certificate route is a live alternative but is not an additional holding or an instruction to treat the appeal as automatically perfected.

**H3 — disposition once H1 is established.** Vacate the appellate certificate denial and remand for reconsideration in light of the Government's changed constitutional characterization and the applicable law. Bousley distinguishes Bailey's substantive statutory meaning from a possible constitutional claim; that distinction prevents treating the origin of a statutory interpretation as itself a conclusive answer to every constitutional claim. It does not establish that Hohn pleaded guilty, that his conviction is unconstitutional, or that his application satisfies §2253(c)(2). The lower court must assess the actual claim and governing procedural law. No certificate, writ, hearing, acquittal, release or vacatur of the trial conviction is ordered. The certificate, if required and later issued, must satisfy the substantial-constitutional-showing condition and identify its qualifying issues.

**HD — dismissal under House.** Two Justices would dismiss this attempted statutory review. They read the certificate condition as preventing the underlying case from entering the court of appeals and view action by circuit judges on the threshold request as insufficient to make it a §1254 case. House supplies a direct statutory construction that remains binding until legitimately overruled; the new certificate terminology and extension to §2255 do not supply the change needed to abandon that construction. Their alternative-path assessment rejects Hohn's temporal route on the merits: the actual appeal began after enactment, and this provision governs that later appellate step. This treats the certificate rule prospectively at the activity it regulates and does not apply a new merits restriction to the earlier district motion. They need not find forfeiture to reach that conclusion.

## Zeran v. America Online, Inc. — June 15, 1998

### Reviewed action and controlling premise

Review is of the Fourth Circuit's affirmance of judgment on the pleadings for AOL. The alleged publication-related omissions arose in 1995; the action against AOL was filed April 23, 1996, after enactment on February 8, 1996. The January 4 action against KRXO is not this suit. Removal timing is disputed. Allegations are accepted under Rule 12(c), not found true after trial. No damages, common-law liability, substantive state-law choice, or independent promise is established.

Landgraf supplies the initial temporal-direction inquiry and, absent sufficient direction, the examination of new consequences for completed events. Rivers rejects purpose or correction alone as temporal direction. Hughes Aircraft applies that inquiry to an amendment affecting a defense and a later-filed action: jurisdictional form and filing date cannot substitute for the consequences analysis. That unanimous substantive holding is especially material to Kennedy, its author, and to every other Associate who joined it. The direction of the change differs here—extinguishing an asserted accrued claim rather than withdrawing a defense—but Landgraf protects existing rights as well as against increased liability.

The original section 230(d)(3) preserves consistent state law and forbids bringing causes or imposing liability under inconsistent state or local law. It does not expressly identify preenactment conduct, accrued claims, pending litigation, or a filing-date transition. Section 230(c)(1) addresses publisher or speaker treatment; section 230(c)(2)'s good-faith restriction protection is a separate provision. Then-subsections (d)(1), (d)(2), and (d)(4) retain their federal-criminal, intellectual-property, and specified communications-privacy limits; definitions were then in subsection (e). No modern amendment or renumbering is used.

## Gebser v. Lago Vista Independent School District, No. 96-1866 — June 22, 1998

### Current rule, supported record, and paths

Kinman has already resolved the recipient-attribution question that Franklin left open. Its controlling rule requires actual notice to an official empowered to correct harassment or its substantial risk, then deliberate and unreasonable disregard of the known danger causing injury or continuation. The rule concerns recipient compensation, not a personal qualified-immunity defense. A reasonable response is not made unlawful solely because harm continues. Kinman's additional circumstantial-awareness formulations were not joined by a majority and cannot be inserted as controlling presumptions.

The principal received an October 1992 complaint about classroom comments, met with Waldrop, received his apology and assurance against repetition, cautioned him, and informed the guidance counselor. He did not report it to the superintendent/Title IX coordinator. The supplied evidence does not identify a report to the principal of the sexual relationship. Gebser made no such report to officials before police discovery; termination followed that discovery. The content and recipient of actual notice, and the response to it, remain separate questions. No rule demands the victim's own report, a written complaint, a confession, or official knowledge of completed intercourse before a known substantial risk can matter.

The evidence about policies is conflicting, and handbook distribution uncertain. Gebser's statement that she probably would have reported if she had known what to do is testimony, not a causation finding. The four do not find those elements proved merely from the existence of a teacher-student relationship. Gebser's reporting testimony and the policy dispute are material evidence under this alternative, not conclusive proof. **Strongest objection to Path B:** Kinman's recent, express five-Justice holding rejected those very substitutes, and Titles VII and IX have different text and enforcement structures. The four must therefore acknowledge their proposal is a renewed disagreement with controlling law, not an application of an unresolved attribution question. They would reconsider the exclusive threshold for the reasons in their prior published position: the intentional discrimination to which Title IX speaks may occur through delegated educational authority, and section 1682's administrative process does not supply a private-suit higher-management prerequisite. ## 1. United States v. Bajakajian — June 22, 1998

### Review scope and supported legal alternatives

The Government's appeal seeks the entire $357,144. Respondent did not cross-appeal the $15,000 forfeiture. **Path A — affirm the refusal of full forfeiture on proportionality.** A criminal forfeiture imposed on the convicted offender as punishment is a fine. Congress's $250,000/five-year maximum and the expressly forfeitable property support a deferential constitutional application; the full amount is not grossly disproportionate. The strongest objection is that this combines a general maximum with the statutory forfeiture authorization to deprive individualized excessiveness review of meaningful work and treats lawful assets too much like criminal proceeds. The judicial comparison must answer the record's lesser culpability assessment, not assume money laundering.

**Instrumentality-only alternative.** Austin leaves relevant considerations beyond instrumentality available, and Alexander independently requires examination of punitive criminal forfeiture in relation to its supporting offenses. ## 2. Pennsylvania Board of Probation & Parole v. Scott — June 22, 1998

### Review scope and supported legal alternatives

The federal exclusion question is decided assuming the violation on which the lower judgment rests. The searchers were parole officers, knew Scott's status, and searched his residence without obtaining consent. The record does not establish a special plan to evade suppression at criminal trial, harassment, effective alternative discipline, or the adequacy of civil remedies. No vote is premised on any such finding. The state-law dispute about construction of the parole condition concerns search validity; the expressly federal suppression ground supplies the presented question. No independent state exclusion rule is inferred.

**Path A — no federal exclusion requirement in the ordinary parole-revocation hearing presented.** The exclusionary rule is a remedial deterrent, not an automatic consequence of every violation. Revocation assesses compliance with conditional liberty through a supervisory proceeding, and excluding probative evidence directly obstructs that task while importing search litigation. Evans, Calandra, and Askin require attention to this additional remedial setting. The legal judgment is that the incremental deterrence from extending criminal-trial exclusion does not justify those institutional costs here, even when the searchers know parole status and are parole officers. The strongest objection is direct rather than speculative institutional incentive: these very officers can use the unlawful fruits for the supervisory consequence they administer, and criminal-trial exclusion need not reach that expected use. Path A must confront that connection rather than assert that parole officers have no enforcement interest.

**Path B — exclusion in this parole-officer/known-status setting.** Exclusion is warranted when parole officers search their supervisee in violation of the Fourth Amendment and the Board seeks the fruits to revoke that person's parole. The institutional link gives the remedy a direct deterrent target absent in Evans's non-law-enforcement-clerk situation. Without exclusion here, the normal foreseeable official use remains available despite the violation. The strongest objection is the loss of reliable evidence in a proceeding concerned with conditional release and the additional costs of resolving search disputes. Path B answers that these costs do not eliminate the ordinary constitutional boundary or justify insulating the institution's own search; it need not require full criminal-trial procedure in every respect or resolve unrelated-police and unknown-status searches.

Evans is not a general good-faith license: its court-employee and objective-reliance predicates are absent. Askin reserves its separate criminal-trial constitutional remedy and does not answer this case in either direction. Fields / Crawley confirms that violation, suppression, consent, independence, attenuation, and reliance remain distinct. It supplies no existing exception or entitlement on these new facts. The competing paths are legal extensions of the remedy, not contrary findings on whether the search was lawful.

## 3. Wisconsin Department of Corrections v. Schacht — June 22, 1998

### Review scope and supported legal alternatives

All eight Associates would reverse the whole-action removal bar and remand to the Seventh Circuit for the personal-capacity appeal. The substantial federal personal-capacity claims supply a federal-question basis independently of the claims against Wisconsin and official-capacity defendants. The sovereign's immunity from particular claims does not erase the court's power to adjudicate the otherwise removable federal claims. A court must respect the actual immunity where properly asserted; it need not treat the entire civil action as outside statutory removal jurisdiction. This determination does not review the personal-capacity merits or reinstate their summary judgment automatically.

**Path A — claim-specific immunity within a removable action.** Sections 1331 and 1441 supply the federal anchor; Hafer preserves the individual defendant/capacity distinction. The immunity of one defendant or claim is not the absence of all original jurisdiction. Section 1447(c)'s lack-of-subject-matter-jurisdiction rule therefore does not require remand of this entire action. The strongest objection is the statutory reference to a removable civil action rather than individually removable claims, coupled with the constitutional characterization of immunity. The answer is that the action actually contains federal claims the federal court may hear, and the immunity attaches at its proper defendant/claim scope instead of eliminating that independent basis.

**Path B — whole-action bar.** Treat an immune federal claim as making the civil action nonremovable in its entirety. This offers a formal reading of the action-wide removal language but fails to explain why a valid federal personal-capacity claim loses its jurisdictional character because it appears beside a claim against the State. It confuses one limit on judicial relief with absence of any statutory original jurisdiction. None of the eight selects it on this record.

**Waiver alternative.** Whether a State's own removal waives its immunity could supply another route in a properly presented case, but no general waiver-by-removal rule is necessary or adopted here. Immunity can be assumed for the specific claim while the remaining federal action survives. ## 5. Swidler & Berlin v. United States — June 25, 1998

### Review scope and supported legal alternatives

The notes record a confidential consultation for legal representation. The client died nine days later. No waiver, crime-fraud predicate, nonlegal character, or criminal defendant's constitutional entitlement has been established. The content of the withheld notes is not found. The question is whether death and substantial grand-jury investigative usefulness suffice to permit the circuit's posthumous balancing exception. Rule 501 supplies common-law development through reason and experience subject to its governing-source qualifications; its separate state-law sentence is not abrogated.

**Path A — privilege survives death without the asserted general criminal-need exception.** Predictable confidentiality protects communication at the time legal advice is sought. Death does not eliminate the living client's reason to care about later disclosure, including effects on reputation and other persons; no empirical claim about this client's subjective motives is needed. The received attorney-client relationship and its continuing protection have a different pedigree from a newly requested privilege. Upjohn supplies predictability and the distinction between communications and independently discoverable facts. Glover's estate-dispute setting serves the deceased client's testamentary intention and is not a general exception for useful evidence. Jaffee's rejection of routine evidentiary balancing supports the same predictability principle, without directly deciding this distinct privilege. The strongest objection is the loss of possibly decisive evidence in a criminal investigation after the client can no longer suffer personal criminal exposure; Rule 501 does not require preservation of a privilege whose function has materially ended.

**Path B — permit a bounded posthumous criminal-need exception.** Death can materially reduce the living-client interests protected by secrecy, and Rule 501's reason-and-experience inquiry permits reconsidering scope. A criminal investigation's substantial need can justify disclosure under the circuit's approach rather than an automatic lifetime-plus rule. The strongest objection is that a case-specific future usefulness assessment is precisely the uncertainty Jaffee identifies, and the decedent's inability to testify makes the communication uniquely valuable without making its prior confidentiality less important. The record establishes neither abandonment nor a constitutional necessity, and a judicially preferred investigation is not by itself an existing exception. A Justice taking Path B must acknowledge that Jaffee remains binding in its psychotherapy field and explain the posthumous attorney-client distinction, not overrule it silently.

The choice does not decide work product. It does not make privilege absolute, prohibit ordinary fact discovery, or resolve waiver, crime-fraud, a properly presented testamentary dispute, or a criminal defendant's competing constitutional right. Nixon's different executive privilege in criminal-trial process does not automatically determine this relationship privilege in grand-jury investigation.
