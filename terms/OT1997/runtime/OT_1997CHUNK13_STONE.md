## C. Issue positions

### Issue 1. Candidate inquiry versus coordination

- **Reach:** Properly before the Court to the extent necessary to review the modified regulation.
- **Stone's position:** Apply the actual expenditure/contribution distinction and the principle that agency power comes from enacted law. A speaker's conversation with a candidate about policy or votes is not, without more, spending undertaken on the candidate's behalf.
- **Proposed holding:** Prohibiting **mere inquiry** under § 114.4(c)(4) exceeds § 441b's permissible treatment of contributions. Actual candidate direction of content, collaboration on distribution, or an agreed service to the campaign may create a legally different transaction.
- **Application:** MRLC's attempt to obtain the meaning of recorded votes is information gathering. There is no factual finding of collaboration making the resulting guide a candidate expense. An agency can investigate the latter without adopting a categorical presumption that the former equals it.
- **Boundary:** The holding is not a right to launder a paid political ad through a staged interview; true coordination remains subject to the statute.

### Issue 2. The written-only contacts condition

- **Reach:** Expressly decided by the First Circuit.
- **Stone's position:** The First Amendment protects political association and communication in the act of gathering and discussing issues, even when the publication is corporate-financed. The FEC may not add a limitation on oral dialogue merely because an audit trail would be convenient.
- **Proposed holding:** In the absence of actual coordination or a congressionally authorized narrow prophylactic rule, § 114.4(c)(5)'s mandate of written questions and written replies is invalid.
- **Reasoning:** Written answers may be good administrative evidence but the Commission has not shown why face-to-face discussion necessarily effects a transfer to a candidate. The agency cannot replace the needed factual distinction with an indiscriminate method-of-contact ban. Commercial treasury restrictions may be permissible under other sources; they do not enlarge this rule's authority.
- **Boundary:** Fraudulent or coordinated services and sham questions intended to supply campaign work can still be regulated on their proved elements.

### Issue 3. Editorial space and prominence

- **Reach:** Expressly decided by the First Circuit.
- **Stone's position:** Apply *Tornillo* and *Hurley* under the source-bound election doctrine. A privately composed issue guide does not owe equal column space to every candidate as a constitutional or default statutory requirement.
- **Proposed holding:** The compulsory equal-space-and-prominence clause is outside the FEC's delegation and burdens editorial control, even when framed as an evenhanded election safeguard.
- **Application:** The FEC may require accurate disclosure where authorized, prohibit a real in-kind corporate contribution, and enforce false reporting rules; it cannot force a private ideological publisher to devote specified pages to a candidate whose positions it criticizes. The distribution of source funds is a legally separate question from forcing a particular text or layout.
- **Boundary:** A government-authored neutral voter guide or voluntary condition on use of a properly defined public resource would raise different sources and is not resolved.

### Issue 4. The unfinished “electioneering message” clauses

- **Reach:** **Not reached on the merits** in this posture. The appellate judgment expressly remanded content, ripeness, and severability.
- **Stone's position:** Enforce the limits of the preserved record. The failure of the FEC to supply a clear operative interpretation is reason for careful lower-court analysis, not a license to infer every possible unlawful application and decide them all.
- **Proposed holding:** No new merits holding. Leave the First Circuit's remand for consideration of whether the clauses can survive independently and what validly promulgated meaning, if any, they bear.
- **Boundary:** The agency may not enforce an undefined broadened category of issue advocacy through mere labels. If later review yields a ripe, supported challenge, the court must resolve it on its own lawful record.

### Issue 5. MRLC's corporate donor funding and *MCFL*

- **Reach:** Not necessary to the controlling statutory judgment; expressly reserved.
- **Stone's position:** Stone's stable source-of-money approach distinguishes voluntary ideological associations from commercial corporate treasuries. But *MCFL*'s actual historical criteria, and MRLC's receipt of some business-corporation funds, prevent pretending the exception is automatically satisfied.
- **Proposed holding:** None as to MRLC's ultimate status. The case resolves the FEC's challenged regulatory means without extending the narrow corporate ideological exception, nullifying *Austin*, or importing later campaign-finance law.
- **Boundary:** If a later case squarely requires corporate categorization, examine the organization's funding sources, voluntary natural-person support, relevant segregation, and the legal test then controlling. Do not invent missing donor proportions here.

**Stone canon note:** This disposition follows the order: delegation, preserved claims, record, remedy. It honors protection of political issue speech while leaving lawful regulation of commercial treasury influence and actual candidate coordination available. It makes no unsupported merits holding from an unfinished remand.

## B. Judgment and remedy

Reject, however, the majority's legal conclusion that reports listing ostensible donors necessarily made the FEC aware, through reasonable diligence, of **Williams's hidden reimbursement**. - **Proposed remedy:** If the required proof yields a timely civil action, restore adjudication of Williams's unresolved defenses and the lawful penalty; reexamine a prospective injunction independently. If diligence or essential tolling fails, dismiss the stale penalty and its merely derivative injunction. No blanket extension of enforcement deadlines.
- **Expected Stone-only outcome:** **Vote to deny certiorari; if review is granted anyway, vote to vacate and remand.** Stone's substantive disagreement with the Ninth Circuit does not, by itself, establish a reason to take the petition.

## C. Issue positions

### Issue 1. A five-year public-penalty deadline is a real deadline

- **Reach:** Necessary statutory rule before applying equitable exceptions.
- **Stone's legal position:** Apply statutory text and the received *Walsh*/*3M* interpretation. Assessment is part of enforcing a penalty; FECA contains no alternative enactment displacing § 2462.
- **Proposed holding:** The five-year clock ordinarily begins when the unlawful transaction occurs, not when the agency chooses to announce “reason to believe” or completes an investigation. No blanket agency-discovery rule applies.
- **Reasoning:** A civil sanction exercises public coercive power. The legislature's choice of repose binds the FEC as much as a private litigant, and delay attributed solely to agency discretion cannot enlarge authority. This conclusion does not answer whether a defendant fraudulently kept facts concealed.
- **Boundary:** Different congressional tolling provisions and independent continuing violations retain their own law; mere continuing effects of a completed contribution are not new violations.

### Issue 2. Concealed source cannot be proven disclosed by a nominal-donor list

- **Reach:** The Ninth Circuit's dispositive rejection of equitable tolling.
- **Stone's legal position:** Apply the received *Holmberg* fraud exception with an actual evidence-based diligence inquiry. No new free-floating agency indulgence is created.
- **Proposed holding:** A campaign report accurately identifying the persons **whose names were used** is not, as a matter of law, notice that Williams was the undisclosed true funder. The agency may invoke fraudulent-concealment tolling if it establishes the concealed transaction, reasonable diligence under the circumstances, and a date at which those operative facts were or should have been discovered.
- **Reasoning:** The majority's contrary inference defeats the fraud doctrine under its own terms: the very mechanism that would mislead a reviewer of ordinary disclosures is treated as the basis for imputing discovery. One cannot demand an audit of every facially ordinary entry as the baseline of diligence without identifying a reason diligence required it. The September 1988 insider complaint is powerful evidence of first actual notice, but it does **not** conclusively establish that an earlier warning could not have been obtained with reasonable care. After that complaint, the agency must account for its lengthy processing and the steps that legal procedure required.
- **Boundary:** Fraud inherent in an offense does not automatically toll every suit. The FEC must show causation and diligence, not simply invoke an enforcement interest. Williams may challenge proof of both.

### Issue 3. Compulsory administrative waiting periods

- **Reach:** If the fraud inquiry establishes a discovery date late enough to matter.
- **Stone's legal position:** Federal law requiring notice and good-faith conciliation before the agency can sue cannot reasonably be read to consume the *full* limitation clock while forbidding a filing. A finite toll limited to the period federal law actually made filing unavailable is a permissible construction of the interaction between § 2462 and § 437g, not an extension for discretionary investigation.
- **Proposed holding:** Suspend the limitations clock for statutorily compulsory notice and conciliation periods proved applicable to this enforcement action; do not suspend it for voluntary internal delay. Determine duration from the statute and established chronology.
- **Application:** Starting in September 1988, the action is roughly thirty-seven days beyond five calendar years; a qualifying mandatory pause could cure that difference. Starting in January 1988, even the Ninth Circuit's maximum 125 days cannot. Because the commencement date and the compulsory-pause question are linked, the majority's refusal to reach the latter does not finally resolve timeliness under the correct inquiry.
- **Boundary:** This does not permit the Commission to initiate investigation years later without a time consequence or manufacture extra conciliation periods at its option.

### Issue 4. Penalty versus preventive injunction

- **Reach:** Only after timeliness and FECA liability are established.
- **Stone's legal position:** Apply *Cope* against an equity workaround of a stale penalty, then independently apply authorized prospective remedial doctrine if the action is timely.
- **Proposed holding:** A ten-year injunction does not follow mechanically from twenty-two historical transactions. The court must identify statutory authority, a concrete basis for prospective prevention, and terms confined to genuinely unlawful conduct.
- **Boundary:** A timely penalty action does not authorize an advisory order forbidding vaguely “similar” lawful campaign activities. If the action is barred, a repackaged derivative injunction cannot rescue it.

**Stone canon note:** The proposed difference from the Ninth Circuit is an evidentiary and legal error in imputing discovery from nominal public reports, together with statutory coordination of compulsory procedures. The five-year statute and the government's burden of proving tolling remain in force. No Supreme Court-wide doctrine is attributed to Stone as existing law.

## C. Issue positions

### Issue 1. The 1996 legislative amendments and Article III

- **Reach:** Threshold issue, taken first.
- **Stone's position:** Apply received mootness law. The revised definitions materially remove the challenged regulation of issue-only speech and nonprofit independent expenditures, and the appellate record supplies no concrete threatened reenactment.
- **Proposed holding:** The claims directed solely at superseded prohibitions are moot. A State's theoretical legislative ability to change its mind is not a continuing live controversy.
- **Record application:** Kentucky changed the text during appeal; the new law no longer governs the speech the old definition had threatened. No past assessment or redressable residual injury to these parties was established for those claims.
- **Boundary:** A credible official threat to reenact, continuing enforcement under old text, or outstanding retrospective relief would require a separate analysis; Stone does not decide their hypothetical merits.

### Issue 2. Nonprofit treasury gifts directly to candidates

- **Reach:** Surviving facial claim.
- **Stone's position:** Apply *National Right to Work Committee* and the direct-contribution distinction retained in *MCFL*. Stone's source-based approach strongly protects a voluntarily supported ideological association's **independent speech**, but does not turn a payment to a candidate into independent expression.
- **Proposed holding:** Kentucky may prevent candidate contributions from KRL's general corporate treasury while permitting its lawful independent issue and electoral advocacy under the amended regime.
- **Reasoning:** The transaction's destination and capacity for candidate obligation matter. KRLPAC remains a voluntary, separately organized path for candidate-directed support. The rule is not an anti-abortion viewpoint exclusion and is not validated merely because the State asserts dislike of large spending; it addresses donor-to-candidate corruption pathways.
- **Boundary:** The holding does not authorize the State to bar independent issue speech or to deny the special *MCFL* treatment of sufficiently voluntary ideological corporations in their own spending.

### Issue 3. Attribution in express independent candidate advertising

- **Reach:** Surviving § 121.190(1) challenge.
- **Stone's position:** Apply election-disclosure scrutiny with special attention to the distinction from *McIntyre*'s anonymous ballot-issue pamphleteering. On this facial record, a sponsor line identifying a paid candidate-election communication materially informs voters and supports policing of coordination without restricting the independent expenditure itself.
- **Proposed holding:** The narrow **candidate independent-expenditure** identification requirement survives this facial attack; the amended statute cannot be applied as a disclosure mandate on ordinary issue advocacy merely because it mentions a candidate.
- **Reasoning:** Kentucky removed the broader issue-ad rule in 1996. Sponsor attribution identifies the accountable purchaser of a candidate-election message and can help connect it to filed transactions. The State's interest is real, though public knowledge of sponsorship is not equivalent to a license to disclose vulnerable associational membership.
- **Boundary:** Stone expressly reserves as-applied relief where an organization proves a reasonable probability of threats or reprisals. *McIntyre* remains meaningful for genuinely anonymous independent issue speech. This is no approval of government permission before publication.

### Issue 4. The $1,000 direct and $1,500 cross-PAC contribution limits

- **Reach:** Preserved facial challenges.
- **Stone's position:** Apply received closely drawn contribution review, including cumulative effects rather than treating each dollar ceiling in isolation. The limitation must serve corruption or its appearance and genuine circumvention prevention, never an effort to equalize speakers' influence.
- **Proposed holding:** The $1,000 per-candidate limit and $1,500 yearly aggregate to all permanent committees survive **on this facial record**. Kentucky may block a donor from using committees as channels to exceed a candidate cap, and the petitioners have not shown the combined rules make effective association impossible.
- **Reasoning:** The per-candidate rule tracks an established federal analogue; state-election scale and local corruption risks are relevant rather than merely rhetorical. The $1,500 cross-committee ceiling burdens multi-organization association substantially more, so Stone does not accept the proposition that its amount is immune from scrutiny. Yet it concerns financing committees empowered to allocate money to candidates, and the record does not quantify a prohibitory effect or demonstrate that its anti-pass-through rationale fails at the facial level.
- **Boundary:** If genuine independent advocacy funds are improperly treated as candidate contributions, or later evidence shows an effectively prohibitory combined cap, this disposition is no shield. Stone rejects a categorical rule that every $1,500 aggregate ceiling is valid everywhere and indefinitely, including through inflation.

### Issue 5. The gubernatorial PAC receipt caps

- **Reach:** Preserved facial claim.
- **Stone's position:** A source-specific limit on total PAC receipts may address dependency on an organized donor channel, but it requires justification tied to corruption, not disapproval of PAC political views.
- **Proposed holding:** Sustain both branches of §121A.030(4) against the record's facial challenge: for a Fund participant, permanent-committee receipts limited to 25 percent of qualifying contributions received, up to $150,000 per election; for a nonparticipant, the separate $150,000 ceiling. Do not decide a later case in which either branch actually disables a candidate's ordinary fundraising or selectively forecloses associations.
- **Reasoning:** The justification does not depend on how many committees must give before a limit binds. A ceiling on the share of a slate's receipts that may come from permanent committees addresses dependency on an organized donor channel. For a Fund participant, tying that share to 25 percent of the slate's own qualifying contributions fits the anti-dependency rationale more closely than a flat figure, because it binds in proportion to the slate's reliance on PAC money rather than at a fixed total. The percentage can bind at lower receipts, before the $150,000 maximum; that operation is the source-specific limit itself, and petitioners did not show on this facial record that it presently prevents effective advocacy or forecloses association. For a nonparticipant, the $1,000-per-PAC ceiling means the $150,000 ceiling restricts gifts only after numerous committees have given. The circuit's one-hundred-and-fifty-committee illustration assumes maximum fundraising and maximum gifts; it is not a premise of this judgment and is not an irrebuttable empirical finding about any gubernatorial race.
- **Boundary:** No new constitutional numerical safe harbor. The cumulative effect of ceilings, party structures, inflation, and actual candidate reliance can matter in a developed as-applied claim, including one by a participating slate whose qualifying contributions are small enough that the percentage branch disables ordinary fundraising.

**Stone canon note:** The position distinguishes source and transaction, independent expenditure and contribution, candidate advertising and issue speech, and facial judgment and future proof. It does not silently convert Stone's elections chapter into governing simulated law.

## C. Issue positions

### Issue 1: A court cannot add an unprinted original-source deadline

- **Reach:** Statutory jurisdiction/threshold first.
- **Received circuit rule and Stone's proposed change:** The Sixth Circuit required a relator to alert the government **before public disclosure** and adopted a broad “based upon” rule. Stone rejects that **circuit construction** where it moves the enacted deadline: § 3730(e)(4)(B) says voluntary delivery to government **before filing the relator's action**. He would not add a prior-publicity condition or equate every independently known fact with a complaint derived from another source. He does not purport to erase the separate textual requirements of direct and independent knowledge or voluntary government disclosure before suit.
- **Proposed holding:** “Based upon” must be assessed by whether the allegations actually derive from public disclosures in the legally relevant sense, not solely whether accusations about similar misconduct overlap. An actual original source with direct independent knowledge does not lose statutory status just because another person informed the public sooner, provided the statutory government-submission requirement is met.
- **Application:** McKenzie's dispatcher experience and early internal objections are evidence she may have independently known how repair reports were falsified. The court nevertheless established that allegations in her complaint closely resembled other public suits and news items. Neither fact alone settles which information her federal fraud allegations actually derive from or whether she voluntarily informed the government **before filing** as the statute specifically requires. Her under-seal filing and subsequent government service cannot simply be assumed to satisfy a prefiling duty. Remand is necessary.
- **Boundary:** The decision creates no private suit from a statute lacking one, no exemption for copied complaints, and no presumption that BellSouth defrauded the government.

### Issue 2: Corporate liability and employee protection are statutory

- **Reach:** The separate retaliation cause survives if the pleaded protected conduct, employer knowledge, and adverse response can be proved.
- **Proposed holding:** The § 3730(h) employment remedy does not rise or fall solely with jurisdiction to maintain the government's distinct qui tam claim. Internal actions that reasonably signal contemplated reporting or a potential FCA claim can be protected under the then-effective statute.
- **Application:** The allegations of persistent objections plus drawing supervisors' attention to litigation involving comparable federal fraud provided enough notice at dismissal stage. Whether the reactions actually caused covered retaliation remains to be determined.
- **Boundary:** Stone gives BellSouth no corporate privilege against enforcement and imposes no penalty because it is large. Statutory misconduct and causation, not corporate wealth, determine liability.
