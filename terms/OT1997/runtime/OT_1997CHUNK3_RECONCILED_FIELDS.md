## What the full comparator establishes

**Personal interests and initial observation, Part I.A–B, 113 F.3d at 320–22.** Fields paid $125 weekly, visited 40–50 times over six months, held a key, could enter in Warner's absence and bring guests. Crawley was a repeated invited visitor, had once slept there, and was present for several hours with Fields and Warner's permission; the reported occasion was not an intended overnight stay. Warner respected their closed bedroom door. The circuit rejected the proposition that drug activity itself destroys a privacy interest and recognized both defendants' own interests. This is actual lower reasoning, not a controlling extension of Olson to every business visitor.

The police could not see this bedroom from the street or sidewalk. They entered the fenced rear yard and then the fenced side yard. The side yard was shared with other tenants and partially enclosed by chain link; no exact survey, gate condition, permission, or exclusive defendant control is established. A plainly visible five-to-six-inch gap below the blinds exposed a well-lit room. Officers needed neither tiptoes nor leaning against the house, and used no visual enhancement. Those are affirmative reported facts, not facts inferred from missing papers.

The panel did more than assert that any blind gap extinguishes privacy. It discussed the officers' vantage, recognized that trespass ordinarily can defeat an anticipated-observer premise, rejected trespass as an automatic constitutional rule, and relied on other tenants' access without the defendants' permission. It distinguished the vacated Blount decision by both shared multiunit access and the larger, easily closed gap. The remand position must confront this reasoning; it cannot describe the panel as having ignored the vantage question altogether. Its objection is that the panel treated these facts as sufficient without adequately distinguishing limited residential sharing from the particular outsider observation, and then inferred absence of subjective privacy. Conversely, affirmance need not adopt the panel's final subjective-expectation formulation. It can rest on objective exposure of these activities from this actual shared location while recognizing that the defendants desired privacy in the room.

**Entry and announcement, Part I.C, at 322–23.** The reliable informant described bagging, a vehicle and possible departure. Police corroborated the car and then observed the activity. By about 9 p.m., although a warrant would take more than an hour and none had been sought, they planned a uniformed front knock while a rear team positioned to intercept flight or enter. Before the planned knock, a man on the back porch called “5-0,” went inside and closed the door. Officers knew Fields's violent history, believed he might be armed, and feared destruction of the observed readily disposable cocaine. They immediately rammed the locked common rear door and passed through the apartment's open door, shouting “police.” The later flight inside and loaded weapon found beneath a mattress cannot retroactively justify the initial force.

The panel used clear-error review for the district court's exigency determination, considered its circuit's illustrative factors, and mentioned the gun actually found. It likewise used “no clear error” language in sustaining the announcement exception under Wilson and Richards. The reconciled affirming route therefore supplies its own legally adequate, pre-force rationale; it does not absorb the after-entry gun into what officers previously knew or simply copy the panel's review formula. The remanding route recognizes the warning as serious potential urgency but does not settle final legality while lawful observation and possible causal dependencies remain unresolved.

**Later warrant and consent.** A warrant arrived about ninety minutes after the apartment was secured. Warner received a rights explanation and signed consent during the securing interval. The panel expressly declined to decide whether exigency or that after-the-fact consent independently justified the initial observation. Neither it nor the published recitation establishes that the warrant decision and issuance were independent of any antecedent illegality, that all evidence was inevitably discoverable, or that consent eliminated any taint. The warrant's existence and the signature are evidence to assess under the appropriate rules, not retrospective authorization.

**Excluded branches.** Informant disclosure, crack/cocaine sentencing, Fields's fine and the jury-pool ruling remain outside review. Reading them to identify the full opinion's disposition creates no authority to revisit them. There is no inference of a litigant's waiver, forfeiture, concession or default from the research absence of original papers. The published account supports a bounded suppression decision under Engine §14; it does not support invented affidavit content or an order to create a missing litigation record.

## Governing premises, own-writing tests and concrete corrections

**A personal substantive right, not a threshold fiction.** Rakas, Jones as preserved in Rakas, and [Olson, 495 U.S. 91, 95–100](../research/chunk3_late/olson_cap.txt), protect an individual's supported expectation outside a formally owned home. Fields's sustained paid control is substantially stronger than mere legitimate presence. Crawley's circumstances require a distinct judgment: Olson's precise holding concerns an overnight guest, while its host-sharing explanation supports but does not compel a limited extension. Assuming Crawley's substantive privacy interest before rejecting an intrusion is not hypothetical Article III jurisdiction. Existing convictions and preserved suppression challenges supply the actual controversy; Lee does not convert this merits inquiry into a speculative-injury dismissal.

**Exposure depends on the observation actually made.** [Ciraolo, 476 U.S. 207, 211–15 and notes 1–3](../research/chunk3_late/ciraolo_cap.txt), which Stevens and O'Connor joined, distinguishes normal privacy precautions from the objective reasonableness of excluding observation from public navigable airspace. It does not make investigative focus itself decisive, and its limited issue excludes a separate photographic challenge. [Riley, 488 U.S. 445](../research/chunk3_late/riley_cap.txt), adds materially different eligible positions. Scalia and Kennedy joined the plurality's actual naked-eye aerial observation analysis. O'Connor's concurrence asks about sufficiently regular public use, not mere compliance with aviation regulations, and expressly places the reasonable-privacy burden on the defendant. Stevens joined Brennan's dissent challenging hypothetical public availability and the allocation of the particular empirical aerial-use burden. Neither aerial case makes this ground-level residential side yard automatically public, and neither requires police to avert their eyes from genuinely exposed activity.

The [Dunn majority, 480 U.S. 294, 300–05 and notes 1–4](../research/chunk3_late/dunn_cap.txt), joined by Stevens and O'Connor, uses proximity, enclosure, actual use and screening as contextual tools rather than a mechanical or first-fence rule. Its lawful vantage was outside the house's curtilage in open fields; that result cannot simply be relabeled a holding that every shared apartment yard is an open field. Scalia's concurrence at 305, withholding the “Third” paragraph, distinguishes actual domestic use from officers' perceptions of it. Knowledge or suspicion of criminal activity does not turn a house into something other than a house. The shared-access affirmance must therefore rest on this ordinary line of sight and reasonable exposure, not on an unsupported finding of noncurtilage or the officers' belief that drug preparation was occurring.

Actual Pinson and Myers protect otherwise unobservable concealed domestic information while preserving an exposed-view distinction. They do not require every naked-eye observation to be valid, every police sensor to be a search, or every private activity to be completely sealed from every possible observer. Scalia, Thomas, Souter and Ginsburg's own joins matter alongside Stevens and O'Connor's; Kennedy and Breyer's published disagreement with the thermal-search premise is relevant evidence but does not displace the actual controlling law. The lower panel's visibility reasoning supplies a serious distinction; it does not itself settle the location from which exposure was obtained.

**Review method and urgency remain separate.** Ornelas's existing allocation governs ultimate reasonable suspicion for the stop and probable cause for the nonwarrant search. The proposed entry-exigency extension rests on the analogous need for a stable constitutional boundary and independent assessment of legal sufficiency, while preserving historical findings, credibility, genuinely factual subsidiary inferences and genuine due weight. It does not declare every mixed question legal. Kennedy's Olson concurrence is concrete counterevidence: he expressly deferred to that state court's fact-specific exigency application without endorsing it. His subsequent Ornelas and Richards joins support the later functional extension, but the change of analytic reach must be stated rather than reading de novo exigency review back into Olson. Scalia's Ornelas dissent and Richards method nonjoin support retaining his objection to this new extension.

Richards already governs the separate announcement inquiry: particularized reasonable suspicion of danger, futility or threatened destruction at the moment force begins, independently reviewed as to ultimate sufficiency. Neither its lower quantum nor the “5-0” warning independently supplies the probable cause and exigency required for warrantless home entry. A shout indicating police presence is relevant, but it need not establish that every occupant already knew the officers' identity, authority and purpose so completely that announcement was necessarily futile. The affirming position can rely on the concrete danger and destruction inference without announcing that broader futility holding. Earlier time to seek a warrant, the tactical plan and the sequence of police conduct remain facts; no categorical police-created-exigency rule, universal duty to obtain a warrant at the first opportunity, or finding of deliberate evasion is invented.

**Remedies require their own predicates.** [Murray, 487 U.S. 533, 537–44 and notes 1–3](../research/chunk3_late/murray_cap.txt), requires genuine independence of both the decision to obtain the warrant and issuance from tainted information. Scalia wrote that opinion; it rejected automatic reliance on an affidavit's omission of the prior entry and required adequate district-court findings. Stevens and O'Connor joined Marshall's dissent emphasizing the need for verifiable independence and the danger of warrant-bypass incentives. Kennedy did not participate. These are eligible actual positions; neither silence nor appointment at a later time supplies another Justice's historical vote.

**Specific correction to the frozen model:** its Stevens paragraph attributes a “separate concern with the remand's factfinding” to his Murray separate dissent. The full source does not support that description. Stevens's separate dissent at 551–52 reiterates his objection to Segura and to incentives for unconstitutional home entries; the inadequate-findings/remand discussion is in Scalia's majority at 543–44. The reconciliation removes that mistaken attribution. Stevens's actual separate dissent and joined Marshall dissent still support scrutiny of an asserted after-entry warrant cure, while governing Murray controls the available doctrine. His outcome does not change.

Wilson, Richards, Pinson, Myers and Ornelas do not make exclusion, a new suppression hearing or plea withdrawal automatic. Consent requires its actual authority, scope and voluntariness predicates and, if relevant, separate taint analysis; a warning and signature alone do not answer those questions. No new universal suppression holding is proposed. Fields was tried to a jury; Crawley entered a conditional plea. Their paths must remain distinct, and a limited corrective remand alone does not mean Crawley has yet prevailed on a reserved suppression question under Rule 11(a)(2).
