## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Statutory jury right under § 504(c)

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone interprets § 504(c) before reaching the Seventh Amendment.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Section 504(c) authorizes an award the court considers just but does not independently confer a statutory jury entitlement.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Section 504(c) does not itself confer a jury right.
- **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The enacted provision establishes the availability and range of statutory damages but contains no express jury command. That conclusion leaves the constitutional allocation question unavoidable.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Congress’s statutory allocation does not by itself settle the Seventh Amendment decisionmaker.

### Issue 2: Seventh Amendment allocation of statutory-damages facts

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the constitutional allocation because Feltner timely demanded a jury and the judge made the pertinent factual determinations.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Copyright damages have a historical legal analogue, and determining willfulness and selecting compensatory or punitive monetary relief within the statutory range perform traditional jury functions.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** When timely demanded, the Seventh Amendment assigns factual issues pertinent to copyright statutory damages, including willfulness and the amount within the lawful range, to the jury.
- **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Markman’s historical-and-functional method points to the jury for these monetary facts. Tull’s treatment of a governmental civil-penalty amount does not transfer copyright damages to the judge. The trial judge may interpret the statute, identify the range, rule on legal sufficiency, and review the resulting award through constitutionally permissible procedures.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** This does not assign injunctions, fees, costs, equitable accountings, or historically judicial questions to juries, and it does not eliminate lawful judicial review of the award.
