## Calderon v. Ashmus

**Alignment and disposition.** 9–0. Rehnquist's entire Court opinion is joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Breyer concurs, joined by Souter (pp. 749–750). Ninth Circuit judgment, 123 F.3d 1199, reversed and remanded **with instructions to dismiss the complaint**.

**Grounds and limits.** This class action sought only an advance ruling about California's eligibility for Chapter 154's expedited federal habeas procedures, rather than a final determination of any prisoner's entitlement to habeas relief. An anticipatory ruling on a potential affirmative defense would isolate a collateral issue that must await another suit for complete resolution; it is not the required Article III case or controversy. *Aetna Life* permits conclusive declaratory relief in a concrete controversy; *Coffman* and *Wycoff* bar this collateral determination. *Preiser* also explains why allowing the action would bypass the habeas exhaustion structure. *Steffel* involved a concrete threat of prosecution that declaratory relief would completely resolve, unlike the pending or future habeas controversies here.

California was undisputedly a unitary-review State; the class comprised capital prisoners whose convictions were affirmed on direct appeal after June 6, 1989. Chapter 154 carried a 180-day filing limit and tighter amendment provisions; Chapter 153 carried a one-year limit. The Court did **not** decide California's Chapter 154 eligibility, the Eleventh Amendment/Ex parte Young issue, or officials' First Amendment challenge to the injunction. The Eleventh Amendment is not coextensive with Article III limitations (p. 745 n.2).

**Breyer/Souter.** Their unqualified join rests on the understanding that some prisoners can obtain a relatively prompt answer in actual habeas litigation: timely bare-bones petitions may present amendment questions whose resolution turns on opt-in eligibility, and a qualifying legal ruling might receive §1292(b) interlocutory review. This is a possible procedural route, not an assured extension, guaranteed amendment, or holding that California is ineligible.

## New Jersey v. New York

**Alignment.** 6–3. Souter's entire opinion is joined by Rehnquist, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Breyer concurs with Ginsburg (pp. 812–814). Stevens dissents alone (pp. 814–829). Scalia dissents with Thomas (pp. 829–832). The two dissents would recognize New York's authority over all the Island but use distinct grounds; Scalia does not adopt Stevens's prescription analysis.

**Components and remedy.** The six-Justice Court rejects all three New York exceptions: Article Second ownership of later fill, prescription/acquiescence, and laches. It rejects New Jersey's high-water-line and pier exceptions, but sustains New Jersey's exception to a judicial convenience adjustment. New Jersey holds sovereignty over later filled land; New York retains the original Island to its **mean low-water line**, including the historical pier area found to be filled land in 1834. The case is **recommitted to the Special Master to prepare a proposed decree**. The May 26 opinion does not itself supply a final surveyed decree or authorize a compromise boundary.

**Material facts and controlling grounds.** The original Island was roughly three acres; federal filling added about 24.5 acres. Article First supplies the general midriver sovereignty boundary; Article Second recognizes New York's jurisdiction over the existing islands. In the Compact's silence, ordinary avulsion principles leave later artificial fill with the sovereign of the submerged land, New Jersey; ordinary gradual accretion is distinct. *Central Railroad* supplies the distinction between underlying sovereignty and Article Third's jurisdiction for navigation and commerce. New York did not press broader independent Article Third claims offered by amici, and the Court did not decide those unpressed alternatives.

Prescription required New York to prove by a preponderance long, continuous sovereign possession/assertion and New Jersey's knowing acquiescence, with actual notice or sufficiently open, notorious, visible, uninterrupted adverse acts. No fixed minimum duration was established. The possible period was 1890–1954, shorter for later fill. New York's title to the original Island made unspecific references to “Ellis Island” equivocal. Federal occupation limited both New York's sovereign acts and the notice reasonably attributable to New Jersey. Vital records, voting records, residents' impressions, and federal usage did not carry the burden: sparse or equivocal acts and inconsistent federal positions fell short. Federal occupation was **not** held categorically to prevent state prescription; that legal question was left open. Laches could not excuse New York's failure to prove its affirmative prescription claim; the Court did not adopt a categorical rule that laches can never apply to a compact dispute.

The low-water boundary follows the ordinary sovereign-boundary rule rather than the Master's reliance on unsuccessful 1827 negotiations. The 1819 map and expert evidence supported the finding that the 1834 pier rested on fill, rather than merely pilings. Congressional consent makes the Compact federal law; *Texas v. New Mexico* precludes relief contrary to its terms merely for convenience or equity. A more convenient line requires agreement of the States with congressional consent. The extent of federal enclave jurisdiction and the precise extent of New York's retained jurisdiction over the original Island were **not decided** (p. 779 n.2).

**Breyer/Ginsburg.** They agree that relevant silence in Articles First and Second invokes background avulsion law rather than creating ambiguity. More substantial ambiguities concern provisions on which New York did not rely. Virtually exclusive federal control made it unreasonable to expect New Jersey to protest each modest New York assertion; an easy prescription finding on this evidence would be unfair in later cases. They do not join the broader proposition that silence can never require contextual construction.

**Stevens.** He accepts the Court's legal rules but disagrees with its assessment of documentary and uncontradicted testimony. Viewed as a whole, censuses, electoral districts and actual voting, vital records, marriages, addresses, governmental services, judicial practice, and federal treatment showed more than sixty years of a shared understanding that the whole Island lay in New York. He infers New Jersey's notice from the public character and nearby location of those acts; the small number of surviving examples does not negate consistent practice. He treats the contrary federal examples and New Jersey labor representations as isolated, mistaken, or ultimately abandoned. Federal control did not remove the importance of state citizenship or census allocation. Prescription therefore vested New York with authority over the whole Island. The inconvenient, building-splitting boundary reinforces his criticism of the majority's factual inference, rather than supplying a separate judicial power to redraw an otherwise fixed boundary.

**Scalia/Thomas.** They agree with Stevens about the parties' long practical understanding but expressly decline to meet prescription's demanding burden. The Compact read as an integrated whole is ambiguous about added land, especially when Article Third's commerce/navigation purpose informs Articles First and Second. Subsequent conduct is evidence of the contract's meaning, as in *Air France v. Saks*, *Choctaw Nation*, and *Vermont v. New Hampshire*. State and federal practice therefore establishes New York's jurisdiction by **interpretation**, not by ousting an otherwise undisputed New Jersey title. Scalia also would consider New York City's Article Third argument rather than restrict consideration to the State's presentation.

## Air Line Pilots Association v. Miller

**Alignment and disposition.** 7–2. Ginsburg's Court opinion is joined by Rehnquist, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas. Breyer dissents, joined by Stevens (pp. 880–886). D.C. Circuit judgment, 108 F.3d 1415, affirmed, leaving its remand for judicial consideration intact.

**Grounds and scope.** Nonmembers who never agreed to union-provided arbitration need not exhaust it before suing over an agency-fee calculation. *Hudson* supplies a protective opportunity for prompt impartial review, not compulsory arbitration; ordinary arbitration rests on consent. *McCarthy*'s deference to congressionally established administrative processes does not justify compulsory exhaustion of a private process. The efficiency objection is met by normal specificity, discovery and case management: the employee must object with specificity appropriate to the litigation stage, while the union retains the burden of proving germaneness. An objector does not acquire an entitlement to an entirely general complaint followed by unexplained demands to prove every expenditure. Escrow does not eliminate the enforceable interest in timely resolution (n.4).

Only the 1992 fee-calculation claim was before the Court. Facial challenges to the agency-shop clause had been resolved against the pilots and were not appealed. There were 153 respondents: 91 in the disputed arbitration and 62 not parties to it. The arbitrator found nongermane expenses of 21.49%, rather than the Union's 19%. Hudson's full transfer to RLA employment was an **unchallenged premise**, not a newly contested resolution of its scope. The decision concerns objectors who have not agreed to arbitrate. Footnote 6 expressly preserves district courts' discretion to defer discovery or other proceedings pending a prompt arbitration, under balanced docket management; access to court does not compel every stage to proceed immediately.

**Breyer/Stevens.** A reasonably prompt, impartial, **nonbinding** arbitration-first rule is a permissible elaboration of Hudson's balance between objectors' rights and the union's ability to collect legitimate fees. Escrow protects against ideological expenditures; subsequent court proceedings remain available without preclusive effect or special legal weight for the award. The process could reduce duplicate expense, conflicting determinations and burdens that consume fee receipts. Consensual binding arbitration is the wrong analogy; court-required nonbinding dispute resolution is closer. A judicial pause in discovery could alleviate many concerns, but it does not answer whether the union itself may require initial recourse.

## Kiowa Tribe of Oklahoma v. Manufacturing Technologies, Inc.

**Alignment and disposition.** 6–3. Kennedy's Court opinion is joined by Rehnquist, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, and Breyer. Stevens dissents, joined by Thomas and Ginsburg (pp. 760–766). Oklahoma Court of Civil Appeals judgment reversed.

**Grounds and limits.** Tribal immunity is federal law, alterable through congressional authorization or tribal waiver, and applies to suits on governmental or commercial contracts made on or off a reservation. Liability under substantive state law and amenability to suit are distinct; *Potawatomi* illustrates that distinction. *Nevada v. Hall* does not make tribal immunity optional state comity, because tribes did not participate in the States' constitutional mutual concessions. The Court criticizes the doctrine's weak origins in *Turner*, recognizes its later holding in *USF&G*, and acknowledges modern commercial and involuntary-victim concerns. It nevertheless follows existing immunity decisions and leaves comprehensive adjustment to Congress. It does not judicially create a commercial-activity or territorial exception, and it does not decide a tort claim.

The note was for $285,000 plus interest and preserved sovereign rights. Its face recited Carnegie, on tribal trust land; respondent asserted execution/delivery and payment obligations in Oklahoma City. The Court expressly called some record details vague. The judgment makes that location dispute immaterial to the contract-immunity rule; it does not establish respondent's asserted execution location as a factual finding. There was no applicable abrogation or waiver.

**Stevens/Thomas/Ginsburg.** Off-reservation conduct ordinarily falls under nondiscriminatory state law under *Mescalero*. Earlier immunity holdings concerned on-reservation or sovereign-function disputes and did not settle a suit without such a nexus. Extending a judge-made immunity rule would preempt state authority without an identified federal interest or statute/treaty; Congress's power to change the rule does not justify judicial enlargement. The result is anomalous compared with domestic and foreign sovereign commercial liability and unjust, particularly for involuntary tort victims unable to bargain for a waiver. They would leave state courts to determine comity for this off-reservation commercial suit; they do not call for wholesale abolition of established tribal immunity.

## County of Sacramento v. Lewis

**Judgment and topology.** 9–0 reversal of 98 F.3d 434, but the Court opinion has **six** joins: Souter, Rehnquist, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg, Breyer. Rehnquist concurs alone (pp. 855–856); Kennedy concurs with O'Connor (pp. 856–858); Breyer concurs alone (pp. 858–859). Stevens concurs **only in the judgment** (p. 859). Scalia concurs **only in the judgment**, with Thomas (pp. 860–865). None of the three judgment-only Justices joins Souter's merits methodology. Breyer joins the opinion while expressly preserving flexibility over the order in which immunity and constitutional questions are addressed.

**Facts, components and limits.** The 75-second, 1.3-mile pursuit reached 100 m.p.h.; Smith followed as close as 100 feet, while stopping at that speed would require about 650 feet. After Willard's motorcycle tipped in a turn, Smith braked but struck passenger Lewis at about 40 m.p.h. The collision was not an intentionally applied means to stop Lewis. Therefore *Brower* and *Hodari D.* preclude a Fourth Amendment seizure, and *Graham*'s more-specific-provision principle does not exclude a substantive due process claim. The Court does not treat every police pursuit as a seizure or every physically abusive act as covered by the Fourth or Eighth Amendment.

For the sudden high-speed chase at issue, aimed at arresting a suspected offender and requiring instant judgments under conflicting responsibilities, only a purpose to cause harm unrelated to legitimate arrest satisfies the conscience-shocking threshold. Negligence is always insufficient; the constitutional sufficiency of intermediate culpability is context-specific. Deliberate indifference can suffice for pretrial custodial medical needs, where actual deliberation and provision for a dependent person's welfare are practical. The emergency pursuit more closely resembles *Whitley*'s prison-disturbance setting. “Actual deliberation” is not the instantaneous technical homicide-law use of deliberation (n.11). No specific evidence supported the complaint's malice language; the record's strongest actual claim was conscious/reckless disregard. There was no showing that Smith sought to terrorize, injure, kill, or induce the lawlessness.

Municipal §1983 claims had been resolved for the county and sheriff's department below and were **not before the Court** (n.2). The merits ruling leaves aside qualified immunity. It expresses no view on a procedural due process claim based on inadequate postdeprivation remedies (n.4), and no view that the conduct is proper under tort law or police policies (n.14). In n.8, the Court treats conscience-shocking executive conduct as a necessary threshold; it does not decide whether satisfying that threshold also requires historical recognition of the asserted right. Do not convert the chase holding into a universal intent-to-injure requirement for all executive action.

**Rehnquist.** Given the petition and briefing's substantive-due-process premise and the alternatives actually presented, conscience-shocking conduct is the correct choice and was not established.

**Kennedy/O'Connor.** They fully join, stressing objective constitutional analysis. Loss of life plainly implicates a textual interest. The troubling subjective connotation of “shocks the conscience” must be disciplined by tradition, precedent, historical understanding, and objective law-enforcement needs. History is a starting point, not invariably the ending point. The police require latitude to pursue suspects who disobey lawful commands, absent intent to injure in circumstances such as these; an opposite rule might encourage flight and danger. No comprehensive causal-participation rule is adopted.

**Breyer.** He fully joins the Court's opinion but agrees with Stevens that *Siegert* should permit lower courts, in appropriate cases, to avoid difficult or poorly presented constitutional questions by deciding qualified immunity first.

**Stevens.** He would reinstate the district court's judgment solely because the asserted right was not clearly established in 1990. The constitutional question is difficult and unresolved; avoidance is preferable. Suits against municipalities, which lack qualified immunity, can develop constitutional law. He does not decide that Smith's conduct either violated or complied with the substantive Constitution.

**Scalia/Thomas.** Applying *Glucksberg*'s careful description and historical inquiry, they find no textual, historical or precedential support for the claimed right against reckless/deliberately indifferent pursuit. Custodial-indifference precedents combine indifference with the State's affirmative restraint disabling self-care; they do not create the asserted chase right. The Court's subjective conscience test and legislative/executive distinction are criticized. Constitutionalizing ordinary causation and tort questions would displace democratically chosen state policy. They reverse on that distinct merits ground, not qualified immunity and not a join in the majority's conscience-shocking framework.

## United States v. Cabrales

**Alignment and disposition.** 9–0, Ginsburg for a unanimous Court; no separate writing. Eighth Circuit judgments, 109 F.3d 471 and 115 F.3d 621, affirmed.

**Grounds and limits.** Counts II and III alleged transactions violating §§1956(a)(1)(B)(ii) and 1957 entirely within Florida. The Missouri source offense created the funds but was not a laundering act constituting those charged crimes. Under Article III, the Sixth Amendment, Rule 18 and *Anderson*, venue follows the acts constituting the charged offense. Section 3237(a) does not make these Florida-only transactions offenses begun in Missouri. No allegation in these counts charged conspiracy, aiding and abetting Missouri trafficking, or interstate transport of the funds by Cabrales. *Hyde* involved conspiracy and overt acts; *Palliser* involved mail sent into another jurisdiction; neither supplies Missouri venue here. Convenience of proof does not alter the conclusion.

The indictment alleged a $40,000 Florida deposit and four $9,500 withdrawals during January 1991. The separate **Count I conspiracy charge remains pending in Missouri and was not part of the appeal**. Missouri prosecution of that conspiracy remains possible if proved, with the Florida laundering as overt acts. The Court leaves open the different possibility of a continuing offense where the launderer herself acquires funds in one district and transports them to another. It does not announce a rule that predicate conduct can never bear on venue under another offense or a differently pleaded theory.

## United States v. Beggerly

**Alignment and disposition.** 9–0. Rehnquist for a unanimous Court. Stevens concurs, joined by Souter (pp. 49–50), expressly joining the Court's opinion without reservation. Fifth Circuit judgment, 114 F.3d 484, reversed and remanded for further proceedings consistent with the opinion.

**Independent-action component.** An independent action in the court that rendered the original judgment does not require a new independent jurisdictional basis: Rule 60(b)'s history and *Pacific Railroad* preserve its ancillary character. But relief is reserved for a grave miscarriage of justice, consistent with *Hazel-Atlas* and *Marshall*, lest it nullify Rule 60(b)(3)'s one-year limit. Failure thoroughly to search government records and fully disclose material concerning the alleged Boudreau grant does not approach that standard. Whether the allegations could support a timely Rule 60(b)(3) motion and whether the grant evidence would change the original outcome are expressly undecided (p. 47 and n.4).

**QTA component.** The 12-year period under §2409a(g) begins when the claimant or predecessor knew or should have known the Government's claim, not when the claimant discovers proof supporting her own title. Additional equitable tolling is inconsistent with that accrual rule, the unusually generous period, and certainty in land ownership. The Beggerlys knew the Government's claim by 1979; the later action was filed June 1, 1994. The original 1982 settlement paid $208,175.87 and quieted title for the United States. Research in 1991 yielded material said to show a 1781 Spanish grant; the Court does not adjudicate its ultimate title effect.

**Stevens/Souter.** They distinguish additional equitable tolling, rejected here, from fraudulent concealment or equitable estoppel where outrageous governmental misconduct prevents a claimant who knows the Government's claim from discovering her own. Such doctrines conceivably could apply in that hypothetical, but the Court properly does not reach it. The concurrence supplies no finding of such misconduct and no general exception granted on this record.
