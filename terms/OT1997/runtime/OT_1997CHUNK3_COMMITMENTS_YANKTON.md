## Enhanced comparison: cession and the savings clause

**Path A — remove the ceded, unallotted settler tract.** Article I presently cedes, sells, relinquishes and conveys all tribal claim, right, title and interest in the specified unallotted lands. Article II commits the United States to $600,000 for them, with actual appropriation and payment arrangements. The Government purchases the land rather than merely selling for the Tribe and remitting uncertain proceeds. The preamble describes surrender of portions of reservations; Article VIII repeatedly treats the lands as already ceded and sold while directing settler disposition. That combination supplies the affirmative territorial choice to which Solem and the retained DeCoteau principle give exceptional weight.

Article XVIII is a substantial countertext. It says that nothing in the agreement abrogates the 1858 treaty, preserves all its provisions as though the agreement had not been made, and separately secures annuities. It cannot truthfully be quoted as saying “annuities only.” The strongest objection to Path A is that title and territorial reservation status are separable: the sale and settlement can be given effect without treating the original boundary as abrogated, so necessity does not by itself justify withdrawing the ceded area.

The provisional conclusion under Path A is that the Act's specific, comprehensive surrender and unconditional purchase identify the ceded tract as withdrawn from the reserved territory, while Article XVIII preserves the treaty relationship and obligations not displaced by that defined transaction. Its general preservation cannot be literal in every respect, because the treaty's protected possession and exclusion of settlers cannot coexist on the sold tract with the commanded transaction. The limiting accommodation is grounded in the specifically described ceded land, not a conclusion that every treaty promise vanishes. The original line does not remain a reservation boundary over that land; continuing treaty protections, annuities and retained interests are not thereby cancelled. This reading uses the positive cession-and-purchase provisions as evidence of the territorial consequence; it does not infer that consequence merely from the logical impossibility of retaining exclusive possession.

The negotiation record explains the importance of preserving federal promises and annuities, but supplies no finding that every signatory discussed or agreed upon the precise future boundary. The commissioners' description of the end of common-property bonds and contemporary requests for state and federal law corroborate the specific-cession reading without independently proving every jurisdictional consequence. Article XVII's permanent liquor rule separately mentions ceded and other lands, and the specific state school-land disposition is consistent with withdrawal, but both admit alternative readings and are supplementary. No operative restoration to the public domain appears in this Act. Subsequent state administration and settler use confirm a reading first established by the Act; conflicting labels and uncertain population figures are not independent grounds.

**Path B — preserve territorial boundaries despite the sale.** The best competing reading gives Article XVIII direct jurisdictional work beyond preserving payments. Congress expressly retained all treaty provisions; a proprietary transfer and permission to settle do not necessarily require removing land from a reservation. On this account the unusually broad savings clause is the affirmative statutory reason to overcome Solem's strong cession-and-price inference. Agency and school retention, allotment protections and continuing institutions fit a sale within retained boundaries, and the absence of boundary discussion makes an inferred territorial surrender less secure. This path respects the important distinction between fee title and reservation status.

Its principal weakness, for the provisional commitments below, is that it treats the present, comprehensive cession and outright federal purchase as insufficiently distinct from the sale-and-disposition mechanism that Solem found compatible with retention, despite the additional statutory surrender language. The savings provision must be reconciled with the specific land surrender as part of the same enacted bargain; it cannot make that combination's presumptive territorial meaning disappear without weighing it. The specific transaction supplies the clear congressional choice for the tract under review, while the clause preserves treaty obligations outside that consequence. No individual commitment below treats the cession presumption as conclusive: the competing clause is examined at full breadth and found reconcilable in that limited way. An Act expressly preserving the jurisdictional boundary over the ceded land would present a different construction.

**Auxiliary statutory provisions.** Article VIII's agency and school sites are called ceded but withheld from settler sale; their status is separately reserved here. Article XVII's liquor rule can do independent work under either boundary reading and does not by itself establish withdrawal. The school sections' express subjection to South Dakota law and the enabling Act's reservation exclusion support the State's structural argument, while a later specific grant could also have operated as a confined exception. Neither reading makes those secondary clauses dispositive. Allotment protections and provisions for continuing institutions show that the transaction did not simply erase every protected land interest or tribal institution. No unverified comparison to DeCoteau's appended instruments supplies the missing analytical step.
