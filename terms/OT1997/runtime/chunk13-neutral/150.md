## A. Case, record, and posture

- **Case name:** *Kentucky Right to Life, Inc. v. Stengel*, No. 97-60.
- **Alias below:** *Kentucky Right to Life, Inc. v. Terry*, 108 F.3d 637 (6th Cir. 1997). The respondent official's name changed for the Supreme Court caption. Plaintiff group includes Kentucky Right to Life, Inc. (KRL), Kentucky Right to Life PAC (KRLPAC), Robert Zoeller, and PAC treasurer Lora Robertson.
- **Entry stage/event:** Petition for certiorari after the Sixth Circuit's March 7, 1997 affirmance. It entered the Court's OT 1997 petition inventory; merits review has not been granted.
- **Question presented for modeling, reconstructed:** Whether Kentucky's restrictions on nonprofit corporate candidate contributions, source identification for independent election expenditures, a $1,000 candidate contribution ceiling, a $1,500 annual aggregate ceiling for gifts to all permanent political committees, and a $150,000 total gubernatorial candidate receipt limit from those committees are consistent with the First Amendment, and which earlier claims remain live after 1996 statutory amendments. No original petition's verbatim question has been authenticated.
- **Judgment below:** The district court granted the State summary judgment or dismissed certain claims as nonjusticiable. The Sixth Circuit held claims concerning superseded restrictions moot to the relevant extent, rejected the remaining First Amendment facial challenges, and affirmed.
- **Relief requested:** Petitioners seek reversal of adverse merits conclusions and, potentially, reinstatement of challenges the appellate court regarded as moot; any requested injunction would operate only against provisions or applications held unlawful.
- **Parties and activity:** KRL is a nonprofit ideological corporation advocating against abortion; KRLPAC is a distinct political committee; Zoeller has donated to them and wishes to do so freely. The entities engage in rallies, fundraising, issue advocacy, and support of or opposition to candidates. Defendants are Kentucky officials administering and enforcing campaign finance law.
- **Material state measures:** Kentucky Revised Statutes §§ 121.025 (corporate candidate contributions), 121.190(1) (paid independent-expenditure sponsor identification), 121.150(6) and 121A.050(1) ($1,000 direct contributions to a candidate per election year), 121.150(10) ($1,500 yearly donations by an individual to all permanent committees together), 121A.030(4) ($150,000 aggregate gubernatorial candidate receipt from all permanent committees).
- **Changed law:** Kentucky's 1996 amendments narrowed “permanent committee” to expressly electoral organizations, excluded independent and issue expenditures from the definition of contributions, allowed nonprofit independent spending and corporate issue communications, confined advertisement sponsor identification to independent election expenditures rather than pure issue speech, and raised the direct candidate contribution ceiling from $500 to $1,000. The Sixth Circuit treated several earlier attacks as moot and did not adjudicate the old statutory text on its merits.
- **Threshold and preservation:** Some early definitional theories had failed in district court for absence of threatened enforcement. The 1996 legislative amendments created distinct mootness barriers. A mere legislative possibility of future reenactment was not shown to be an announced plan. The remaining challenges were facial; evidence of actual inability to conduct effective political advocacy is not established in the appellate record.
- **Key dates:** Original suit in fall 1995; legislative changes in 1996 while appeal was pending; Sixth Circuit argument November 13, 1996 and decision March 7, 1997; OT 1997 begins October 6, 1997.
- **Related:** *Buckley v. Valeo*, *FEC v. National Right to Work Committee*, *FEC v. Massachusetts Citizens for Life*, *California Medical Association v. FEC*, and *McIntyre v. Ohio Elections Commission* supply preexisting reference points. This is Kentucky state-election legislation, not direct review of an FEC federal rule.

## B. Law entering the case


### Issue 1. Moot claims after genuine statutory amendment

- **Controlling simulated law:** Unverified. Public Article III mootness remains the provisional anchor.
- **Other governing law:** *Kremens v. Bartley*, 431 U.S. 119 (1977); *Burke v. Barnes*, 479 U.S. 361 (1987); *Massachusetts v. Oakes*, 491 U.S. 576 (1989). Amendment removing the challenged restraint ordinarily ends a purely prospective challenge; *City of Mesquite v. Aladdin's Castle*, 455 U.S. 283 (1982), recognizes a material reenactment risk in its particular circumstances.
- **Open point:** Whether there is a concrete, non-speculative likelihood that Kentucky will restore the superseded definitions or prohibition, or any remaining injury under the old enactment.

### Issue 2. Nonprofit corporate direct gifts versus independent speech

- **Controlling simulated law:** Unverified.
- **Received public doctrine:** *FEC v. National Right to Work Committee*, 459 U.S. 197 (1982), sustained federal corporate solicitation/contribution safeguards, including against a nonprofit organization. *MCFL*, 479 U.S. 238 (1986), protected a distinctive ideological nonprofit's **independent expenditures** under its facts but distinguished direct contributions. *Buckley*, 424 U.S. 1 (1976), treats contribution limits differently from independent expenditure limits.
- **Other governing law:** Ky. Rev. Stat. § 121.025, as narrowed by the 1996 revisions concerning issues and independent expenditures.
- **Open point:** Whether petitioners prove that remaining restrictions operate on genuine uncoordinated issue communications, rather than direct candidate contributions covered by received contribution doctrine.

### Issue 3. Paid electoral advertisements' source identification

- **Controlling simulated law:** Unverified.
- **Received doctrine:** *Buckley* permitted appropriately tied disclosure of campaign finance information to serve electoral accountability and detect improper influence. *McIntyre v. Ohio Elections Commission*, 514 U.S. 334 (1995), protected anonymous independent advocacy in a ballot-measure leaflet context, requiring attention to the burden of **on-ad disclaimers** rather than equating them automatically with filed reports.
- **Other governing law:** As amended, Kentucky § 121.190(1) distinguishes independent candidate-election expenditures from issue advocacy.
- **Open point:** Whether compelled on-ad identification is sufficiently fitted to accountability in the candidate-election category and whether any demonstrated threats or reprisals require an as-applied exemption.

### Issue 4. Direct, aggregate PAC, and gubernatorial receipt caps

- **Controlling simulated law:** Unverified.
- **Received doctrine:** *Buckley* upheld direct candidate contribution limits and allowed anti-circumvention controls; *California Medical Association v. FEC*, 453 U.S. 182 (1981), recognized regulation of gifts to multicandidate committees. The governing distinction is the restriction of contribution rather than an independent expenditure, with prevention of quid pro quo corruption or its appearance and evasion as relevant interests.
- **Other law:** $1,000 individual or PAC-to-candidate limit, $1,500 donor-to-all-PACs limit, and $150,000 gubernatorial candidate aggregate PAC receipts.
- **Open point:** Whether the aggregate restrictions are facially too low in a Kentucky state-and-local setting, especially when donor-to-PAC aggregate gifts have a smaller ceiling than gifts to multiple individual candidates. The Sixth Circuit's abstract scenario for the governor's limit is not a factual finding about every future candidate.

## C. Decision-material litigation positions

**Mootness:** Kentucky says the amendments removed the allegedly prohibited issue expenditures, nonprofit independent spending, overbroad PAC definition, and mandatory source labels on issue-only ads. Petitioners stress the State can reenact the old terms and chilling effects can survive repeal. The Sixth Circuit found no governmental intent to reenact; on the record, the first contention is strong, but preserved injury from a completed violation would require separate facts.

**Corporate direct candidate contributions:** The State relies on accepted prophylactic regulation of money delivered to candidates, even when a nonprofit makes the gift. Petitioners invoke *MCFL*'s recognition that an ideological membership corporation differs from an ordinary commercial firm. The critical distinction is that *MCFL* addressed independent expenditures and did not establish a blanket exemption for direct corporate gifts.

**Disclaimers:** Petitioners rely on *McIntyre* and the constitutional value of anonymous political advocacy. The State distinguishes election-candidate advocacy from issue and ballot speech and identifies interests in informing voters of financial sponsorship and exposing disguised contributions. The appellate court sustained a disclosure obligation limited by the 1996 amendment; whether the State proved a need for attribution **on each advertisement** rather than through reports remains a serious merits question.

**Contribution ceilings:** Kentucky points to documented corruption in its history, source- and candidate-directed contributions, the *Buckley* federal $1,000 analogy, and the capacity of multiple PACs to circumvent per-candidate caps. Petitioners stress associational burden from $1,500 across **all** permanent committees even where the donor may give to many candidates individually. The Sixth Circuit held that anti-circumvention and smaller local election budgets supported the rules; it deemed the $150,000 gubernatorial ceiling unlikely to bind absent donations from over 150 committees at their individual maximum.

## D. Lawful paths and uncertainty

1. **Mootness first:** Dismiss only the parts of the challenge the 1996 amendments genuinely removed; adjudicate remaining contribution and disclaimer claims if a live controversy continues.
2. **Affirm remaining facial judgments:** Distinguish direct contributions from independent speech, preserve a limited sponsor-identification requirement, and hold these monetary ceilings not shown facially unconstitutional while reserving supported as-applied claims.
3. **Reverse a discrete provision:** A court could find the $1,500 cross-PAC donor ceiling too burdensome relative to its circumvention rationale or find the on-ad identification obligation inadequately tailored, without undoing other provisions.
4. **Narrow or remand:** If required facts concerning the effect of aggregate ceilings or as-applied retaliation are missing, decline a blanket judgment and remand only a properly preserved component.

There is no permission to assume that a facially challenged limit actually prevented these petitioners' advocacy, or that state corruption facts establish every form of regulation. The posture may support a more limited disposition than a comprehensive ruling on campaign finance.
