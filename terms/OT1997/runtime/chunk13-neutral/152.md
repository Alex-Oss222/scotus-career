## A. Case, record, and posture

- **Case:** *Libertarian Party of Florida v. Smith*, 687 So. 2d 1292 (Fla. 1996).
- **Supreme Court petition:** No. 96-1867. **Term:** October Term 1997. **Simulation:** petition proposed for grant; merits date unassigned.
- **Question presented, reconstructed:** Whether Florida may rebate part of candidates' filing fees to executive committees of political parties with at least five percent of the State's registered voters, while denying the rebate to smaller recognized parties, consistently with First and Fourteenth Amendment political association and equal protection.
- **Judgment below:** Florida Supreme Court approved the First District Court of Appeal decision sustaining Fla. Stat. § 99.103(1) (1993).
- **Record:** Libertarian Party, its executive committee, and a House candidate sought declaratory and injunctive relief. The statute returned a portion of filing fees paid by candidates of major parties to those parties for expenses. Parties under the five-percent voter-registration threshold received no rebate. The challengers conceded the initial candidate fees were lawfully collected. A Libertarian candidate had satisfied the separate requirements to appear on the ballot.
- **Relief sought:** Declaration that the rebate distinction is unconstitutional and an injunction against its discriminatory operation.
- **Threshold:** Federal question is reviewable from a state court of last resort. Independent state free-association claims were considered coextensive in this case; distinguish entitlement to government funds from access to the ballot.
- **Dates:** Florida Supreme Court decision December 5, 1996; rehearing denied February 17, 1997.
- **Participation:** None established.

## B. Law entering the case

The controlling First and Fourteenth Amendment inquiry includes *Anderson*, the Court's *Burdick v. Takushi* (1992) decision measuring actual burdens against precise state interests, and *Norman v. Reed* (1992), which protects otherwise qualified minor-party candidates against unjustified cross-component disqualification. Neither decision requires states to give every political organization identical financial support.

### Issue 1: Equal treatment in election financing

- **Governing law:** First and Fourteenth Amendments; *Anderson v. Celebrezze*, 460 U.S. 780 (1983), and *Burdick v. Takushi*, 504 U.S. 428 (1992), use burden-sensitive scrutiny for voting and election administration. *Buckley v. Valeo*, 424 U.S. 1 (1976), permits public election financing schemes with objective demonstrated-support distinctions, subject to constitutional restrictions.
- **Other law:** Florida statute distinguished qualifying parties based on registered-voter share, rather than candidates' individual ballot access.
- **Open point:** Whether using major-party registration to distribute a percentage of qualified candidates' compulsory filing fees is legitimate electoral finance or state-maintained institutional advantage after the ballot-access gate has already been passed. *Buckley*'s approval of specific demonstrated-support public financing does not answer that different mechanism automatically.

## C. Decision-material litigation positions

- **Below:** The Florida court treated the rebate as financial support, not a ballot-access prerequisite, and found a sufficiently reasonable relationship to fostering established parties and preventing factionalism.
- **Petitioners:** A tax- or fee-funded political benefit to two dominant parties converts an already-qualified smaller party's candidates' fees into an institutional advantage denied to that smaller party. Ballot qualification has already established a nonfrivolous candidacy.
- **Respondents:** Subsidizing stable parties through a neutral registration threshold is permissible, no candidate is removed from a ballot, and the First Amendment supplies no right to equal public assistance for every party.
- **Limit:** A neutral threshold need not distribute money equally; a State may not use an ostensibly minor subsidy as a disguised ballot exclusion or viewpoint sanction.

## D. Lawful paths and uncertainty

1. Affirm: rebate is a limited public financing allocation, not a fee on voting or ballot eligibility, with no record of severe burden.
2. Reverse the categorical rebate preference if it treats already-qualified minor-party participation as an insufficiently valuable form of political association and serves no support-related interest other than maintaining major parties' fiscal advantages.
3. Remand for impact findings if the comparative value of rebates is indispensable. The record quoted by the state high court does not quantify a party-specific constitutional injury sufficient for such findings to be assumed.
