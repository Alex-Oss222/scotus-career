## A. Case, record, and posture

- **Case:** *Ino Ino, Inc. v. City of Bellevue*, 132 Wash. 2d 103, 937 P.2d 154 (Wash. 1997).
- **Supreme Court petition:** No. 97-858. **Term:** October Term 1997. **Simulation:** petition proposed for grant; date for merits unassigned.
- **Question presented, reconstructed:** Whether municipal restrictions on adult cabaret dancing, hours, visibility, spacing and licensing violate federal free-speech protections, and to what extent the Supreme Court may review state-court dispositions resting independently on the Washington Constitution.
- **Judgment below:** Washington Supreme Court affirmed much of Bellevue's regulatory ordinance; reversed the ruling sustaining a 14-day managerial-license processing period without temporary licenses as a prior restraint **under the state constitution**; sustained a narrowed construction of the outdoor-performance prohibition; remanded on attorney fees.
- **Record:** Bellevue regulated adult cabarets by four-foot spacing for table dances, eight-foot stage distance, lighting, closing hours from 2 a.m. to 10 a.m., licensing of dancers/operators/managers, and restrictions on outdoor performances. Adult-club corporations and three performers challenged the ordinance under federal and Washington speech and other protections. They gave evidence that near-patron performances supplied income and conveyed a message; the City relied on prevention of sexual contact and secondary effects.
- **Relief sought:** Invalidity of federal speech restrictions and related relief; petitioners also prevailed below on one independent state constitutional claim.
- **Threshold:** Adequate and independent state-law ground, separate state and federal claims, distinct natural-person speakers and institutional operators, preservation of state-law attorney-fee issues.
- **Dates:** Washington high court decided May 1, 1997.
- **Participation:** No recusal identified.

## B. Law entering the case

Federal expressive-conduct and licensing rules are governed by the decisions in force at the petition stage. Washington's separate free-speech clause provides an independently sufficient basis for the narrower ruling against the 14-day manager-license processing period; the state high court's interpretation of that clause cannot be displaced on federal review.

### Issue 1: Protected expression and neutral conduct regulation

- **Governing law:** *Barnes v. Glen Theatre, Inc.*, 501 U.S. 560 (1991), recognizes nude dancing's expressive component subject to valid conduct regulation; *United States v. O'Brien*, 391 U.S. 367 (1968), examines incidental burdens on expression; *City of Renton v. Playtime Theatres*, 475 U.S. 41 (1986), addresses adult-business secondary effects; *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781 (1989), deals with manner restrictions.
- **Open point:** Whether spacing and hours remain genuinely secondary-effects regulations and leave expressive activity realistically available.

### Issue 2: Licensing and prior restraint

- **Governing law:** *FW/PBS, Inc. v. City of Dallas*, 493 U.S. 215 (1990), addresses procedure for licensing sexually oriented businesses; First Amendment and Washington Const. art. I, § 5, are separate legal sources.
- **Open point:** The Washington high court identified an independently sufficient *state* basis for the managerial-license ruling; federal certiorari cannot be used to extinguish that independent protection.

### Issue 3: Federal fee remedy

- **Governing law:** 42 U.S.C. §§ 1983, 1988; prevailing-party fee recovery for federal civil-rights violations cannot automatically be grounded in success solely under the state constitution.

## C. Decision-material litigation positions

- **Below:** Regulations addressed sexual contact and other secondary effects; most survived federal standards. But a manager could be unable to operate for 14 days with no temporary licensing, an impermissible restraint under Washington's independent free-speech clause.
- **Petitioners:** Distancing eliminates economically viable table performances, suppresses erotic content under a conduct label, and licenses expression through potentially obstructive process.
- **City:** Objective distance, lighting, hours, and licensing measures prevent contact and regulate secondary conduct; state law can preserve an additional safeguard without federal invalidation of the rest.
- **Limit:** Economic losses of a venue are relevant to actual speech availability but do not create a constitutional exemption from all generally applicable safety rules.

## D. Lawful paths and uncertainty

1. Affirm federal rulings on spacing and other conduct measures if supported by secondary-effects evidence; leave independent state invalidation untouched.
2. Reverse a specific federal restriction if the record reveals content suppression or complete loss of a meaningful expressive outlet.
3. Dismiss any federal challenge to the independent state-constitutional licensing judgment for want of power to alter it; remand fee questions solely under their proper source.
