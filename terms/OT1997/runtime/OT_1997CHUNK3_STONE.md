### Issue 1: Constitutional taxation — nonresidents and alimony

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** May New York deny nonresidents an alimony deduction that it allows residents when calculating tax on income earned in New York?
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Article IV, §2 Privileges and Immunities Clause; substantial justification and the relationship between the discrimination and that justification.

Here the State must explain the actual treatment of resident and nonresident income and deductions. Reich governs any withdrawal of a clearly offered refund remedy after payment.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The Commerce Clause cases are analogies; they do not replace the Article IV test.

### Issue 1: Capital sentencing — mitigation instructions

* **Stone's Legal Position:** Eighth Amendment mitigation principles under Lockett, Eddings, and Boyde; assess the charge as a whole and whether there is a reasonable likelihood of forbidden application.

* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Stone would examine what this jury was legally allowed to do, not insist on preferred wording. If the actual charge lacks that route, Stone must follow those reversals; a preference against detailed instructions cannot overcome them.

Texas require an effective route for the jury to give material mitigation its independent culpability significance; admission of evidence and partial relevance to dangerousness alone are insufficient. Delo allows a supporting-evidence requirement for a particular mitigating instruction, and Tuilaepa rejects mandatory numerical weights or labels. The question here is whether the whole charge already supplies the effective route that was absent in Graham and Johnson. If an actual constitutional error is found, revised Brecht retains the State’s Chapman burden on the properly presented collateral claim; historical Brecht’s more lenient standard is not simulated law.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The actual transcript must support the unrestricted-consideration premise; the jury cannot be barred from giving effect to relevant mitigation.

* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** No rule compelling waiver of a valid privilege, and no presumption that every unsuccessful denial is dishonest.

## B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

* **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Reverse and remand with instructions to deny enforcement of the challenged Board order.
* **Stone's Remedy and Remand Position:** The Board may not enforce this order on the theory that Allentown was required to satisfy the existing good-faith reasonable-doubt threshold before conducting the safeguarded poll. In future cases the Board may regulate polling through a lawful standard tied to interference, restraint, coercion, or another source-grounded statutory concern, and may retain or strengthen procedural safeguards against threats, reprisals, promises of benefit, coercive atmosphere, or unfair labor practices. The Court does not authorize retroactive substitution of a new, more demanding polling threshold against Allentown in this proceeding.

## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Validity and application of the Board's employer-polling standard

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the validity of the polling standard because the granted challenge fairly includes whether the Board required an unlawfully high showing before permitting the poll. That statutory ground is sufficient to reverse the order, so Stone does not decide whether the First Amendment independently invalidates the rule. He also adopts the substantial-evidence ground as an independently sufficient fallback preserving the same judgment if the Court sustains the Board's standard.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Stone rejects the Board's existing unitary good-faith reasonable-doubt threshold for employer polling. The Act protects collective bargaining, but it also protects employees' freedom to choose whether an incumbent union continues to represent them. After the period of conclusive majority support has expired, the Board may prevent coercive or manipulative polling, but §§ 8(a)(1) and 8(a)(5) do not justify making a properly safeguarded poll practically unavailable unless the employer already possesses essentially the same evidentiary showing needed for the more consequential act of withdrawing recognition. Polling, a Board election, and unilateral withdrawal of recognition have materially different legal consequences. Treating them as though they require the same predicate is insufficiently connected to the Board's asserted concerns about disruption and employee choice. Stone would preserve the Board's authority to require neutral purpose, notice, assurances against reprisal, secret ballot, advance notice of the poll, and absence of coercive unfair labor practices, and would permit the Board to formulate a different source-grounded threshold prospectively. He would not preserve the present standard merely to favor either employers or unions.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** The NLRB may regulate employer polling to prevent interference, restraint, coercion, or manipulation of employees' representational choice, but it may not condition a safeguarded poll on the same stringent good-faith reasonable-doubt showing used for materially more consequential actions when that requirement makes polling practically unavailable and lacks an adequate basis in the Act. The challenged order therefore may not be enforced.
- **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The Board's authority must trace to the Act's protection of employees, not to a free-standing preference for bargaining stability. Here the ALJ found compliance with the Board's polling safeguards, the poll was conducted by secret ballot under independent supervision, and the record supplied no finding that the poll itself threatened, restrained, or coerced employees. Once the conclusive period protecting the incumbent union had expired, a neutral poll could serve employee choice by testing whether the union still possessed majority support. By contrast, withdrawing recognition immediately changes the bargaining relationship, and a Board election carries distinct statutory consequences. Those differences undermine the Board's decision to impose one stringent predicate across materially different acts. Stability remains legitimate, but it cannot make a rebuttable presumption of majority support practically untestable. The Board may regulate genuinely coercive polling and may formulate a lower, rationally fitted threshold, but the existing unitary standard exceeds what this record and the Act justify.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Stone does not invalidate certification bars, contract bars, conclusive presumptions during their lawful duration, the Board's authority to prohibit threats, reprisals, promises, coercive questioning, or unfair labor practices, or the distinct standard governing unilateral withdrawal of recognition. He does not hold that every employer may poll at will or decide whether the First Amendment independently requires broader protection.
- **Fallback Actually Held:** Even if the Court sustains the existing good-faith reasonable-doubt standard, Stone would still deny enforcement because the Board's finding that Allentown lacked such a doubt is not supported by substantial evidence on the whole record. The Board discounted materially probative employee statements and effectively demanded proof approaching actual loss of majority support, rather than applying the uncertainty standard it announced. This fallback preserves the same judgment and does not validate the Board's polling rule as Stone's own position.

## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Deliberate observation from a fenced nonpublic vantage

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches whether the officers’ deliberate vantage-point observation was a search.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Officers conduct a search when they deliberately obtain a view into a substantially screened bedroom from a fenced, nonpublic vantage unavailable from the public way. Limited visibility from an area accessible to cotenants is not general publication, and plain view requires lawful presence and access.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Officers conducted a search when they crossed the fenced, nonpublic side yard to obtain a deliberate view into the substantially screened bedroom.
- **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Fields’s repeated paid access and key, together with Crawley’s permitted presence, supported a reasonable privacy interest in the bedroom. The officers deliberately used a restricted vantage to obtain interior information unavailable from the public way.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** This does not recognize privacy in every apartment common area or restrict observation from a public or lawfully accessible vantage.

### Issue 2: Police-created exigency and later entry

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches whether the asserted evidence-destruction risk justified the later warrantless entry and leaves the remedial consequences for remand.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Once officers possessed probable cause, they could not create their own warrantless-entry justification by choosing a tactic designed to expose or flush the occupants rather than pursuing a warrant. A warning and movement foreseeably produced by that plan do not establish an independent exigency.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Officers may not justify warrantless entry by an evidence-destruction risk foreseeably produced by their decision to alert the occupants instead of pursuing a warrant.
- **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The officers sought no warrant and instead executed a roust-and-entry plan whose predictable effect was to warn the occupants. The resulting movement therefore cannot supply the emergency the plan was designed to create.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** This does not prohibit entry for an independent safety emergency, hot pursuit, or destruction risk not created by police. Suppression, consent, independent source, attenuation, and the later warrant remain for remand.

## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Privacy Act — continued retention

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Reach the statutory construction question only after establishing jurisdiction over Lindblom's claim. Initial collection is not challenged; continued maintenance is the conduct at issue.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Section 552a(e)(7) requires examination of continued maintenance. Initial pertinence does not conclusively answer whether the information remains pertinent to and within the scope of an authorized law-enforcement activity. The proposed construction is set out in the complete test below.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Reject the collection-only construction and remand for application of the Continuing Pertinence Test. Do not find the actual records unlawful without the required record-specific assessment.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Congress regulated maintenance as well as collection. The lawful acquisition of information at one time does not by itself establish the statutory justification for maintaining it at every later time. A closed investigation can still have records that retain a qualifying connection; the inquiry concerns the actual remaining relationship. The contents and function of the challenged records, including any justified continued indexing or use, require assessment under the test. The supplied record does not permit a categorical finding that all retention is lawful or that all records must be destroyed.

### Continuing Pertinence Test

Proposed by Justice Stone for OT1997

1. **Does the challenged record fall within the statutory protection?**

   The plaintiff identifies records describing their First Amendment activities and establishes that the government continues to maintain them. The court first checks whether retention is independently authorized by statute or by the individual.

2. **Can the government identify an authorized law-enforcement activity?**

   The agency must establish the legal authority for that activity. It need not prove the plaintiff committed a crime. Intelligence investigations, criminal investigations, and authorized civil enforcement may qualify.

3. **Does the information remain pertinent to that activity?**

   The court examines the actual relationship, not a hypothetical future possibility. Relevant considerations include:

   - Why the information was collected.
   - What investigative purpose it still serves.
   - Whether it preserves necessary context or evidence.
   - Whether subsequent events have changed its relevance.
   - Whether continued use or indexing exceeds the justified investigative scope.

   No single consideration automatically decides the case.

4. **What disposition and remedy does the law authorize?**

   If a qualifying connection remains, the agency may retain the information under the statutory exception. If no qualifying connection remains, the court determines the violation and available relief under the Privacy Act.

   The court separately accounts for lawful evidence-preservation and federal records-management duties.

### Issue 2: First Amendment standing

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Decide standing on the evidence at summary judgment; do not decide the constitutional merits after finding standing absent.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Leave the separate constitutional standing dismissal undisturbed on this record.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The Foundation did not establish the causal mechanism connecting the FBI's maintenance of records to the predicted behavior of grant-seekers or prospective employers. Lindblom supplied no separate factual basis for his own constitutional standing. The result rests on the evidentiary insufficiency of these claims and does not declare that reputational or associational injury is categorically inadequate. It does not dispose of the distinct statutory claim by assumption.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** No general right to deletion, automatic damages remedy, constitutional merits ruling, or presumption that lawful political association establishes criminal wrongdoing is adopted. The statutory cause of action, jurisdiction, record-specific application, and available relief remain governed by their respective legal requirements.
