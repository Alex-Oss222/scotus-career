## Corrected record retained

The State taxes' invalidity remains fixed. Cotton Petroleum establishes that concurrent State and tribal taxation was possible; the established invalidity of these excessive taxes does not mean the State lacked all authority to tax the extraction. The United States intervened as trustee in June 1983. The 1988 ruling treated the original reservation-tax approval as covering the ceded-strip minerals and Interior's purported exclusion as legally mistaken. Its distribution ruling concerned the registry funds under the 1982 agreement; it did not adjudicate a pre-agreement Westmoreland tax debt.

Interior's actual refusals, Westmoreland's pass-through problem and its unwillingness to pay without approval remain commercial and procedural facts. They cannot be restated as a conclusive holding that the Tribe lacked lawful taxing authority. The lessee's unpreserved refund claim, the agreement's treatment of its liability, the Tribe's separate claim against the State and the registry distribution remain distinct. The agreement does not establish that the Tribe released its independent State claim. Severance-tax receipts at issue precede the January 1983 registry deposits; gross-proceeds receipts precede the November 1987 deposits. The two periods must not be collapsed.

The operative pleadings seek restitution and interest. No compensatory-loss claim or proved loss amount is supplied. The existing record does not authorize an invented decision allowing a proposed amendment, a new market-injury finding, or an automatic additional monetary award.

## Comparator test and correction of the refreshed three judgments

All historical Justices rejected the Ninth Circuit's compulsory transfer of the entire collection. The disagreement concerned how far the Court's reasoning would restrict consideration of a lesser restitution claim. The full separate opinion is decisive: Souter expressly accepted reversal of the whole-collection award because Montana could tax below the excessive level, while arguing that the Tribe's existing prayer encompassed partial disgorgement of excessive receipts. He considered the same 1988 approval ruling, the Tribe's ownership of the coal and the difference between its own restitution claim and a taxpayer refund. Those premises supported keeping partial relief available, not affirming the entire award.

The newly supplied 1988 ruling and June 1983 intervention correct an incomplete neutral account. They are not changes to the historical facts on which the comparator Justices acted. The majority itself recites the approval ruling and explains its limited distribution scope in note 9. Stevens and Breyer joined its rejection of total disgorgement on that record; Souter's separate writing independently rejected total disgorgement. The refreshed three affirmances therefore have no supported changed historical premise. They are corrected here to reversal of the compulsory whole-collection remedy, while retaining the substantive concern for the Tribe's independent federally protected interest.

The entering law does not supply a different result. United States v. California separates economic burden, direct entitlement and derivative refund claims; it does not award all invalid taxes to the owner of an affected resource. Fulton's remedial provisions preserve claimant-specific, procedurally lawful relief and do not require a predetermined refund. Chickasaw Nation's fuel-incidence rule does not convert every preemption violation into an entitlement to all taxes paid by a different taxpayer. These authorities allow careful treatment of the Tribe's own claim but provide no Justice-specific reason to disregard Cotton Petroleum's distinction between all receipts and excessive receipts.

## Proposition-level alignment and exact remand options

All eight support reversal of the Ninth Circuit's mandatory transfer of the entire preregistry collection and remand. All eight support the narrow proposition that invalidity of the excessive taxes, the Tribe's lawful tax coverage and its protected interest do not alone establish a right to every dollar Westmoreland paid. No Justice revives the invalid taxes, disturbs the completed registry distribution or reinstates Westmoreland's lapsed taxpayer refund claim.

Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer support rejection of the established full demand and leave any further course to the Federal Rules and the trial court's lawful handling of the case. This is permission, not a direction to award damages, reopen evidence, allow amendment or award a specified lesser amount. The lack of a pleaded and proved compensatory measure prevents an appellate compensatory award on this record; it does not establish that the Tribe suffered no injury.

O'Connor and Souter support that judgment and its concurrent-taxing-power ground, but require substantive room for the existing partial restitution claim. They would permit the lower courts to determine the excessive share and weigh restitution of **all, some or none of that excessive share**, with the registry receipts and other equities considered. This is not permission to recover all State taxes simply by labeling all of them excessive, nor a conclusion that an additional penny is owed. Their independent property-interest rationale does not require a dollar-for-dollar compensatory-loss finding. Their disagreement with the historical majority concerned foreclosure of that inquiry, not whether the original entire-collection direction should survive.

The shared controlling draft may stop at the common narrow rejection and expressly preserve proper consideration of independent partial relief. Eight votes for that limited proposition do not establish eight votes for O'Connor and Souter's affirmative restitution framework, or for reasoning that treats Interior's refusal as a dispositive legal disability. If additional grounds effectively foreclose the partial claim, the two Justices' refusal must remain visible in the opinion topology and in a substantive separate position. Authorship remains for assembly under the assignment rules.
