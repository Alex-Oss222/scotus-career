## Enhanced competing paths

### Statutory noncompliance and the original waiver

All eight accept the conceded defects under §626(f)(1)(B), (F), and (G): no ADEA-specific reference, fourteen rather than at least twenty-one consideration days, and no seven-day contractual revocation opportunity. A general awareness or actual-consultation theory cannot establish original compliance. The text, full statutory appendix, and exact entering-law text supply the affirmative requirements and §626(f)(3)'s validity burden. A hypothetical newly compliant waiver or paragraph (2) settlement is not in the record. No Justice needs to decide an additional attorney-advice defect, group-program requirements, every other statutory condition, or actual misunderstanding.

### Ratification path A — statutory conditions remain unsatisfied

The six reversal supporters treat “may not waive” and “unless at a minimum” as conditions on the release's effect on the ADEA claim. Mere retention supplies none of the missing protections and cannot give the same relinquishment binding effect by relabeling it a later affirmation. Even accepting the lower court's reported later-awareness premise, the legal defect remains. The strongest contrary argument is that ratification traditionally addresses precisely those instruments unenforceable at formation and that Congress did not name the doctrine. They reject it because its asserted operation circumvents the statute's concrete conditions; Congress need not list every contract label for enforcing a defective ADEA waiver. The ruling does not determine all possible ratifications, settlements, or non-ADEA consequences.

Scalia also rejects ratification **here**, but by a distinct route: retained common-law ratification cannot operate while the original impediment remains. Knowledge cures a concealed fraud or mistake in an appropriate case; learning that the OWBPA safeguards were omitted does not insert those safeguards, just as prior knowledge of their omission would not make execution compliant. He does not infer that the statute abolished ratification generally. This supplies a narrow common conclusion with the six, without resolving their tender disagreement.

### Ratification path B — later informed affirmation of a voidable release

Thomas distinguishes the statutory requirements for an original knowing-and-voluntary waiver from common-law affirmation after discovery of the defect. The reported retention after awareness and decision to sue is the relevant lower-court premise, not an invented date of legal advice, new signed promise, or independent factual finding. Common-law preservation principles, surviving Astoria, Isbrandtsen, and Norfolk Redevelopment as discussed in his full comparator, and his actual Mezzanatto treatment of lawful consent support his construction. The detailed waiting and revocation protections naturally govern original execution and do not readily translate to months of informed retention.

The strongest objection is that the proposed ratification would extinguish a claim although neither execution nor retention satisfied Congress's conditions; Scalia adds that the impediment has not disappeared. Thomas's response distinguishes later informed election from initial formation, treats the agreement as voidable, and finds no sufficiently expressed statutory displacement of that doctrine. He accepts the reported lower-court ratification predicate as adequate for this judgment, without moving §626(f)(3)'s original-compliance burden to Oubre. No further factual finding is made about exact knowledge chronology or the complete contract. This position differs substantively from Scalia's, despite shared affirmance.

### Tender path A — allow the action without prior return

The six reversal supporters conclude that conditioning this action on tender would give operative effect to the defective release and enable avoidance of the statutory protections. General concerns about workers who may have spent severance illustrate the statute's operation; Oubre's own inability to repay is unknown and is not a necessary factual predicate. They need not accept Entergy's asserted common law as universally correct. The strongest contrary argument is that restoration merely conditions repudiation of an exchange and does not validate the original waiver. The six reject that distinction for this asserted bar, while preserving the different question of lawful later accounting.

### Tender path B — restoration remains a distinct condition

Scalia and Thomas conclude that the statute's original-waiver requirements do not displace the common-law tender condition. In their view, requiring restoration before repudiating the exchange is distinct from finding a valid original waiver. Scalia uses this ground alone for affirmance; Thomas treats it separately from his ratification ground. The strongest contrary argument is practical and statutory: the condition may block the very ADEA suit the invalid waiver could not release. They regard a restoration obligation, with the common law's actual flexibility, as consistent with retention of background contract rules. This is a legal disagreement on the same record, not a finding that Oubre could pay. Neither supplies a new universal exception rule, debt, repayment deadline, or damages award.

### Accounting, voidability, and remedial alternatives

One could preserve possible restitution or offset without deciding entitlement; alternatively, one could classify the entire transaction as wholly void, or order immediate repayment as a consequence of removing the defense. Only the bounded reservation is supported by the six-Justice common framework and this incomplete contract record. Absolute nullity could allow the employer to invoke its own noncompliance to cancel benefits even for a worker who never seeks to sue. Immediate repayment adjudication would require legal and contractual predicates not established here.

O'Connor and Breyer therefore expressly characterize the procedurally defective arrangement as voidable at the protected worker's election rather than absolutely void. Filing the ADEA action rejects the promise not to sue. Once suit may proceed without tender as a precondition, an employer may ask for restitution, relief from a reciprocal obligation, or a permissible credit against damages; success depends on governing law, the agreement, and the actual facts. Their explanation adds no present award and does not validate mere-retention ratification. Thomas accepts voidability but draws different consequences for ratification and tender. No combination across those disagreements creates a single controlling framework beyond the six's actual shared propositions.

## Judgment, scope, and final-join instructions

The six reversal supporters reverse the Fifth Circuit judgment and remand for further proceedings consistent with removing the defective-release/retention bar to the ADEA claim. Their mandate does not establish constructive discharge, age motivation, willfulness, damages, fees, reinstatement, or entitlement to trial. It does not revive every state-law claim, cancel every part of the agreement, create a debt, order repayment, or determine the effect of the release on other rights. Oubre retains her ordinary liability burden; Entergy retains lawfully available independent defenses and any separately supported accounting request. The EEOC's rejection is not a civil liability judgment.

Scalia and Thomas would leave the defense judgment in place but receive no discrimination finding or money judgment. Their grounds must be stated separately. The published current framework, if assembled from the six supported full joins, must not state that every common-law doctrine is abolished or that employer recovery is impossible. A proposed absolute-voidness or automatic-restitution section would require a fresh exact join analysis and is unsupported by this handoff. The three conceptual positions on voidability, ratification, and tender cannot be merged by label or by judgment alignment.
