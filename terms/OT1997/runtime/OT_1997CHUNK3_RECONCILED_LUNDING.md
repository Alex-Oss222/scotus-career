## Historical comparison and the material correction

The complete historical report, **522 U.S. 287–328**, records reversal and remand by O'Connor, Stevens, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Breyer; Ginsburg dissented with Rehnquist and Kennedy. There were no partial joins or nonparticipation. The actual historical split among the eight current Associates was six to two on the Article IV merits.

The two provisional departures are not supported by a changed premise. Kennedy and Ginsburg's own historical positions accepted two grounds for affirmance: alimony as attribution of income between former spouses, evaluated under substantial equality in the tax system; and, independently assuming an expense characterization, the source/home-State connection of these Connecticut alimony payments. Their objection also distinguished general personal exemptions from particular deductions and challenged using income correlation to require every personal deduction or credit. Those are Article IV grounds. Simulated Fulton, Oregon Waste, West Lynn Creamery, Carbone, SSC and Tracy retain their Commerce Clause fields; none overrules or changes the Article IV source-allocation distinction, decides alimony, or supplies identical-deduction treatment. Their shared joins in those cases therefore provide no Justice-specific reason to reject the same source and income-attribution grounds they actually found sufficient here.

The payer, recipient, divorce decree, challenged paragraph, tax computation, review scope and requested relief remain materially the same. No changed record or remedy removes the historical premise. The frozen neutral packet expressly offers source-allocation and divided-family-income defenses, so correcting the two rows requires no new neutral fact or new doctrinal holding. The correction is qualitative: the historical full writing identifies substantive grounds that the provisional rows rejected without identifying a changed legal or factual premise. It is not a copied poll.

## Reconciled propositions and component boundaries

**Shared reach and governing standard.** All eight reach only the supplied Article IV Privileges and Immunities question. The completed highest-state-court adjudication supplies §1257(a) finality on this record; no petition date, timing waiver or grant is invented. Toomer, Shaffer, Travis and Austin supply substantial equality, a substantial reason beyond noncitizenship, and the required close relationship between the difference in treatment and that reason. States retain considerable leeway and genuine source-based income/deduction distinctions. Neither ordinary rationality alone, absolute mathematical equality, a least-restrictive-means requirement, nor the Commerce Clause's compensatory-tax checklist replaces that inquiry.

**C1 — six-Associate invalidity ground.** Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Breyer hold §631(b)(6)'s categorical refusal of any qualifying nonresident alimony deduction in New York-source income inadequately justified under Article IV in this scheme. Alimony's personal character and the State's narrower nonresident income base do not by themselves establish the needed close relation. The obligation adjusts income available to the payer and does not acquire an exclusively Connecticut source merely from the divorce decree or residence of the former spouses. Shaffer and Travis preserve justified source limits; they do not automatically immunize every personal-deduction denial.

**C2 — six-Associate treatment of the offered justifications.** The six consider the whole reported formula: qualifying alimony is recognized in hypothetical resident liability and the federal-adjusted-gross-income denominator, but §631(b)(6) denies it in the New York-source numerator. The initial benefit therefore does not establish substantial equality in the ultimate apportioned burden. The known $3,724 additional assessment plus interest establishes this controversy's concrete consequence; it does not establish payment, the full return, total effective rate or an actual percentage above 100%. The legally permitted ratio is uncapped, and no ceiling may be invented.

For these six, the income-splitting justification does not cure the categorical payer-residence line. The paragraph denies a nonresident payer's deduction without conditioning that denial on whether New York taxes the recipient; it allows the corresponding resident-payer adjustment without that condition. The statutory mismatch is a legal feature, not an empirical finding about how many former spouses live in each State. The actual Connecticut-to-Connecticut transaction does not eliminate the Lundings' own assessment injury or prevent examining the classification that produced it. Neither Goodwin's reported treatment of home-State property/medical expenses nor prior summary dispositions concerning other deductions decide this specific alimony paragraph in the complete current formula. The six distinguish those settings rather than overruling every source-allocation rule or treating summary dispositions as devoid of precedential effect.

**K/G ground A — income attribution and substantial equality.** Kennedy and Ginsburg reject C1 and C2's constitutional application. They understand the qualifying payer deduction and recipient inclusion as allocating taxable income between former spouses, rather than recognizing a payer expense that must follow every source of earnings. Where both former spouses are New York residents, New York includes the transfer in the recipient's tax base and permits the payer deduction; where both are Connecticut residents, the recipient is outside New York's resident tax base and denying the deduction preserves New York taxation of its source income. They regard that relation between source taxation and assignment of the taxable person as a substantial, sufficiently connected reason in this case, not merely the bare fact of nonresidence.

The strongest contrary point is the statutory line's imperfect fit in mixed-residence couples. They accept substantial rather than mechanically precise equality, and do not infer that an imperfect fit in a different former couple entitles these taxpayers to remove the corresponding New York-source income from New York taxation. Their historical writing's Travellers/Baldwin discussion confirms this treatment of fair distribution and federal-state structure; it is evidence of their own published view, not a new replacement for the frozen packet's Toomer/Shaffer/Travis/Austin law. No numerical household frequency, interstate-payment balance or actual aggregate revenue equivalence is found. In particular, historical dissent note 3's suggested balance is expressly an assumption and is not adopted as a factual predicate.

**K/G ground B — independent personal-expense/source justification.** Even assuming alimony is an expense rather than income attribution, Kennedy and Ginsburg would affirm independently because the particular payment is rooted in the Connecticut marital relationship: a Connecticut payer pays a Connecticut former spouse under a Connecticut decree, and the payment is not an expense of producing New York professional income. They read Shaffer/Travis's permissible source limits, with the reported Goodwin rationale, to allow New York to leave this personal expense to the home State's law and policy. That rationale does not rest solely on the word “personal” or give nonresidence unlimited force; it supplies the identified home-State connection in this record. They distinguish Travis's generally available personal exemption, whose denial raises the effective threshold for all nonresidents, from a particular deduction for this nonlocal obligation.

The six rejecting this alternative find that alimony lacks the fixed geographic situs of an identified property expense and that this payer-income adjustment cannot be allocated entirely by residence or decree location. The two affirming Associates reject that application: correlation with overall income does not distinguish alimony from many personal expenditures, and the majority's express disclaimer of a universal deduction requirement does not, in their view, provide a sufficient limiting distinction. This is a substantive disagreement over Article IV's application and scope, not a dispute cured by narrower remedial language. No new Connecticut statutory-factor finding is necessary; the supplied nature and source of the obligation suffice for their alternative ground.

**K/G scope objection.** Their historical objection to reliance on other former couples remains part of their merits reasoning: possible unconstitutional treatment of a nonresident payer with a New York recipient is not the Lundings' own transaction. They do not vote to dismiss this case for lack of Article III standing; the concrete assessment permits review. Nor do they adopt a new universal facial-challenge rule from an unprovided source. They would affirm this actual assessment under both grounds above and decline relief based only on hypothetical burdens to others.

**C3 — other States and constitutional fields.** All eight preserve Austin's rule that another State's credit or deduction cannot cure discrimination established under Article IV. The six find such discrimination and reject the amici's unsupported claim of an overall offset. Kennedy and Ginsburg find the challenged allocation justified in the first place; their ground does not require Connecticut to enact an income tax or credit. Connecticut's lack of earned-income taxation for these taxpayers in 1990 illustrates the actual dispute but supplies neither a universal fact about all Connecticut taxes nor a substitute constitutional test. The Equal Protection and Commerce Clause theories are outside the reviewed merits issue. Federal alimony qualification remains undisputed; §71 supplies recipient inclusion and §215 the payer deduction, not the reversed descriptions embedded in the historical dissent's secondary-source quotation.

**C4 — remedy and stage.** The six reversing Associates would reverse the Court of Appeals' sustaining judgment and remand for lawful severability, correction of the challenged assessment and constitutionally adequate relief. They do not prescribe the taxpayers' formula as the sole remedy, reinstate every part of the intermediate judgment, presume payment or order a $3,724 refund plus interest. McKesson/Harper/Fulton govern backward-looking correction when the actual payment/review predicates obtain; Reich independently forbids withdrawal of a clearly held-out postpayment remedy after payment. Those conditions remain unproved here. No unrelated tax year, final assessment against another person, new evidence order, compulsory hearing or retained Supreme Court jurisdiction follows.

Kennedy and Ginsburg would affirm the New York Court of Appeals and sustain the challenged assessment against this Article IV claim. They do not join C4's constitutional-correction mandate, though their prior Reich/Fulton joins remain law when a violation and the necessary remedial predicates exist. Their present position gives no assessment collection order, fixed interest calculation, new tax formula or merits decision on the unpresented constitutional theories.
