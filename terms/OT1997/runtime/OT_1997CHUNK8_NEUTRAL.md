* **Entry Stage and Event:** Review authorized by the user for the new OT1997 simulation; a distinct Supreme Court merits matter is proposed. The authorization does not resolve a statutory vehicle obstacle identified below.

* **Question Presented:** 1. Whether excluding religious worship services from a public school’s limited public forum, while permitting discussion of religious subjects and comparable secular community activities, is a permissible subject-matter restriction or unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination.
  2. Whether avoiding an Establishment Clause violation supplies a constitutionally sufficient reason for excluding private religious worship from the forum. [Source formulation; not a verbatim petition quotation.]

* **Question Granted:** 1. Whether excluding religious worship services from a public school’s limited public forum, while permitting discussion of religious subjects and comparable secular community activities, is a permissible subject-matter restriction or unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination.
  2. Whether avoiding an Establishment Clause violation supplies a constitutionally sufficient reason for excluding private religious worship from the forum.

* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Second Circuit upheld the district’s exclusion of worship services from school facilities.
* **Relief Requested:** Bronx Household seeks reversal and equal access subject to neutral operational rules.
* **Material Record:** The district permitted broad after-hours uses addressing education, community, culture, character training, and ethical education but denied a church’s proposed worship services and religious instruction. The activities were privately sponsored, outside instructional hours, and could be identified as nongovernmental. The record facts below are from the Second Circuit’s opinion, 127 F.3d 207 (2d Cir. 1997); bracketed pin cites are to the numbered paragraphs of the Public.Resource.Org text linked in Section I.E.
  - **Parties and school:** The Bronx Household of Faith, an evangelical Christian church, and its pastors, Jack Roberts and Robert Hall, sued Community School District No. 10, its board president and the Board of Education of the City of New York over the refusal to permit the church to use the gymnasium-auditorium of M.S. 206B, the Anne Cross Mersereau Middle School, for weekly religious worship services. [¶1] M.S. 206B serves students aged 9 through 12 in grades 5 through 7. [¶12]
  - **Prior rentals:** The church had previously rented the M.S. 206B building twice, in 1994 and 1995, for programs of sports and games, and once in 1995 for a banquet. [¶13]
  - **The request:** Pastor Hall asked to rent the gymnasium each Sunday “for the purpose of conducting church worship services.” He wrote that the services would include “hymn singing, communion, Bible reading, Bible preaching and teaching,” and he cited overcrowding at the church’s present meeting location. [¶13]
  - **The denial:** A District 10 official denied the request under the SOP provision barring after-school use of school facilities by outside organizations for religious services or instruction. The application was denied again after the pastor asked the superintendent to reconsider. [¶13]
  - **New York Education Law § 414:** Section 414 does not list religious worship among the uses of school property it authorizes. It allows districts to permit uses including educational instruction; social, civic and recreational meetings and entertainments, and other community-welfare uses, that are “non-exclusive and open to the general public”; civic forums and community centers; and graduation exercises of nonprofit schools “provided that no religious service is performed.” [¶3]
  - **SOPs 5.6.1 and 5.6.2:** The New York City Board of Education’s Standard Operating Procedures give primary use of school premises to Board activities and next preference to community, youth and adult group activities. They permit use “[f]or the purpose of instruction in any branch of education, learning or the arts” (SOP 5.6.1) and for “social, civic and recreational meetings and entertainments, and other uses pertaining to the welfare of the community” (SOP 5.6.2). [¶¶4-6]
  - **SOP 5.9:** SOP 5.9, a Board of Education policy that governs District 10, provides: “No outside organization or group may be allowed to conduct religious services or religious instruction on school premises after school. However, the use of school premises by outside organizations or groups after school for the purposes of discussing religious material or material which contains a religious viewpoint or for distributing such material is permissible.” [¶¶4, 11, 12]
  - **Uses in 1994-95:** The parties agreed that during the 1994-95 school year District 10 facilities were used for tutoring programs; Boy Scout training, which Judge Cabranes’s opinion describes as including “ethical education”; Girl Scout training “in skills and character”; Pius XII Beacon programs including after-school tutoring and “character training, with no religious content”; a Grace Lutheran Church program on Black History Month; Greek language and history classes for young people offered by the Greek Orthodox Community of Riverdale; concerts; workshops on drugs and AIDS; and adult GED classes. [¶12; Cabranes, J., ¶57 & n.1]
  - **Stipulation:** The parties stipulated that “Community School District 10 has consistently followed Policy 5.9 by never renting school facilities to non-school groups or organizations, including Bronx Household of Faith, for the purpose of conducting religious worship services and religious instruction on school premises.” [¶15]
  - **Scope of the record:** The record covered school uses during a single school year. It disclosed no request to use the premises for discussing religious material or secular matters from a religious viewpoint. [¶32; Cabranes, J., ¶49]
  - **District court:** The district court (Preska, J.) granted summary judgment to the defendants on cross-motions. It found a limited public forum and found restrictions reasonably related to preserving access “primarily for educational purposes,” and it dismissed the remaining claims without analysis. [¶2]
  - **Second Circuit majority:** Judge Miner, writing for the court on a panel with Judges Van Graafeiland and Cabranes, held M.S. 206B a limited rather than open public forum and held SOP 5.9’s prohibition of religious services and instruction reasonable and viewpoint neutral. The court reasoned that “it is reasonable for state legislators and school authorities to avoid the identification of a middle school with a particular church” and to consider “the effect upon the minds of middle school children of designating their school as a church.” It found the non-exclusive-use provisions of no significance. [¶¶23, 28-33]
  - **Establishment Clause and other claims below:** The court stated that “there is no need for the Board of Education to rely on the Establishment Clause in limiting the use of the public school forum.” It rejected the Establishment Clause, Free Exercise and Equal Protection claims, held the RFRA claim moot after City of Boerne v. Flores, and affirmed. The affirmance covered both the services and the instruction parts of SOP 5.9 as applied to the church. [¶¶28, 35, 39, 40, 42, 43, 63]
  - **Judge Cabranes:** Judge Cabranes concurred in part and dissented in part. Stating that he was constrained by circuit precedent and the parties’ factual stipulations, he agreed that the forum was limited, and he concurred in upholding the ban on religious services as reasonable and viewpoint neutral, finding “no real secular analogue” to services and describing that ban as a form of content discrimination. He dissented from “that part of the judgment upholding the ban on religious instruction.” He reasoned that the District allowed “secular instruction related to ‘ethics’ and ‘character,’ paradigmatic contexts for religious instruction,” and that the instruction ban discriminates on the basis of viewpoint under Lamb’s Chapel and Rosenberger. He noted that the parties had stipulated that the church sought the gymnasium for “religious worship services,” so any difficulty in separating services and instruction from other religious speech was not presented. [¶¶45-46, 56-57, 61-63]

* **Material Record:** In 1904, the Crow Tribe ceded part of its Montana Reservation to the United States for settlement by non-Indians. The United States holds rights to minerals underlying the ceded strip in trust for the Tribe. In 1972, with the approval of the Department of the Interior and pursuant to the Indian Mineral Leasing Act of 1938 (IMLA), Westmoreland Resources, Inc., a non-Indian company, entered into a mining lease with the Tribe for coal underlying the ceded strip. After executing the lease, Westmoreland signed contracts with its customers, four utility companies, allowing it to pass on to the utilities the cost of valid taxes. Westmoreland and the Tribe renegotiated the lease in 1974. The amended lease had an extendable ten-year term, and set some of the highest royalties in the United States. In 1975, Montana imposed a severance tax and a gross proceeds tax on all coal produced in the State, including coal underlying the reservation proper and the ceded strip. Westmoreland paid these taxes without timely pursuit of the procedures Montana law provides for protests and refunds. Some six months after the State imposed its taxes, the Crow Tribal Council adopted its own severance tax. The Department of the Interior approved the Tribe's tax as applied to coal underlying the reservation proper but, because of a limitation in the Tribe's constitution, did not approve as to coal beneath the ceded strip. The Tribe again enacted a tax for coal mined on the ceded strip in 1982, and again the Department rejected the tax. In 1978, the Tribe brought a federal action for injunctive and declaratory relief against Montana and its counties, alleging that the State's severance and gross proceeds taxes were preempted by the IMLA and infringed on the Tribe's right to govern itself. The District Court dismissed the complaint. The Ninth Circuit reversed, holding that the Tribe's allegations, if proved, would establish that the IMLA preempted the State's taxes. The Court of Appeals noted, however, that the Tribe had paid none of Westmoreland's taxes and apparently would not be entitled to any refund in the event that the taxes were declared invalid. Crow Tribe v. Montana, 650 F. 2d 1104, 1113, n. 13 (Crow I). In 1982, the Tribe and Westmoreland entered into an agreement, with Interior Department approval, under which Westmoreland agreed to pay the Tribe a tax equal to the State's then-existing taxes, less any tax payments Westmoreland was required to pay to the State and its subdivisions. The agreement achieved, prospectively, the federal permission the Tribe had long sought. It allowed the Tribe to have an approved tax in place so that, if successful in the litigation against Montana, the Tribe could claim for itself any tax amounts Westmoreland would be ordered to pay into the District Court's registry pendente lite. It also enabled Westmoreland to avoid double taxation, and absolved the company from any tax payment obligation to the Tribe for the 1976-1982 period. In 1983, the District Court granted a motion by the Tribe and Westmoreland to deposit severance tax payments into the District Court's registry, pending resolution of the controversy over Montana's taxing authority. In 1987, the court granted the same interim relief for the gross proceeds taxes. Later that year, the United States intervened on behalf of the Tribe to protect its interests as trustee of the coal upon which Montana's taxes were levied. After trial, the District Court determined that federal law did not preempt the State's taxes on coal mined at the ceded strip. The Ninth Circuit again reversed, concluding that the taxes were both preempted by the IMLA and void for interfering with tribal self-governance. Crow Tribe v. Montana, 819 F. 2d 895, 903 (Crow II). The Court of Appeals stressed ,i n t e r alia, that the State's taxes had at least some negative impact on the marketability of the Tribe's coal. Id., at 900. This Court summarily affirmed. When the case returned to the District Court in 1988, the court ordered distribution of the funds in its registry to the United States as trustee for the Tribe. Subsequently, the United States and the Tribe filed amended complaints against Montana and Big Horn County to recover taxes paid by Westmoreland prior to the 1983 and 1987 orders directing deposits into the court's registry. Neither the Tribe nor the United States requested, as additional or alternate relief, recovery for the Tribe's actual financial losses attributable to the State's taxes. After trial, the District Court concluded that the disgorgement remedy sought by the Tribe was not appropriate. Key to the court's decision was this Court's holding in Cotton Petroleum Corp. v. New Mexico, 490 U. S. 163, that both State and Tribe may impose severance taxes on on-reservation oil and gas production by a non-Indian lessee. Cotton Petroleum indicated that Montana's taxes on ceded strip coal were invalidated in Crow II not because the State lacked power to tax the coal at all, but because its taxes were “extraordinarily high.” 490 U. S., at 186-187, n. 17. The District Court also considered that Westmoreland would not have paid coal taxes to the Tribe before 1983, for Interior Department approval was essential to allow pass-through to the company's customers. Furthermore, under the 1982 lease agreement, the Tribe and Westmoreland stipulated that Westmoreland would have no tax liability to the Tribe for the 1976-1982 period. Moreover, the deposited funds, Westmoreland's post-1982 tax payments, had been turned over in full to the United States for the benefit of the Tribe. The court further noted that Westmoreland did not timely endeavor to recover taxes paid to the State and counties, and the Tribe did nothing to prompt Westmoreland to initiate appropriate refund proceedings. The court received additional evidence concerning the effect of Montana's taxes on the marketability of Montana coal and described the parties' conflicting positions on that issue, but made no findings on the matter. The Ninth Circuit again reversed, holding that the District Court had ignored the law of the case and abused its discretion.
