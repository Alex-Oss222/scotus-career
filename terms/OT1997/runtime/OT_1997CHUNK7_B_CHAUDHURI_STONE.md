## B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

* **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Reverse in part and remand. Reverse the Sixth Circuit's judgment insofar as it upholds the officially organized spoken prayers, the invocations and benedictions Tennessee State University formerly included at its functions; affirm it insofar as it rejects the challenge to the genuine, officially designated moments of silence; let it stand as to the two 1993 audience recitations of the Lord's Prayer, which were private religious acts outside the grant and need no merits ruling, and as to any other matter outside the two granted questions; remand for further proceedings consistent with the granted questions.
* **Stone's Remedy and Remand Position:** The university may not select or invite a speaker to deliver an invocation, benediction or other spoken prayer as part of an official university function. It remains free to solemnize its functions through secular means, to designate a genuine moment of silence, and to permit genuinely private religious expression. The lower courts apply that rule to each function at which the record shows official arrangement and program placement of a spoken prayer. After Chaudhuri sued and moved to enjoin the prayers, the university replaced them with moments of silence in 1993, and the Sixth Circuit stated that his damages claim keeps the challenge to the former prayers live. Stone accepts that live controversy for the granted prayer question. Chaudhuri sought compensatory damages, and the defendants include four named university administrators as well as the State of Tennessee and the university. The Sixth Circuit held the prayer challenge live insofar as he seeks damages and decided its merits. Section I records no contrary jurisdictional argument and nothing that confines the damages claim to the State, the university or official-capacity defendants. Under Arizonans for Official English v. Arizona (March 3, 1997), applying Will and Hafer, a damages request against the State or an official-capacity defendant that is not a §1983 damages person could not by itself preserve the controversy, so the live controversy rests on the damages claim against the named administrators. The opinion below does not address their capacity, the damages claim's statutory basis or any immunity. Whether damages can actually be recovered therefore requires separate consideration of the defendants, their immunities and the available remedies; any personal-damages immunity is assessed under Harlow and Anderson v. Creighton as of the date of the challenged conduct. Stone decides no damages, immunity or defendant-specific question. Any declaratory or prospective relief requires a live prospective controversy, including any realistic recurrence, for the lower courts to determine; any such relief must reach only the university's official arrangement of prayer and preserve genuine moments of silence and private religious expression, consistent with Tanford's limit of relief to the official practice.

## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Officially designated moment of silence

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the merits of the officially designated moment-of-silence question. During moments of silence at the May and August 1993 commencements, audience members recited the Lord's Prayer aloud. The May recitation was spontaneous by all accounts; TSU officials said they had no advance knowledge of it and denied complicity in the August recitation; and Chaudhuri conceded that there is no evidence of university complicity in either. On that record the recitations were private religious acts of audience members, outside the grant, and the judgment stands as to them; Stone states the official/private line below only to fix the limit of the holding.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Under the Lemon test as updated in Agostini, the inquiry identifies the governmental act and the function it performs; purpose is evidence of that function, not a freestanding prong. A genuine moment of silence is the university's own act, but the act is silence: it does not organize worship, prescribe religious profession or observance, or direct participants toward prayer, and each participant remains free to reflect, pray or do neither. Adoption of the moment as an alternative to spoken prayer does not by itself make it a devotional exercise. Audience members who spontaneously pray during the silence, without university direction, engage in their own private religious exercise; that prayer is not the university's and does not establish religion.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** A public university does not violate the Establishment Clause by designating a genuine moment of silence at an official function, including one adopted as an alternative to spoken prayer, when the university neither prescribes nor directs prayer or other religious exercise during it.
- **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Section I shows that the university replaced spoken prayer with moments of silence in 1993: first at the May commencement, when President Hefner announced there would be no verbal prayers and that a moment of silence would be acceptable "to solemnize the occasion," and then at all university functions where prayers had been offered. The record shows speakers asking the audience to rise or stand for silence, not any official instruction to pray during it, including at the August commencement, where the speaker who called for silence was identified in the program as a local pastor. Silence alone communicates no prescribed religious content, so its validity turns on actual design and administration. That the 1988 counsel letter described a moment of silence as an "appropriate vehicle for allowing the tradition of prayer without running afoul of the Constitution," and that the university adopted one as a replacement during this litigation, does not by itself make the silence a devotional exercise: the letter was the system general counsel's advice, the purpose President Hefner stated was to solemnize the occasion, and Section I shows no official direction to pray during the silence. Tanford stated that a moment of silence is not prohibited by its holding but did not decide that it is permissible; Stone would decide that question here for a genuine moment of silence. The official-worship prohibition applied in Tanford reaches the university's own worship, not private prayer by individuals who choose to pray, and Chaudhuri concedes no university complicity in the audience recitations.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** A nominal moment of silence used as a direction to pray, an officially led or prompted recitation, or university complicity in audience prayer would present the university's own religious exercise and a different case. Stone makes no merits ruling on the 1993 audience recitations, which lie outside the grant.

### Issue 2: Officially arranged university invocation

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the merits where university officials arranged an invocation, benediction or other spoken prayer as part of an official university function. Stone reads the first granted question's "officially arranged, nonsectarian invocations" to include the benedictions that formed part of the same former prayer practice: Section I shows that TSU formerly included invocations and benedictions at certain university events and that the Sixth Circuit upheld the former nonsectarian prayers as a single practice. The university discontinued the spoken prayers in 1993. The damages claim against the named administrators supplies the live controversy, as stated in Part B; Stone does not decide whether a prospective claim against the spoken prayers remains live, which the Sixth Circuit said appeared moot and Judge Jones would have held not moot.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Under the Lemon test as updated in Agostini, government may not organize worship as its own activity, and that specific prohibition controls when it resolves the conduct. A public university that selects or invites a speaker to lead an official audience in prayer and places the prayer in its program organizes worship as a university activity. The constitutional wrong is the university's sponsorship of worship, not whether listeners are persuaded, pressured or offended. A secular purpose of solemnizing the event cannot justify the religious means the university selected. Nonsectarian wording, the audience's adulthood and formally voluntary attendance do not cure that sponsorship. The violation therefore does not depend on Chaudhuri's alleged practical employment pressure to attend, which Stone need not resolve: Section I records his assertion that faculty were required to attend and that his evaluations included a "university service" component, and the defendants' contrary showing, including the dean's affidavit. That dispute remains relevant to the separate Lee and Ingebretsen pressure ground, which Stone does not reach. The Sixth Circuit, deciding before the Court's December 22, 1997 reversal in Tanford, cited the Seventh Circuit's Tanford decision for the prayers' secular purposes, the de minimis character of the religious gesture and of any entanglement, and the absence of coercion to participate. To that extent its reasoning does not survive Tanford: a secular ceremonial objective cannot justify the prohibited means, a de minimis view of advancement or entanglement does not determine whether worship is officially organized, and the audience's maturity and freedom to abstain bear on coercion but do not change whose worship is presented.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** A public university violates the Establishment Clause when its officials select or invite a speaker to deliver an invocation, benediction or other spoken prayer as part of an official university function. The violation does not depend on whether attendance is compulsory, whether the audience is adult, whether the prayer is nonsectarian, whether an employee shows practical pressure to attend, or whether the function also serves secular purposes.
- **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Section I shows that TSU formerly included invocations and benedictions at certain university events, and that Chaudhuri objected to prayers at functions such as graduation exercises, faculty meetings, dedication ceremonies and guest lectures. After the 1988 counsel letter, TSU officials decided that the prayers should be generic and nonsectarian and asked that references to Jesus Christ be omitted; many were delivered by Dr. James Haney, a clergyman and TSU associate professor, and others by local religious leaders at TSU's invitation. The typical commencement prayer in the record opens "Let us pray" and addresses the "Heavenly Father." These were actual devotional prayers placed in the university's own events. That TSU did not review the prayers in advance and gave no other content guidelines does not make them private: Tanford holds that official selection and incorporation of prayer into a university's official program establish attribution, that independent wording does not make the exercise private, and that the audience's maturity and freedom to abstain bear on coercion but do not change whose worship is presented. Tanford decided only commencement and reserved other adult ceremonial arrangements; Stone would apply the same official-worship prohibition to the university's other official functions because the governmental act is the same. Marsh's legislative-prayer setting supplies no established permissible relationship for these functions, as Tanford held for commencement, and Sherman's civic pledge is distinct from actual prayer. The rule applies to each function at which the record shows official arrangement and program placement of a spoken prayer. Tanford and this case would then be consistent applications of the Lemon test as updated in Agostini: neither voluntary attendance nor nonsectarian wording permits official prayer.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The position does not reach genuinely spontaneous private remarks, private religious expression in a genuine forum, nondevotional ceremonial or historical acknowledgment, the separate pressure ground, any claim outside the two granted questions, or individual liability, immunity and damages beyond the remand stated in Part B.
