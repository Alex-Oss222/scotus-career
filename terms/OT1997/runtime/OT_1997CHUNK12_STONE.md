> ## Stone's disposition in Clinton v. City of New York (1998)
> 
> AFFIRMED. The Line Item Veto Act's unilateral cancellation authority is unconstitutional under Article I, §§ 1 and 7.
> 
> Stone would invalidate the challenged cancellations while preserving Congress's ability to delegate genuine fiscal discretion to the President.
> 
> His governing distinction would be:
> 
> The President may administer spending, withhold funds where the law permits, and propose the repeal of expenditures. But the President may not use a general policy judgment to nullify a statutory decision Congress has already enacted.
> 
> The reasoning rests on three principles:
> 
> 1. Legislative authority: Congress decides the legal existence and scope of spending programs, statutory obligations, and tax benefits. It can enact contingent provisions and authorize substantial executive discretion.
> 2. Executive authority: The President implements those decisions, exercises delegated choices, and may seek legislative reconsideration. Execution is not an independent power to rescind the law itself.
> 3. Reciprocal accountability: Neither branch can make its objections legally conclusive merely because the other fails to assemble a supermajority. Changes to enacted law must follow constitutional lawmaking procedures.
> 
> This is more than a categorical prohibition on presidential discretion. Stone's opinion would distinguish a valid delegation under Field v. Clark, 143 U.S. 649 (1892), from the cancellation mechanism in this case. It would also preserve the statutory limits on executive withholding recognized in Train v. City of New York, 420 U.S. 35 (1975).&#x20;

> ## Proposed opinion language for Stone
> 
> ### Clinton v. City of New York, 524 U.S. 417 (1998)
> 
> **Opinion of Chief Justice Stone**
> 
> The Constitution does not require the political branches to agree upon the wisdom of federal expenditures. It requires them to exercise their respective powers through the procedures by which law is made, administered, and revised.
> 
> The Line Item Veto Act presents a substantial question because it addresses a genuine difficulty in the legislative process. Congress frequently combines expenditures, conditions, and tax provisions into legislation that the President must accept or reject as a whole. A President may reasonably conclude that some provisions serve the public interest while others do not. Congress may reasonably seek to accommodate those objections without abandoning the broader legislative agreement.
> 
> The Constitution permits considerable flexibility in resolving that difficulty. It does not, however, permit Congress to confer upon the President an unrestricted authority to substitute his subsequent judgment for legislative decisions that have acquired the force of law.
> 
> ### I. The distinction between execution and cancellation
> 
> Article I commits legislative power to Congress. Article II commits the execution of law to the President. Neither provision requires Congress to determine every detail of federal administration.
> 
> Congress may establish appropriations subject to ceilings, prescribe conditions governing expenditures, authorize the selection of projects, and permit executive officials to determine whether specified contingencies have occurred. Within those limits, the President may exercise substantial discretion.
> 
> *Field v. Clark*, 143 U.S. 649 (1892), illustrates that principle. Congress may enact a rule whose operation depends upon an executive determination. Such an arrangement is permissible when Congress has established the governing policy and the executive determination implements that policy according to the law.
> 
> The authority challenged here differs in a constitutionally significant respect.
> 
> The President is permitted to cancel provisions that Congress has enacted, not because a specified contingency subsequently alters their application, but because the President determines that their continued operation is undesirable under the Act's general fiscal criteria.
> 
> A determination that cancellation would reduce the deficit, preserve essential governmental functions, and avoid injury to the national interest is not meaningless. Those requirements impose political and procedural responsibilities. They do not, however, identify an independent factual contingency or confine the President to executing the substantive decision embodied in the canceled provision.
> 
> The authority is one of selective legal nullification, exercised upon a subsequent judgment of policy.
> 
> The Constitution permits the President to recommend that Congress reconsider an enacted expenditure. It does not permit the President to accomplish that reconsideration by himself.
> 
> ### II. Congressional consent and reciprocal control
> 
> The Government emphasizes that Congress knowingly enacted the cancellation mechanism and retained authority to disapprove its use.
> 
> We give that legislative judgment due consideration. Congress may authorize executive choices, and the mere existence of substantial discretion does not render a statute unconstitutional.
> 
> But congressional consent cannot enlarge the constitutional power of either branch.
> 
> Under the Act, a presidential cancellation takes effect without further legislation. Congress must then enact a disapproval measure to restore the canceled provision. The President retains his constitutional power to veto that measure.
> 
> The practical consequence is that Congress may enact a provision through ordinary legislation, yet later require supermajorities to preserve that provision against the President's subsequent objection.
> 
> The difficulty is not that Congress has voluntarily diminished its influence. Congress may make many such choices in establishing administrative arrangements. The difficulty is that the Act makes the President's unilateral policy judgment legally operative as a revision of enacted law.
> 
> The Constitution provides mechanisms by which each branch may resist the judgment of the other. It does not authorize either branch to dispense with the constitutional procedures required to make its preferred result law.
> 
> That limitation applies reciprocally. Congress may not reserve a unilateral legislative veto over executive action in disregard of bicameralism and presentment. *INS v. Chadha*, 462 U.S. 919 (1983). Nor may it create an equivalent post-enactment power in the President simply by describing the authority as cancellation rather than amendment.
> 
> The Constitution protects legislative participation in making law and executive independence in administering it. Neither protection requires the elimination of legitimate disagreement between the branches.
> 
> ### III. The preservation of executive fiscal discretion
> 
> Our decision does not require the President to expend every dollar Congress authorizes, regardless of the terms of the appropriation.
> 
> Whether funds must be obligated, allotted, or expended depends upon the statute that governs them. See *Train v. City of New York*, 420 U.S. 35 (1975).
> 
> Congress remains free to establish discretionary spending programs, enact conditional appropriations, authorize adjustments within statutory limits, and prescribe fiscal contingencies to be implemented by executive officers.
> 
> Nor does the Constitution prevent the President from identifying objectionable expenditures and submitting proposals for their rescission.
> 
> Congress may provide expedited procedures for considering such proposals, consistent with the constitutional authority of each House to determine its rules and the requirements governing enactment of legislation.
> 
> A procedure that facilitates legislative reconsideration is different from one that makes legislative reconsideration necessary to undo an executive cancellation already given legal effect.
> 
> We do not decide whether any particular alternative arrangement would be valid. Its constitutionality would depend upon the authority granted, the decisions reserved to Congress, and the legal consequences of executive action.
> 
> The present holding leaves the political branches substantial freedom to design cooperative fiscal arrangements. It does not permit them to eliminate the distinction between implementing enacted law and revising it.
> 
> ### IV. Application and disposition
> 
> The President's cancellation of §4722(c) of the Balanced Budget Act of 1997 eliminated a statutory limitation upon potential federal recoupment liability. His cancellation of §968 of the Taxpayer Relief Act of 1997 eliminated a legislatively authorized tax-deferral provision.
> 
> The challenged cancellations therefore altered substantive legal consequences, not merely the timing or manner of administering discretionary appropriations.
> 
> We express no judgment concerning whether either provision represented prudent fiscal policy. The President's objections may have been substantial. Congress's decisions may have been mistaken. Those considerations furnish grounds for legislative reconsideration, not an independent executive authority to extinguish enacted law.
> 
> The cancellation provisions of the Line Item Veto Act exceed the authority Congress may confer consistently with Article I.
> 
> The challenged cancellations are consequently without legal effect. The statutory provisions Congress enacted retain their force, subject to their ordinary terms, qualifications, and lawful administration. Questions of particular financial liability, eligibility, and implementation remain to be determined under those statutes.
> 
> The judgment of the District Court is **affirmed**.
> 
> **It is so ordered.**
> 
> ### What this establishes in Stone's doctrine
> 
> The operative rule is narrower than a general prohibition on budgetary delegation. Stone preserves a substantial sphere of executive implementation but rejects subsequent, discretionary nullification of Congress's completed statutory choices.
> 
> It also leaves a constitutional route for expedited presidential rescission proposals without pretending that the judiciary can require Congress to enact, debate, or approve them. That is legislation for the political branches to design, not a replacement procedure the judiciary can impose.
> 
> For standing, this disposition assumes Stone accepts both sets of challengers as having concrete economic interests. New York's exposure to revived financial liability is straightforward; Snake River's loss of an active, transaction-related statutory bargaining advantage is more contestable. Recognizing the latter should be expressly confined to the particular commercial activity established in the record, not extended to anyone who hoped indirectly to benefit from a tax provision.&#x20;
