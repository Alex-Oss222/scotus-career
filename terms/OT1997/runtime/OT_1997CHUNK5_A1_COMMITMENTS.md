## Proposed components and alternatives

**K1 — statutory rule.** Section 523(a)(6) requires a willful injury, meaning deliberate or intentional injury, in addition to the separately stated malicious character; intentionally undertaking an act that negligently or recklessly causes harm does not alone meet that requirement. A malpractice judgment based on negligence or recklessness therefore is not excepted from discharge under this paragraph merely because the doctor consciously selected the treatment that caused it. This is no categorical medical-debt exemption: an adequately established intentional injury presents a different question.

**K2 — precedent and scope.** Tinker's actual intentional invasion of a legally protected right and McIntyre's deliberate appropriation differ from negligence or recklessness in treatment. Their rejection of a personal-hostility prerequisite does not erase the injury requirement. To the extent Tinker's broader language is invoked to make merely reckless conduct sufficient under the present §523(a)(6), reject that reading; no wholesale overruling of its intentional-injury holding, universal conversion rule or adoption of old-Act partnership imputation follows. Davis reinforces the distinction between tort liability and the additional discharge-exception predicates.

**K3 — judgment and remedy.** Affirm the Eighth Circuit's judgment that this negligence/recklessness malpractice debt is dischargeable under §523(a)(6). The described bankruptcy-court legal characterization of exceptionally deficient treatment does not establish the missing federal willfulness predicate. The existing malpractice judgment and harm remain accepted; they are not erased or relitigated. No new intent finding, independent malice definition, subjective-substantial-certainty rule, damages apportionment, other discharge exception or mandatory-insurance rule is adjudicated.

**Enhanced competing path.** The creditors' strongest broader path treats deliberately inferior treatment, withdrawal of antibiotics and cancellation of a transfer cumulatively as an intentional invasion of the patient's rights, relying on Tinker's implied-malice language and McIntyre's refusal to demand personal animus. It rightly distinguishes willful injury from spite and prevents a malpractice label from immunizing actual intentional harm. Its decisive defect on this record is that voluntary treatment choices and disregard of risk do not themselves establish deliberate injury. The record supplies no separate finding of subjective substantial certainty, and the Court may not supply one. Davis rejects automatic movement from a tort judgment to nondischargeability, while Tinker itself distinguished negligent driving from intentionally running over another. The intentional-injury reading gives the modifier in the enacted phrase real work without importing a criminal-law burden or a universal knowledge-of-illegality requirement.

The legislative reports described in the clean addition confirm that reckless-disregard interpretations were rejected, but the enacted text, common-law distinctions and record are sufficient. The reports' conversion discussions do not contradict the need to separate an intentional deprivation from an honest or otherwise nonwillful conversion. No Justice must join legislative history as an independently controlling premise to adopt K1–K3.
