## Threshold conclusion and alternatives

**Selected narrow conclusion:** Reach the challenge to the later mandamus restriction. An instruction to recalculate an excessive award does not on this supplied sequence necessarily determine that the plaintiff must accept the new figure without an election. The later command makes that restriction operative and explicit after the district court addressed the constitutional objection. Respondents' attributed earlier-petition omission therefore does not prevent review of this later restriction. This conclusion does not reopen the earlier excessiveness determination or depend on excusing an established waiver of an already decided election issue.

**Earlier-opportunity alternative:** If the first mandate had already necessarily and conclusively imposed the same no-election command, respondents would have a materially stronger argument that this is a delayed attempt to attack that decision. A later enforcement order is not invariably a new opportunity to relitigate settled questions. The strongest support is the court of appeals' eventual description of its mandate as requiring final judgment. The strongest obstacle is that recalculation and elimination of a remittitur election are distinct commands, and the supplied earlier mandate did not expressly contain the latter. The district court's later memorandum and election ruling also show that the constitutional question was concretely presented before the challenged mandamus action. The model does not infer a contrary unquoted term in the earlier mandate or earlier petition.

**Later-restriction alternative:** A party may challenge a later order that imposes a restriction not necessarily resolved by the earlier mandate. Its strongest objection is that absence of express words does not always mean an issue remained open: a mandate can decide a matter by necessary implication. That objection is accepted as a limit but does not establish the implication here. Recalculation is fully compatible with a conditional reduced award, so the later no-election command presents a distinct legal injury. The selected conclusion rests on this procedural distinction, not a universal rule that later presentation cures every omission.

**Date-eligible legal support:** *In re Sanford Fork & Tool Co.*, 160 U.S. 247, 255–259 (1895), distinguishes matters actually settled by a mandate from matters left open, and permits examination of the opinion to ascertain the mandate's reach. It does not make every later enforcement act independently reviewable or authorize disregard of a settled command. *Messenger v. Anderson*, 225 U.S. 436, 444 (1912), distinguishes law-of-the-case practice from a jurisdictional limit on the rendering court's power; it does not establish a universal exception to preservation rules or an entitlement to review. Neither precedent alone resolves an omission from a prior petition. They support the limited inquiry into what the earlier mandate decided, after which this record's later restriction and actual presentation dispose of the objection.

Gasperini supplies the substantive election rule and the Associates' own differing public positions. It is not cited as having decided this preservation issue.
