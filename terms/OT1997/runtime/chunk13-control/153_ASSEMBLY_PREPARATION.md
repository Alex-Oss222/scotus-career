## Actual scheme and evidence that must survive assembly

1. More than five percent in the last gubernatorial election supplies established-party status for all races. More than five percent in specified other statewide elections supplies statewide status; more than five percent in a district or subdivision election supplies status there. LPI's combined 1994 trustee vote was 5.5 percent, its gubernatorial vote was 1.68 percent, and it lacked the qualifying congressional-district result.
2. The established-party primary gate is 0.5 percent of the relevant qualified primary electorate. The ordinary statewide new-party cap is the **lesser of 25,000 signatures or one percent of voters in the last statewide general election**. The district new-party requirement is the lesser of five percent of voters in the last regular district election or that statewide cap. Do not shorten the statewide rule to an unconditional 25,000 requirement.
3. The separate postdecennial-redistricting district rule is 5,000 signatures. The established-party 600-signature redistricting exception is distinct and is not at issue. The exceptional six 1992 qualifiers do not prove ordinary-year accessibility.
4. The ordinary new-party district average was about 7,610 signatures; the seven plaintiff districts averaged about 9,337. The established-party primary averages were approximately 658 Democratic and 532 Republican signatures. Different denominators, different ballot stages and different nomination functions prevent a bare ten-to-one constitutional conclusion; the figures still matter to actual burden.

## Operative holdings and explanations to carry into the Record

The following are proposed controlling formulations supported by the eight Associates, not adopted text or new law created by this memorandum. In the Record, identify the actual writing and its joins once for the common coalition, then keep holdings, precedent treatment and resulting law distinct.

**1. Continuing controversy.** A challenge to recurring party classification and congressional nomination requirements remains reviewable when the completed election's short calendar would otherwise prevent review and the same requirements continue to govern the party's access. The completed 1996 primary therefore does not moot this controversy, and reaching the merits supplies no authority to reopen that election. Norman supplies the applicable election-continuity principle. The Court does not turn the apparent insufficiency of particular petition sheets into a separately decided standing ground.

**2. Relevant electorate and the complete nomination system.** The First and Fourteenth Amendments permit Illinois, on this record, to require a party that has not demonstrated support in a congressional district to use its general-election support-petition route rather than an established-party primary. Courts must evaluate the actual complete system and the burden imposed; success in a different statewide contest, a lower primary petition percentage or an isolated prior qualification neither automatically resolves the challenge nor makes the nomination stages constitutionally interchangeable.

Anderson and Burdick supply the burden-sensitive inquiry: the character and magnitude of the actual injury are weighed against the State's precise administrative interests and their relation to that injury. Severe restrictions require a compelling justification and narrow tailoring; reasonable nondiscriminatory restrictions ordinarily can rest on important regulatory interests. Jenness permits a meaningful support petition in its operating setting; it does not create a universal five-percent safe harbor. Norman permits support measurements for distinct electorates while protecting authorized expansion and independently qualified components. Timmons permits proportionate nomination administration where meaningful alternatives remain and supplies no interest in preserving existing competitors as such.

Here, the party's trustee-based statewide status does not establish support in each congressional electorate. Primary admission concerns an already established party's nomination contest; the district petition supplies the new party's showing for the general ballot. The actual alternative, together with legitimate interests in meaningful support, avoiding confusion and orderly ballots, sustains the challenged route at the demonstrated scope. The numerical disparity remains relevant, but the published record does not establish a genuine dispute of severe cumulative practical exclusion requiring a trial. **Literal impossibility is not the constitutional threshold.** Neither the two 1994 qualifiers, the disputed 1996 acceptance count, nor the special 1992 route conclusively establishes an adequate ordinary route. No categorical full-slate or deadline approval follows.

The principal contrary view treats the combined recognition and nomination structure as an unjustified comparative burden despite the availability of an alternate route. The Court answers it through the distinct relevant electorate, the actual nomination functions and the demonstrated burden, not a conclusion that major parties have a constitutional title to preferred treatment. No independently qualified component has been shown disabled because another component failed. Supported future cumulative-burden challenges remain open.

**3. Congressional qualifications.** Thornton's existing rule that States may not add substantive congressional qualifications is restated by Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer in Part III-A. A genuine nomination procedure requiring electoral support does not add a personal qualification for service in Congress merely because candidates must comply before reaching the ballot. These requirements therefore do not violate the Qualifications Clause; that conclusion leaves their separate First and Fourteenth Amendment scrutiny intact. Thornton supplies the distinction between election procedure and a disguised substantive eligibility exclusion. The eight Associates share this application in Part III-B; the three who withhold III-A do not newly adopt constitutional exclusivity. Stone supplies only the ninth rejection vote, under fallback.

**4. Consequent relief and retained limits.** The Court affirms the judgment for the officials and grants no new election relief. It orders no primary rerun, automatically expanded established-party status, replacement petition number or new statewide qualification. Norman's protection of independently qualified components survives; a future plaintiff remains able to prove a materially different actual burden. This paragraph expresses the remedy and boundaries of the preceding holdings, not an additional freestanding constitutional test.

Material precedent treatment: Anderson/Burdick are applied without alteration to actual burden and precise interests; Jenness is applied in context, without a percentage safe harbor; Norman is applied to separate electorates and its qualified-component protection is retained; Timmons is applied at its alternative-access and administrative-interest scope, without incumbent protection; Thornton's genuine-procedure distinction is applied and its existing exclusivity holding is not enlarged or repudiated. Munro may support prospective election administration if used, but supplies no blanket exemption from assessing the actual burden. No new checklist, automatic evidentiary presumption, universal slate rule or independently pleaded equal-protection adjudication is supported.
