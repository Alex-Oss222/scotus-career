The site falls within that category on affirmative parcel evidence, not merely because it is now owned in fee by a non-Indian. No judgment vote changes. ## Enhanced issue-specific alternatives

### 1. Reach the live declaration or dismiss after the liner waiver

**Reach:** The original controversy concerned a real permitted facility on an identified tract. The State remains bound by the operative judicial declaration classifying that tract as reservation land and determining the territorial legal regime for its permitting authority. The EPA waiver resolves the specific composite-liner demand but does not vacate that declaration or eliminate its present site-specific legal effect. Reversal can alter that legal relationship. Lee requires concrete application throughout review; the actual permit and declaration supply the connection missing from the conditional future patient/provider conflicts there.

**Competing path:** If the waiver had exhausted every present consequence and only a wish to erase precedent remained, Article III would bar a merits decision. That is the strongest threshold objection, and anticipated similar projects alone would not answer it. The released posture instead establishes a subsisting territorial determination about this actual facility. All eight reach the merits on that basis, without creating a rule that every adverse declaration automatically survives termination of its underlying controversy. No new enforcement threat is posited. Lee's limited corrective-jurisdiction doctrine does not itself supply merits power.

### 2. Give the cession territorial effect or preserve the boundary while transferring title

**Ceded-land diminishment:** Articles I and II effect a present, comprehensive surrender of tribal claim, right, title, and interest in the specified unallotted lands in return for an unconditional federal $600,000 purchase obligation. The federal appropriation and distribution provisions confirm a purchase rather than a sales agency remitting whatever proceeds settlers later generate. The surrender-of-portions context and Article VIII's treatment of the lands as ceded and sold fit that meaning. Solem assigns this combination exceptional evidentiary weight, and actual Hagen preserves it as a lawful path to clear congressional intent.

**Strongest objection:** Title and reservation status are legally distinct. Article XVIII expressly preserves “all provisions” of the 1858 treaty “the same as though this agreement had not been made,” with a separate annuity promise. Those words offer a serious basis for reading the transaction as a sale inside retained boundaries. Neither present fee ownership nor the word “cede” alone defeats that argument.

**Boundary-preservation path:** Read the savings clause as the express reason to overcome the cession inference, allowing settlement while retaining the territorial line. Allotment protections, continuing institutions, Article VIII's reserved uses, and absence of a negotiated boundary discussion are consistent with this construction. Its strongest weakness is the comprehensive cession-and-guaranteed-purchase transaction to which the controlling sources attach exceptional territorial significance, rather than mere opening or sale. Article XVIII must be reconciled with that specific transaction and cannot literally preserve the treaty's exclusion of the very settlement the agreement commands.

**Reconciled conclusion:** All eight choose the first path after weighing both, not by treating the presumption as conclusive. The statutory combination supplies the territorial consequence; the logical inconsistency of retaining exclusive possession does not alone prove the separate jurisdictional consequence. The limited annuity/past-entitlement reassurance explains Article XVIII in the context of the entire bargain and the documented negotiation concerns. The clause does not reverse the defined surrender or retain the original line over the ceded settler tracts. The clear-and-plain requirement and tribal ambiguity canon remain; neither permits disregarding an otherwise clear whole-act construction, and neither is displaced as a general rule.

### 3. Full savings-clause construction or an additional residual-rights rule

The verified record contains the broad clauses, concern that promised federal assistance might cease, the commissioners' threats, and the prominent annuity discussions. It contains no express boundary colloquy establishing what each member understood. All eight may therefore explain the clause's limited function by that context without finding informed tribal consent to every future jurisdictional consequence. The strongest textual objection is that treating general preservation and particular annuity protection as serving the same reassurance risks redundancy. The context and conflict with the specifically enacted surrender answer that objection here; the provision retains real work preserving the promised payments.

The Court need not independently adjudicate every possible obligation in the 1858 treaty. It should neither declare every other treaty entitlement cancelled nor turn a general reservation into an affirmative holding that an unspecified entitlement survives. Specific allotment, agency, institutional, and payment protections remain on their actual legal foundations. A literal claim that Article XVIII contains only annuity words is barred. A supported construction that its purpose in this dispute is protection of annuities and associated entitlements rather than the exterior boundary is compatible with all eight commitments. No unavailable DeCoteau appendix analogy is needed for that conclusion.

### 4. Secondary provisions and contemporary context

**Reinforcement path:** The state school sections comport with the enabling Act's exclusion of reservation land until extinguishment/restoration and support the cession reading. The express distinction in Article XVII between ceded lands and other lands, with a continuing liquor prohibition despite existing reservation liquor restrictions, is consistent with withdrawing the ordinary ceded lands. The commissioners' common-property account, contemporary requests for state and federal law, and the proclamation's cession terms likewise corroborate.

**Strongest objections:** A specific school grant could operate as a limited exception; state law could govern school uses rather than all territorial jurisdiction. A permanent liquor clause could do work even under a retained boundary. Article VIII reserves governmental uses and cautions against total termination. Assimilation rhetoric and references to public-domain restoration in discussion do not insert an operative restoration command into this Act. Congress considered several agreements together, and no universal tribal understanding of precise boundaries is established.

All eight give the secondary provisions and context reinforcing weight while acknowledging those objections. None treats them as independently sufficient here. This supports the full bounded framework rather than a rule that the auxiliary evidence is irrelevant. Article VIII and the specific limits of the record remain important reasons to reserve other land categories.

### 5. Later administration, settlement, and territorial scope

The mixed congressional, executive, judicial, and cartographic descriptions provide no dominant later interpretation and little force against the statutory ground. State administration, settler occupation, the changed population, and the 1962 tribal constitutional terminology provide subordinate corroboration, not later legal events diminishing the reservation. The strongest objection is that every surplus-land opening invites settlement and that precise population estimates here conflict. All eight accept that objection to treating demographics as determinative; no exact percentage, ethnicity count, nonexercise of authority, or modern landholding threshold controls.

A broader path would declare the entire reservation disestablished and every tract subject to the same jurisdiction. It fails because the site lies in the supported ceded/homestead category, other parcels have different retained protections, and the dispute does not require their classification. A narrower procedural remand would be warranted by an actual unresolved jurisdictional or parcel-classification defect; none is established for this site. A lack of original research papers or a complete regional survey is not such a defect. All eight choose the merits holding and bounded reversal, with a remand for consistent disposition of the actual remaining controversy.
