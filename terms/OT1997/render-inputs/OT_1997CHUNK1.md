## Event

Brown v. Williams, No. 97-5370. Filing order, October 20, 1997; October Term 1997, chunk 1. Brown seeks leave to file his certiorari petition without prepaying the docketing fee after the Sixth Circuit dismissed his appeal for nonpayment. The Court also considers a separate proposed fee-and-form condition on future noncriminal certiorari petitions. The order issues without oral argument.

## Participation

All nine Justices participate: Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer.

## Public Action

The motion for leave to proceed in forma pauperis is granted. The proposed prospective fee-and-Rule-33.1 condition on future noncriminal certiorari petitions is declined. No action is taken on certiorari.

## Judgment & Remedy

The present petition may proceed under the in forma pauperis rules without prepayment. No new payment deadline, refiling obligation, or future filing restriction is imposed. The Sixth Circuit's judgment is not reversed or reopened, and no relief on the underlying allegations follows.

## Opinion Topology

Per curiam opinion and order of the Court addressing both filing questions. No separate writing is filed.

## Holdings

**Present financial leave.** Rule 39 permits the present financial motion on the established inability-to-prepay premise. Discretionary denial under Rule 39.8 requires a supported finding that the particular submission is frivolous or malicious; earlier unsuccessful filings do not supply that finding. The Court's per curiam opinion applies that rule and declines denial on this record.

Brown's allegations concern interference with judicial access as well as laundry sabotage and judicial bias. No allegation is found true. But the available account and eight previous unsuccessful petitions do not establish that the complete present paper lacks an arguable review ground or is malicious. Martin and Demos require attention to present access content; its label alone does not establish an arguable claim. In re Vey illustrates a supported grant, while Vey v. Clinton permits denial despite indigence when the particular paper's developed grounds justify it. That denial remains available on its own predicate. Granting this motion establishes neither an access violation nor entitlement to certiorari, full briefing, counsel, or relief from a lower-court default.

**Prospective control.** A future noncriminal-certiorari fee-and-form condition requires demonstrated applicant-specific abuse, fit to that filing category, and support for the inadequacy of individual-paper treatment. The Court's per curiam opinion applies those independent requirements and declines the condition here.

Eight prior unsuccessful petitions and an earlier Rule 39.8 denial do not identify relevant repetition or failed individual controls. Keeping paid and criminal channels open would limit the condition's reach, but would not supply its justification. The rule creates no numerical threshold, universal warning requirement, duty to exhaust every conceivable alternative, or categorical prohibition on supported restrictions.

## Precedent Treatment

- Martin and Demos are applied: filing history does not replace examination of the present paper, and prospective control needs separate support.
- In re Vey and Vey v. Clinton are applied at their distinct scopes: financial qualification and particular-paper screening remain separate; the June Vey denial is undisturbed.
- Zatko and Martin's companion denial retain authority for supported current-submission refusals.
- Day, Sassower, and the subsequent applicant-specific cases retain the independent justification requirement for future controls. McDonald and Sindram's targeted extraordinary-writ authority does not automatically justify a different filing category.

## Law After Decision

Existing filing-access rules are applied without a new presumption, numerical threshold, or privileged claim category. Financial leave permits processing; certiorari and the underlying merits remain distinct. Supported current-paper denials and adequately justified future restrictions remain available.

## Separate Writings

No separate writing is filed.

## Procedure After Action

The certiorari petition remains pending for ordinary consideration under the Court's jurisdictional and review rules. This order supplies no grant or denial of review and no deadline for a future action.

## Source Notes

Rules 39, 38, and 33.1 govern the filing questions. The record describes eight earlier unsuccessful petitions without establishing a more precise uniform period or that those petitions repeated the current claim. The underlying allegations remain disputed, and no quotation from an unreproduced petition or affidavit is attributed to Brown.

## Event

Bates v. United States, No. 96-7185. Merits decision, November 4, 1997; argued October 7, 1997; October Term 1997, chunk 1. On certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1), the Court reviews the Seventh Circuit's reinstatement of a twelve-count indictment, 96 F.3d 964, after pretrial dismissal. The question is whether knowing and willful misapplication under the version of 20 U.S.C. §1097(a) applicable to the alleged 1990 conduct additionally requires an intent to injure or defraud.

## Participation

All nine Justices participate at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The judgment is affirmed on the presented statutory question.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 9–0. Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer. **Opposing:** none. The prosecution may proceed subject to every actual offense element and properly available objections. No conviction, guilt, evidentiary sufficiency, sentence, or instructional error is adjudicated.

## Opinion Topology

Justice Ginsburg delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Breyer. No separate writing is filed.

## Holdings

**Knowing and willful misapplication does not require an additional purpose to injure or defraud.** Under the applicable §1097(a), the Government must establish the actual knowing and willful culpable misapplication; an ordinary unpaid refund, accounting mistake, insolvency, or breach of a funding condition is not sufficient by itself. Ginsburg's opinion, joined by all nine Justices, controls this construction.

The express culpability terms perform substantial work. Wells requires fidelity to enacted elements without eliminating required fault; Ratzlaf gives willfulness its contextual statutory force; Staples and Morissette preserve culpability and the received meaning of criminal terms. These authorities do not require the same additional purpose in every offense. The bank-misapplication analogy rests on a distinct history in which earlier intent language disappeared during a technical revision. That history does not establish incorporation of the same purpose into §1097. After text, context, and that history are considered, no unresolved ambiguity justifies adding the proposed element through lenity.

The indictment alleges knowledge of refund duties and deliberate diversion, but allegations remain unproved. Rejecting an extra fraudulent purpose does not settle the complete meaning of willfulness, including knowledge of illegality, or every conversion theory. It creates no general rule that statutory silence always defeats inherited criminal meaning. The later refund-specific amendment is not applied retroactively. Aiding-and-abetting and independent indictment issues receive no expanded holding or assumed preservation.

## Precedent Treatment

- Wells is applied to the distinction between actual offense elements and a proposed additional condition.
- Ratzlaf's contextual construction and independently sufficient residual-lenity ground remain intact; they do not supply this statute's extra purpose.
- Staples, Morissette, and Liparota retain their protection against eliminating the fault required by the particular offense. No universal mental-state formula is adopted.

## Law After Decision

The applicable §1097(a) requires knowing and willful culpable misapplication, without a separate injurious or fraudulent purpose. Proof of the real elements remains necessary. The decision adds that statute-specific construction while leaving the full willfulness definition and other offenses unchanged.

## Separate Writings

No separate writing is filed.

## Procedure After Action

The reinstated prosecution returns to ordinary pretrial proceedings. Properly available independent objections retain their own requirements; the Court directs no particular trial outcome.

## Source Notes

United States v. Bates, 96 F.3d 964 (7th Cir. 1996), supplies the pretrial posture, twelve 1990 allegations, and conduct-date statutory wording. The allegations are assumed for the construction question only. The question stated here is an analytical formulation, not a quotation from a petition.

## Event

State Oil Co. v. Khan, No. 96-871. Merits decision, November 4, 1997; argued October 7, 1997; October Term 1997, chunk 1. On certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1), the Court reviews the Seventh Circuit's antitrust disposition, 93 F.3d 1358. The question is whether a vertical agreement setting a maximum resale price is unlawful per se under Sherman Act §1 and whether Albrecht should remain controlling.

## Participation

All nine Justices participate at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The antitrust disposition is vacated and the case remanded for rule-of-reason review. Albrecht's maximum-vertical-price per se rule is overruled.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Vacated and remanded on the antitrust component, 9–0. Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer. **Opposing:** none. The separate affirmance of the state-contract dismissal remains undisturbed. Neither party receives a judgment of ultimate antitrust liability or nonliability, and the district court's antitrust summary judgment is not automatically restored.

## Opinion Topology

Justice Scalia delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer. No separate writing is filed.

## Holdings

**Vertical maximum-price agreements receive rule-of-reason review.** A vertical agreement imposing a maximum resale price is evaluated for its competitive effects rather than condemned per se merely for imposing that ceiling. Albrecht is overruled to that extent. Scalia's opinion has all nine Justices' full joins and controls both the replacement rule and its limits.

Per se condemnation needs a sound basis for treating the category as predictably harmful. A ceiling can restrict services or facilitate an unlawful arrangement, but it can also restrain dealer markups and promote interbrand competition. Sylvania's treatment of vertical distribution and Atlantic Richfield's distinction between price benefits and cognizable injury undermine conclusive condemnation of the entire class. The identified harmful effects remain matters for proof under the rule appropriate to the actual restraint.

Statutory precedent deserves substantial respect. Here the defect is concrete: Albrecht's categorical premise prevents examination of arrangements whose competitive effects differ. The reliance interest in retaining categorical condemnation is limited because antitrust remedies remain available under the replacement standard. Lower courts properly followed Albrecht until this Court changed it; their duty of obedience is not a reason to preserve an unsound classification here.

State Oil's rebate obligation creates an actual price agreement. It cannot be relabeled a unilateral suggestion to avoid the precedent question. The rule change does not determine market power, competitive harm, injury, damages, or the expert evidence's ultimate significance. Harmful service suppression, exclusion, exploitation of dealer dependence, disguised minimum prices, and proved horizontal arrangements remain subject to the governing rules. Whether any such circumstance occurred here remains for the appropriate further proceedings. Minimum resale-price and horizontal price-fixing rules are unchanged; a dealer's lost margin alone is not antitrust injury.

## Precedent Treatment

- Albrecht v. Herald Co., 390 U.S. 145, is expressly overruled insofar as it makes vertical maximum resale-price agreements unlawful per se.
- Sylvania and Business Electronics retain their distinct treatment of vertical restraints and actual price agreements; their principles inform the revised classification without resolving every arrangement.
- Atlantic Richfield and Brunswick retain the independent antitrust-injury requirement.
- Kodak and Brooke Group retain their separate evidence and competitive-harm rules. The decision creates no general stare decisis checklist from Hubbard's divided rationales.

## Law After Decision

From November 4, 1997, vertical maximum resale-price agreements receive rule-of-reason review. They gain no immunity: actual competitive effects, injury, and other applicable requirements remain necessary. Minimum-price and horizontal restraints keep their distinct rules.

## Separate Writings

No separate writing is filed.

## Procedure After Action

The Seventh Circuit shall return the antitrust matter for proceedings under the rule of reason and consideration of properly available remaining issues and defenses. The classification change neither finally resolves nor automatically reopens every expert-evidence or injury question. The contract dismissal stands.

## Source Notes

Khan v. State Oil Co., 93 F.3d 1358 (7th Cir. 1996), supplies the actual agreement, rebate mechanism, summary-judgment posture, injury-evidence discussion, and separate contract affirmance. Lower-court treatment of evidence is not a finding of actual competitive harm or damages.

## Event

City of Monroe v. United States, No. 97-122. Summary merits disposition, November 17, 1997; October Term 1997, chunk 1. On direct appeal under 28 U.S.C. §1253 from the three-judge judgment, 962 F. Supp. 1501, the Court decides whether approval of Georgia's 1968 municipal-election default independently permits Monroe's majority-vote rule. The matter is resolved without oral argument.

## Participation

All nine Justices participate.

## Public Action

The challenged §5 injunction is reversed insofar as it bars the independently precleared statewide default, and the case is remanded.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 7–2. Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, and Ginsburg. **Opposing:** Souter and Breyer. The conflicting plurality injunction must be removed within the scope decided. The Court grants no preclearance to the 1966 local amendment or other charter revisions and adjudicates no §2 liability, damages, completed-election challenge, or replacement-system design.

## Opinion Topology

The Court issues a per curiam opinion. All nine Justices join Part I's review-channel and specific-submission principles. Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, and Ginsburg join Part II's independent-default application and remedy. Justice Souter dissents from Part II and the judgment, joined by Justice Breyer.

## Holdings

**Approval scope is a proper enforcement question.** An enforcement court may determine the actual scope and applicability of the Attorney General's preclearance, but may not grant substantive preclearance or reconsider the approval's substantive wisdom. Part I, joined by all nine Justices, controls this distinction and the requirement that actual changes be fairly identified.

**This independently approved default governs Monroe's silent charter.** Approval of the identified statewide majority default supplies clearance for its direct application to a municipality whose charter lacks the plurality provision to which the default yields. Part II, joined by seven Justices, controls that application. Monroe's unprecleared 1966 amendment does not negate authority independently supplied by the rule actually submitted and approved.

The relevant legal source is the 1968 default, not the local amendment. Monroe's earlier charter did not prescribe plurality. City of Rome's charter did, making its rejection of implied clearance for local amendments materially different. The statewide submission identified the majority rule; giving it effect here does not approve every local implementation or undisclosed amendment. The objection that a state submission cannot silently clear local changes remains correct, but it does not require duplicate approval of this identified direct rule.

Lopez, Young, and Foreman require actual submission scope and reject clearance by association, long use, or state compulsion. Their requirements remain fully operative. An approved delegation of discretion or statewide compilation does not silently clear distinct local methods. The result depends on the silent charter and sufficiently identified default; an ambiguous submission would not establish the same approval. Independent discrimination claims and genuinely distinct unapproved changes remain available for their proper proceedings.

## Precedent Treatment

- City of Rome is distinguished by its express plurality charter; its rejection of implied preclearance of local amendments remains binding.
- McCain and Clark retain the demand for specific submission and the submitting authority's responsibility for ambiguity.
- Lopez, Young, and Foreman are applied to the actual identified rule; no exemption for state-compelled administration is created.
- Bossier's substantive preclearance rules and §2's independent operation remain separate from this scope decision.

## Law After Decision

The approved majority default has independent effect for the stated charter and submission circumstances. The general requirement of specific preclearance remains unchanged. Neither the local amendment nor unidentified practices gain approval, and clearance supplies no immunity from independent discrimination law.

## Separate Writings

Souter's dissent, joined by Breyer, would affirm. The relevant change is Monroe's move from its established plurality practice, and charter silence does not make that change adequately disclosed by approval of a facial statewide default. City of Rome, McCain, and Clark require identification of the practical local change; Lopez, Young, and Foreman preserve that requirement. The dissent agrees that courts may determine approval scope and cannot reconsider substantive approval, but concludes that the statewide submission does not establish clearance of this local switch. It would retain prospective nonuse and the plurality injunction without deciding discrimination claims or completed-election consequences.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns for proceedings implementing the limited removal of the conflicting injunction. No new approval request or evidentiary hearing is automatically required. Any genuinely distinct change outside the approved default remains subject to ordinary coverage and preclearance requirements.

## Source Notes

The record identifies the pre-1966 plurality practice, the silent earlier charter, the unprecleared 1966 amendment, and the submitted and approved 1968 default. No broader or narrower undisclosed qualification in an approval letter is found. The decision concerns scope of approval, not substantive approval of majority voting in every setting.

Jeffries v. Harleston, Second Circuit No. 93-7876. Merits decision, November 17, 1997; October Term 1997, chunk 1. The Court reviews under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) the April 4, 1995 judgment, 52 F.3d 9, directing judgment for defendants after the shortening of Professor Leonard Jeffries's department-chair term. | Concurrence in vacatur and Part II; disagreement with the substantive rule and remand limits | Stone-Zsela | None |
| Dissent | O'Connor | Scalia, Thomas |

## Holdings

**The actual speech and service relationship govern the employment balance.** The appellate balance cannot sustain final defense judgment on this record and rationale. Neither retained tenure nor lack of entitlement to a longer chair term categorically defeats a retaliation claim; a reasonable forecast is relevant but does not itself decide constitutional protection. Part I, joined by Souter, Stevens, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer, controls this application.

Pickering and Connick require attention to public expression and actual institutional needs. The address concerned curriculum and racial history, occurred at an unaffiliated off-campus event, and was not assigned or controlled as an official duty. Introduction by the chair title did not establish sponsorship. Course assignments, tenure evaluations, records, meetings and budget work were real responsibilities, but the supported record identified no special policymaking, confidential or additional public-contact obligation. Recruitment, fundraising, faculty interaction and community relationships remain relevant institutional interests. The panel gave its forecast and the retained professorship more legal weight than that combination justified in resolving the whole case for defendants.

The jury's findings of no actual disruption and reasonable expected disruption both stand. Prevention need not await realized harm. Waters protects a genuinely relied-upon reasonable account only when it describes legally disciplinable speech; it creates no complete controlling investigation code. Perry, Umbehr and O'Hare preserve the difference between entitlement to a benefit and retaliation through its withdrawal. The Court adopts neither categorical protection of every part of this address, an actual-loss requirement, quantified damages to the institution, nor NTEU's entire prospective-ban burden for this individual action.

**Nine successful votes do not automatically establish an independent lawful cause.** If protected conduct supplies a substantial or motivating cause, each defendant's actual responsibility and any preponderance same-decision defense must be examined. Different punitive or animus findings do not alone establish that the same action would have occurred without protected speech. Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, controls that rule and rejection of the blanket causal ground.

Mt. Healthy and the actual-cause holdings of Umbehr, O'Hare and Crawford-El require a real counterfactual, not a conceivable lawful reason. The nine one-year votes are important evidence, but their lack of the six defendants' attributed animus does not itself answer that inquiry. Harleston and Reynolds did not vote; a causally effective proposal may matter, but procurement alone proves no liability. Everett, Badillo and Jacobs cast defeated removal votes; Bloom abstained. Those roles neither establish nor eliminate other supported causal contributions. Claims against Bernstein remain discontinued. A genuine independent lawful Board decision or a supported personal defense may still be dispositive. Ordinary competent direct and circumstantial proof applies without a special heightened motive burden.

**Further proceedings remain bounded.** Part III, joined by Souter, Stevens, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer, leaves actual verdict harmonization and consequences, sustainable individual defenses, capacity attribution and relief to ordinary adjudication. It orders no compulsory new trial. Harlow and Anderson v. Creighton require particularized personal-damages immunity at the late-October 1991 conduct date, and a distinct date for any preserved March 1992 claim. Later decisions supply no retrospective notice. Punitive answers do not replace protection, cause or immunity; prospective relief has separate live-remedy requirements. Elapsed time alone establishes neither mootness nor continued chair eligibility.

## Precedent Treatment

- Pickering, Connick, and Waters retain the contextual balance, genuine lawful ground and permissible forecast. No complete Waters investigative framework is adopted.
- Perry, Umbehr, and O'Hare are applied to the actual employment benefit, service relationship and causal inquiry.
- Mt. Healthy, Kimberlin, and Crawford-El retain ordinary proof and the actual same-decision defense.
- NTEU's broad prospective-restraint allocation and Settle's student-curriculum holding remain confined to their settings.
- Harlow and Anderson v. Creighton retain conduct-date immunity; no later merits rule establishes earlier notice.

## Law After Decision

The decision applies the role-sensitive employment balance and defendant-specific cause rules to this record, displacing the blanket defense judgment. It establishes no categorical academic-speech exemption, reaction-based exclusion, investigation protocol, universal retrial requirement, or final personal liability. Each controlling part has a direct majority; no combined plurality rule is necessary.

## Separate Writings

Stone-Zsela would classify the address as citizen speech as a matter of law and expressly narrow the disruption rule: offense, controversy, audience hostility, institutional embarrassment, reputational pressure, and disruption consisting of reactions to the viewpoint may not themselves justify punishment of this private public-concern speech. A professor's title and subject matter do not make an unaffiliated address university speech. Assigned teaching, administration and delegated chair speech remain subject to institutional control. Independently proved failure to perform duties, threats, harassment, unlawful discrimination in official work, insubordination, misuse of authority and other regulable misconduct remain lawful grounds.

He joins Part II because the nine votes cannot be declared lawful superseding causes by assuming reaction-generated disruption sufficient. Jeffries must prove each defendant's substantial or motivating causal role, and a defendant may prove the same action would occur for an independently lawful reason. Stone-Zsela considers the conflicting verdicts and differentiated roles to require a limited new trial on those questions, immunity and relief. He would neither reinstate inconsistent verdicts nor decide particular liability, qualified immunity, compensatory or punitive damages, or precise prospective relief. This compulsory-trial remedy and categorical substantive limitation do not command the Court.

O'Connor's dissent, joined by Scalia and Thomas, would affirm on the employment balance. The independent public-concern setting and nonpolicymaking characterization deserve weight, but course assignments, tenure evaluations, internal representation and budget work create a consequential relationship. The identified recruitment, fundraising, faculty-interaction and community interests, supported by the reasonable-expectation finding, justify the chair-term action without waiting for actual harm. The dissent performs that legal balance rather than treating sincere belief, offensive content, retained tenure or the jury's forecast as automatically decisive. It neither invents institutional sponsorship or deficient performance nor adopts the nine-vote rationale or a final immunity determination as an alternative holding.

The Second Circuit must conduct or direct further proceedings consistent with Parts I–III. Actual claim scope, verdict forms, instructions and preservation govern available dispositions. No March 1992 claim is created, no discontinued claim revived, and no current chair appointment or award is automatically restored.

## Event

United States v. Rybar, Third Circuit No. 95-3185. Merits decision, November 24, 1997; October Term 1997, chunk 1. The Court reviews under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) the December 30, 1996 judgment, 103 F.3d 273, sustaining possession convictions under 18 U.S.C. §922(o). Conditional pleas preserve the Commerce Clause and Second Amendment challenges to Counts I and III.

## Participation

All nine Justices participate at submission and decision.

## Public Action

The Commerce Clause challenge is rejected. The appellate judgment affirming possession Counts I and III is vacated, and the case is remanded for the preserved Second Amendment application.

## Judgment & Remedy

| Component | Supporting | Opposing |
|---|---|---|
| Reject the commerce challenge, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | None |
| Recognize personal invocation of the arms guarantee, 9–0; the claim proceeds to application | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | None |
| Vacate the appellate affirmance and remand the arms question, 8–1 | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Stone-Zsela would affirm Counts I and III |

The remand orders no acquittal, release, return of weapons, new trial, automatic hearing, or resentencing. Any consequential relief depends on the lawful disposition of the affected possession counts. Dismissed NFA transfer Counts II and IV remain outside the judgment.

## Opinion Topology

Kennedy delivers the opinion of the Court. All other Justices join Parts I–II on commerce power and personal invocation. Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer join Part III's vacatur and remand. Stone-Zsela concurs in Parts I–II and dissents from Part III and the judgment.

## Holdings

**Congress may regulate these possessions as part of the machinegun-market regime.** Section 922(o)'s possession component constitutionally reaches Rybar's possession of the two completed machineguns because Hale already established that possession may be included as a necessary anti-evasion and anti-impairment component of Congress's direct prohibition on additions to the post-1986 civilian machinegun market. Part I, joined by all nine Justices, rejects Rybar's commerce challenge; the possession component's validity does not depend on adding a transaction-specific interstate-crossing element to §922(o).

Rybar possessed and sold two completed submachine guns in consecutive gun-show transactions. The sales make the market relationship concrete, while the offenses remain possession. The enacted regime restricts both transfer and possession of post-ban machineguns, subject to the statute's government-related channels and prior-lawful-possession exception. A transfer restriction would be materially impaired if newly prohibited stock could be acquired, retained, held, concealed or circulated free of the possession component; Rybar's transactions fall at the center rather than the edge of that anti-evasion mechanism. Lopez requires the Court to identify the statutory class and the interstate mechanism before aggregation or downstream magnitude can perform constitutional work. The mechanism here is not gun violence, fear, insurance cost, productivity, marketability in the abstract, or presumed foreign or military provenance; it is the fitted relationship between possession and enforcement of the valid national machinegun-transfer and stock regime. Hale controls: Rybar creates no broader Commerce category and does not enlarge Hale's anti-evasion rule, but shows that the rule applies when possession occurs in actual commercial transfer circumstances at the core of the regulated market.

The Court does not rewrite §922(o) to require proof that the particular machinegun crossed a State line in Rybar's transaction; the constitutional ground lies in the valid market regime and the necessary inclusion of the defined possession class, not in an omitted case-specific crossing element. Marketability alone, remote interstate history alone and generalized crime costs are each insufficient. The Court does not decide a materially different possession statute lacking a fitted anti-evasion relationship to a valid interstate regime, and it does not establish that every object subject to national regulation may be federally possessed forever because it once moved in commerce. No universally mandatory legislative-findings rule or general police power is adopted. Government-related statutory channels and lawful pre-May 19, 1986 possession retain their statutory scope; manufacture date alone does not establish the latter exception.

**The personal arms claim requires a separate application.** Part II, joined by all nine Justices, holds that Rybar may invoke the personal guarantee without militia enrollment or service. Military usefulness neither excludes an arm nor makes it immune from regulation; a commercial setting does not categorically erase the claim. Necessary acquisition remains connected to protected keeping. Love, Hale and the preserved Miller inquiry require assessment of the actual weapons, conduct and restriction independently of commerce authority and registration duties.

**The displaced affiliation premise requires a bounded return here.** Part III, joined by eight Justices, vacates the appellate affirmance because its material militia requirement is invalid and its additional discussion supplies no adequate independent weapon-and-restriction application. The Chinese Type 54 and U.S. Military M-3 are identified completed automatic weapons, and their transactions are settled inputs. Their known characteristics strengthen the arguments for regulation, but classification alone does not resolve the constitutional restriction. Hale's registration holding does not establish the possession prohibition's validity.

Further consideration may use the existing record or ordinary proceedings genuinely needed. No empirical study, new universal scrutiny tier or comprehensive weapons catalogue is required. The Court does not decide ultimate validity or invalidity, make every lower legal error demand remand, or resolve receivers, conversion parts, accessories, ordinary semiautomatic arms or every §922(o) application.

## Precedent Treatment

- Hale is followed without enlargement: it supplies the anti-evasion/material-impairment rule for the post-1986 civilian machinegun market; its registration rule remains distinct from the possession prohibition.
- Harris and Bishop/Stokes are distinguished as vehicle-taking cases with their own economic/instrumentality mechanism and enacted vehicle nexus.
- American Life League is distinguished as direct interference with an interstate service market.
- Love and Hale's personal-holder and acquisition principles displace a militia-affiliation prerequisite while preserving actual application.
- Miller retains its actual weapon/record significance, without automatic military-use exclusion or immunity.
- Lopez is applied through the Court's controlling sequence: the regulated class and interstate mechanism come first, and downstream consequences cannot create the missing connection. Staples remains a statutory culpability decision and supplies no constitutional weapon classification.

## Law After Decision

The commerce challenge fails under Hale's existing anti-evasion/material-impairment rule; Rybar adds no new Commerce gateway or doctrine. As of this decision, Lopez supplies connection before magnitude and reserves genuine Necessary-and-Proper inclusion; Harris applies a direct vehicle and economic-taking mechanism with an enacted nexus, Stone-Zsela separately emphasizing the instrumentality mechanism; American Life League addresses direct obstruction of an interstate service market; Hale makes the anti-evasion/material-impairment doctrine operational; Bishop/Stokes applies Harris without a new rule; Pic-A-State treats an interstate procurement transmission as interstate commerce itself; Mussari/Schroeder directly protects a legally fixed cross-border payment obligation; and Rybar applies Hale. No interstate-externality branch has been adopted as controlling law. The personal-holder boundaries remain controlling, while the two weapons' ultimate protection against this possession restriction remains open on remand. The Court adopts no categorical automatic-weapons exclusion, general danger exception, universal scrutiny tier or automatic-remand rule.

## Separate Writings

Stone-Zsela would affirm both possession convictions through an independent constitutional application. Rybar holds the personal right without militia affiliation, and a substantial possession restriction cannot be justified merely by calling it commercial regulation. He treats preservation of prior lawful stock as a transition limiting expansion, not evidence that older weapons are safer; age alone establishes no exception. Government channels do not make civilian military usefulness either disqualifying or immune. The continuing availability of ordinary firearms describes the restriction's reach, but substitutes alone would not justify prohibiting a protected arm. His conclusion rests on the identified weapons and mechanism, not invented casualty findings. It establishes no universal rule for dangerous or military arms, no equivalence between machineguns and ordinary semiautomatic firearms, and no result for receivers, conversion components or accessories. **Federal bribery — no demonstrable-effects requirement.** Thomas's opinion, joined by all nine Justices, holds that §666 does not require proof that the bribe demonstrably affected federal funds.

Wells supports that distinction without construing §666 itself; context and Ratzlaf's residual lenity do not establish the proposed condition.
