## Controlling holdings

### A retrospective access claim must identify a concrete lost judicial opportunity

**Holding and operative rule:** A claim seeking relief for past obstruction of court access must identify a nonfrivolous underlying judicial action, intentional obstruction causing loss or material impairment of the opportunity to pursue it, and presently available relief for that loss that cannot be obtained through surviving litigation. Ordinary notice pleading requires identification of these substantive predicates, not proof of certain success or a futile prior filing.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's opinion, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer, controls for eight Justices. Thomas concurs only in the judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** Lewis v. Casey ties access to actual injury to a judicial opportunity; the same need applies when the alleged obstruction has already ended. Without an identifiable underlying action and causally lost relief, a court cannot distinguish a denied legal opportunity from an additional description of the underlying wrong. Harbury's complaint failed to identify the lost cause of action. Swierkiewicz preserves ordinary notice pleading and rejects a requirement to plead evidentiary proof; it does not remove the substantive elements of the claim. The grave allegations must receive their proper procedural assumption, but their gravity cannot identify a missing cause or remedy. The asserted constitutional category and a Bivens damages channel are assumed only to resolve these defects, so their source and availability need not be decided. The actual truth of the allegations, official immunity, foreign-affairs defenses and the surviving torts' elements likewise are not necessary to this disposition.

**Precedent treatment:** Lewis v. Casey is extended from actual hindrance of current litigation to the necessary injury and remedial connection for an asserted lost past opportunity; Swierkiewicz retains ordinary notice pleading.

### The clarified theory also fails to identify distinct available relief

**Holding and operative rule:** Even treating counsel's intentional-infliction theory and lost emergency injunction as part of the pleaded case, this access count identifies no presently attainable remedy unavailable through the surviving tort action. That independently sufficient defect requires dismissal of the revived count.

**Authority:** The same eight Justices join this alternative holding; Thomas's judgment agreement supplies no additional rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** The requested emergency intervention could no longer save Harbury's husband when this suit was brought; that relief is unavailable under either theory. The identified intentional-infliction claim against the CIA defendants remains pleaded in counts 18 and 19. If it can be maintained, relief for its demonstrated consequences remains potentially available through that claim, and Harbury identifies no additional presently obtainable access remedy. This is an examination of the actual clarified cause and requested relief, not a presumption that all torts with similar labels compensate identical harm. If the predicate fails for a reason independent of the alleged deception, that failure cannot establish a viable underlying action for access purposes. Intentional deception can impede legal action, but it does not automatically create an additional damages channel for the same alleged consequences. Merely naming the same tort on amendment does not answer the independently adjudicated defect. Ordinary amendment rules remain applicable to any properly sought amendment on its actual allegations; the mandate orders none.

**Precedent treatment:** Lewis requires injury-fitted relief, not a duplicative access label. Malesko supplies no automatic new federal damages channel from an allegation of serious injury.

## Separate Writings

**Thomas, concurring in the judgment.** The Constitution does not impose the information duty asserted here: officials need not provide national-security information or answer informal requests in the claimed manner. That narrower objection suffices to reject this access count. The affirmative practical-assistance obligation addressed in Lewis concerned persons in custody and does not create this outside-custody duty. Thomas does not join the Court's assumed-right framework or its two alternative analyses, and does not assert a general license to obstruct actual judicial proceedings.
