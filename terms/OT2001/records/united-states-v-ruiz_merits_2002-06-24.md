## Controlling holdings

### I. The asserted legal error must be examined to determine sentence-review authority

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 3742(a)(1) permits a court to determine whether an asserted constitutional condition connected to a sentence establishes that the sentence was imposed in violation of law; a merely discretionary refusal to depart, when the judge understood the discretion, supplies no such appeal. A mistaken belief that departure authority was absent could supply legal error, but that premise is not present here.

**Authority:** Part I of Justice Breyer's Opinion of the Court has the direct support of Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Thomas agrees in his dissent that the claimed statutory basis can be determined, without joining the Court opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that disagreement with the sentencing judge's choice differs from a claim that the choice followed an unconstitutional bargaining condition. The judge understood departure authority and declined to exercise it. That excludes the mistaken-authority route, but Ruiz's refusal of the waiver, withdrawal of the offer and request for the associated sentencing treatment connect her legal allegation to the sentence sufficiently to require its examination.

Mine Workers supplies the power to determine jurisdiction. Steel Co.'s insistence on an actual jurisdictional basis is respected: the constitutional question must be resolved to determine the asserted statutory basis, rather than being decided as unrelated merits advice on assumed jurisdiction. This inquiry creates neither general review of discretionary leniency nor a guaranteed bargain. The constitutional answer and its jurisdictional consequence follow in Parts II–VI.

### II. The Constitution imposes no universal duty of advance impeachment disclosure

**Holding and operative rule:** The Constitution imposes no universal duty to disclose impeachment information before a plea. The Government need not provide impeachment information concerning informants or other witnesses as a general prerequisite to a plea agreement, and minor, speculative, cumulative or ordinary credibility matters need not precede a binding plea. A competent defendant must actually understand the right relinquished, the charge and the constitutionally relevant consequences and act voluntarily; that requirement does not guarantee advance knowledge of every witness weakness or every fact useful to counsel's assessment.

**Authority:** Part II of the Opinion of the Court has eight direct joins: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Thomas agrees with its rejection of a universal impeachment duty on his separate trial-function ground, without joining its reasoning.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a constitutional trial-disclosure guarantee does not itself fix the timing of every item useful to bargaining. Brady, Bagley and Kyles protect fair adjudication through favorable evidence, including combined impeachment and preparation uses. Brady v. United States and the received plea cases nevertheless permit a knowing, voluntary decision made with uncertainty about the prosecution's proof. Knowledge of the charge and the constitutional choice differs from complete knowledge of evidentiary consequences.

The contrary position is substantial: known weakness in a decisive witness may materially affect investigation, competent advice and willingness to plead. But the usefulness of impeachment varies with the information and the defendant's existing knowledge, and an actually informed choice can accept uncertainty about credibility without making that evidence irrelevant. Practical importance therefore does not convert every credibility matter into a constitutional prerequisite to a plea. Whether information labeled impeachment that also bears on whether the offense can be established, or other impeachment of particular significance, must precede a plea is a different question from the universal duty rejected here.

Legitimate early-resolution and witness-confidentiality interests also weigh against making trial-oriented preparation a constitutional prerequisite to a plea agreement. These are general considerations, not findings of danger to a witness here. The continuing factual-innocence disclosure promise and ordinary plea safeguards form part of this setting; neither is treated as proof of compliance or an automatic cure. Godinez and Bousley retain their requirements of capacity, actual choice and knowledge of the offense. Vonn preserves the judge's distinct duties and does not establish forfeiture here. The holding rejects a universal impeachment-disclosure duty, not a supported claim of actual deception, ineffective assistance or an uninformed plea.

### III. Before a binding plea, the Government must disclose information within a substantive exculpatory floor

**Holding and operative rule:** Before securing a binding guilty plea or an irrevocable waiver through a plea agreement, the Government must disclose information known to the prosecution team that gives a substantial evidentiary basis to negate an element of the charged offense, to show that the accused did not commit the charged conduct, or to establish a legally available complete affirmative defense. The information must be materially significant to an objectively reasonable plea decision; minor, speculative, cumulative or ordinary credibility matters do not qualify. The Government may not avoid this floor by labeling such information impeachment. The duty reaches information already known to the prosecution team; it imposes no duty to complete trial preparation, to investigate or to generate evidence.

**Authority:** Part III of the Opinion of the Court has five direct joins: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas dissent.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the plea guarantee and the disclosure guarantee converge where information bears on whether the offense or a complete defense can be established. Brady v. United States permits a knowing, voluntary plea despite uncertainty about the prosecution's proof, and Boykin requires an actually informed and voluntary choice. A defendant who surrenders trial while the prosecution team holds information giving a substantial evidentiary basis to negate an element, to show that the accused did not commit the charged conduct, or to establish a complete defense the law makes available does not merely choose under uncertainty about proof; the choice is made without the information that bears most directly on whether there is any offense to admit. Bousley's insistence on knowledge of the real offense points the same way. Brady v. Maryland and Kyles place responsibility for favorable information on the prosecution team as a whole, so the floor reaches what the team knows, not only what the trial prosecutor holds.

The floor is confined. The information must be materially significant to an objectively reasonable plea decision; minor, speculative or cumulative items and ordinary credibility matters do not qualify. Its significance turns on whether the offense or the defense can be established, not on a prediction of how a jury would weigh credibility. A label does not control: credibility evidence that itself supplies a substantial basis to negate an element, as when it shows that the only witness to an element could not have observed it, falls within the floor, while ordinary impeachment does not. The duty concerns information already known; it imposes no obligation to complete trial preparation, to investigate or to generate evidence, and it is not open-file discovery. The agreement's continuing promise to disclose information establishing factual innocence is narrower than the floor: it does not reach information that supplies a substantial basis to negate an element or that supports a complete affirmative defense despite admitted conduct. The Court decides no disclosure duty broader than this floor.

### IV. Protective measures are available, but a plea agreement may not demand an irrevocable waiver of the floor

**Holding and operative rule:** Legitimate confidentiality, witness-safety and early-resolution interests may be accommodated through redaction, delayed disclosure that still precedes the binding plea, and adequate summaries, with judicial supervision when needed. The Government may decline to negotiate an immediate plea when it cannot make the required disclosure, but it may not demand, as a term of a plea agreement, an irrevocable waiver of disclosure within the floor.

**Authority:** Part IV of the Opinion of the Court has the same five direct joins. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas dissent.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the floor's limits answer the practical objections to pre-plea disclosure without making concealment of floor information a bargaining term. Early resolution and witness confidentiality are legitimate interests, and the record supplies no finding of danger to any witness here. Those interests are met by measures fitted to them: redaction of identifying details, sequencing that delays disclosure until shortly before the plea becomes binding, and summaries adequate to convey the substance of the information, under judicial supervision where a genuine privilege or safety concern is shown. When no such measure suffices, the Government may decline to offer an immediate plea and proceed on the ordinary trial schedule, where the trial-disclosure rules apply. What it may not do is obtain, through the agreement itself, an irrevocable surrender of information within the floor. A defendant cannot knowingly evaluate a waiver of information whose existence and content are withheld at the moment of choice, and the floor exists to secure the informed character of that choice. Nothing in this Part requires an offer or limits the terms the Government may otherwise propose.

### V. An otherwise available bargaining benefit cannot be conditioned on surrender of the floor

**Holding and operative rule:** The Government may not condition an otherwise available plea-bargaining benefit, including a sentencing recommendation, on the defendant's surrender of the disclosure floor. The restriction is confined: there is no right to an offer, the Government keeps its discretion to choose terms, to bargain firmly and to withdraw an offer for lawful reasons, and other knowing and voluntary waivers remain available. After disclosure, the defendant may still plead.

**Authority:** Part V of the Opinion of the Court has the same five direct joins. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas dissent.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a benefit the Government would otherwise extend may not be withheld because the defendant refuses to surrender the floor. Lack of an entitlement to the benefit does not answer the claim: an unconstitutional condition can cause injury although the recommendation itself is discretionary. Wade v. United States holds that prosecutorial discretion over sentencing-related motions remains subject to constitutional limits even though no defendant is entitled to a motion; a recommendation withheld as the price of refusing a constitutional protection falls within that principle. Bordenkircher v. Hayes remains: the Government may present hard choices in the give-and-take of bargaining and may withdraw or refuse offers for lawful reasons. Mezzanatto remains: a defendant may knowingly and voluntarily agree to a defined use of the defendant's own statements. Surrender of the floor differs from those choices because it is made without knowledge of the information surrendered and defeats the informed character of the very plea it accompanies. This is a constitutional limit on plea terms confined to the floor, not a general bar on waivers demanded at the outset of bargaining, and it leaves every other lawful term available. The Government may offer the same benefit on terms that preserve the floor, and once disclosure is made the defendant may plead on any lawful terms.

### VI. The withdrawn recommendation was conditioned on surrender of the floor; the vacatur is affirmed for a limited remand

**Holding and operative rule:** The proposed agreement's affirmative-defense waiver demanded surrender of the floor, as did its impeachment waiver to the extent it would reach information within the floor; the continuing factual-innocence promise did not cover the floor's element and complete-defense categories. Because the fast-track recommendation was withdrawn after Ruiz refused the waiver, her constitutional allegation is connected to the sentence, and section 3742(a)(1) supports review; the Ninth Circuit's sentence vacatur is affirmed on these grounds. On remand the district court determines whether Ruiz's rejection of the impermissible component materially contributed to the withholding of the recommendation, and whether the Government would have withheld it for independent lawful reasons; refusal of the impermissible component is not such a reason. The decision orders no automatic departure, enforcement of the unaccepted offer, plea withdrawal, innocence finding or prescribed sentence; Ruiz's conviction stands.

**Authority:** Part VI of the Opinion of the Court has the same five direct joins. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would reverse the sentence vacatur.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Ruiz's claim does not depend on proof that the Government held floor information. Her claim is that a recommendation otherwise available to her was conditioned on a waiver that reached the floor and was withdrawn when she refused it; the absence of a particular withheld item limits the remedy but not the validity of the condition. A sentence imposed after the Government withheld a recommendation as the price of refusing the floor, if that refusal materially contributed, rests on an unconstitutional condition. The judge's correct understanding of departure discretion does not cure that defect, because the defect lies in the Government's withholding of the recommendation that would have informed the exercise of that discretion. Section 3742(a)(1) therefore reaches the claim, and the Ninth Circuit's vacatur stands on these grounds rather than on its broader disclosure rule.

Wade v. United States's requirement of a substantial threshold showing before an evidentiary hearing about a prosecutor's refusal applies. The admitted record supplies that showing: the offer contained the impermissible component on its face, Ruiz refused the waiver and the offer was withdrawn. Generalized allegations would not suffice, and the inquiry extends only to the two remand questions. Because the rejected waiver also reached impeachment information outside the floor, which this decision does not require to be disclosed, the district court must determine whether rejection of the impermissible component materially contributed to the withholding, and whether the Government would have withheld the recommendation for independent lawful reasons. If that refusal materially contributed and the Government would not have withheld the recommendation for independent lawful reasons, the court resentences under its existing departure authority without treating refusal of the impermissible component as a lawful reason to deny the recommendation's consideration. The unaccepted offer is not enforced, no reduction is guaranteed, and Ruiz's later plea without an agreement neither assented to the rejected waiver nor becomes subject to withdrawal. No innocence prerequisite or second harmlessness hurdle is added to a completed constitutional violation.

### Reach, components and proposed judgment

The eight Associates divide four to four on the judgment. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer would affirm the Ninth Circuit's sentence vacatur on a confined ground: the identified waiver is invalid to the extent it demands surrender of information within a substantive exculpatory floor, and the remand must determine whether refusal of that component contributed to the withdrawn recommendation. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would reverse because the identified disclosure condition is not constitutionally invalid, leaving the district judge's discretionary refusal of the two-level reduction undisturbed. On either path no accepted bargain is enforced, no automatic discount is awarded, and Ruiz's later guilty plea is not vacated.

R1 is appellate authority under section 3742(a); R2 is advance impeachment disclosure; R3 is the affirmative-defense term, the innocence promise and any substantive exculpatory floor; R4 is waiver, causal injury and fitted sentencing relief. The distinction between R2 and R3 must remain visible. No path is a declaration that impeachment can never influence a sensible plea decision, or that every prospective surrender of unknown information is valid.

### Competing paths and strongest objections

**R1-A — determine the legal claim necessary to appellate authority.** A mere discretionary refusal to depart, when the judge understands the discretion, does not become appealable because the defendant wanted a better sentence. Ruiz nevertheless identifies an asserted constitutional condition connected to the denied recommendation and resulting sentence. A court can determine whether that assertion establishes a sentence imposed in violation of law; authority to determine its own jurisdiction does not create unrestricted sentence review. Resolution of the constitutional theory determines whether this route to sentence relief exists. Mine Workers supplies that limited jurisdiction-determining power; the packet's Steel Co. requirement prevents an assumption of jurisdiction to deliver unrelated merits advice.

**R1-B — decline all consideration because the judge knew departure discretion existed.** Its strongest support is the undisputed discretionary ruling. Its defect is failure to distinguish disagreement over discretion from the claimed constitutional ground. All eight permit examination of that ground to determine whether the statutory basis exists; none recognizes a general appellate power to reconsider the discretionary choice itself.

**R2-A — no constitutional requirement of advance impeachment disclosure as a condition of this early plea arrangement.** Brady, Bagley and Kyles protect fair adjudication through usable favorable evidence; they do not by themselves establish a right to receive the government's full impeachment preparation before deciding whether to bargain. Brady v. United States recognizes informed voluntary choice without complete certainty about the prosecution's strength. Godinez separates capacity from actual waiver; Bousley requires real knowledge of the offense. Those requirements remain, but they are not an entitlement to know every fact that might change the expected value of trial. The defense remains able to decline the bargain, seek trial and invoke the trial-disclosure rules.

The strongest counterargument is that credibility evidence can be decisive to competent advice, and disclosure after a guilty plea may have no useful trial-stage function. Kyles's attention to preparation makes that argument substantial. The selected answer is about constitutional timing and the scope of informed choice, not about evidence being unimportant. A knowing relinquishment of trial can be made while accepting uncertainty about witness credibility; the present legal challenge establishes no misinformation about the charge, actual false representation, proved concealed innocence material or deficient legal assistance. Legitimate early-resolution and witness-confidentiality interests also counsel against requiring full trial preparation as a constitutional prerequisite to this bargain. They are general legal considerations, not invented findings of danger to a particular witness here.

**R2-B — impose a qualified duty for information materially important to plea advice.** It would protect important credibility information without mandating an open file, and permit judicial safeguards for demonstrated safety or privilege concerns. Its strongest difficulty is the proposed constitutional trigger: material importance to a bargaining assessment is wider than the received fair-trial rule, and the packet supplies neither a particular item nor an actual advice defect requiring that extension. Stevens would accept that broader duty but treats the narrower floor in R3-B as sufficient for this judgment; no other Justice in this provisional assessment adopts it for this offer. None concludes that counsel's presence or a signature automatically defeats a supported plea-invalidity claim.

**R3-A — reject categorical invalidity of the affirmative-defense clause in the presented agreement.** The innocence-disclosure promise remains operative and distinct; it is not redescribed as encompassing every favorable use or every affirmative defense. On the presented early-bargaining terms, the Constitution would not require the Government to identify and disclose every item bearing on a potential affirmative defense before making this offer. The same informed-choice principles permit acceptance of evidentiary uncertainty. This does not decide the treatment of a proved concealed item establishing factual innocence, an affirmative misrepresentation about a known defense, or a claim that an actual plea lacked knowledge of the offense or effective assistance. No particular withheld defense is found on this record. Its strongest difficulty is that the innocence promise reaches only information establishing factual innocence, so a known complete defense or information negating an element could be withheld under the clause until the plea is binding.

**R3-B — recognize a confined substantive exculpatory floor that a plea agreement cannot waive.** Before a binding plea or an irrevocable waiver through a plea agreement, the Government would disclose information known to the prosecution team that gives a substantial evidentiary basis to negate an element, to show that the accused did not commit the charged conduct, or to establish a legally available complete affirmative defense, when the information is materially significant to an objectively reasonable plea decision. Minor, speculative, cumulative and ordinary credibility matters fall outside it, a label does not control, and there is no duty to complete trial preparation, investigate or create evidence. A complete legal defense may matter even when factual conduct is admitted and so may fall outside the innocence promise; that is why the two categories cannot be merged. The strongest objection is that the plea guarantee requires knowledge of rights, charge and consequences rather than evidentiary knowledge. The answer is that this floor concerns whether there is an offense to admit or a complete defense to forgo, not the strength of the proof, and that redaction, delay and adequate summaries meet legitimate confidentiality and early-resolution interests. It does not pronounce every conceivable nondisclosure before a plea a constitutional violation.

**R4-A — reverse the invalid-condition basis without enforcing a hypothetical contract.** Mezzanatto permits an actual informed limited waiver but does not establish a universal power to demand any surrender. Here Ruiz never accepted the agreement. The Court therefore determines whether the offered condition supplied the claimed constitutional injury; it does not enforce an agreement she made. If the challenged requirement is not constitutionally invalid, neither rejection of it nor the lost favorable recommendation makes this discretionary sentence unlawful on the presented theory.

**R4-B — affirm the vacatur and remand for causal and remedial determination.** An unconstitutional condition can cause a cognizable loss even without an entitlement to the underlying benefit; absence of a discovered hidden item is not a universal answer to such a claim. This path needs an unlawful condition, which R3-B supplies. The withdrawal after Ruiz refused the waiver connects the allegation to the sentence, and Wade's substantial threshold for an evidentiary hearing is met by the conditioned offer, refusal and withdrawal. Causal injury and authorized relief still need adjudication: whether refusal of the impermissible component materially contributed to the withholding, and whether the Government would have withheld the recommendation for independent lawful reasons. A court could not simply award the value of an unaccepted offer. No separate harmless-error test or innocence prerequisite is added to the definition of a completed constitutional violation.

## Holdings

### I. The asserted legal error must be examined to determine sentence-review authority

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 3742(a)(1) permits a court to determine whether an asserted constitutional condition connected to a sentence establishes that the sentence was imposed in violation of law; a merely discretionary refusal to depart, when the judge understood the discretion, supplies no such appeal. A mistaken belief that departure authority was absent could supply legal error, but that premise is not present here.

**Authority:** Part I of Justice Breyer's Opinion of the Court has the direct support of Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Thomas agrees in his dissent that the claimed statutory basis can be determined, without joining the Court opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that disagreement with the sentencing judge's choice differs from a claim that the choice followed an unconstitutional bargaining condition. The judge understood departure authority and declined to exercise it. That excludes the mistaken-authority route, but Ruiz's refusal of the waiver, withdrawal of the offer and request for the associated sentencing treatment connect her legal allegation to the sentence sufficiently to require its examination.

Mine Workers supplies the power to determine jurisdiction. Steel Co.'s insistence on an actual jurisdictional basis is respected: the constitutional question must be resolved to determine the asserted statutory basis, rather than being decided as unrelated merits advice on assumed jurisdiction. This inquiry creates neither general review of discretionary leniency nor a guaranteed bargain. The constitutional answer and its jurisdictional consequence follow in Parts II–VI.

### II. The Constitution imposes no universal duty of advance impeachment disclosure

**Holding and operative rule:** The Constitution imposes no universal duty to disclose impeachment information before a plea. The Government need not provide impeachment information concerning informants or other witnesses as a general prerequisite to a plea agreement, and minor, speculative, cumulative or ordinary credibility matters need not precede a binding plea. A competent defendant must actually understand the right relinquished, the charge and the constitutionally relevant consequences and act voluntarily; that requirement does not guarantee advance knowledge of every witness weakness or every fact useful to counsel's assessment.

**Authority:** Part II of the Opinion of the Court has eight direct joins: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Thomas agrees with its rejection of a universal impeachment duty on his separate trial-function ground, without joining its reasoning.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a constitutional trial-disclosure guarantee does not itself fix the timing of every item useful to bargaining. Brady, Bagley and Kyles protect fair adjudication through favorable evidence, including combined impeachment and preparation uses. Brady v. United States and the received plea cases nevertheless permit a knowing, voluntary decision made with uncertainty about the prosecution's proof. Knowledge of the charge and the constitutional choice differs from complete knowledge of evidentiary consequences.

The contrary position is substantial: known weakness in a decisive witness may materially affect investigation, competent advice and willingness to plead. But the usefulness of impeachment varies with the information and the defendant's existing knowledge, and an actually informed choice can accept uncertainty about credibility without making that evidence irrelevant. Practical importance therefore does not convert every credibility matter into a constitutional prerequisite to a plea. Whether information labeled impeachment that also bears on whether the offense can be established, or other impeachment of particular significance, must precede a plea is a different question from the universal duty rejected here.

Legitimate early-resolution and witness-confidentiality interests also weigh against making trial-oriented preparation a constitutional prerequisite to a plea agreement. These are general considerations, not findings of danger to a witness here. The continuing factual-innocence disclosure promise and ordinary plea safeguards form part of this setting; neither is treated as proof of compliance or an automatic cure. Godinez and Bousley retain their requirements of capacity, actual choice and knowledge of the offense. Vonn preserves the judge's distinct duties and does not establish forfeiture here. The holding rejects a universal impeachment-disclosure duty, not a supported claim of actual deception, ineffective assistance or an uninformed plea.

### III. Before a binding plea, the Government must disclose information within a substantive exculpatory floor

**Holding and operative rule:** Before securing a binding guilty plea or an irrevocable waiver through a plea agreement, the Government must disclose information known to the prosecution team that gives a substantial evidentiary basis to negate an element of the charged offense, to show that the accused did not commit the charged conduct, or to establish a legally available complete affirmative defense. The information must be materially significant to an objectively reasonable plea decision; minor, speculative, cumulative or ordinary credibility matters do not qualify. The Government may not avoid this floor by labeling such information impeachment. The duty reaches information already known to the prosecution team; it imposes no duty to complete trial preparation, to investigate or to generate evidence.

**Authority:** Part III of the Opinion of the Court has five direct joins: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas dissent.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the plea guarantee and the disclosure guarantee converge where information bears on whether the offense or a complete defense can be established. Brady v. United States permits a knowing, voluntary plea despite uncertainty about the prosecution's proof, and Boykin requires an actually informed and voluntary choice. A defendant who surrenders trial while the prosecution team holds information giving a substantial evidentiary basis to negate an element, to show that the accused did not commit the charged conduct, or to establish a complete defense the law makes available does not merely choose under uncertainty about proof; the choice is made without the information that bears most directly on whether there is any offense to admit. Bousley's insistence on knowledge of the real offense points the same way. Brady v. Maryland and Kyles place responsibility for favorable information on the prosecution team as a whole, so the floor reaches what the team knows, not only what the trial prosecutor holds.

The floor is confined. The information must be materially significant to an objectively reasonable plea decision; minor, speculative or cumulative items and ordinary credibility matters do not qualify. Its significance turns on whether the offense or the defense can be established, not on a prediction of how a jury would weigh credibility. A label does not control: credibility evidence that itself supplies a substantial basis to negate an element, as when it shows that the only witness to an element could not have observed it, falls within the floor, while ordinary impeachment does not. The duty concerns information already known; it imposes no obligation to complete trial preparation, to investigate or to generate evidence, and it is not open-file discovery. The agreement's continuing promise to disclose information establishing factual innocence is narrower than the floor: it does not reach information that supplies a substantial basis to negate an element or that supports a complete affirmative defense despite admitted conduct. The Court decides no disclosure duty broader than this floor.

### IV. Protective measures are available, but a plea agreement may not demand an irrevocable waiver of the floor

**Holding and operative rule:** Legitimate confidentiality, witness-safety and early-resolution interests may be accommodated through redaction, delayed disclosure that still precedes the binding plea, and adequate summaries, with judicial supervision when needed. The Government may decline to negotiate an immediate plea when it cannot make the required disclosure, but it may not demand, as a term of a plea agreement, an irrevocable waiver of disclosure within the floor.

**Authority:** Part IV of the Opinion of the Court has the same five direct joins. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas dissent.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the floor's limits answer the practical objections to pre-plea disclosure without making concealment of floor information a bargaining term. Early resolution and witness confidentiality are legitimate interests, and the record supplies no finding of danger to any witness here. Those interests are met by measures fitted to them: redaction of identifying details, sequencing that delays disclosure until shortly before the plea becomes binding, and summaries adequate to convey the substance of the information, under judicial supervision where a genuine privilege or safety concern is shown. When no such measure suffices, the Government may decline to offer an immediate plea and proceed on the ordinary trial schedule, where the trial-disclosure rules apply. What it may not do is obtain, through the agreement itself, an irrevocable surrender of information within the floor. A defendant cannot knowingly evaluate a waiver of information whose existence and content are withheld at the moment of choice, and the floor exists to secure the informed character of that choice. Nothing in this Part requires an offer or limits the terms the Government may otherwise propose.

### V. An otherwise available bargaining benefit cannot be conditioned on surrender of the floor

**Holding and operative rule:** The Government may not condition an otherwise available plea-bargaining benefit, including a sentencing recommendation, on the defendant's surrender of the disclosure floor. The restriction is confined: there is no right to an offer, the Government keeps its discretion to choose terms, to bargain firmly and to withdraw an offer for lawful reasons, and other knowing and voluntary waivers remain available. After disclosure, the defendant may still plead.

**Authority:** Part V of the Opinion of the Court has the same five direct joins. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas dissent.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a benefit the Government would otherwise extend may not be withheld because the defendant refuses to surrender the floor. Lack of an entitlement to the benefit does not answer the claim: an unconstitutional condition can cause injury although the recommendation itself is discretionary. Wade v. United States holds that prosecutorial discretion over sentencing-related motions remains subject to constitutional limits even though no defendant is entitled to a motion; a recommendation withheld as the price of refusing a constitutional protection falls within that principle. Bordenkircher v. Hayes remains: the Government may present hard choices in the give-and-take of bargaining and may withdraw or refuse offers for lawful reasons. Mezzanatto remains: a defendant may knowingly and voluntarily agree to a defined use of the defendant's own statements. Surrender of the floor differs from those choices because it is made without knowledge of the information surrendered and defeats the informed character of the very plea it accompanies. This is a constitutional limit on plea terms confined to the floor, not a general bar on waivers demanded at the outset of bargaining, and it leaves every other lawful term available. The Government may offer the same benefit on terms that preserve the floor, and once disclosure is made the defendant may plead on any lawful terms.

### VI. The withdrawn recommendation was conditioned on surrender of the floor; the vacatur is affirmed for a limited remand

**Holding and operative rule:** The proposed agreement's affirmative-defense waiver demanded surrender of the floor, as did its impeachment waiver to the extent it would reach information within the floor; the continuing factual-innocence promise did not cover the floor's element and complete-defense categories. Because the fast-track recommendation was withdrawn after Ruiz refused the waiver, her constitutional allegation is connected to the sentence, and section 3742(a)(1) supports review; the Ninth Circuit's sentence vacatur is affirmed on these grounds. On remand the district court determines whether Ruiz's rejection of the impermissible component materially contributed to the withholding of the recommendation, and whether the Government would have withheld it for independent lawful reasons; refusal of the impermissible component is not such a reason. The decision orders no automatic departure, enforcement of the unaccepted offer, plea withdrawal, innocence finding or prescribed sentence; Ruiz's conviction stands.

**Authority:** Part VI of the Opinion of the Court has the same five direct joins. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would reverse the sentence vacatur.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Ruiz's claim does not depend on proof that the Government held floor information. Her claim is that a recommendation otherwise available to her was conditioned on a waiver that reached the floor and was withdrawn when she refused it; the absence of a particular withheld item limits the remedy but not the validity of the condition. A sentence imposed after the Government withheld a recommendation as the price of refusing the floor, if that refusal materially contributed, rests on an unconstitutional condition. The judge's correct understanding of departure discretion does not cure that defect, because the defect lies in the Government's withholding of the recommendation that would have informed the exercise of that discretion. Section 3742(a)(1) therefore reaches the claim, and the Ninth Circuit's vacatur stands on these grounds rather than on its broader disclosure rule.

Wade v. United States's requirement of a substantial threshold showing before an evidentiary hearing about a prosecutor's refusal applies. The admitted record supplies that showing: the offer contained the impermissible component on its face, Ruiz refused the waiver and the offer was withdrawn. Generalized allegations would not suffice, and the inquiry extends only to the two remand questions. Because the rejected waiver also reached impeachment information outside the floor, which this decision does not require to be disclosed, the district court must determine whether rejection of the impermissible component materially contributed to the withholding, and whether the Government would have withheld the recommendation for independent lawful reasons. If that refusal materially contributed and the Government would not have withheld the recommendation for independent lawful reasons, the court resentences under its existing departure authority without treating refusal of the impermissible component as a lawful reason to deny the recommendation's consideration. The unaccepted offer is not enforced, no reduction is guaranteed, and Ruiz's later plea without an agreement neither assented to the rejected waiver nor becomes subject to withdrawal. No innocence prerequisite or second harmlessness hurdle is added to a completed constitutional violation.

## Precedent Treatment

- United States v. Mine Workers, 330 U.S. 258: applied for authority to determine the statutory jurisdictional basis, not unrestricted appellate review of a discretionary sentence.
- Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment, 523 U.S. 83: its requirement of an actual jurisdictional basis remains; the constitutional issue here determines that claimed basis rather than supplying advisory merits review.
- Brady v. Maryland, 373 U.S. 83: its favorable-evidence protection for fair trial adjudication is preserved; a distinct pre-plea floor is recognized for information that negates an element, shows the accused did not commit the charged conduct or establishes a complete affirmative defense.
- United States v. Bagley, 473 U.S. 667: its trial protection for impeachment information remains; it establishes no universal duty of advance impeachment disclosure before a plea.
- Kyles v. Whitley, April 19, 1995: prosecution-team responsibility is applied to the pre-plea floor, which reaches information the team already knows; its trial-disclosure and collective-materiality rules retain their force.
- Strickler v. Greene, June 17, 1999: its actual suppression, reliance and retrospective materiality inquiries remain distinct from the prospective floor and from relief from a completed plea.
- Brady v. United States, 397 U.S. 742: preserved; a knowing, voluntary plea may accept uncertainty about the prosecution's proof, but not the withholding of known information within the floor.
- Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238: applied; an actually informed and voluntary constitutional choice is required.
- Bordenkircher v. Hayes, 434 U.S. 357: retained; hard bargaining and the withdrawal of offers for lawful reasons remain permissible, short of conditioning a benefit on surrender of the floor.
- Godinez v. Moran, June 24, 1993: competence remains distinct from an actually informed and voluntary choice.
- Bousley v. United States, May 18, 1998: knowledge of the real offense remains necessary to the constitutional plea choice and supports the floor's element and conduct categories.
- United States v. Mezzanatto, January 18, 1995: its limited knowing and voluntary impeachment-use waiver remains operative; it establishes neither assent to Ruiz's rejected term nor a power to demand surrender of the floor.
- Wade v. United States, May 18, 1992: its constitutional limits on prosecutorial sentencing discretion are extended to a recommendation withheld for refusal of an unconstitutional plea condition, and its substantial-threshold requirement for an evidentiary hearing is applied and met on the admitted record.
- United States v. Vonn, March 4, 2002: its mandatory plea-warning duty and relevant-whole-record review retain their scope; no Rule 11 forfeiture is inferred in this different constitutional-condition appeal.

## Law After Decision

The Constitution imposes no universal duty of advance impeachment disclosure before a plea. Before securing a binding guilty plea or an irrevocable waiver through a plea agreement, the Government must disclose information known to the prosecution team that gives a substantial evidentiary basis to negate an element, to show that the accused did not commit the charged conduct, or to establish a legally available complete affirmative defense, when materially significant to an objectively reasonable plea decision; minor, speculative, cumulative and ordinary credibility matters do not qualify, labels do not control, and no duty to investigate or create evidence arises. Redaction, delayed disclosure before the plea becomes binding and adequate summaries are available; the Government may decline to negotiate but may not demand an irrevocable waiver of the floor or condition an otherwise available bargaining benefit on its surrender. Section 3742 permits examination of a connected constitutional allegation; a recommendation withheld after refusal of such a condition supports review and a remand limited to material contribution and independent lawful reasons, with Wade's threshold applying. Actual competence, knowledge of the offense, voluntary choice, counsel duties and distinct deception or suppression claims retain their own requirements. No right to an offer, universal waiver rule or independent sentencing change results.

## Separate Writings

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, concurring, joined by Justice Stevens.** He joins the Court's opinion, which states the minimum the Constitution secures against waiver, and would go further. He would hold that, before obtaining a binding plea, the prosecution must make timely disclosure of favorable information known to its case team after reasonable inquiry that can materially assist investigation, counsel's advice or the plea decision, including materially significant impeachment and affirmative-defense evidence, and a blanket prospective waiver cannot excuse that duty. A witness's known inability to support the accusation can matter before trial precisely because it changes whether counsel should recommend surrendering trial; calling it impeachment does not make its significance disappear. The obligation is not complete discovery, a guarantee of perfect knowledge or a duty to create evidence or finish trial preparation, and genuine privilege and safety concerns call for protective arrangements, sequencing and judicial review compatible with meaningful decision-making.

Prospective disclosure is measured by practical usefulness, while relief from a completed plea or sentence requires its own causal and materiality showing. Mezzanatto remains intact because the defendant there knowingly accepted a defined use of his own inconsistent account; a blanket surrender of unknown information needed to assess the very plea demanded is different. He rejects neither all limited informed waivers nor bargaining pressure categorically. On remand the sentencing court must address the lost fast-track recommendation without treating refusal of the invalid waiver as a lawful reason to deny consideration; no automatic two-level reduction, compelled agreement, plea withdrawal or damages follows. He finds no particular suppressed item or involuntary plea and would leave the conviction undisturbed.

**Justice O'Connor, dissenting, joined by Justice Scalia, Justice Kennedy and Justice Thomas.** O'Connor, Scalia and Kennedy join Parts I and II, and Thomas agrees with those propositions in his separate dissent; all four would reverse the sentence vacatur. A constitutionally valid plea requires a competent defendant's actual understanding of the right relinquished, the charge and the relevant consequences, and a voluntary choice; Brady v. United States permits that choice despite uncertainty about the prosecution's proof. The disclosure guarantee of Brady v. Maryland protects the fairness of trial. Neither guarantee supplies a pre-plea floor defined by whether information gives a substantial evidentiary basis concerning an element, the conduct or a defense, a measure of evidentiary weight the plea guarantee has not required. The presented agreement preserved a continuing promise to disclose information establishing factual innocence, and concealment of such information, affirmative misrepresentation about a known defense, defective advice and an actually uninformed plea remain remediable on their own requirements; the dissent does not immunize them. Mezzanatto confirms that a knowing, voluntary and limited waiver may be part of a bargain. Because the affirmative-defense term was valid in this agreement's context, withdrawing the recommendation after Ruiz refused it imposed no unconstitutional condition; that conclusion rests on the term's validity, not on Ruiz's lack of an entitlement to the recommendation. Vonn's distinction between an obligation and its actual effect also counsels against a remand on a record that identifies no withheld item before a judge who understood departure discretion. They would leave the sentence and conviction undisturbed and would not direct a remand that risks placing a value on an offer Ruiz declined.

**Justice Thomas, dissenting.** He joins Justice O'Connor's dissent. He agrees that the asserted statutory basis may be determined and that no universal duty of advance impeachment disclosure exists, without joining the Court's opinion, whose Part II relies in part on predicted degree of assistance and practical balancing. Brady protects against an unfair trial; that guarantee does not itself create a bargaining-stage disclosure duty, regardless of how useful the information might be to a defendant. The Court's floor still defines the duty by the predicted significance of information to a plea decision, but usefulness supplies neither the source nor the boundary of this fair-trial guarantee. His objection is not that such information is unimportant. He also rejects any enforceable promise from the unaccepted offer. His position does not endorse every waiver.

## Procedure After Action

The Ninth Circuit's sentence vacatur is affirmed, and the case returns through the Ninth Circuit to the district court for the remand inquiry: whether Ruiz's rejection of the impermissible component materially contributed to the withholding of the fast-track recommendation, and whether the Government would have withheld it for independent lawful reasons. Any resentencing proceeds under the court's existing authority, without an automatic departure, enforcement of the unaccepted offer or a prescribed sentence. Ruiz's conviction remains undisturbed.
