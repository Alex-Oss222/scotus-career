## Controlling holdings

### I. Section 1983 (Part I; five votes: O'Connor, Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas)

**Controlling proposition:** The EPA regulations do not create an individual statutory effects right absent from Title VI. Section 601's intentional-discrimination protection and §602's agency-directed implementing authority do not confer the individual effects right asserted here. A regulation may elaborate an individual right that Congress has conferred, but it cannot create a missing one; EPA's disparate-impact provisions, 40 C.F.R. §7.35(b) and (c), therefore supply no right for §1983 to enforce. The rejection rests on the absence of a right, not on congressional foreclosure of an established one, and it does not foreclose §1983 enforcement when a different statute actually confers the individual entitlement a regulation implements. The availability of Powell's bounded direct prospective action does not convert the regulation into the individual right §1983 enforces. The rejection of the §1983 effects basis for the injunction is affirmed.

**Authority:** Part I of O'Connor's opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; with the author, five Justices support it. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer dissent from Part I. The proposition has direct majority support; no aggregation of distinct rationales is required.

**Controlling explanation:** Powell decided this question in a holding separate from its direct-action holding. Section 601 confers a personal right against intentional discrimination, and §602 authorizes federal agencies to effectuate that guarantee through regulations; neither provision confers an individual right against effects that §601 itself does not prohibit. Sandoval applied Powell's direct-action holding and left this holding undisturbed. Blessing requires a particular congressional entitlement, not simply a beneficial program or a binding agency obligation. Wright and Wilder remain governing at their scope, but each rested on a statute that itself conferred the entitlement a regulation elaborated. Elaboration gives content to a right the statute confers; it cannot create a broader one, and treating every valid implementing regulation as a new individual entitlement would remove Congress's role in defining the right. The source of a direct statutory action and the source of a right under a different remedial statute are distinct questions, so the availability of Powell's prospective action does not answer the §1983 question. The residents identify no new statutory text, contrary intervening decision or concrete reliance that would justify reopening so recent a statutory precedent.

**Precedent treatment:** Powell v. Ridge, April 3, 2000, is applied and reaffirmed at its separate §1983 holding, which Alexander v. Sandoval, April 24, 2001, left undisturbed. Blessing v. Freestone, 520 U.S. 329 (1997), Wright v. Roanoke Redevelopment and Housing Authority, 479 U.S. 418 (1987), and Wilder v. Virginia Hospital Association, 496 U.S. 498 (1990), retain the distinction between identifying and elaborating a statutory right.

### II. Direct action (Part II; all nine)

**Controlling proposition:** Plaintiffs concretely affected by a covered recipient's methods may bring the Powell prospective action to enforce valid EPA §602 disparate-impact regulations, including 40 C.F.R. §7.35(b) and (c), if the actual regulations apply and the violation is proved. The plaintiffs must establish (1) an applicable §602 regulation validly issued under the authority Congress conferred; (2) a defendant subject to it through the required federal-assistance relationship; (3) a concrete exclusion, denial of benefits or other material discriminatory effect within the regulation's actual terms, attributable to an identified recipient policy or method of administration; (4) an actual or imminent continuing effect that the requested prospective relief can meaningfully correct; and (5) an appropriate defendant and a remedy compatible with applicable immunity, statutory and equitable limits. Section 7.35(b) reaches criteria or methods of administering the covered program that have the prohibited effect, including its alternative concerning defeat or substantial impairment of the program's objectives for protected persons; §7.35(c) governs site or location selection under its own purpose-or-effect terms and must be pleaded and proved on those terms. Disproportionate statistics may support proof, but a numerical racial difference alone does not conclusively establish a violation; the residents must connect any disparity to the challenged permitting or administrative method and to the regulation's operative prohibition. The recipient may contest comparison, causation, materiality and regulatory coverage, and may invoke any qualification or defense the valid regulation supplies. No prior agency finding is required. Relief is declaratory and prospective equitable relief matched to an established violation. Regulatory validity and coverage are decided on remand insofar as genuinely contested. The appellate rejection of this directly implied Title VI prospective claim is vacated, and the claim returns for the remaining merits and remedy questions. No judicial order equalizing environmental conditions issues, and unequal exposure is not presumed to violate Title VI.

**Authority:** Part II is joined by all nine Justices. Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer formed the majorities for Powell's direct-action holding and Sandoval's Court opinion; O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas apply that holding as controlling statutory precedent, as in Sandoval. The proposition has direct majority support; no aggregation of distinct rationales is required.

**Controlling explanation:** Powell holds that a person suffering a concrete injury within a federally assisted program may bring a private action against a covered recipient's ongoing violation of a valid, duly issued §602 regulation prohibiting discriminatory effects, for declaratory and appropriately confined prospective equitable relief. The action exists within Title VI's particular statutory context and rests on that holding, not on a regulation's own force. Sandoval applied the holding to a different agency's regulation and a different state program and held that neither distinguished Powell; the action reaches every valid Title VI effects regulation within its bounded prospective scope. EPA's §7.35(b) and (c) are §602 effects regulations of that kind, and a state permitting program receiving EPA assistance is within the action if coverage is established. The appellate disposition rests on the premise that no private action enforces §602 effects regulations. That premise is contrary to Powell and Sandoval, and the residents' direct theory, pleaded on February 13, 2001 and not withdrawn by the amendment, therefore returns for adjudication. Statutory precedent carries special force because Congress can alter a construction it disagrees with; Congress has not amended Title VI, no intervening decision undermines Powell or Sandoval, and the Court applies them at their actual scope. The claim requires what Powell requires, and relief is confined to declaratory and prospective equitable relief; §602's notice, voluntary-compliance, hearing, congressional-reporting and program-specific termination procedures continue to govern the agency's own powers. The burden on the Waterfront South neighborhood is evidence; it must be tied to the challenged permitting method and to the regulation's terms, and it does not itself establish a violation.

**Precedent treatment:** Powell v. Ridge's direct-action holding is applied to EPA's §602 regulations, 40 C.F.R. §7.35(b) and (c), and to a state permitting program at its bounded prospective scope, without extension beyond that scope. Alexander v. Sandoval, April 24, 2001, is applied: Powell's action reaches every valid Title VI effects regulation within its bounded prospective scope and is not a program-specific exception.

### III. Section 601, jurisdiction, proper officers and interim relief (Part III; all nine)

A proper connected state officer may be subject to prospective federal relief on an established violation; neither NJDEP's amenability nor a particular permit violation is presumed. The appellate judgment is vacated insofar as it forecloses further proceedings on the §601 claim, and that claim is preserved and remanded. Existing findings receive ordinary review; the remand directs no new hearing.

**Authority:** Part III, including the vacatur and remand of the §601 claim, is joined by all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Powell and Sandoval each preserve §601's private action for intentional discrimination, distinct from the bounded effects action and from §1983. ## Holdings

### I. Section 1983 (Part I; five votes: O'Connor, Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas)

**Controlling proposition:** The EPA regulations do not create an individual statutory effects right absent from Title VI. Section 601's intentional-discrimination protection and §602's agency-directed implementing authority do not confer the individual effects right asserted here. A regulation may elaborate an individual right that Congress has conferred, but it cannot create a missing one; EPA's disparate-impact provisions, 40 C.F.R. §7.35(b) and (c), therefore supply no right for §1983 to enforce. The rejection rests on the absence of a right, not on congressional foreclosure of an established one, and it does not foreclose §1983 enforcement when a different statute actually confers the individual entitlement a regulation implements. The availability of Powell's bounded direct prospective action does not convert the regulation into the individual right §1983 enforces. The rejection of the §1983 effects basis for the injunction is affirmed.

**Authority:** Part I of O'Connor's opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; with the author, five Justices support it. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer dissent from Part I. The proposition has direct majority support; no aggregation of distinct rationales is required.

**Controlling explanation:** Powell decided this question in a holding separate from its direct-action holding. Section 601 confers a personal right against intentional discrimination, and §602 authorizes federal agencies to effectuate that guarantee through regulations; neither provision confers an individual right against effects that §601 itself does not prohibit. Sandoval applied Powell's direct-action holding and left this holding undisturbed. Blessing requires a particular congressional entitlement, not simply a beneficial program or a binding agency obligation. Wright and Wilder remain governing at their scope, but each rested on a statute that itself conferred the entitlement a regulation elaborated. Elaboration gives content to a right the statute confers; it cannot create a broader one, and treating every valid implementing regulation as a new individual entitlement would remove Congress's role in defining the right. The source of a direct statutory action and the source of a right under a different remedial statute are distinct questions, so the availability of Powell's prospective action does not answer the §1983 question. The residents identify no new statutory text, contrary intervening decision or concrete reliance that would justify reopening so recent a statutory precedent.

**Precedent treatment:** Powell v. Ridge, April 3, 2000, is applied and reaffirmed at its separate §1983 holding, which Alexander v. Sandoval, April 24, 2001, left undisturbed. Blessing v. Freestone, 520 U.S. 329 (1997), Wright v. Roanoke Redevelopment and Housing Authority, 479 U.S. 418 (1987), and Wilder v. Virginia Hospital Association, 496 U.S. 498 (1990), retain the distinction between identifying and elaborating a statutory right.

### II. Direct action (Part II; all nine)

**Controlling proposition:** Plaintiffs concretely affected by a covered recipient's methods may bring the Powell prospective action to enforce valid EPA §602 disparate-impact regulations, including 40 C.F.R. §7.35(b) and (c), if the actual regulations apply and the violation is proved. The plaintiffs must establish (1) an applicable §602 regulation validly issued under the authority Congress conferred; (2) a defendant subject to it through the required federal-assistance relationship; (3) a concrete exclusion, denial of benefits or other material discriminatory effect within the regulation's actual terms, attributable to an identified recipient policy or method of administration; (4) an actual or imminent continuing effect that the requested prospective relief can meaningfully correct; and (5) an appropriate defendant and a remedy compatible with applicable immunity, statutory and equitable limits. Section 7.35(b) reaches criteria or methods of administering the covered program that have the prohibited effect, including its alternative concerning defeat or substantial impairment of the program's objectives for protected persons; §7.35(c) governs site or location selection under its own purpose-or-effect terms and must be pleaded and proved on those terms. Disproportionate statistics may support proof, but a numerical racial difference alone does not conclusively establish a violation; the residents must connect any disparity to the challenged permitting or administrative method and to the regulation's operative prohibition. The recipient may contest comparison, causation, materiality and regulatory coverage, and may invoke any qualification or defense the valid regulation supplies. No prior agency finding is required. Relief is declaratory and prospective equitable relief matched to an established violation. Regulatory validity and coverage are decided on remand insofar as genuinely contested. The appellate rejection of this directly implied Title VI prospective claim is vacated, and the claim returns for the remaining merits and remedy questions. No judicial order equalizing environmental conditions issues, and unequal exposure is not presumed to violate Title VI.

**Authority:** Part II is joined by all nine Justices. Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer formed the majorities for Powell's direct-action holding and Sandoval's Court opinion; O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas apply that holding as controlling statutory precedent, as in Sandoval. The proposition has direct majority support; no aggregation of distinct rationales is required.

**Controlling explanation:** Powell holds that a person suffering a concrete injury within a federally assisted program may bring a private action against a covered recipient's ongoing violation of a valid, duly issued §602 regulation prohibiting discriminatory effects, for declaratory and appropriately confined prospective equitable relief. The action exists within Title VI's particular statutory context and rests on that holding, not on a regulation's own force. Sandoval applied the holding to a different agency's regulation and a different state program and held that neither distinguished Powell; the action reaches every valid Title VI effects regulation within its bounded prospective scope. EPA's §7.35(b) and (c) are §602 effects regulations of that kind, and a state permitting program receiving EPA assistance is within the action if coverage is established. The appellate disposition rests on the premise that no private action enforces §602 effects regulations. That premise is contrary to Powell and Sandoval, and the residents' direct theory, pleaded on February 13, 2001 and not withdrawn by the amendment, therefore returns for adjudication. Statutory precedent carries special force because Congress can alter a construction it disagrees with; Congress has not amended Title VI, no intervening decision undermines Powell or Sandoval, and the Court applies them at their actual scope. The claim requires what Powell requires, and relief is confined to declaratory and prospective equitable relief; §602's notice, voluntary-compliance, hearing, congressional-reporting and program-specific termination procedures continue to govern the agency's own powers. The burden on the Waterfront South neighborhood is evidence; it must be tied to the challenged permitting method and to the regulation's terms, and it does not itself establish a violation.

**Precedent treatment:** Powell v. Ridge's direct-action holding is applied to EPA's §602 regulations, 40 C.F.R. §7.35(b) and (c), and to a state permitting program at its bounded prospective scope, without extension beyond that scope. Alexander v. Sandoval, April 24, 2001, is applied: Powell's action reaches every valid Title VI effects regulation within its bounded prospective scope and is not a program-specific exception.

### III. Section 601, jurisdiction, proper officers and interim relief (Part III; all nine)

**Controlling proposition:** The preserved §601 intentional-discrimination claim remains independently cognizable and proceeds on its own proof and procedural footing. Disparate effects may be evidence relevant to purpose, but they do not alone establish intentional discrimination. A substantial federal question supports jurisdiction independently of the merits. A proper connected state officer may be subject to prospective federal relief on an established violation; neither NJDEP's amenability nor a particular permit violation is presumed. The appellate judgment is vacated insofar as it forecloses further proceedings on the §601 claim, and that claim is preserved and remanded. The suspended preliminary injunction does not automatically revive; any renewed interim relief must rest on an available claim and the requisite findings, justified on the existing record and lawful current circumstances. Existing findings receive ordinary review; the remand directs no new hearing.

**Authority:** Part III, including the vacatur and remand of the §601 claim, is joined by all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Powell and Sandoval each preserve §601's private action for intentional discrimination, distinct from the bounded effects action and from §1983. The February 13, 2001 complaint pleaded that claim against NJDEP and Commissioner Shinn, and the amendment added §1983 without withdrawing it; the district court itself identified the intentional-discrimination charge as a remaining claim when it called for supplemental briefing. Insofar as the appellate disposition forecloses the §601 claim, it reaches a claim it did not decide. Verizon Maryland separates substantial federal jurisdiction, defendant immunity and ultimate entitlement, so the district court may hear the claim without any assumption that it will succeed. Ex parte Young permits prospective relief only against an officer connected to a continuing violation, and only after that violation is established; naming Commissioner Shinn does not establish that connection, and whether NJDEP itself may be sued is for the district court on the record. The jurisdiction and officer rules apply equally to the direct claim. The suspended injunction rested on the §1983 effects theory and does not automatically revive.

**Precedent treatment:** Verizon Maryland Inc. v. Public Service Commission of Maryland, May 20, 2002, and Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908), are applied to jurisdiction and prospective connected-officer relief for both remanded claims; neither decides either claim's merits or NJDEP's amenability. Powell and Sandoval are applied at their stated preservation of §601's intentional-discrimination action.

## Precedent Treatment

Powell's two holdings remain controlling and are applied: its bounded direct prospective action reaches EPA's §602 regulations, 40 C.F.R. §7.35(b) and (c), and a state permitting program at its bounded prospective scope, without extension beyond that scope, and its separate §1983 holding is reaffirmed. Alexander v. Sandoval is applied: Powell's action reaches every valid Title VI effects regulation within its bounded prospective scope and is not a program-specific exception. Section 601's private action for intentional discrimination, as Powell and Sandoval preserve it, is preserved; disparate effects are evidence relevant to purpose but do not alone establish it. Blessing's requirement of a particular congressional entitlement is applied, and Wright and Wilder are retained at their scope as regulatory elaboration of rights the statutes themselves confer. Verizon Maryland and Ex parte Young preserve the separation among jurisdiction, defendant, merits and remedy; prospective relief runs only against an officer connected to a continuing violation, after the violation is established. No precedent is overruled.

## Law After Decision

EPA's §602 disparate-impact regulations create no individual statutory right for §1983 to enforce. Powell's bounded direct prospective action enforces valid EPA §602 effects regulations, including 40 C.F.R. §7.35(b) and (c), against a covered recipient's ongoing violation on proof of Powell's elements; disproportionate statistics may support but do not alone establish a violation, and relief is declaratory and prospective equitable relief fitted to an established violation. Section 601's private action for intentional discrimination remains independently available on its own proof; disparate effects may be evidence of purpose but do not alone establish it. Prospective relief against a state officer requires an established federal violation and the officer's actual connection to it; a state agency's own amenability is a separate question. No judicial order equalizes environmental conditions, and unequal exposure is not presumed to violate Title VI.

## Separate Writings

Justice Stevens, joined by Justices Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, concurs in Parts II and III and dissents from Part I. He would vacate the whole judgment and return the §1983 theory along with the direct and intentional claims. On §1983 he adheres to his Powell opinion and would reconsider Powell's holding to the extent it rejects this identified entitlement: the statutory beneficiaries, nondiscrimination protection, express implementation authority and a valid, sufficiently definite implementing requirement can, in his view, define the individual protection that §1983 enforces, and Blessing's particular-right inquiry and Wright's and Wilder's recognition of authorized elaboration support that conclusion. The availability of Powell's direct action, which he joins the Court in applying, does not in his view answer whether the regulation-elaborated protection is a right §1983 enforces; either channel would independently suffice. He acknowledges that Powell's §1983 holding is current law. He does not treat every regulation or administrative command as a right, establish a general entitlement to equal environmental burdens, order a permit outcome or restore the suspended injunction. His disagreement with Part I leaves his joins in Parts II and III intact.

## Procedure After Action

The Third Circuit's judgment is affirmed insofar as it rejected the §1983 effects basis for the injunction and vacated insofar as it rejected the directly implied Title VI prospective claim and forecloses further proceedings on the preserved §601 intentional-discrimination claim. The direct claim returns for the remaining merits and remedy questions under Part II, and the §601 claim for adjudication on its own proof of discriminatory purpose and procedural footing, both with ordinary proper-defendant questions under Part III. The injunction remains suspended unless renewed interim relief is justified on the existing record and lawful current circumstances. An independent permit-law claim not preserved in this appeal is not decided.
