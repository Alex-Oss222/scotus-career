## Controlling holdings

### I. The asserted regulation-created effects right is not enforceable under §1983

**Controlling proposition:** Section 601's intentional-discrimination protection and §602's agency-directed implementing authority do not confer the additional individual effects right asserted here. A regulation may elaborate an individual right that Congress has conferred, but it cannot create a missing one; EPA's disparate-impact provisions, 40 C.F.R. §7.35(b) and (c), therefore supply no right for §1983 to enforce. The rejection rests on the absence of a right, not on congressional foreclosure of an established one.

**Authority:** Part I of O'Connor's opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; with the author, five Justices support it. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer dissent from Part I. The proposition has direct majority support; no aggregation of distinct rationales is required.

**Controlling explanation:** Powell decided this question. Section 601 confers a personal right against intentional discrimination, and §602 authorizes federal agencies to effectuate that guarantee through regulations and administrative enforcement; neither provision confers an individual right against effects that §601 itself does not prohibit. Sandoval followed Powell's companion direct-action holding and left this holding undisturbed. Blessing requires a particular congressional entitlement, not simply a beneficial program or a binding agency obligation. Wright and Wilder remain governing at their scope, but each rested on a statute that itself conferred the entitlement a regulation elaborated; they do not supply the missing broader effects right. Treating every valid implementing regulation as a new individual entitlement would remove Congress's role in defining the right. The residents identify no new statutory text, contrary intervening decision or concrete reliance that would justify reopening so recent a statutory precedent, and the seriousness of the environmental burden does not supply a right Congress withheld. This conclusion does not foreclose an independently conferred statutory right or the original intentional-discrimination claim.

**Precedent treatment:** Powell v. Ridge, April 3, 2000, is applied and reaffirmed at its §1983 holding, which Alexander v. Sandoval, April 24, 2001, left undisturbed. Blessing v. Freestone, 520 U.S. 329 (1997), Wright v. Roanoke Redevelopment and Housing Authority, 479 U.S. 418 (1987), and Wilder v. Virginia Hospital Association, 496 U.S. 498 (1990), retain the distinction between identifying and elaborating a statutory right.

### II. No directly implied Title VI action enforces the effects regulations

**Controlling proposition:** Title VI supplies no private action to enforce the additional disparate-impact prohibitions of EPA's §602 regulations where the challenged conduct does not violate §601, whatever the form of relief sought. Assuming without deciding that 40 C.F.R. §7.35(b) and (c) validly implement §602, the residents cannot enforce them in a private suit, and a different pleading label, a request confined to prospective relief or the particular permit at issue does not change that answer. Valid regulations remain enforceable through the means Congress authorized: agency petition and complaint processes and §602's notice, voluntary-compliance, hearing and funding-termination procedures.

**Authority:** Part II is joined by the same five Justices: Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, each a member of the Powell and Sandoval majorities, who adopt this ground on their own view and as governing precedent. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer dissent from Part II on their Powell and Sandoval dissents. The proposition has direct majority support; no aggregation of distinct rationales is required.

**Controlling explanation:** Sandoval, following Powell, holds that Congress has not authorized private enforcement of these additional effects prohibitions and that a regulation cannot supply the missing authorization by its own force. Section 602 commits enforcement of the agency's rules to a distinct public structure: notice to the recipient, an effort to secure voluntary compliance, a hearing and express finding, a report to the appropriate congressional committees, and funding termination confined to the noncompliant program. The residents' direct theory is the claim Powell and Sandoval reject: it would enforce §7.35's effects terms on a showing of effects, without proof that NJDEP violated §601. Neither a request for an injunction, a different state program nor a finding rather than an allegation of disparate effects distinguishes it, as Sandoval held of Powell, and the permit-specific setting does no more. The original direct theory was pleaded and not abandoned; it fails because the action it asserts does not exist, not for any want of preservation. The residents offer no new statutory text, contrary intervening decision or concrete reliance that would warrant reopening two recent statutory precedents. The burden on the Waterfront South neighborhood is serious, and Congress gave the funding agency the means to address a recipient's noncompliance with its regulations; a court may not add the private remedy Congress withheld. The Court decides no regulation's validity, limits no agency enforcement and recognizes no general authority to equalize environmental conditions.

**Precedent treatment:** Alexander v. Sandoval, April 24, 2001, and Powell's direct-action holding are applied to the retained EPA permitting theory at their Title VI scope; neither is overruled in whole or in part, and regulatory validity remains assumed, not decided.

### III. The preserved §601 claim, jurisdiction, proper officers and the limited remand

**Controlling proposition:** A genuinely preserved §601 intentional-discrimination claim remains independently cognizable, apart from the impact-only routes, and proceeds on its own proof and procedural footing. The residents must prove discriminatory purpose; disparate effects may be weighed with other evidence, but intent may not be inferred from unequal environmental conditions alone. A substantial federal question supports jurisdiction independently of the claim's merits. Prospective relief may issue against a proper state officer only upon an established §601 violation and the officer's actual connection to the challenged conduct; NJDEP's own amenability to suit is not assumed. The appellate judgment is vacated only insofar as it forecloses further proceedings on that claim, and the case is remanded solely for that claim and ordinary proper-defendant issues.

**Authority:** Part III, including the limited vacatur and remand, is joined by all nine Justices. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer join it while stating separately that they would vacate the whole judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** Powell and Sandoval each preserve §601's private action for intentional discrimination; they deny only the additional effects routes. The February 13, 2001 complaint pleaded that claim against NJDEP and Commissioner Shinn, and the amendment added §1983 without withdrawing it. The appellate disposition rests on the failure of the §1983 theory; insofar as it forecloses the §601 claim, it reaches a claim it did not decide, and the limited vacatur protects the claim the residents actually preserved. Verizon Maryland separates substantial federal jurisdiction, defendant immunity and ultimate entitlement, so the district court may hear the claim without any assumption that it will succeed. Ex parte Young permits prospective relief only against an officer connected to a continuing violation, and only after that violation is established; Commissioner Shinn is named, but naming him does not establish that connection or the scope of relief, and whether NJDEP itself may be sued is for the district court on the record. The suspended injunction rested on the §1983 effects theory and does not revive; any renewed preliminary relief must rest on the §601 claim's own showing and the ordinary equitable requirements. Existing findings receive ordinary review; remand does not direct a new hearing or wholesale reopening. The permit is neither invalidated nor sustained on its regulatory merits, no order directs EPA to begin enforcement proceedings, and the residents retain the agency petition and complaint processes existing law provides.

**Precedent treatment:** Verizon Maryland Inc. v. Public Service Commission of Maryland, May 20, 2002, and Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908), are applied to jurisdiction and prospective connected-officer relief; neither decides the §601 claim's merits or NJDEP's amenability. Powell and Sandoval are applied at their stated preservation of §601's intentional-discrimination action.

## Precedent treatment and resulting law

Powell's two holdings remain controlling: Title VI supplies no directly implied private action to enforce a §602 regulation's additional disparate-impact prohibition, and a regulation cannot create the broader individual effects right asserted under §1983. Sandoval, which followed Powell without overruling it in whole or in part, governs the direct route whatever the form of relief, with regulatory validity assumed. Blessing, Wright and Wilder retain their particular-right and regulatory-elaboration principles. Verizon Maryland and Ex parte Young preserve the separation among jurisdiction, defendant, merits and remedy. No precedent is overruled.

Neither §1983 nor a directly implied Title VI action enforces EPA's additional disparate-impact requirements on a showing of effects alone; valid §602 regulations remain enforceable through agency petition and complaint processes and §602's administrative procedures. Section 601's private action for intentional discrimination remains independently available on its own proof: discriminatory purpose may not be inferred from unequal environmental conditions alone, and no general right to equalize environmental or cumulative pollution burdens exists. Prospective relief against a state officer requires an established federal violation and the officer's actual connection to it; a state agency's own amenability is a separate question. The four-Justice proposals to reconsider Powell and Sandoval remain noncontrolling.

## Holdings

### I. The asserted regulation-created effects right is not enforceable under §1983

**Controlling proposition:** Section 601's intentional-discrimination protection and §602's agency-directed implementing authority do not confer the additional individual effects right asserted here. A regulation may elaborate an individual right that Congress has conferred, but it cannot create a missing one; EPA's disparate-impact provisions, 40 C.F.R. §7.35(b) and (c), therefore supply no right for §1983 to enforce. The rejection rests on the absence of a right, not on congressional foreclosure of an established one.

**Authority:** Part I of O'Connor's opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; with the author, five Justices support it. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer dissent from Part I. The proposition has direct majority support; no aggregation of distinct rationales is required.

**Controlling explanation:** Powell decided this question. Section 601 confers a personal right against intentional discrimination, and §602 authorizes federal agencies to effectuate that guarantee through regulations and administrative enforcement; neither provision confers an individual right against effects that §601 itself does not prohibit. Sandoval followed Powell's companion direct-action holding and left this holding undisturbed. Blessing requires a particular congressional entitlement, not simply a beneficial program or a binding agency obligation. Wright and Wilder remain governing at their scope, but each rested on a statute that itself conferred the entitlement a regulation elaborated; they do not supply the missing broader effects right. Treating every valid implementing regulation as a new individual entitlement would remove Congress's role in defining the right. The residents identify no new statutory text, contrary intervening decision or concrete reliance that would justify reopening so recent a statutory precedent, and the seriousness of the environmental burden does not supply a right Congress withheld. This conclusion does not foreclose an independently conferred statutory right or the original intentional-discrimination claim.

**Precedent treatment:** Powell v. Ridge, April 3, 2000, is applied and reaffirmed at its §1983 holding, which Alexander v. Sandoval, April 24, 2001, left undisturbed. Blessing v. Freestone, 520 U.S. 329 (1997), Wright v. Roanoke Redevelopment and Housing Authority, 479 U.S. 418 (1987), and Wilder v. Virginia Hospital Association, 496 U.S. 498 (1990), retain the distinction between identifying and elaborating a statutory right.

### II. No directly implied Title VI action enforces the effects regulations

**Controlling proposition:** Title VI supplies no private action to enforce the additional disparate-impact prohibitions of EPA's §602 regulations where the challenged conduct does not violate §601, whatever the form of relief sought. Assuming without deciding that 40 C.F.R. §7.35(b) and (c) validly implement §602, the residents cannot enforce them in a private suit, and a different pleading label, a request confined to prospective relief or the particular permit at issue does not change that answer. Valid regulations remain enforceable through the means Congress authorized: agency petition and complaint processes and §602's notice, voluntary-compliance, hearing and funding-termination procedures.

**Authority:** Part II is joined by the same five Justices: Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, each a member of the Powell and Sandoval majorities, who adopt this ground on their own view and as governing precedent. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer dissent from Part II on their Powell and Sandoval dissents. The proposition has direct majority support; no aggregation of distinct rationales is required.

**Controlling explanation:** Sandoval, following Powell, holds that Congress has not authorized private enforcement of these additional effects prohibitions and that a regulation cannot supply the missing authorization by its own force. Section 602 commits enforcement of the agency's rules to a distinct public structure: notice to the recipient, an effort to secure voluntary compliance, a hearing and express finding, a report to the appropriate congressional committees, and funding termination confined to the noncompliant program. The residents' direct theory is the claim Powell and Sandoval reject: it would enforce §7.35's effects terms on a showing of effects, without proof that NJDEP violated §601. Neither a request for an injunction, a different state program nor a finding rather than an allegation of disparate effects distinguishes it, as Sandoval held of Powell, and the permit-specific setting does no more. The original direct theory was pleaded and not abandoned; it fails because the action it asserts does not exist, not for any want of preservation. The residents offer no new statutory text, contrary intervening decision or concrete reliance that would warrant reopening two recent statutory precedents. The burden on the Waterfront South neighborhood is serious, and Congress gave the funding agency the means to address a recipient's noncompliance with its regulations; a court may not add the private remedy Congress withheld. The Court decides no regulation's validity, limits no agency enforcement and recognizes no general authority to equalize environmental conditions.

**Precedent treatment:** Alexander v. Sandoval, April 24, 2001, and Powell's direct-action holding are applied to the retained EPA permitting theory at their Title VI scope; neither is overruled in whole or in part, and regulatory validity remains assumed, not decided.

### III. The preserved §601 claim, jurisdiction, proper officers and the limited remand

**Controlling proposition:** A genuinely preserved §601 intentional-discrimination claim remains independently cognizable, apart from the impact-only routes, and proceeds on its own proof and procedural footing. The residents must prove discriminatory purpose; disparate effects may be weighed with other evidence, but intent may not be inferred from unequal environmental conditions alone. A substantial federal question supports jurisdiction independently of the claim's merits. Prospective relief may issue against a proper state officer only upon an established §601 violation and the officer's actual connection to the challenged conduct; NJDEP's own amenability to suit is not assumed. The appellate judgment is vacated only insofar as it forecloses further proceedings on that claim, and the case is remanded solely for that claim and ordinary proper-defendant issues.

**Authority:** Part III, including the limited vacatur and remand, is joined by all nine Justices. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer join it while stating separately that they would vacate the whole judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** Powell and Sandoval each preserve §601's private action for intentional discrimination; they deny only the additional effects routes. The February 13, 2001 complaint pleaded that claim against NJDEP and Commissioner Shinn, and the amendment added §1983 without withdrawing it. The appellate disposition rests on the failure of the §1983 theory; insofar as it forecloses the §601 claim, it reaches a claim it did not decide, and the limited vacatur protects the claim the residents actually preserved. Verizon Maryland separates substantial federal jurisdiction, defendant immunity and ultimate entitlement, so the district court may hear the claim without any assumption that it will succeed. Ex parte Young permits prospective relief only against an officer connected to a continuing violation, and only after that violation is established; Commissioner Shinn is named, but naming him does not establish that connection or the scope of relief, and whether NJDEP itself may be sued is for the district court on the record. The suspended injunction rested on the §1983 effects theory and does not revive; any renewed preliminary relief must rest on the §601 claim's own showing and the ordinary equitable requirements. Existing findings receive ordinary review; remand does not direct a new hearing or wholesale reopening. The permit is neither invalidated nor sustained on its regulatory merits, no order directs EPA to begin enforcement proceedings, and the residents retain the agency petition and complaint processes existing law provides.

**Precedent treatment:** Verizon Maryland Inc. v. Public Service Commission of Maryland, May 20, 2002, and Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908), are applied to jurisdiction and prospective connected-officer relief; neither decides the §601 claim's merits or NJDEP's amenability. Powell and Sandoval are applied at their stated preservation of §601's intentional-discrimination action.

## Precedent Treatment

Powell's two holdings remain controlling: Title VI supplies no directly implied private action to enforce a §602 regulation's additional disparate-impact prohibition, and a regulation cannot create the broader individual effects right asserted under §1983. Sandoval, which followed Powell without overruling it in whole or in part, governs the direct route whatever the form of relief, with regulatory validity assumed. Blessing, Wright and Wilder retain their particular-right and regulatory-elaboration principles. Verizon Maryland and Ex parte Young preserve the separation among jurisdiction, defendant, merits and remedy. No precedent is overruled.

## Law After Decision

Neither §1983 nor a directly implied Title VI action enforces EPA's additional disparate-impact requirements on a showing of effects alone; valid §602 regulations remain enforceable through agency petition and complaint processes and §602's administrative procedures. Section 601's private action for intentional discrimination remains independently available on its own proof: discriminatory purpose may not be inferred from unequal environmental conditions alone, and no general right to equalize environmental or cumulative pollution burdens exists. Prospective relief against a state officer requires an established federal violation and the officer's actual connection to it; a state agency's own amenability is a separate question. The four-Justice proposals to reconsider Powell and Sandoval remain noncontrolling.

## Separate Writings

Justice Stevens, joined by Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, concurs in Part III and the limited remand and dissents from Parts I and II. He would vacate the whole judgment and return the §1983 and direct theories along with the intentional claim. On §1983 he adheres to the Powell dissent and would reconsider Powell's holding to the extent it rejects this identified entitlement: the statutory beneficiaries, nondiscrimination protection, express implementation authority and a valid, sufficiently definite implementing requirement can, in his view, define the individual protection that §1983 enforces, and Blessing's particular-right inquiry and Wright's and Wilder's recognition of authorized elaboration support that conclusion. On the direct route he adheres to the Sandoval dissent: §601's beneficiaries and §602's express implementation direction support a bounded prospective action to enforce valid, sufficiently definite §602 requirements against a proper covered defendant, and he would reconsider Powell's direct-action holding and Sandoval to that extent. Either channel would, in his view, independently suffice. He acknowledges that Powell and Sandoval are current law and does not treat their recency or continuing disagreement alone as a sufficient reason to overrule them. The action he would allow requires the specific EPA requirement, coverage, prohibited conduct, causal injury and fitted relief, and §7.35(c)'s site-selection provision would require its own pleading and proof. He does not treat every regulation or administrative command as a right, establish a general entitlement to equal environmental burdens, order a permit outcome or restore the suspended injunction. His disagreement with Parts I and II leaves his join in Part III's intent, jurisdiction and proper-officer requirements intact.
