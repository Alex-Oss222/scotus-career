## Controlling holdings

### I. Probable cause alone does not authorize entry into a home

**Holding and operative rule:** Police may not make a nonconsensual entry into a home to arrest or search on probable cause alone: an appropriate warrant or a valid exception must independently authorize entry. When officers rely on exigency, both probable cause and circumstances sufficient to justify immediate entry without a warrant must exist; an arrest and a search incident to it cannot supply the missing authority to cross the threshold.

**Authority:** Part I of the per curiam Opinion of the Court, joined by all nine Justices. Stone-Zsela's disagreement about the formal disposition does not qualify his join in this rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a basis for believing a crime occurred is different from permission to enter a home. Payton protects that threshold even when probable cause would authorize an arrest in public. The Louisiana court treated the drugs' discovery on Kirk's person as sufficient because the officers had probable cause to arrest and could search incident to arrest. That reasoning omits the antecedent question whether they could lawfully enter to make the arrest.

The officers' concern that the departing buyer's arrest would lead to destruction of evidence is an asserted justification requiring assessment on the actual record. It neither establishes exigency by itself nor becomes irrelevant because probable cause existed. McArthur permitted a limited temporary restraint under a combined preservation justification and diligent warrant pursuit; it does not authorize this entry on probable cause alone. Kyllo likewise separates the need for authority at the time of an intrusion from a warrant obtained later. The later warrant cannot retroactively authorize the initial entry.

### II. The omitted exigency and evidence inquiries require remand under the correct rule

**Holding and operative rule:** The Louisiana Court of Appeal's judgment is reversed insofar as it sustained the warrantless entry, arrest and search on probable cause alone, and the case is remanded for proceedings consistent with Payton. The receiving court must decide the actual exigency issue and address any properly remaining causal, independent-source and evidence consequences under their governing requirements; the conviction's ultimate disposition follows those determinations.

**Authority:** Part II of the per curiam Opinion of the Court, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; eight votes for reversal and remand. Stone-Zsela agrees that the omitted inquiries require remand but would vacate the appellate judgment instead.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the appellate court's categorical legal error requires correction. Its omission of the exigency inquiry does not establish either that an emergency existed or that none existed. The competing record-based arguments must receive the adjudication the incorrect probable-cause rule displaced.

Entry authority and the admissibility of particular evidence are separate questions. Murray preserves use of a genuinely independent later source; a later warrant is not independent merely because it was issued after entry, and independence affecting admissibility does not validate the earlier intrusion. If that ground remains properly available, the court must determine whether the warrant and recovered evidence actually rested on a source independent of the unlawful entry, including whether the entry prompted the decision to seek the warrant and whether information from it affected issuance. Those are ordinary factual and legal applications on remand. The record supplies no final exigency or independence determination for this Court to substitute. The mandate therefore leaves the ultimate suppression and conviction consequences for the receiving court under the corrected rule.

## Precedent treatment and resulting law

- Payton v. New York, 445 U.S. 573: applied to require authority to cross the home threshold in addition to probable cause; ordinary arrest and incident-search authority do not dispense with that requirement.
- Illinois v. McArthur, 531 U.S. 326: distinguished because its confined temporary preservation measure rested on particularized probable cause, actual destruction risk, limited execution and diligent warrant pursuit together; it creates no automatic authority to enter a home.
- Kyllo v. United States, 533 U.S. 27: its separation of advance authority from a later warrant is preserved; its technological-search holding is not extended.
