## Event, chronology and entering law

Rush Prudential HMO, Inc. v. Moran, No. 00-1021. Merits decision, June 20, 2002. Argued January 16, 2002. On writ of certiorari to the Seventh Circuit. The question is whether ERISA preempts Illinois's requirement of independent physician review of a disputed medical-necessity determination and provision of a covered service found necessary. Rush denied the out-of-network procedure recommended for Moran; she obtained it at her expense, and the independent reviewer found it necessary. Rush refused reimbursement. Following removal, her reimbursement claim proceeded as an ERISA benefits action. The Seventh Circuit reversed the district court's preemption ruling and entered judgment for Moran.

### Issue 1: Insurance regulation can define the medical-necessity obligation

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** The substantive saving question is distinct from the accepted federal benefits vehicle and the unchallenged defendant status.
* **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether the independent physician requirement is an enforceable insurance obligation consistent with federal remedial exclusivity.
* **Stone’s Legal Position:** Apply UNUM Life Insurance Co. of America v. Ward to this additional insurance term; do not expand the available remedies.
* **Stone’s Proposed Holding:** Illinois may require this risk-bearing HMO to obtain independent medical review and honor a covered service found medically necessary. In this application the rule regulates insurance and supplies the content of the benefits obligation; enforcement remains through §502 rather than a competing state damages action.
* **Reach Within the Question:** The holding reaches review of medical necessity within insured coverage. It does not authorize an outside reviewer to rewrite every plan exclusion, impose consequential damages, or remove deemer-clause protection from self-funded plans.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The insurer’s assumption of medical-cost risk remains insurance even though it also arranges treatment. A state can regulate the conditions under which the promised protection is payable, including the medical assessment controlling a disputed necessity condition. The strongest contrary argument is that a binding reviewer resembles alternative adjudication. Here, however, the reviewer supplies a limited professional determination and no enlarged legal remedy. ERISA does not guarantee that a carrier will retain every contractual claim to unreviewable medical discretion. Recognizing the saved term while keeping judicial recovery within the federal benefits action respects both provisions. Statutory structure is decisive: the saving clause preserves substantive insurance regulation while §502 controls the judicial remedy. UNUM v. Ward is extended to this narrowly defined medical-necessity term; Pilot Life’s concern with a competing enforcement remedy is distinguished because the reviewer neither awards punitive damages nor creates a separate cause of action. This reconciliation answers both preemption objections without deciding independent-review systems that resolve coverage law or bind self-funded plans.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Other independent-review systems, unchallenged defendant status, and a self-funded plan’s statutory protection remain outside the holding.

**Final compatibility:** Stone affirms and joins both holdings and the remedy in full. The opinion preserves the limited medical determination, insured-plan boundary and unchanged federal benefits remedy. No standing fallback is used.

## Controlling holdings

### Independent medical review regulates this insured coverage

**Holding and operative rule:** Illinois HMO Act §4-10 is saved from ERISA preemption as applied to this risk-bearing HMO: when the primary care physician and HMO disagree about the necessity of a covered service the physician proposes, it requires timely independent physician review and provision of the service if found medically necessary. The rule supplies an enforceable insured-coverage condition; the three insurance guideposts are not indispensable conjunctive requirements.

**Authority:** The signed opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, controls with six votes.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that furnishing medical care does not erase the insurance function of an HMO that assumes its members' medical-cost risk. Metropolitan Life permits States to regulate insurance-contract terms, and UNUM v. Ward applies that principle to a mandatory condition governing payment. Section 4-10 directs its command to insured HMO coverage and makes independent medical assessment part of the policy relationship. Those industry and policy-relationship features confirm the common-sense classification as insurance regulation; the Court need not decide whether independent review also independently transfers risk. The law therefore cannot be dismissed as ordinary regulation of health care merely because the insurer arranges treatment as well as paying for it. This holding concerns the insured arrangement before the Court and leaves the deemer clause's protection of self-funded plans intact.

**Precedent treatment:** Metropolitan Life Insurance Co. v. Massachusetts: applied to substantive insurance-contract regulation. UNUM Life Insurance Co. of America v. Ward: extended from notice-prejudice to this limited medical-necessity term, preserving its nonconjunctive guideposts and separate conflict inquiry.

### The saved medical determination remains enforceable through ERISA

**Holding and operative rule:** The binding physician determination under §4-10 is compatible with ERISA because it supplies the medical-necessity predicate for existing covered benefits while judicial enforcement remains under §502(a)(1)(B). It neither creates an independent state cause of action nor authorizes consequential or punitive damages, and ERISA does not guarantee that an insurer may preserve contractual medical discretion against an otherwise saved insurance condition.

**Authority:** The signed opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, controls with six votes.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a mandatory medical assessment can define what the insurer owes without creating a competing remedy. Pilot Life bars an alternative state enforcement scheme; Moran instead seeks the benefits promised by her insured coverage through ERISA's own action. The unaffiliated reviewer holds the same class of professional license as the primary care physician and resolves the limited medical-necessity question, rather than awarding additional relief or exercising general arbitral jurisdiction. Firestone governs review of discretion a plan actually possesses; it does not entitle an insurer to retain discretion that valid insurance regulation removes. UNUM likewise allows a saved policy condition to inform a federal benefits action while separately rejecting an administrative reassignment rule on its own ground. Rush's strongest objection—that binding review necessarily substitutes an outside adjudication for the federal scheme—thus overlooks the limited determination and unchanged remedy here. Great-West construes the distinct §502(a)(3) equitable channel and does not restrict the benefits action Moran invokes. The reviewer's favorable medical-necessity decision supports the existing benefits judgment. The Court does not decide an unraised proper-defendant objection, the proper initial vehicle for compelling review, or systems resolving different coverage questions. The authority of this reviewer does not settle a separate experimental-treatment exclusion or a challenge to biased or inaccurate review, which are outside the presented dispute.

**Precedent treatment:** Pilot Life Insurance Co. v. Dedeaux: distinguished because no competing state remedy is supplied. Firestone Tire & Rubber Co. v.
