## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: A lawful settlement may resolve the original action

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** The matter is a merits request for a consent decree; no absent party’s rights or unsubmitted technical findings can be adjudicated by implication.
* **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether the submitted agreement may replace the specified existing decree terms.
* **Stone’s Legal Position:** Apply the governing enforcement and modification framework while recognizing consent as a legitimate means of resolving this controversy. No new substantive apportionment test is proposed.
* **Stone’s Proposed Holding:** The Court may enter the parties’ comprehensive modified decree when their consent is established, the obligations remain within the interstate controversy, and the instrument preserves lawful judicial responsibility. The decree controls through its actual definitions, accounting provisions, and reserved authority. Approval settles the pleaded claims but is not a finding that every disputed allegation was true or that administrative representatives may revise allocations beyond their express power.
* **Reach Within the Question:** The holding covers this submitted stipulation and decree. It neither constitutionalizes the technical measures nor transfers judicial power to the committee.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The Court’s duty is to make the parties’ lawful resolution enforceable with precision. The earlier distinction between enforcement and modification guards against treating every later complaint as a new equitable allocation. Consent can resolve those competing claims, but the resulting text must state what changes and how compliance is measured. The proposed committee’s value lies in administering that text. Retaining the judicial role where the decree provides for it prevents routine administration from becoming an unbounded power to alter the bargain. The earlier Nebraska decisions distinguish enforcement of an entitlement from contested alteration of the allocation; Stone reaffirms that distinction. This consensual resolution does not require a new balancing of interstate equities or a finding that a contested-modification burden was met on every surrendered allegation. The decree and the Court’s retained judicial responsibility supply the decisive legal boundaries. Because the parties request a specific instrument, a new general allocation rule would do no work necessary to judgment.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Contested rights of absent persons and changes beyond the submitted stipulation are outside this original proceeding’s settlement request.

## Law of the decision

**Operative rule and authority:** The Court enters the following complete Modified North Platte Decree. Each injunction, limit, definition, exception, accounting requirement, reserved right and retained power operates according to its own terms. The Court's order and decree bind the parties; they establish no new general equitable-apportionment standard.

**Controlling explanation:** The parties have agreed to resolve this existing interstate controversy through specified obligations. The earlier Nebraska decisions distinguish enforcement of an established entitlement from contested alteration of an allocation; they do not prevent this lawful consensual modification. The decree preserves independent storage and other enumerated rights, makes its terms superior to committee procedures, and retains judicial authority over implementation, amendment and unresolved committee matters. Those limits answer the concern that administrative implementation could become an uncontrolled power to change allocations. Consent settles the claims without converting their disputed allegations into findings.

**Precedent treatment:** Nebraska v. Wyoming (April 20, 1993 and May 30, 1995) remains governing for its enforcement, priority and modification distinctions; the present decree supplies the parties' exact agreed replacement obligations.

## Precedent Treatment

- Nebraska v. Wyoming, 325 U.S. 589, 665 (1945), and 345 U.S. 981 (1953): the prior decree is replaced by the complete decree entered today.
- Nebraska v. Wyoming (April 20, 1993): its Inland Lakes entitlement and distinction between reservoir-priority limits and absolute canal caps are preserved in III and IV.
- Nebraska v. Wyoming (May 30, 1995): its distinction between permission to litigate connected claims and proof of those claims remains; the negotiated modification does not turn the earlier rejected unilateral ceiling into an adjudicated general rule.
