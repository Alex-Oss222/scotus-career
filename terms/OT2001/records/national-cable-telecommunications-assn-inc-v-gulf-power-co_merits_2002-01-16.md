## Controlling holdings

### I. Adding Internet service does not remove a qualifying cable-system attachment from the statute

**Holding and operative rule.** An attachment by a cable television system that satisfies 47 U.S.C. §224(a)(4) remains within the FCC's authority under §224(b) when it carries Internet service together with cable television. Sections 224(d) and (e) prescribe mandatory rates within their respective terms; they do not exhaust the broader attachment definition or permit the agency to evade an applicable formula.

**Authority.** Part I of Kennedy's opinion has the full support of Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplying seven votes.

**Controlling explanation.** Congress defined the regulated attachment before prescribing methods for setting its rate. An attachment by a cable system does not cease to meet that definition when the system adds a service. The limitation to attachments used solely for cable service controls the cable formula's application; it does not rewrite the general definition. Section 224(b)'s direction to ensure just and reasonable rates, terms and conditions and its rulemaking grant therefore continue to govern covered attachments. Iowa Utilities Board supplies the distinction between an actual congressional assignment and the limits governing its exercise. The utilities' method objection deserves examination when a particular rate method is challenged, but does not establish a categorical absence of authority. Nor must the Commission resolve Internet classification to answer this coverage question: either possible classification leaves an attachment by a cable system within the general definition. Mandatory formulas remain mandatory when their conditions are met. Mead requires an actual delegation before an agency interpretation receives controlling force; the enacted text itself resolves this question, so no new deference rule is needed. The Eleventh Circuit's categorical exclusion cannot stand. Classification of Internet service and validity of a particular rate are outside the questions decided.

### II. Qualifying wireless equipment is a covered attachment

**Holding and operative rule.** Section 224 covers an attachment by a provider offering telecommunications to the public for a fee, without excluding the provider's equipment because it serves wireless transmission. Both the attachment and its owner must satisfy the statutory definitions; the utility's wire-use condition does not impose a separate wire-only restriction on the attached provider's service.

**Authority.** All nine Justices join Part II of Kennedy's opinion.

**Controlling explanation.** The telecommunications-service definition addresses service offered for a fee regardless of the facilities used. The reference to wire communications in the utility definition identifies the covered owner and structure; it does not alter the separately defined service or exclude physical wireless equipment attached to that structure. Reading the definitions together covers both wireline attachments used by wireless providers and qualifying wireless equipment. The contrary construction relocates an owner-related condition into a service limitation Congress did not enact. Iowa Utilities Board's distinction between statutory eligibility and the duties governing eligible facilities supports preserving those separate functions. The statute's exclusions for railroads, cooperatively organized persons and federal or state ownership remain, as do the incumbent-local-exchange-carrier limitation in §224(a)(5) and §224(c)'s allocation to qualifying state regulation. The holding reaches qualifying poles, ducts, conduits and rights-of-way; it does not extend authority to every independent wireless tower or property outside those definitions. The wireless categorical exclusion is reversed, with lawful rates, terms and conditions governed by the applicable statutory provisions.
