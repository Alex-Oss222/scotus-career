## Event, record and entering law

Argued November 26, 2001, on writ of certiorari to the Supreme Court of South Carolina, State v. Kelly, 540 S.E.2d 851. The state court sustained the death sentence, treating the challenged evidence as character and prison-adaptability material and relying alternatively on an earlier statutory sentencing stage. Kelly seeks a new penalty proceeding with an accurate explanation of the nondeath alternative; the State seeks affirmance and independently contends that the permitted argument and charge were adequate. The event date follows the approved merits calendar. Review reaches the preserved federal sentencing claim, including direct-review error effect, and does not reopen guilt.

The evidence and argument included an armed escape plan, a proposed guard-hostage attack and the prosecutor's portrayal of continuing dangerous character. The defense requested the statutory explanation of imprisonment until death without parole or the listed early-release mechanisms. After denial, it sought ordinary meaning and a statement that future danger was not at issue; the judge gave the former but refused the latter. Defense closing described permanent imprisonment, including a slow death and never again seeing daylight. The court confined misconduct evidence to character rather than proof of statutory aggravators. The State's brief also relied on the absence of a jury parole question or a parole-exclusion direction and on good-time testimony. Once an aggravator was found, the jury's only choices were death and life without ordinary parole.

## Controlling holdings

### Part I. The actual punishment choice and complete presentation establish a parole-information violation

**Holding and operative rule:** When the State's evidence or argument materially places future dangerousness in issue and, after the required aggravating finding, the jury can select only death or life without ordinary parole, due process requires an effective opportunity to explain that ineligibility through accurate defense presentation or an adequate instruction. The prospective message of the complete presentation controls despite a character label or the absence of prescribed words; on this record the permitted duration arguments and ordinary-meaning instruction did not convey the legally unavailable parole alternative adequately.

**Authority:** Part I of Justice Souter's opinion, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies six direct votes for the complete rule and its application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the jury must be able to evaluate the State's future-danger case against its actual sentencing alternatives. Simmons supplies the effective opportunity to answer that case, and Shafer establishes that the judge's earlier conditional power to impose a term of years does not create a third option when the jury chooses punishment after finding an aggravator. Gardner protects the opportunity to answer material capital-sentencing information; Skipper protects a response to the State's future-danger premise.

The evidence of a proposed hostage attack and armed escape, considered with the prosecutor's portrayal of an enduring dangerous character, invited a prospective inference. The same material could address past conduct, retribution or prison adaptability. Those uses did not erase its future-danger message. Limiting misconduct evidence to character rather than statutory aggravators likewise did not remove that message from punishment selection. The State need not use a particular phrase or present an expert prediction. This conclusion does not make every brutal crime sufficient, or rest on a rule that escape alone necessarily predicts further violence.

The State separately contends that counsel's descriptions of permanent confinement, the judge's ordinary-meaning charge, the absence of a jury parole inquiry or an instruction excluding parole consideration, and the good-time testimony adequately informed the jury. Taken together, those features did not explain the legal unavailability of ordinary parole. Rhetorical duration and an undefined ordinary meaning did not supply that answer. A jury question or proof of actual confusion is unnecessary. An effective defense explanation can suffice without a judicial instruction or fixed script. The State remains free to present truthful danger within prison or through escape; parole ineligibility does not make that danger impossible. The violation concerns the withheld legal answer, not a prohibition on that evidence.

### Part II. Error effect remains for the state court under Chapman's burden

**Holding and operative rule:** On remand, the State must prove beyond a reasonable doubt on the existing sentencing record that the omission did not contribute to selection of death; the future-danger predicate and informational inadequacy are settled. If the State cannot satisfy that burden, it must provide a new lawful penalty proceeding if it continues to seek death, or obtain a lawful noncapital sentence; the present judgment returns that inquiry without itself vacating the death sentence or ordering an immediate penalty hearing.

**Authority:** Part II of Justice Souter's opinion, joined by Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies five direct votes. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela does not join this part and would require the new penalty proceeding now if death is pursued. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would affirm on their distinct no-violation grounds.

**Controlling explanation:** Chapman supplies the State's burden on direct review. Simmons applies that standard to the particular sentencing presentation rather than classifying denial of parole information as structural error. Shafer preserves the distinction between identifying the violation and determining whether it affected selection of death. The state court rejected this claim without performing the required error-effect inquiry. The Court returns that inquiry under the settled danger and adequacy determinations.

The State's adequate-information contention is a claim of compliance, not a concession about the effect of an established violation. The Court infers neither waiver nor harmlessness from it. Substantial aggravating evidence alone does not answer whether the missing legal explanation contributed to the sentence. The Chief Justice would find the State's burden unsatisfied on this record. The controlling opinion instead requires the state court to assess the existing presentation under Chapman, without treating the violation itself as the answer. Guilt and the independent sentences remain intact because this claim concerns punishment selection.

Guilt and the independent sentences remain intact because this claim concerns punishment selection.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Simmons v. South Carolina, 512 U.S. 154:** Applied for the effective opportunity to explain ordinary-parole ineligibility when future danger is raised; its record-sensitive, nonstructural error rule remains intact.
- **Shafer v. South Carolina, 532 U.S. 36:** Applied to the actual post-aggravator binary choice and the separate inquiries into trigger, informational adequacy and error effect. This case settles trigger and adequacy while returning only effect.
- **Gardner v. Florida, 430 U.S. 349:** Applied through Simmons for a meaningful opportunity to answer material capital-sentencing information.
- **Skipper v. South Carolina, 476 U.S. 1:** Applied through Simmons for the opportunity to meet a future-danger premise; its independent mitigation protection remains intact.
- **Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18:** Applied to require proof beyond a reasonable doubt that the omission did not contribute to death selection.

## Law After Decision

Effective January 9, 2002, the combined future-danger presentation triggers Simmons despite its concurrent character and prison-adaptability uses, and the actual defense argument and ordinary-meaning charge are constitutionally inadequate. The existing contextual trigger, effective alternative means of explanation and State-burden direct-review harmlessness standard remain in force. The danger and adequacy determinations bind the remand; the state court must perform the distinct error-effect inquiry. No controlling rationale requires fixed words or treats every omission as structural error.

## Separate Writings

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by no other Justice.** He joins Part I in full. The escape planning and proposed violence while confined invited the inference that the omitted parole information answers. Under Chapman, the complete presentation leaves reasonable doubt whether the misleading sentencing premise contributed to death. Strong aggravation does not resolve that separate inquiry. He would therefore reverse the death-sustaining judgment and require a new lawful penalty proceeding now if the State continues to seek death, preserving guilt. This is a record-specific harmfulness determination, not structural error or an automatic new-hearing rule in every capital case. Simmons and Shafer supply the governing protection and the separate error-effect standard; his disagreement concerns its application and the resulting immediate remedy.

**Justice Scalia, dissenting, joined by no other Justice.** He would affirm against this federal claim because the Constitution does not supply this further parole-information obligation. Gardner's unavailable adverse material and Skipper's excluded rebuttal evidence do not establish a general duty to explain parole law. He accepts Shafer's account of the statutory alternatives without joining the new constitutional application. Treating evidence capable of forecasting danger as sufficient risks erasing Simmons's conditional limit. He resolves neither informational adequacy nor harmlessness and does not join the other dissents.

**Justice Kennedy, dissenting, joined by no other Justice.** He accepts Simmons and Shafer's contextual rule but finds the trigger absent here. In his view the State actually used the material for retribution, character and inability to adapt safely to prison, rather than meaningfully invoking danger from ordinary future release. Parole ineligibility does not answer danger to guards or an escape risk. Evidence capable of supporting a prediction is not necessarily a State invocation of the particular danger the rule addresses. He requires no verbal formula and does not deny that circumstantial evidence can establish the trigger on a different record. He would affirm without separately deciding adequacy or error effect.

**Justice Thomas, dissenting, joined by no other Justice.** Assuming the sentencing alternatives and future-danger predicate, he finds the actual information adequate. Defense argument conveyed permanent imprisonment, the judge directed the ordinary meaning of life, and the jury received neither a direction barring parole consideration nor a response to a parole inquiry that displaced the explanation. The good-time evidence also belongs in the full presentation. As in his Shafer dissent, substance can satisfy Simmons without the requested statutory formula. He therefore would affirm for compliance, not absence of danger or harmlessness. His constitutional disagreement with Simmons remains a reservation, not an independent ground, and he makes no finding about the jurors' actual beliefs.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns to the Supreme Court of South Carolina for the State-burden Chapman inquiry on the existing sentencing record. If the State cannot prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the omission did not contribute to death selection, a new lawful penalty proceeding is required if death is pursued; a lawful noncapital sentence remains available. The present judgment does not itself vacate the death sentence or order an immediate penalty hearing. Guilt and independent sentences remain intact.
