## Controlling holdings

### I. This implementation provision permits bounded consideration of cost

**Holding and operative rule:** After EPA establishes the relevant connection between upwind emissions and another State's nonattainment or interference with maintenance, §110(a)(2)(D)(i)(I) permits the availability of highly cost-effective controls to inform the emissions reduction required under that interstate implementation duty. Cost is permitted, not required; it cannot itself establish the interstate nexus, authorize unrelated reductions or replace the enacted air-quality objective.

**Authority:** Part I of Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court is joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Those seven directly adopt this statutory rule and its qualifications.

**Controlling explanation:** The evaluative term significant appears in an implementation assignment with no single enacted numerical cutoff. Congress assigned EPA binding plan-adequacy review, correction of interstate deficiencies and rulemaking under §301, while §110(a)(2)(A) recognizes diverse controls and economic techniques. Mead's actual-delegation threshold is therefore met, and Chevron permits a reasonable implementation choice within the remaining ambiguity; official endorsement or formal procedure alone would not suffice. EPA first identified interstate contribution through magnitude, frequency and relative modeled effects, then assessed highly cost-effective controls. The physical-contribution objection does not defeat that bounded implementation judgment because a demonstrated pollution nexus remains indispensable. Whitman excludes cost from §109 national-standard setting but preserves provision-specific implementation questions; it neither forbids all §110 cost considerations nor independently validates this benchmark. Union Electric's state-plan approval setting is distinct. MCI still forbids replacing a statutory command with administrative preference. The independent explanation of the exact $2,000 cutoff, particular modeling and surviving technical disputes lie outside these questions.

### II. A lawful budget preserves state selection when real means remain

**Holding and operative rule:** A federally authorized statewide emissions budget does not unlawfully displace the States' control choices when actual options among sources and measures remain and the Act supplies lawful federal implementation instead of an unavoidable command to enact a prescribed state program. States need not adopt EPA's illustrative source mix or optional model trading program; the mandatory approval rule protects any submission meeting all applicable requirements.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join Part II of the Chief Justice's opinion at the lawful-budget level. Scalia and Thomas do not thereby approve cost-derived amounts they regard as unauthorized.

**Controlling explanation:** Train supplies initial state source-control choice, which can exceed the constitutional floor. The supported options include stationary or mobile measures, EPA's optional trading program, a state program or no trading. EPA and OTAG found VOC controls inadequate for this long-distance NOx transport problem; that is an attributed finding supporting this pollutant-specific result, not a statutory prohibition on all VOC measures. States can add them for associated local disbenefits. New York and Hodel distinguish federal regulation with a genuine alternative from compelled state lawmaking. Section 110(c)(1) requires a federal plan within two years of a missing/incomplete required submission finding or full/partial disapproval unless the State cures and obtains approval before promulgation. Those triggers are not newly found here. Under §110(k)(3), EPA must approve a compliant whole submission; it may approve a compliant portion and disapprove another, but the revision is not fully compliant until entirely approved. The constitutional alternative cannot erase the statute's greater means-choice protection.

### III. Congress supplied the guidance; agency self-restraint supplies none

**Holding and operative rule:** The interstate provision constitutionally guides the authorized implementation judgment through identified pollutants, a demonstrated interstate relationship, downwind national-standard nonattainment or maintenance, significant-contribution limits and defined plan-adequacy/correction functions. EPA's present geographic restraint or chosen cost ceiling cannot cure a delegation lacking adequate congressional guidance.

**Authority:** Part III is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. These seven directly support constitutional sufficiency and the no-agency-cure application. Scalia and Thomas do not reach this issue.

**Controlling explanation:** Whitman requires legislative criteria and independently rejects agency cure. The statute identifies the subject, purpose and limits; Congress need not select each numerical budget. Permitted cost analysis operates within that task after the nexus is shown, rather than allowing the agency to choose its own unrelated objective. Section 110(k)(5) requires the revision necessary to correct substantial inadequacy, public findings and state notice. EPA may establish a reasonable submission deadline no later than eighteen months after notice; appropriate original-plan requirements apply and dates may be adjusted, except an unelapsed Part D attainment date. Section 301 confers no authority to replace the statutory objective. An unexplained particular figure can fail ordinary review even though Congress's delegation is sufficient. The Court rejects the lower court's reliance on the confined exercise of power as an independent constitutional repair.

**Common statutory boundaries:** Section 110(a)(2)(D) requires adequate plan provisions consistent with Title I prohibiting emissions that significantly contribute to nonattainment in another State or interfere with maintenance by another State of the applicable national primary or secondary ambient air-quality standard. Significant contribution qualifies the nonattainment branch; the separate maintenance branch requires interference with maintenance. The adjacent significant-deterioration/visibility and §§115/126 compliance duties remain in their own settings and are not newly adjudicated here. Section 110(a)(2)(A) calls for enforceable limits and other means or techniques, including fees, marketable permits and emissions-right auctions, with necessary or appropriate schedules. Those methods do not independently authorize replacement of the statutory air-quality objective. Under §110(k)(5), correction follows substantial inadequacy to attain or maintain the relevant national standard, adequately mitigate the identified interstate transport or otherwise comply with the Act. To the extent EPA considers appropriate the finding subjects the State to requirements applicable when the deficient plan was developed and submitted; EPA may adjust applicable dates, but not an unelapsed Part D attainment date. The mandatory federal-plan, approval and notice rules stated above are not findings that any new trigger has occurred or new judicial deadlines.

**Review scope and remedy:** This judgment resolves the three categorical questions for the one-hour ozone standard. The eight-hour findings and their review remain stayed. The independent adequacy of the exact cost cutoff and particular unreviewed technical choices are outside the questions presented. The circuit's Wisconsin, Missouri and Georgia vacaturs/remands and its notice remands concerning the electric-generating-unit definition and the control level for large stationary internal-combustion engines remain intact. The sustained portions remain operative subject to those existing corrections; no additional agency remand is ordered on the three questions decided here.

### Governing statutory structure and qualifications

Section 110(a)(2)(D) requires adequate plan provisions, consistent with Title I, prohibiting sources or other emissions activity from emitting a pollutant in amounts that significantly contribute to another State's nonattainment or interfere with its maintenance of the applicable national primary or secondary standard. The adjacent deterioration/visibility branch and the distinct requirements to assure compliance with §§115 and 126 remain operative in their own settings; their presence is not a decision of a new claim under them.

Section 110(a)(2)(A) requires enforceable emission limits and other control measures, means or techniques, including economic incentives such as fees, marketable permits and auctions of emissions rights, with necessary or appropriate schedules and timetables. It confirms the implementation setting and the range of control methods. It is not, standing alone, permission to redefine a statutory air-quality objective. Under §110(k)(3), EPA must approve an entire submission if it meets all applicable requirements. EPA may approve a compliant portion and disapprove another portion, but the revision is not treated as meeting the Act's requirements until the entire revision is approved. That mandatory approval structure protects state choice within the national requirements.

Section 110(k)(5) operates after EPA finds substantial inadequacy to attain or maintain the relevant national standard, adequately mitigate the identified interstate transport, or otherwise comply with the Act. EPA must require the revision necessary to correct the inadequacy, must notify the State and make its findings and notice public, and may set a reasonable submission deadline no later than eighteen months after notice. To the extent EPA considers appropriate, the finding subjects the State to the requirements applicable when it developed and submitted the deficient plan; EPA may adjust applicable dates, but may not adjust a Part D attainment date unless that date has elapsed. None of those qualifications is replaced by a judicial deadline in this case.

Section 110(c)(1) requires a federal implementation plan within two years after EPA finds a missing required submission or failure to satisfy the minimum completeness criteria, or disapproves a submission wholly or partly, unless the State corrects the deficiency and obtains approval before the federal plan is promulgated. The federal plan is an actual statutory alternative to state administration; this case makes no new finding that a particular trigger has occurred. Section 301(a)(1) permits regulations necessary to carry out EPA's statutory functions. That power and the specific interstate and plan-review assignments supply an actual rulemaking delegation, but no authority to add a different environmental objective.

### Issue I — May control cost inform the reduction required from a contributing State?

#### Six-Justice sufficient rule

After EPA establishes a State's relevant interstate contribution through air-quality analysis, §110(a)(2)(D)(i)(I) permits it to consider the availability of highly cost-effective controls in determining the emissions reduction required to eliminate the contribution it treats as significant under this implementation provision. The statute does not require cost consideration, benefits exceeding costs, or a general cost-benefit balance. Cost alone cannot establish the interstate contribution or authorize regulation of unrelated emissions; the national air-quality objective and the demonstrated interstate relation remain necessary boundaries.

The text requires judgment about significance within an implementation program; it supplies neither a numerical physical-contribution cutoff nor a single required method for selecting the reducible amount. Congress assigned EPA to judge plan adequacy and correct interstate deficiencies, while expressly contemplating diverse control techniques. Read together, those provisions leave room for a reasonable implementation judgment incorporating control availability and expense after the relevant pollution connection is established. Mead's actual-delegation threshold is satisfied by the specific plan-review and interstate duties together with §301 rulemaking exercised through this binding rule. Chevron then permits the agency's reasonable resolution of the remaining implementation ambiguity. Formal procedure alone would not create that power, and the conclusion does not rest solely on the absence of an express cost prohibition.

EPA's two-stage method matters: it first used magnitude, frequency and relative contribution of modeled interstate effects to identify contributing States, then used highly cost-effective controls to identify the required reduction. The cost inquiry therefore supplements an established air-quality connection rather than creating one. The six would sustain that legal role for cost; they would not independently find every modeling result correct, make $2,000 per ton a statutory or constitutional safe harbor, or decide an unpresented challenge to the numerical selection's explanation.

Whitman governs §109's health- and welfare-based national standards and expressly preserves provision-specific implementation questions. It neither bars all cost consideration under §110 nor itself authorizes this particular use. Union Electric's refusal to make economic infeasibility a ground for rejecting a qualifying state plan does not decide the different federal interstate-adequacy judgment. Train preserves initial state selection of source controls within the statutory result. Arkansas v. Oklahoma confirms that a demonstrated interstate problem can receive authorized federal implementation, while MCI prevents administrative policy from replacing an actual statutory command. The rule here must remain within the enacted interstate condition; none of those cases supports a general presumption of federal power over anything environmentally useful.

#### Two-Justice sufficient contrary rule

Scalia and Thomas read the phrase describing amounts that contribute significantly to nonattainment or interfere with maintenance as a pollution criterion. Cost measures the expense of eliminating emissions, not the amount's contribution to another State's failure to meet a standard. Congress's express recognition of economic methods gives States ways of meeting a required result; it does not authorize EPA to redefine that result by how cheaply controls can be installed. Once the statutory question is resolved at that level, Chevron supplies no authority to substitute the agency's preferred cost criterion.

They would require EPA to identify the prohibited contribution under the enacted air-quality criterion and reconsider the budget determination that substituted highly cost-effective removability for that criterion. They do not require elimination of every molecule of interstate pollution or a unique judicially chosen significance number. Neither would forbid every consideration of expense in choosing means under an independently authorized implementation provision. They refuse to preserve this same budget by simply renaming the stage at which its cost formula operates.

#### Adversarial test and join boundaries

The sustaining path's strongest objection is Sentelle's distinction between physical contribution and control expense: calling the measure implementation does not permit it to alter an emissions-defined duty. The six answer with the provision's delegated judgment concerning which contribution is significant in a multi-state implementation setting, the prior physical connection and the retained statutory air-quality objective. They must therefore reject a rationale resting only on an assumption that cost is always permitted unless forbidden. The contrary path's strongest objection is that significance itself calls for evaluative choice and Congress supplied an implementation framework expressly accommodating economic techniques. The two dissenters answer that evaluative choice must concern the enacted pollution relationship, not a different measure of significance selected from policy preferences. Their remedy is agency reconsideration, not judicial replacement.

No Justice adopts a rule that Whitman's national-standard cost exclusion automatically decides §110. No Justice treats cost-effectiveness and benefit-cost balancing as synonyms. The exact benchmark's independent reasonableness is outside this supplied review, not a factual gap filled by the Court.

### Issue II — Do statewide NOx budgets unlawfully displace the States' choice of measures?

All eight would reject the categorical challenge to a lawful statewide emissions budget, with Scalia and Thomas expressly withholding approval of the cost-derived amount for the reasons above. Train gives States initial responsibility to select source-by-source limitations and other measures that satisfy the Act; EPA may enforce the applicable result but may not displace that choice merely because it prefers a particular source program. A statewide budget does not itself cross that line when the State retains real options concerning which sources and measures will supply the necessary reduction.

The record supplies actual options: stationary or mobile-source measures, participation in EPA's optional trading program, a State's own trading program, or no trading program. The model program's particular source requirements attach to the State's election to use that option; they do not govern every State independently of its choice. The prohibition on replacing required NOx reductions with VOC controls does not show a forbidden federal means choice on this record: EPA and OTAG concluded VOC reductions would not adequately address the long-distance transport problem for which the budget was required. States retain authority to add VOC controls for associated local disbenefits. The Court does not make every pollutant-specific demand lawful by definition or sustain nominal freedom consisting only of permission to spend more.

The strongest contrary path is that a tightly set budget and the inability to choose VOC reductions effectively determine state control policy, regardless of optional trading. Its answer is the actual range of retained source and program choices and the supported pollutant-specific distinction. No supplied fact shows that only one source plan can meet the budget. The Court may rely on those attributed findings; it may not invent technical impossibility or assume every future budget preserves meaningful alternatives.

The constitutional structure reinforces, but does not replace, this statutory analysis. New York v. United States and Hodel distinguish a lawful federal regulatory alternative from a command that an unwilling State legislate or assume private liabilities. Section 110(c)(1)'s federal plan mechanism supplies the relevant alternative when its statutory triggers occur. FERC v. Mississippi retains its cooperative-regulation scope. Neither that alternative nor the absence of commandeering would authorize EPA to remove the greater choice Train and the statute preserve. The eight reject the present categorical state-choice objection on the actual statutory options; no freestanding expansion of constitutional federal power is necessary.

### Issue III — Does Congress supply sufficient guidance for the authorized cost-informed implementation?

Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer would sustain the delegation under the governing intelligible-principle doctrine. The principle comes from Congress: identified air pollutants governed by national standards, a demonstrable interstate emissions relationship, nonattainment and maintenance endpoints, significant contribution, adequate plan provisions consistent with Title I, and defined plan-review and correction functions. These connected limits identify the subject, purpose and boundaries of the delegated judgment. Congress need not choose the single dollar figure or resolve every technical implementation question itself.

The six expressly reject the lower court's suggestion that a relatively confined exercise of power to date can cure missing legislative guidance. Whitman controls that point: EPA's actual modesty, geographic footprint, selected cost ceiling or promise to restrain itself supplies no missing congressional principle. The present rule is sustained constitutionally because the statute provides sufficient limits, independently of those choices. A particular number can still be unexplained or unlawful under ordinary review even when the underlying delegation is constitutional; this ruling adjudicates no unreviewed numerical-method challenge.

The strongest contrary constitutional path argues that permitting expense to define significant contribution lets the agency choose its own regulatory ambition without a congressional stopping point. The six answer that cost does not become an independent statutory objective or jurisdictional predicate: it operates inside the enacted interstate air-quality task, after the contribution is shown and subject to the mandatory adequacy and consistency requirements. That conclusion applies the received doctrine, not a claim that any public-interest instruction would suffice. No court-created price cap is used as constitutional repair.

Scalia and Thomas do **not reach** this constitutional question after their statutory-reversal conclusion. They join neither constitutional invalidation nor the six-Justice sufficiency holding conditionally. Their prior Whitman agreement that agency self-limitation cannot cure a standardless assignment remains current law; it does not supply a new constitutional merits vote in this case. Thomas's published reservation concerning the possible limits of the received doctrine is retained as a reservation, not converted into an unpresented reconsideration or a vote to invalidate this provision.

## Holdings

### I. This implementation provision permits bounded consideration of cost

**Holding and operative rule:** After EPA establishes the relevant connection between upwind emissions and another State's nonattainment or interference with maintenance, §110(a)(2)(D)(i)(I) permits the availability of highly cost-effective controls to inform the emissions reduction required under that interstate implementation duty. Cost is permitted, not required; it cannot itself establish the interstate nexus, authorize unrelated reductions or replace the enacted air-quality objective.

**Authority:** Part I of Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court is joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Those seven directly adopt this statutory rule and its qualifications.

**Controlling explanation:** The evaluative term significant appears in an implementation assignment with no single enacted numerical cutoff. Congress assigned EPA binding plan-adequacy review, correction of interstate deficiencies and rulemaking under §301, while §110(a)(2)(A) recognizes diverse controls and economic techniques. Mead's actual-delegation threshold is therefore met, and Chevron permits a reasonable implementation choice within the remaining ambiguity; official endorsement or formal procedure alone would not suffice. EPA first identified interstate contribution through magnitude, frequency and relative modeled effects, then assessed highly cost-effective controls. The physical-contribution objection does not defeat that bounded implementation judgment because a demonstrated pollution nexus remains indispensable. Whitman excludes cost from §109 national-standard setting but preserves provision-specific implementation questions; it neither forbids all §110 cost considerations nor independently validates this benchmark. Union Electric's state-plan approval setting is distinct. MCI still forbids replacing a statutory command with administrative preference. The independent explanation of the exact $2,000 cutoff, particular modeling and surviving technical disputes lie outside these questions.

### II. A lawful budget preserves state selection when real means remain

**Holding and operative rule:** A federally authorized statewide emissions budget does not unlawfully displace the States' control choices when actual options among sources and measures remain and the Act supplies lawful federal implementation instead of an unavoidable command to enact a prescribed state program. States need not adopt EPA's illustrative source mix or optional model trading program; the mandatory approval rule protects any submission meeting all applicable requirements.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join Part II of the Chief Justice's opinion at the lawful-budget level. Scalia and Thomas do not thereby approve cost-derived amounts they regard as unauthorized.

**Controlling explanation:** Train supplies initial state source-control choice, which can exceed the constitutional floor. The supported options include stationary or mobile measures, EPA's optional trading program, a state program or no trading. EPA and OTAG found VOC controls inadequate for this long-distance NOx transport problem; that is an attributed finding supporting this pollutant-specific result, not a statutory prohibition on all VOC measures. States can add them for associated local disbenefits. New York and Hodel distinguish federal regulation with a genuine alternative from compelled state lawmaking. Section 110(c)(1) requires a federal plan within two years of a missing/incomplete required submission finding or full/partial disapproval unless the State cures and obtains approval before promulgation. Those triggers are not newly found here. Under §110(k)(3), EPA must approve a compliant whole submission; it may approve a compliant portion and disapprove another, but the revision is not fully compliant until entirely approved. The constitutional alternative cannot erase the statute's greater means-choice protection.

### III. Congress supplied the guidance; agency self-restraint supplies none

**Holding and operative rule:** The interstate provision constitutionally guides the authorized implementation judgment through identified pollutants, a demonstrated interstate relationship, downwind national-standard nonattainment or maintenance, significant-contribution limits and defined plan-adequacy/correction functions. EPA's present geographic restraint or chosen cost ceiling cannot cure a delegation lacking adequate congressional guidance.

**Authority:** Part III is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. These seven directly support constitutional sufficiency and the no-agency-cure application. Scalia and Thomas do not reach this issue.

**Controlling explanation:** Whitman requires legislative criteria and independently rejects agency cure. The statute identifies the subject, purpose and limits; Congress need not select each numerical budget. Permitted cost analysis operates within that task after the nexus is shown, rather than allowing the agency to choose its own unrelated objective. Section 110(k)(5) requires the revision necessary to correct substantial inadequacy, public findings and state notice. EPA may establish a reasonable submission deadline no later than eighteen months after notice; appropriate original-plan requirements apply and dates may be adjusted, except an unelapsed Part D attainment date. Section 301 confers no authority to replace the statutory objective. An unexplained particular figure can fail ordinary review even though Congress's delegation is sufficient. The Court rejects the lower court's reliance on the confined exercise of power as an independent constitutional repair.

**Common statutory boundaries:** Section 110(a)(2)(D) requires adequate plan provisions consistent with Title I prohibiting emissions that significantly contribute to nonattainment in another State or interfere with maintenance by another State of the applicable national primary or secondary ambient air-quality standard. Significant contribution qualifies the nonattainment branch; the separate maintenance branch requires interference with maintenance. The adjacent significant-deterioration/visibility and §§115/126 compliance duties remain in their own settings and are not newly adjudicated here. Section 110(a)(2)(A) calls for enforceable limits and other means or techniques, including fees, marketable permits and emissions-right auctions, with necessary or appropriate schedules. Those methods do not independently authorize replacement of the statutory air-quality objective. Under §110(k)(5), correction follows substantial inadequacy to attain or maintain the relevant national standard, adequately mitigate the identified interstate transport or otherwise comply with the Act. To the extent EPA considers appropriate the finding subjects the State to requirements applicable when the deficient plan was developed and submitted; EPA may adjust applicable dates, but not an unelapsed Part D attainment date. The mandatory federal-plan, approval and notice rules stated above are not findings that any new trigger has occurred or new judicial deadlines.

**Review scope and remedy:** This judgment resolves the three categorical questions for the one-hour ozone standard. The eight-hour findings and their review remain stayed. The independent adequacy of the exact cost cutoff and particular unreviewed technical choices are outside the questions presented. The circuit's Wisconsin, Missouri and Georgia vacaturs/remands and its notice remands concerning the electric-generating-unit definition and the control level for large stationary internal-combustion engines remain intact. The sustained portions remain operative subject to those existing corrections; no additional agency remand is ordered on the three questions decided here.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Whitman v. American Trucking Associations, Inc.:** Section 109's exclusion of implementation cost remains; the distinct §110 implementation question is resolved on its own text. Congress's guidance requirement and the independent no-agency-cure rule govern the constitutional analysis.
- **United States v. Mead Corp. and Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.:** Applied to actual delegated force and a reasonable resolution of genuine implementation ambiguity; formal procedure or institutional endorsement alone supplies neither authority nor validity.
- **Barnhart v. Walton:** Its formal delegated-choice ground confirms permission for reasonable implementation within an actual assignment; it creates no presumption authorizing every cost factor or power from longstanding practice.
- **Train v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc., 421 U.S. 60:** Its initial state source-control choice is applied; statutory choice remains independently protective even where the constitutional federal alternative exists.
- **Union Electric Co. v. EPA, 427 U.S. 246:** Its rejection of economic infeasibility as a basis for refusing a qualifying state plan does not decide this distinct federal interstate-adequacy question.
- **Arkansas v. Oklahoma:** Authorized federal interstate implementation and supported technical judgments retain their force; they do not supply authority apart from Congress's statutory assignment.
- **MCI Telecommunications Corp. v. American Telephone & Telegraph Co.:** Its prohibition on replacing a mandatory statutory task with agency policy remains binding.
- **AT&T Corp. v. Iowa Utilities Board and New York v. FERC:** Actual federal assignments, constraints on their exercise and preserved state functions remain distinct; neither decision supplies a general presumption of regulatory power.
- **New York v. United States; Hodel v. Virginia Surface Mining & Reclamation Assn., 452 U.S. 264; FERC v. Mississippi, 456 U.S. 742:** Their distinction between lawful federal regulation with genuine state alternatives and compulsory state administration applies. The constitutional floor does not erase greater statutory means-choice protections.

## Law After Decision

Section 110 permits the bounded cost-informed implementation judgment after a demonstrated interstate pollution nexus. It neither mandates cost consideration nor generally imports cost into national-standard setting. States retain genuine selection among compliant source measures and programs, backed by the mandatory approval rule and a lawful federal-plan alternative. Congress supplied sufficient guidance for this assignment; administrative self-restraint cannot supply missing legislative limits. The one-hour review and the circuit's separate corrections define the judgment's reach.

## Separate Writings

**Scalia, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by Thomas.** The enacted criterion concerns amounts of pollution contributing to another State's nonattainment or interfering with maintenance. Cheapness of removal measures expense, not that pollution relationship. Congress's express economic techniques permit ways of meeting an authorized result; they do not authorize EPA to redefine the result through cost. MCI and the limiting-criteria analysis in Iowa Utilities Board require those enacted words to constrain agency choice independently; Chevron supplies no power to replace them. Walton's acceptance of reasonable formal implementation does not help when the disputed choice falls outside the statutory criterion. The two would remand the affected cost determination for agency reconsideration under the pollution standard, without imposing a unique significance number or requiring elimination of every molecule of interstate emissions. They would not preserve the same amount merely by renaming its stage.

They join Part II because Train and the actual alternatives distinguish a lawful statewide result from compelled source selection. That join does not approve the challenged cost-derived amounts. Having resolved the rule on statutory grounds, they do not reach the constitutional sufficiency question. Their existing agreement that agency self-restraint cannot cure missing congressional guidance remains unaffected. The dissent preserves the separate geographic and notice corrections and chooses no judicial replacement budget.
