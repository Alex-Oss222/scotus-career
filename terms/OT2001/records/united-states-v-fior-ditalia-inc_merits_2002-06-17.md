## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Reasonable estimation implements an existing tax obligation

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** The authority issue is cleanly presented because accuracy was expressly withdrawn from dispute.
* **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether assessment of the employer requires a prior assessment of each employee.
* **Stone’s Legal Position:** Apply §§6201, 3111 and 3121(q) together. Their assessment and employer-liability terms resolve this question without a presumption of unlimited administrative power.
* **Stone’s Proposed Holding:** Section 6201 permits a reasonable aggregate estimate of an employer’s liability under §§3111 and 3121(q). A prior determination or assessment of every employee’s liability is not a statutory prerequisite. A method remains subject to the substantive tax exclusions and an available challenge to factual accuracy.
* **Reach Within the Question:** The rule authorizes a method of calculation, not a conclusive presumption that all receipts generate taxable tips at the same rate. It does not change refund jurisdiction or statutory filing limits.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The employer owes the tax because the statute treats qualifying tips as its remuneration, rather than because the government has successfully collected from an employee. Requiring a prior individual assessment inserts a procedural condition Congress did not state. The counterargument identifies real limitations of a rough estimate, but those limitations concern whether the assessment measures the actual statutory base. A supported challenge could require correction for exempt amounts, wage ceilings, or differing tipping patterns. This taxpayer deliberately presented only the legal-power issue and cannot obtain a factual recalculation on hypothetical defects. The enacted allocation of tax liability is decisive; administrative practicality confirms the construction but cannot enlarge the taxable base. A method-specific rule is necessary for future assessments because the asserted legal defect is the absence of an employee-by-employee prerequisite, while the accuracy stipulation makes any wider factual ruling unnecessary.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The correctness of a different estimate and a supported claim of abusive agency conduct are not decided.

## Controlling holdings

### Reasonable aggregate estimation may assess the employer's independent tax

**Holding and operative rule:** Sections 3111, 3121(q) and 6201 permit the IRS to assess an employer's own FICA liability through a reasonable aggregate estimate of taxable tip wages without first determining or assessing every employee's liability. Estimation implements the existing tax; it does not enlarge the statutory wage base.

**Authority:** Breyer's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg, controls for the coalition stated in Opinion Topology.

**Controlling explanation:** The employer owes tax because qualifying tips count as its remuneration, not because the government first assesses or collects an employee's tax. Section 6201's general assessment power includes reasonable estimation of that obligation. The references to an employee in the wage definition retain their substantive work but do not add a sequence of individual assessments before assessment of the employer. The dissent rightly insists that recipient-specific limits matter; it does not follow that those limits forbid every aggregate method of determining the lawful total. Section 446's particular accounting authority does not negate general assessment power elsewhere. Section 6205's regulated interest-free correction mechanism is distinct and does not require an authorizing regulation before this assessment. For subtitle F, Section 3121(q) treats tips omitted from an employee's statement, including an inaccurate or incomplete statement, as paid when the Secretary makes notice and demand on the employer. Penalties and interest therefore do not arise merely from the earlier underreporting; the employer must subsequently fail to pay the demanded amount timely. Initial employer reporting based on employee statements does not immunize qualifying omitted tips from later lawful assessment. Administrative convenience confirms the statutory construction but cannot supply a new taxable category.

**Precedent treatment:** Cleveland Indians separates tax computation, timing and benefit accounting; United Dominion applies the computation actually supplied by the governing scheme. Neither adds an employee-assessment prerequisite here.

### The estimate remains bounded by taxable wages and the actual litigation stipulation

**Holding and operative rule:** Reasonable aggregate estimation must retain the exclusion of noncash tips, the exclusion of cash tips below $20 for an employee in a calendar month, and the annual wage bases applicable to each tax component and employee for 1991 and 1992. A taxpayer may ordinarily challenge an estimate's reasonable factual foundation, but this restaurant's express stipulation forecloses an accuracy challenge in this case while preserving its unsuccessful statutory-authority argument.

**Authority:** Breyer's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg, controls for the coalition stated in Opinion Topology.

**Controlling explanation:** A tax estimate must approximate the liability Congress imposed, not simply treat all receipts or tips as taxable wages. The later removal of Medicare's wage ceiling and the later-enacted tip credit do not govern these tax years. Supported differences between cash and credit-card tipping, below-threshold tips, annual ceilings, cash returned through credit-card transactions or deducted card charges can show a method likely to overstate liability. A taxpayer need not reconstruct every exact tip merely to expose an unreasonable foundation. None of those possible defects is a proved error here: the restaurant deliberately limited the litigation to legal power and conceded the underlying estimates and assumptions. That concession does not itself create statutory authority, but it prevents converting hypothetical inaccuracies into a factual recalculation. The Court therefore rejects the categorical legal bar and applies the actual stipulation governing the refund and counterclaim. General concern about reporting-program pressure establishes no improper motive in this assessment.

**Precedent treatment:** Brockamp's distinct refund time and amount limits remain unchanged; permission to estimate neither expands refund jurisdiction nor suspends those limits.

### Components and competing supported paths

The employer's FICA liability is distinct from the employees' income-tax or FICA collection obligations. Sections 3111 and 3121(q) govern the employer's taxable tip wages, including the applicable notice-and-demand timing for unreported tips. Section 6201 supplies assessment authority; §6205's adjustment mechanism remains distinct. Every authorized assessment remains bounded by actual taxable wages: noncash tips, cash tips below the employee's monthly threshold and the applicable annual bases cannot be taxed merely by calling an amount an estimate. The 1991–1992 wage bases govern; later changes to Medicare's base and the later-enacted §45B credit cannot retroactively alter those years.

The taxpayer expressly confined this litigation to the legal authority for an aggregate employer assessment and conceded the factual assumptions and estimates for that purpose. That stipulation does not concede statutory power. Conversely, it prevents turning hypothetical inaccuracies into findings that this assessment in fact double-counted tips, missed an actual wage-base adjustment or overstated cash tips. The refund claim and the government's counterclaim turn on the same challenged statutory premise. Neither side's success on that premise supplies an unpresented recalculation.

**Authorization:** general assessment power permits a reasonable estimate of an independently imposed employer tax. Congress did not require separate employee assessments, collection from employees or allocation of Social Security benefit credits before assessing the employer. The applicable exclusions and wage bases constrain the lawful amount and remain available in a proper factual challenge. The principal objection is that an aggregate credit-card extrapolation may obscure the employee-specific facts needed to determine those statutory limits. The response on this submitted record is that the employer reserved no challenge to the estimates or factual assumptions, and the Court need not approve an inaccurate assessment to reject a categorical lack of authority.

**No authority for this aggregate method:** Congress defined the employer's liability through the individual recipient's taxable wages, with exclusions and annual bases that cannot be replaced by an estimate of restaurant-wide receipts alone. Section 6201 authorizes assessment of the tax Congress imposed; it does not redefine that base. This path does not require first assessing or collecting tax from an employee, and does not prohibit supported estimates directed to an employee's taxable wages. Its strongest counterargument is that the statute does not prescribe such an individualized computational prerequisite and that the taxpayer's stipulation removes the factual defects invoked to justify one. The narrower response is structural: agreement that an aggregate factual estimate is accurate does not decide whether that estimate supplies the statutorily required wage determination. The Court must state that legal premise expressly rather than pretend the record proves computational error.

## Rules and competing applications

The five would hold that §§3111, 3121(q) and 6201 permit the IRS to assess the employer's own FICA liability through a reasonable aggregate estimate. Liability is imposed on the employer's taxable wages as a whole, including qualifying tips treated as paid by the employer; the definitional reference to an employee does not require a series of employee assessments before assessing that employer. The method must estimate the tax Congress imposed and cannot erase a taxable-wage exclusion. General assessment authority includes reasonable implementation, not an authority to invent additional taxable receipts.

The tax years are 1991 and 1992. Noncash tips and cash tips below $20 for an employee in a calendar month remain excluded. The applicable annual bases apply separately to the relevant tax components and recipients. The later elimination of Medicare's wage ceiling is not imported into these earlier years. An aggregate method cannot lawfully treat every dollar of restaurant receipts or all tips as taxable wages regardless of those limits. The 1993 credit does not retroactively govern these assessments.

Ordinarily a taxpayer may demonstrate that a proposed aggregate method likely yields an inaccurate result; the taxpayer need not reconstruct every tip with exact precision merely to expose a method lacking a reasonable foundation. Cash-versus-credit tipping differences, tips below the monthly threshold, annual wage ceilings, cash returned through a credit-card transaction and deducted credit-card charges are possible objections where supported. They are not established errors here. The restaurant's express litigation choice forecloses a new accuracy contest while preserving its legal argument. That choice permits rejection of the categorical no-authority theory without declaring every conceivable formula reasonable.

Section 446's particular accounting authority does not negatively strip the IRS of general assessment authority for other taxes. Section 6205's regulated interest-free adjustment mechanism is distinct from the IRS's assessment function and supplies no prerequisite authorizing regulation for this estimate. These are responses to the existing statutory-authority issue, not new factual premises. Section 3121(q)'s special subtitle F notice-and-demand timing for unreported or incompletely reported tips remains operative. The employer's obligation initially to report on employee statements does not immunize qualifying unreported wages from a subsequent lawful employer assessment; penalties and interest cannot be backdated by ignoring the statute's actual payment timing. No separate penalty or interest amount is adjudicated.

The three would hold that the statutory taxable-wage and notice-and-demand scheme requires employee-specific determinations before calculating the employer's liability from them. Section 6201 authorizes assessment of an existing tax; it does not redefine the base specified through each recipient's wages. Recipient-specific monthly exclusions and annual limits therefore perform substantive work that a restaurant-wide total cannot simply replace. Accurate aggregate receipts do not necessarily supply the legal determination the statute requires, so the stipulation does not concede authority. Estimates remain possible within that employee-based method.

The strongest contrary argument is that the operative employer-tax sections tax total wages and prescribe no individual-computation prerequisite. The three answer through the combined operation of §3121(q)'s employee-specific tax base and notice-and-demand provision, rather than by finding a computational error the taxpayer conceded away. The five respond that those provisions define the lawful liability and its timing without prohibiting reasonable aggregate determination of that liability. The dispute is thus a genuine statutory-method disagreement on an agreed factual submission.

The historical dissent also discussed employer recordkeeping protections, collection parity and potential reporting-program pressure as contextual reasons for its reading. They do not supply new adjudicated facts here or a separate ruling that this restaurant was coerced. The frozen sufficient ground is the employee-based statutory determination. There is no need to decide whether each hypothetical abuse would independently violate the law or whether a formal employee audit is mandatory. The three's reservation is part of the final join boundary, not an affirmative holding for the five's position on that separate question.

## Holdings

### Reasonable aggregate estimation may assess the employer's independent tax

**Holding and operative rule:** Sections 3111, 3121(q) and 6201 permit the IRS to assess an employer's own FICA liability through a reasonable aggregate estimate of taxable tip wages without first determining or assessing every employee's liability. Estimation implements the existing tax; it does not enlarge the statutory wage base.

**Authority:** Breyer's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg, controls for the coalition stated in Opinion Topology.

**Controlling explanation:** The employer owes tax because qualifying tips count as its remuneration, not because the government first assesses or collects an employee's tax. Section 6201's general assessment power includes reasonable estimation of that obligation. The references to an employee in the wage definition retain their substantive work but do not add a sequence of individual assessments before assessment of the employer. The dissent rightly insists that recipient-specific limits matter; it does not follow that those limits forbid every aggregate method of determining the lawful total. Section 446's particular accounting authority does not negate general assessment power elsewhere. Section 6205's regulated interest-free correction mechanism is distinct and does not require an authorizing regulation before this assessment. For subtitle F, Section 3121(q) treats tips omitted from an employee's statement, including an inaccurate or incomplete statement, as paid when the Secretary makes notice and demand on the employer. Penalties and interest therefore do not arise merely from the earlier underreporting; the employer must subsequently fail to pay the demanded amount timely. Initial employer reporting based on employee statements does not immunize qualifying omitted tips from later lawful assessment. Administrative convenience confirms the statutory construction but cannot supply a new taxable category.

**Precedent treatment:** Cleveland Indians separates tax computation, timing and benefit accounting; United Dominion applies the computation actually supplied by the governing scheme. Neither adds an employee-assessment prerequisite here.

### The estimate remains bounded by taxable wages and the actual litigation stipulation

**Holding and operative rule:** Reasonable aggregate estimation must retain the exclusion of noncash tips, the exclusion of cash tips below $20 for an employee in a calendar month, and the annual wage bases applicable to each tax component and employee for 1991 and 1992. A taxpayer may ordinarily challenge an estimate's reasonable factual foundation, but this restaurant's express stipulation forecloses an accuracy challenge in this case while preserving its unsuccessful statutory-authority argument.

**Authority:** Breyer's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg, controls for the coalition stated in Opinion Topology.

**Controlling explanation:** A tax estimate must approximate the liability Congress imposed, not simply treat all receipts or tips as taxable wages. The later removal of Medicare's wage ceiling and the later-enacted tip credit do not govern these tax years. Supported differences between cash and credit-card tipping, below-threshold tips, annual ceilings, cash returned through credit-card transactions or deducted card charges can show a method likely to overstate liability. A taxpayer need not reconstruct every exact tip merely to expose an unreasonable foundation. None of those possible defects is a proved error here: the restaurant deliberately limited the litigation to legal power and conceded the underlying estimates and assumptions. That concession does not itself create statutory authority, but it prevents converting hypothetical inaccuracies into a factual recalculation. The Court therefore rejects the categorical legal bar and applies the actual stipulation governing the refund and counterclaim. General concern about reporting-program pressure establishes no improper motive in this assessment.

**Precedent treatment:** Brockamp's distinct refund time and amount limits remain unchanged; permission to estimate neither expands refund jurisdiction nor suspends those limits.

## Precedent Treatment

- United States v. Cleveland Indians Baseball Co., April 17, 2001: preserves separate tax timing, annual wage bases and benefits questions; its lawful-regulation holding is not blanket deference to every estimate.
- United Dominion Industries, Inc. v. United States, June 4, 2001: the actual enacted and regulatory computation governs; aggregation there does not authorize a different tax base here.
- United States v. Brockamp, 519 U.S. 347 (1997): refund filing and amount limits retain their distinct force; no estimation rule is drawn from them.
- United States v. Craft, April 17, 2002: the substantive taxable interest and federal implementation remain distinct; the decision does not itself decide this FICA method.

## Law After Decision

The IRS may reasonably estimate an employer's aggregate taxable tips without prior employee assessments. Each substantive exclusion, annual base and notice-and-demand timing rule remains enforceable. In a case presenting accuracy, the taxpayer may demonstrate that the method lacks a reasonable foundation. This case ends the categorical authority challenge on its stipulated factual submission.

## Separate Writings

**Souter, dissenting, joined by Scalia and Thomas.** Section 6201 authorizes assessment of the tax Congress imposed; it does not replace the employee-based taxable wages specified by Section 3121(q) with restaurant-wide receipts. The monthly exclusion, annual bases and notice-and-demand structure require determinations tied to the recipient before the employer's liability is computed from them. Estimates of particular employees' taxable wages remain possible. The dissent reserves whether a formal employee audit must precede the employer action; it does not adopt a collection or benefits-credit prerequisite. Accurate aggregate factual estimates do not resolve the disputed legal method, so the restaurant's stipulation does not concede authority. Cleveland Indians separates the applicable annual tax limits from other functions, while United Dominion's aggregation followed that different statutory scheme. The dissent would affirm the refund judgment on the employee-based determination ground, without finding numerical error or an abusive motive.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns for entry of a judgment implementing the statutory holding and the parties' stipulation governing the refund and counterclaim. The conceded factual accuracy is not reopened. No independent employee liability, benefit-credit allocation, exact tax balance, penalty or interest amount is adjudicated in this employer case.
