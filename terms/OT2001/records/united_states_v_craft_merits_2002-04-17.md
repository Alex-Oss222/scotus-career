### Competing rules and remedies

O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer would reverse the Sixth Circuit's attachment ruling and remand. Stevens, Scalia and Thomas would affirm that ruling. All eight preserve the distinction among attachment, valuation, priority, sale authority, nondebtor ownership and the excluded fraudulent-transfer theory.

**Attachment rule supported by five.** After demand and nonpayment, §6321 reaches a taxpayer's existing state-created rights that together amount to property or rights to property, even when the State protects the marital estate from ordinary separate creditors and requires the other spouse's consent to disposition. Michigan gives this taxpayer present use and enjoyment, exclusion of strangers, survivorship and a necessary role in a joint conveyance and its proceeds. Federal classification is not controlled by the state-law label that the marital estate is indivisible. No one of those incidents is made a universal sufficient test; their existing economic substance together supports attachment. The lien reaches the taxpayer's rights before the conveyance to his wife. The absence of unilateral alienation limits the interest and its possible value, but does not erase its present benefits.

Drye applies the state-definition/federal-classification division. National Bank of Commerce illustrates that local creditor restrictions do not settle the federal lien question; Bess separates the reachable economic right from other interests in the same arrangement. Drye's unilateral accept-or-redirect power is powerful evidence of property, not an exclusive requirement silently added to §6321. Rodgers concerns enforcement and the nondebtor's protections; it supplies neither a half-value rule nor permission to take the wife's interest. Romani preserves specific priority rules after attachment and is not an entireties exemption.

**No-attachment rule supported by three.** Section 6321 attaches property belonging to the delinquent person; it does not create an individual property estate from rights belonging to an inseparable marital ownership. On Michigan's terms the husband cannot unilaterally withdraw, sell, partition or redirect the estate. Drye and National Bank of Commerce concerned actual unilateral powers over value already held by the taxpayer. Joint enjoyment and a veto on conveyance do not establish such separate ownership here. Federal law may disregard a creditor exemption after identifying a taxpayer's property, but may not manufacture the antecedent individual asset. This ground would affirm the reviewed attachment judgment without deciding what another tenancy, a completed severance or a separately owned income right would entail.

**Alternative-path testing.** The attachment path's strongest difficulty is identifying a real individual interest without collecting what belongs to the wife. Its answer is a lien on the existing bundle only, with separate valuation and statutory enforcement safeguards; no automatic half-share is recognized. The no-attachment path's strongest difficulty is that the taxpayer enjoys substantial present economic benefits and can prevent their disposition, so treating the state unity label as dispositive risks letting state collection policy defeat federal classification. Its answer must rest on Michigan's actual inseparability and lack of unilateral extraction, not on the bare immunity of the property from ordinary creditors. The three no-attachment Justices retain that narrower account; they do not deny every restricted or nontransferable interest can be federal property.

## Law After Decision

Michigan entireties restrictions do not defeat federal attachment to the taxpayer's existing beneficial rights considered together. The lien follows those actual rights, while their valuation, competing priorities, enforcement authority and the nondebtor spouse's independent interests retain separate legal work. The rule does not turn a bare future expectancy into property or assume identical marital-property law in every State.

## Separate Writings

**Thomas, dissenting, joined by Stevens and Scalia.** Section 6321 reaches property belonging to the taxpayer; it cannot create a distinct individual estate from inseparable marital ownership. Michigan gives the husband neither unilateral withdrawal nor a power to sell, partition or redirect the estate. Drye and National Bank of Commerce involved those actual powers over value, and therefore do not settle this case. Joint enjoyment and a veto on conveyance are meaningful benefits, but remain incidents of the marital entity's ownership rather than separately owned assets. The longstanding collection treatment supports this statutory caution; it does not replace the inquiry. The dissent does not make a creditor-exemption label conclusive or declare all restricted, nontransferable interests unreachable. It would affirm the attachment ruling without deciding a different tenancy, completed severance or separate income right.

**Scalia, dissenting, joined by Thomas.** Entireties ownership also gives institutional security to the nondebtor spouse, including a spouse whose domestic contribution yields no separate income. Removing that protection through a new federal property classification counsels particular statutory caution. This additional ground neither finds that Mrs. Craft held that domestic role nor creates a general marital-assets exemption. Stevens joins the common statutory dissent, but not this additional writing.
