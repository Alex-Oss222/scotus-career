## Controlling holdings

**I. Apparent minority does not replace exploitation or obscenity.** A criminal definition substantially reaching nonobscene depictions produced without exploiting an actual child cannot be sustained merely because the depicted person appears to be a minor. Section 2256(8)(B)'s restriction fails the narrow tailoring required for this content-based burden on protected expression, including its wholly virtual realistic and actual-adult applications.

**Authority:** Parts I and II of Justice Ginsburg's Opinion of the Court are joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer. Those six directly support both rules and the statutory qualifications below.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Ferber's exclusion rests on injuries to actual children in production and circulation; visual resemblance creates neither injury by itself. Miller's independent obscenity elements remain necessary to that category. Reno and Playboy require the Government to justify this protected-speech burden, while Broadrick requires substantial protected reach relative to legitimate applications. The asserted grooming, demand and proof difficulties are serious interests, but they do not establish the fit required for this prohibition. A lawful image's possible misuse cannot itself supply production abuse, and difficulty proving an actual child does not permit suppression of substantial protected substitutes. A realistic-image construction does not cure that defect or supply the absent serious-value protection. Actual exploitation, obscenity and independently unlawful solicitation retain their own predicates.

**II. Presentation does not transform a protected work into contraband.** Section 2256(8)(D) is independently substantially overbroad because promotional presentation can make a nonobscene, nonexploitative work prohibited material in connected distribution and possession offenses, including for persons unconnected with the presentation. A court may not save this definition by rewriting it as a separately defined intentional unlawful-offer offense.

**Authority:** The same six directly join Part II and this independent invalidity ground.

**Controlling explanation:** Ginzburg makes pandering evidence relevant within an obscenity inquiry; it does not automatically remove protection from the underlying nonobscene work. The enacted definition is broader than the Government's proposed knowing-promoter construction, and ordinary scienter does not confine all connected liability to the original promoter. Reno permits genuine severance but not invention of absent offense limits. A differently enacted unlawful-offer offense lies outside the question, as does the validity of the identifiable-actual-minor definition in (C).

**Operative statutory qualifications.** A minor is under eighteen. Sexually explicit conduct covers actual or simulated genital-genital, oral-genital, anal-genital or oral-anal intercourse, whether between persons of the same or opposite sex, bestiality, masturbation, sadistic or masochistic abuse and lascivious exhibition of the genitals or pubic area; mere nudity does not itself establish the last category. Section 2252A(c) applies only to (a)(1)–(4), requires actual adult performers at production, and requires that the defendant did not advertise, promote, present, describe or distribute the work as depicting minors. It excludes wholly generated images and ordinary possession under (a)(5). Section 2252A(d), separately, concerns possession of fewer than three images: prompt, good-faith reasonable destruction steps or reporting the images to law enforcement and affording access to each image, without retaining any image or copy or permitting anyone other than law enforcement access to one. It requires reasonable destruction steps, not successful destruction, and provides no right to retain protected expression. The unchallenged (C) definition concerns a recognizable actual person who was a minor at the relevant creation, adaptation or modification or whose image as a minor was used; proof of the person's actual identity is unnecessary. These provisions do not cure the challenged protected reach.

## Precedent Treatment

- **New York v. Ferber, 458 U.S. 747, and Osborne v. Ohio, 495 U.S. 103:** Their actual-child injury predicates are preserved; they are not extended to an invented image or an adult performance solely because it resembles a child.
- **Miller v. California, 413 U.S. 15:** Obscenity remains an independent category with its own elements, not a substitute apparent-age rule.
- **Reno v. American Civil Liberties Union:** Its protected-adult-expression and substantial-overbreadth reasoning applies; its distinction between genuine severance and judicial rewriting defeats the proposed new offense limits.
- **United States v. Playboy Entertainment Group, Inc.:** Its content-based scrutiny and governmental justification burden govern the fit of the challenged criminal restrictions.
- **United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc.:** Knowledge of depiction characteristics remains distinct from constitutional authority to suppress those characteristics; this case changes no surviving factual-knowledge requirement.
- **Broadrick v. Oklahoma, 413 U.S. 601:** Substantial protected reach, rather than a conceivable unusual application, supports facial relief here.
- **Ginzburg v. United States, 383 U.S. 463:** Pandering may inform an obscenity inquiry; it does not independently make a protected nonobscene work contraband for all possessors.

## Law After Decision

The two challenged definitions cannot support enforcement against the substantial protected expression they cover. Apparent minority without exploitation or obscenity does not suffice, and promotional characterization does not automatically transform a protected work into prohibited material. Independent actual-child, obscenity and unlawful-conduct rules retain their predicates. The identifiable-actual-minor category and a differently enacted unlawful-offer offense are outside this decision.

## Separate Writings

**Thomas, concurring in the judgment.** Thomas agrees with both invalidations but does not join the Court's principal reasoning. Enforcement of actual-child prohibitions is the Government's strongest concern. The present predictive showing does not establish a technological proof obstacle sufficient for this breadth, but practical impossibility of distinguishing fabricated from actual-child images could present a different constitutional question. An appropriate affirmative defense or another narrowly drawn restriction might matter on that demonstrated premise. He neither requires a particular future defense nor approves shifting the actual-child element through a current presumption. His agreement in judgment does not endorse a categorical foreclosure of every enforcement-based restriction affecting protected expression.

**O'Connor, concurring in the judgment in part and dissenting in part; Scalia joins Part II of her writing only.** In Part I she agrees with invalidation of the youthful-adult application: adult performers' protected expression remains exposed because the limited adult defense does not reach ordinary possession. She separately agrees that (D)'s presentation category lacks an adequately justified compelling fit, particularly when describing adult performers as minors removes the defense. In Part II she would preserve (B) for wholly virtual images virtually indistinguishable from actual-child images. The narrow construction excludes cartoons, sculptures and plainly unreal depictions. Congress may respond to supported enforcement predictions before actual proof failure becomes universal; in her view the child-protection interest and limited application satisfy the constitutional inquiry. Broadrick and application-severability principles permit preserving that application without extending the adult defense to possession. Her vagueness conclusion is confined to the realistic-image construction. Scalia joins only this virtual-image sustaining ground, not her adult or presentation invalidations.

**Scalia, dissenting.** Scalia would sustain both definitions under narrowing constructions. The statutory conduct requirement reaches actual or realistically simulated hardcore sexual acts, not merely a film's subject matter, and apparent minority means realistic images virtually indistinguishable from depictions of actual children. He adopts O'Connor's narrow virtual-image enforcement rationale but does not accept her adult-application invalidation. For (D), he reads offense scienter together with the definition to confine liability to knowing pandering by the responsible actor, excluding innocent downstream possession. Ginzburg supports the relevance of deliberate presentation to this confined transaction in his view. The Court regards that reading as alteration of a definition used in connected offenses and finds the remaining protected reach substantial. Scalia does not treat all nonobscene adult expression as unprotected or enlarge §2252A(c)'s defense.
