## Event, chronology and entering law

United States v. Drayton, No. 01-631. Argued April 16, 2002; merits decision June 17, 2002, on certiorari to the Eleventh Circuit's reversal of suppression denials. The accepted question is whether the February 4, 1999 coordinated drug-and-weapons encounter aboard the bus effected a seizure before the searches. Three officers boarded after the driver checked tickets and left: one observed from the driver's area while two proceeded from the rear. Brown agreed to a bag search and patdown and was arrested; Drayton then underwent a patdown. Brown entered a conditional plea; Drayton was convicted after trial. Independent consent voluntariness and a Terry alternative are outside the question reviewed.

## Controlling holdings

### I. Objective freedom to decline governs the bus encounter

**Holding and operative rule:** Police questioning aboard a bus is a seizure only when, considering the whole objectively communicated encounter, a reasonable passenger would not feel free to decline the requests or terminate the interaction. Constraints arising from the passenger's decision to travel are not themselves police restraint; absence of a refusal warning is relevant but neither necessary nor sufficient to establish seizure.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Part I, joined by all eight other Justices; nine direct votes for the governing framework, with a divided application.

**Controlling explanation:** Bostick adjusts the ordinary freedom-to-leave inquiry to a passenger whose own travel choice already limits movement. The inquiry concerns police-created restraint, not whether a person could conveniently abandon the trip. Robinette rejects an indispensable warning while retaining examination of actual communicated authority. Physical obstruction is not necessary to a seizure, and quiet requests do not prove its absence. Number, position, words and conduct must be considered together. An officer's undisclosed willingness to accept refusal is not an objective assurance, just as an undisclosed coercive purpose would not establish detention. Neither the bus setting nor one isolated circumstance controls.

**Precedent treatment:** Bostick and Robinette are applied without creating a mandatory warning or categorical bus rule; Bond's protection against additional physical intrusion remains distinct.

### II. Neither respondent was seized before the search

**Holding and operative rule:** The coordinated encounter did not objectively compel either Brown or Drayton to cooperate before the challenged searches: the officers used quiet requests, concealed their weapons, left the aisle passable, gave no command to remain and exercised no control over the passengers' tickets. Brown's arrest was relevant to the later request to Drayton but did not, in the circumstances of that request, itself detain him; the seizure-based suppression judgment is reversed.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Part II, joined by the other four Court-application Justices identified in Opinion Topology; five direct votes. Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg dissent from this application and disposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The officer observing from the driver's area and the colleagues approaching from the rear presented coordinated authority. The Court considers that combined arrangement rather than tallying isolated absences of coercion. It nevertheless conveyed requests capable of refusal: the observer did not block passage, the questioning officer spoke conversationally, weapons remained concealed and the officers did not direct passengers to stay or surrender tickets. The driver, not the police, handled the tickets. An announced drug-and-weapons purpose did not transform those requests into commands.

Brown's arrest after his patdown changed the setting for Drayton, but neither the arrest nor the other officers' positions communicated that Drayton was detained. Cooperation remains a factual circumstance, not a substitute for the objective legal judgment. The Court does not infer freedom from an officer's uncommunicated intent or use the discovered drugs to justify earlier conduct.

The accepted question concerns seizure. Finding no seizure removes the antecedent unlawful-detention ground for suppression but does not decide independent consent voluntariness or an unpresented Terry justification. Arvizu preserves that separation of stop and search questions. The respondents' distinct conviction postures and any independently preserved issues remain for lawful further proceedings.

**Precedent treatment:** Bostick's contextual rule is applied to each respondent; Arvizu preserves the separate consent inquiry. Knights supplies no advance passenger authorization or suspicion for this encounter.
