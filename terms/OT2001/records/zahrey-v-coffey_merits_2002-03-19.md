### I. Deliberate corruption of the process causing confinement violates due process

**Holding and operative rule:** An investigating official violates due process by knowingly creating materially false evidence for use in legal proceedings and thereby causing an unjustified deprivation of physical liberty. The rule requires the official's own deliberate falsification, materiality and actual causal deprivation; a false statement alone, mistake, disputed inference, innocent receipt of another's falsehood, acquittal or unused fabricated evidence does not suffice.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that government may not deliberately corrupt the evidentiary process it uses to imprison a person. Mooney, Pyle and Napue establish the protection against government procurement of a liberty deprivation through known falsehood. Their principle applies to the actual confinement allegedly procured here, even though Zahrey was eventually acquitted. The injury is the loss of liberty caused by the false process, not the existence of an inaccurate statement or an unsuccessful prosecution.

The complaint alleges Coffey's personal participation in manufacturing accusations intended for use against Zahrey, their use and eight months of confinement. That favorable account supplies the required link at the pleading stage. An alternative account that Coffey innocently received other officials' false information would not establish his deliberate wrongdoing; neither association nor pooled knowledge supplies the missing personal element.

Albright's fractured judgment on a claimed substantive right against prosecution without probable cause supplies no majority rule extinguishing this independent procedural injury. The Court does not recognize a general substantive right to be free of malicious prosecution or turn every inaccurate probable-cause assessment into a due-process claim. Nor does it decide an independently presented Fourth Amendment claim or when that Amendment's protection ends: those questions are outside the granted due-process issue. The narrow protection here concerns purposeful material corruption of the process that actually procures otherwise unjustified confinement. Buckley's denial of absolute immunity by itself would not establish this constitutional injury; the additional elements do that work.

### II. The same official's later advocacy does not automatically erase the earlier causal act

**Holding and operative rule:** An official's later protected advocacy does not retrospectively confer absolute immunity on his own earlier investigative fabrication or, merely because he changes roles, necessarily sever that act's causal connection to the resulting confinement. The claimant must prove material causal responsibility for the actual deprivation; a truthful, independently sufficient basis for the same custody or a genuinely independent, untainted intervening decision may defeat causation.

**Controlling explanation:** Buckley classifies investigation and advocacy separately, and Fletcher preserves the same separation for personal evidence and legal presentation. An official cannot make his investigative manufacture immune simply by personally presenting its product. Nor is that change of function itself an independent judgment that necessarily breaks causation. On the pleaded account, Coffey created the material for the use that allegedly produced Zahrey's confinement; the Court does not infer the causal connection solely from foreseeability or a prosecutorial title.

Franks and Malley show why legal process does not automatically insulate the person who procured it through material deception. Fletcher and Clanton preserve the separate need for personal responsibility, materiality and injury: a truthful account may still justify the same custody, and the claimant must meet the causal requirements rather than rely on the mere presence of false evidence. The complaint's favorable allegations cannot be rejected by assuming such an independent truthful basis at dismissal.

Coffey conceded investigative capacity only for this appeal. The actual functions remain for adjudication on remand; he may seek absolute immunity on that record. Zahrey concedes protection for the grand-jury and bail advocacy, which remains protected. This holding withdraws no advocacy immunity and adopts no rule that every foreseeable third-party intervention preserves causation. The same-person chain alleged here is sufficient to reject the categorical interruption theory.

### III. The alleged knowing procurement of detention was sufficiently clearly unlawful in 1996

**Holding and operative rule:** On allegations that an investigating official deliberately manufactured material evidence to procure the claimant's actual unjustified confinement and personally carried out that intended use, qualified immunity does not require dismissal under the law existing in 1996. This conclusion preserves the ordinary defense on materially different facts and requires proof of the official's own deliberate conduct, materiality, causal deprivation and injury under the applicable civil rules.

**Controlling explanation:** Harlow and Anderson require objective, particularized conduct-date notice, not a prior case with every feature repeated. By 1996, Mooney, Pyle and Napue established the deliberate process-deception principle; Franks and Malley made material official deception causing detention constitutionally significant and denied automatic insulation by judicial process. White v. Frank and Jones v. City of Chicago reinforced that an official's own corrupt procurement of confinement could not be detached from its intended consequence.

But the asserted uncertainty does not encompass a lawful entitlement deliberately to manufacture the material premise of process used to imprison the target. Due process protects against an investigator's deliberate material corruption of the evidentiary process that actually causes unjustified confinement. The same official's subsequent protected use does not automatically erase his earlier actionable causal act. On the stated deliberate creation, intended use and actual deprivation allegations, the personal defense fails under 1996 law. Actual function and the ordinary elements, causation, injury and supported defenses remain for adjudication. The rule creates no liability for fabrication alone, no general malicious-prosecution entitlement and no exception to absolute immunity for advocacy. ## Separate Writings

Justice Scalia dissents, joined by Justice Thomas. They would reverse the reinstatement of the reviewed personal damages claim because the asserted due-process boundary was not sufficiently clear in 1996. They do not decide that deliberate fabrication can never violate the Constitution or that a properly presented Fourth Amendment claim necessarily fails.

Mooney, Pyle and Napue concern deliberate falsehood in the adjudication of guilt. Franks and Malley identify a specific seizure-based protection against material deception. Those authorities forcefully condemn the alleged conduct, but the present theory combines investigative fabrication, pretrial rather than postconviction custody, and the same official's later absolutely protected advocacy. Buckley separated immunity from an actionable constitutional injury and expressly reserved the latter. Albright produced distinct approaches to the constitutional source of a prosecution-related injury, not a settled general due-process rule.

The adverse decision on remand in Buckley, 20 F.3d 789, and Rhodes, 939 F.Supp. 1256, demonstrate substantive uncertainty about a closely related theory. The objection is therefore more than the absence of a factual twin. Anderson requires particularized legal clarity; neither the moral wrongfulness of lying nor a later explanation of why the claim should succeed supplies that clarity at the conduct date. The dissenters would not make every circuit disagreement sufficient for immunity, but find this combination of source, injury and capacity uncertainties material. Later Fletcher and Clanton cannot settle the question retrospectively.

Both Justices accept that title and subsequent advocacy do not transform a conceded investigative act into protected advocacy. They also preserve the distinction between lack of absolute immunity and constitutional or damages liability. Their proposed judgment rests on qualified immunity for this pleaded theory, leaves the ultimate scope of a properly presented constitutional claim open, and supplies neither approval of the alleged misconduct nor an extension of judgment to other defendants or excluded claims.
