## Controlling holdings

### Serious functional difficulty, rather than total incapacity, limits this civil commitment

**Holding and operative rule.** Due process permits this form of civil commitment only upon proof of a serious present functional impairment producing serious difficulty controlling the dangerous conduct and substantially connected to the qualifying mental condition and demonstrated danger. Complete inability to control every act is unnecessary, but diagnosis and predicted recidivism alone do not distinguish an eligible person from a dangerous ordinary recidivist; Kansas must establish the required functional connection under its beyond-a-reasonable-doubt burden.

**Authority.** Breyer's opinion speaks for Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplying seven votes for the full rule and its application remand.

**Controlling explanation.** Civil confinement needs a substantive basis different from a prediction that a person will commit another crime. Foucha supplies that requirement of lawful present predicates rather than danger alone. A genuine disorder must produce sufficiently serious difficulty controlling the conduct that makes this custody permissible. The dissent's disorder-and-danger formulation does not adequately express that functional boundary when diagnosis and prediction could include ordinary repeated crime. At the other extreme, complete incapacity would exclude serious impairment merely because some capacity to choose remains. Neither liberty's protection nor psychiatric uncertainty requires that unrealistic line. The condition's nature and severity and evidence of actual impairment must be assessed together; clinical categories inform the legal finding without deciding it. Addington recognizes uncertainty in psychiatric judgments but supplies no reduction in Kansas's more protective burden. No mathematical measure, compulsory clinical label, involuntariness of every act or universal jury-instruction formula is required. Personality disorder is neither automatically sufficient nor automatically disqualifying. Hendricks's controlling punishment judgment concerned its own application; its non-dispositive due-process discussion did not settle today's rule. Seling likewise keeps actual punitive operation distinct from substantive civil eligibility. On remand the Kansas courts must evaluate the verdict and evidence under the stated rule. A verdict framed only in statutory diagnosis and likely repetition does not necessarily establish the necessary impairment.

**Limits and questions not reached.** The functional showing remains required for this form of commitment regardless of whether the condition is described as emotional, cognitive or volitional. The Court classifies no particular emotional diagnosis as sufficient or insufficient; clinical terminology does not replace the required functional assessment. A new punishment adjudication, facial redesign of commitment, periodic-review procedures and treatment or alternative-placement requirements lie outside the grant. A preserved operational punishment objection requires Crane-specific facts and the independent Hendricks-Seling inquiry.
