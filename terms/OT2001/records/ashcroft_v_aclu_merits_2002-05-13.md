### Community standards alone do not establish COPA's facial invalidity

**Holding and operative rule.** COPA's use of contemporary community standards does not, standing alone, establish facial invalidity: constitutional review must assess the complete offense, its limiting elements and defenses, and its actual burden on protected expression together. This rejection of automatic invalidity supplies neither a presumption that COPA survives nor a holding that varying local standards are constitutional in every application to the Web.

**Authority.** Part II of Kennedy's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg, supplies seven direct votes for this confined rule and explanation. Part III's vacatur, remand and preservation of the injunction also receive Breyer's join. The Court adopts no national-community construction and uses no narrowest-grounds aggregation.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the First Amendment inquiry concerns the enacted restriction as a whole. Reno protects adult Internet expression against substantially overbroad criminal restrictions; it does not turn every reference to community standards in a different law into an independent constitutional defect. COPA contains commercial-coverage, whole-material, prurience, specified-content and serious-value limits that must do their actual legal work. Miller supplies the obscenity framework, and Pope separates serious value from local acceptance. Their relevance does not establish that material lacking serious value for minors is unprotected for adults.

The challengers' strongest objection rests on the existing finding that Web speakers cannot exclude visitors geographically: a restrictive community may consequently burden expression nationwide. The Court preserves that fact and the verification burdens on cost, anonymity and access. But geographic variation alone does not determine the protected reach of all the statutory requirements operating together. Nor does the presence of limiting elements establish that their combined operation satisfies the Constitution. Substantial overbreadth requires examination of protected expression in relation to the statute's legitimate reach, not isolation of one phrase.

Reno and Playboy continue to require justification of the actual protected-adult-speech burden, including consideration of concrete, plausibly effective, less restrictive means. The statutory commercial-purpose label does not reduce literary or other protected expression to advertising. Each defense, provider exclusion and privacy provision retains its own conditions. Free Speech Coalition distinguishes ordinary construction from judicial addition of a new offense limitation. The Court therefore neither mandates a national standard nor finally approves local standards. ## Event

Ashcroft v. American Civil Liberties Union, No. 00-1293, 535 U.S. 564. Argued November 28, 2001; decided May 13, 2002. October Term 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Third Circuit under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1), reviewing 217 F.3d 162. The Third Circuit affirmed the February 1, 1999 preliminary injunction against the Child Online Protection Act on the ground that its community-standards feature independently made the statute facially invalid. The question is whether that feature alone supports the appellate disposition; other grounds for the preliminary injunction were not adjudicated by that rationale.

COPA, 47 U.S.C. §231, reaches knowing commercial World Wide Web communications, made with knowledge of the material's character in interstate or foreign commerce, available to any person under seventeen and containing material harmful to minors. Commercial purpose requires a regular business course with a profit objective and knowing posting or solicitation of the material; actual profit and a sole or principal business are unnecessary. This statutory category does not make protected expression commercial advertising. Harmful material includes obscenity or material satisfying all three requirements: taken as a whole and with respect to minors, prurient appeal or pandering judged by the average person applying contemporary community standards; patently offensive depictions, descriptions or representations of sexual acts or contacts, actual or simulated and including normal or perverted acts, or lewd displays of genitals or post-pubescent female breasts, with respect to minors; and, taken as a whole, lack of serious literary, artistic, political or scientific value for minors. The community-standards phrase appears expressly in the prurience clause.

Subsection (a) permits a fine up to $50,000, imprisonment up to six months, or both; intentional violations carry an additional fine up to $50,000 per violation, and the separate civil penalty is also up to $50,000 per violation. For the latter two, each day is a separate violation. Subsection (b)(1)–(3) excludes a telecommunications carrier to the extent it provides telecommunications service, an Internet-access-service provider in that capacity, and an information-location service in that capacity. Subsection (b)(4) separately protects intermediaries transmitting, storing, retrieving, hosting, formatting or translating another's communication without selecting or altering its content; deletion consistent with subsection (c) or §230 does not defeat that paragraph's protection. Subsection (c)(1) supplies a good-faith access-restriction defense through a credit card, debit account, adult access code or adult personal identification number, an age-verifying digital certificate, or other reasonable measures feasible with available technology. Subsection (c)(2) separately protects good-faith implementation or restriction activity against a cause of action only when that activity violates no law carrying a criminal or civil penalty. Subsection (d) separately requires protection against unauthorized access to collected information and prior written or electronic consent for disclosure, from the adult concerned or the parent or guardian of a person under seventeen. Disclosure is permitted when necessary to communicate, to conduct a legitimate related business activity, or under an authorizing court order.

The preliminary findings include speakers' inability to exclude Web visitors geographically and verification burdens affecting expense, anonymity and adult access. Those findings remain part of the inquiry; the Court does not substitute assumed future technology for the existing record.

## Separate Writings

**O'Connor, concurring.** She joins the confined holding because the present showing does not identify sufficient otherwise protected material made unlawful solely through community variation once prurience and serious value are considered. Pope's objective-value inquiry and Sable's distinction between an adult ban and confined protection matter, but the Web's inability to select recipients prevents treating conventional mail and telephone distribution as conclusive. She affirmatively regards a national standard as constitutionally permissible and necessary for reasonable Internet obscenity regulation. Her judgment neither forecloses an adequately supported facial challenge nor denies later as-applied relief.

**Thomas, concurring, joined by Scalia.** The prurience and objective serious-value requirements materially narrow the relevant category compared with the CDA. Miller, Pope, Hamling and Sable support rejecting community variation as a sufficient objection to this statutory category; the existing showing does not establish substantial variation in the covered material. This broader explanation would reject the community-standards objection more fully than the Court's common ground. It does not adopt a national-standard amendment, resolve COPA's other constitutional defects, or treat statutory commercial purpose as permission to suppress protected adult literature. They join continued interim protection while the other injunction grounds are considered.

**Souter, concurring, joined by Kennedy and Ginsburg.** The extent of protected expression cannot be measured until the statute's reach is determined. Commercial coverage, the relevant whole work or communication, the different ages within the under-seventeen category and venue consequences affect the relationship between legitimate regulation and protected expression. Reno, Playboy and Free Speech Coalition require attention to actual adult access, not an assumption that value for minors cures the adult burden. These Justices expressly preserve the community-standards challenge as part of the integrated inquiry: after construction, geographic variation may still prove decisive. Their join does not approve local standards as constitutionally harmless, depend on hypothetical geographic controls, or adopt a national standard.

**Breyer, concurring in part and in the judgment.** He joins the statutory background and disposition but reads community standards to mean the national adult community. The ordinary phrase in context, its legislative explanation and avoidance of serious constitutional difficulty support that construction in his view. A local standard would give restrictive communities practical control over protected speech distributed nationally. Variation among juries applying one national standard is different from variation among the governing substantive standards themselves. This construction is necessary to his agreement, not a merely optional possibility. It does not resolve the other COPA objections, and he agrees that the injunction should continue.

**Stevens, dissenting.** He would affirm the preliminary injunction on the community-standards ground. His objections in Smith and Sable, together with Reno's adult-access rule, apply to the established inability of national Web speakers to avoid restrictive local audiences. Serious value for minors does not define all expression adults may constitutionally receive. Narrowing the commercial category or the whole-work unit may reduce absolute coverage without curing substantial overbreadth relative to legitimate applications. Verification burdens do not erase that injury. He rejects insertion of a national standard into language with an established community-based meaning. His position preserves the legitimate interest in protecting children and ordinary restrictions on unprotected material; it does not require invalidating every future Internet safeguard.
