## Controlling holdings

### I. The bounded assigned-claim mechanism satisfies the Take Care Clause

**Holding and operative rule:** A relator may prosecute the False Claims Act's partially assigned federal pecuniary claim after initial governmental declination without violating the Take Care Clause, where the relator has the Act's case-specific claimant role and the government retains the actual intervention, dismissal, settlement, participation, discovery and alternate-remedy powers with the qualifications set out below. The holding rests on that combined structure, not a general authority to transfer executive functions to private persons, and initial declination does not constitute approval of every later litigating act.

**Authority:** Justice Breyer's opinion, joined throughout by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg, supplies seven direct votes for both constitutional holdings and their limits.

**Statutory conditions incorporated in this holding:**

| Sections 3730(b)(2)–(4) | The complaint and written disclosure of substantially all material evidence and information possessed by the relator must reach the government. Early qui tam practice informs the nature of that claimant's interest; neither that practice nor the civil label conclusively resolves executive responsibility.

The dissent correctly identifies a public interest beyond the relator's personal share: litigation may bind federal interests, and the Executive does not select, direct or remove the relator as an ordinary prosecutor. The statute nevertheless gives the government the first opportunity to take over and preserves meaningful later means of protecting the claim, investigation and enforcement interests. The table's conditions are part of those powers. A hearing requirement is not an unrestricted executive veto, and conditioned intervention is not at-will supervision. Those protections must be assessed with the relator's limited proprietary role rather than tested as though Congress had created a general prosecutor.

Morrison supplies a functional inquiry into interference with executive performance; its independent criminal office and supervisory arrangement are not identical to this assigned civil claim or a mandatory checklist for every claimant. The conditions also protect the assignee's existing procedural and financial interests and the court's adjudicative function. The arrangement does not disable the Executive's constitutional responsibility merely because it initially leaves litigation to the relator. No particular governmental dismissal, settlement, discovery or intervention request has been refused here. Whether a court may override a supported executive choice in such a later dispute requires its own record; that absent-facts question does not postpone the present decision about the statute's allocation.

### II. A case-specific relator is not a continuing federal officer

**Holding and operative rule:** A relator enforcing this partially assigned pecuniary claim under the False Claims Act is a private claimant with a defined litigation role, not the occupant of a continuing federal office requiring appointment under the Appointments Clause. The distinction turns on the position and actual functions: it is not established merely by the absence of salary, formal title, appointment or final adjudicative power, and it authorizes no private criminal prosecution, rulemaking or unrestricted sovereign coercive power.

**Authority:** The seven-Justice opinion identified above directly adopts this independent Appointments Clause holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that significant consequences for government interests do not turn every statutory civil claimant into a federal officeholder. Vermont Agency establishes a real financial assignment. The Act permits this claimant to seek a judicial remedy and a contingent personal share; it does not establish an enduring governmental position with continuing jurisdiction and general duties to adjudicate, regulate or prosecute public matters.

Freytag requires attention to significant statutory duties. Landry applies that principle to an actual continuing hearing office and rejects final-decision power as indispensable. Edmond and Hilario examine the combined hierarchy governing actual offices rather than making one factor universally decisive. Those cases do not eliminate the antecedent distinction between an office and a litigant. Morrison also shows why limited duration alone does not disprove officeholding: its independent counsel occupied a governmental office even though its jurisdiction and tenure were limited.

The dissent views litigation of the retained federal claim as governmental prosecution through a role continuing until judgment. That identifies the action's public consequence but does not establish a governmental position independent of the particular assigned claim. The relator's statutory status and party powers persist to enforce that claim, subject to the government controls described above. The office conclusion therefore rests on the entire function and structure, not on relabeling executive authority as private or on denying its importance. The two Article II inquiries are distinct; Article III standing resolves neither by itself.

### III. The rejection of the two constitutional dismissal grounds stands

The government remains a nonintervenor on the merits unless it lawfully exercises its intervention power, and Vermont Agency's sovereign-State defendant limitation remains applicable within its own scope.

**Authority:** The same seven Justices adopt the disposition and its statutory limits.

**Controlling explanation:** Both asserted constitutional grounds for dismissal fail under the assigned-claim structure presented. The allegations of health-care fraud remain allegations for ordinary litigation. The government's appellate defense of the statute does not become merits intervention, and rejection of Article II challenges does not establish statutory coverage of every defendant. Vermont Agency's distinct defendant-coverage rule survives. The appellate remand thus proceeds under the actual allocation of party powers rather than a judicially created governmental takeover.

On merits review of the Fifth Circuit's en banc judgment reversing dismissal of a False Claims Act action. The questions are whether a nonintervened relator prosecution violates the Take Care Clause and whether the relator must be appointed under the Appointments Clause. The government declined merits intervention and defended the statute on appeal. The health-care-fraud allegations remain allegations. Defendants seek constitutional dismissal; Riley seeks continuation of the action.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated at merits submission and decision: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

## Public Action

The en banc judgment rejecting the Take Care and Appointments Clause challenges is affirmed.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 7–2, on both the Take Care Clause and Appointments Clause components.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Scalia and Thomas.

The Fifth Circuit's reversal of dismissal and remand remain effective. The underlying action proceeds under the statute's actual allocation of party powers and ordinary statutory and merits requirements.

## Holdings

### I. The bounded assigned-claim mechanism satisfies the Take Care Clause

**Holding and operative rule:** A relator may prosecute the False Claims Act's partially assigned federal pecuniary claim after initial governmental declination without violating the Take Care Clause, where the relator has the Act's case-specific claimant role and the government retains the actual intervention, dismissal, settlement, participation, discovery and alternate-remedy powers with the qualifications set out below. The holding rests on that combined structure, not a general authority to transfer executive functions to private persons, and initial declination does not constitute approval of every later litigating act.

**Authority:** Justice Breyer's opinion, joined throughout by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg, supplies seven direct votes for both constitutional holdings and their limits.

**Statutory conditions incorporated in this holding:**

| Provision | Governmental power and controlling qualification |
|---|---|
| Section 3730(b)(1) | Voluntary dismissal requires written consent and reasons from both the court and the Attorney General. |
| Sections 3730(b)(2)–(4) | The complaint and written disclosure of substantially all material evidence and information possessed by the relator must reach the government. The complaint is filed in camera, remains sealed for at least sixty days, and is served on the defendant only when the court orders. The government may elect intervention within sixty days after receiving both the complaint and the material evidence and information; good cause supports extensions of the seal period. Before that period or an extension expires, the government must proceed and conduct the action or notify the court of declination, allowing the relator to conduct it. |
| Section 3730(c)(1) | If the government proceeds, it has primary responsibility and is not bound by the relator's acts; the relator remains a party subject to subsection (c)(2)'s particular limits. |
| Section 3730(c)(2)(A) | The government may dismiss over the relator's objection after notifying the relator of its motion and providing an opportunity for a hearing. The settlement standard in subparagraph (B) is not the dismissal standard. |
| Section 3730(c)(2)(B) | Settlement over objection requires a hearing and the court's determination that the settlement is fair, adequate and reasonable under all the circumstances. Good cause permits an in-camera hearing. |
| Section 3730(c)(2)(C) | Upon the government's showing that unrestricted relator participation would interfere with or unduly delay its prosecution, or would be repetitious, irrelevant or for harassment, the court may in its discretion limit participation, including witness numbers, testimony length or cross-examination. |
| Section 3730(c)(2)(D) | On a defendant's distinct showing that unrestricted participation would be for harassment or cause undue burden or unnecessary expense, the court may limit the relator's participation. |
| Section 3730(c)(3) | After declination, the relator conducts the case. At the government's request it receives all pleadings and deposition transcripts, with transcripts at its expense. The court may allow later government intervention for good cause without limiting the relator's status and rights. |
| Section 3730(c)(4) | Whether or not the government proceeds, an in-camera showing that particular discovery would interfere with a criminal or civil investigation or prosecution arising from the same facts permits a stay of that discovery for no more than sixty days. An extension requires a further in-camera showing of reasonable diligence in the governmental investigation or proceedings and interference from the proposed discovery. This is not an indefinite stay of the whole case for any policy reason. |
| Section 3730(c)(5) | The government may pursue an available alternate remedy, including administrative civil penalties, while preserving the relator's same rights in that proceeding. Findings or conclusions bind parties to the section 3730 action when finally determined on appeal to the appropriate federal court, when the time for such appeal expires, or when judicial review is unavailable. |

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this defined financial assignment and the government's retained protections permit the action. Vermont Agency establishes the relator's assigned financial injury for Article III but expressly leaves Article II unanswered. Early qui tam practice informs the nature of that claimant's interest; neither that practice nor the civil label conclusively resolves executive responsibility.

The dissent correctly identifies a public interest beyond the relator's personal share: litigation may bind federal interests, and the Executive does not select, direct or remove the relator as an ordinary prosecutor. The statute nevertheless gives the government the first opportunity to take over and preserves meaningful later means of protecting the claim, investigation and enforcement interests. The table's conditions are part of those powers. A hearing requirement is not an unrestricted executive veto, and conditioned intervention is not at-will supervision. Those protections must be assessed with the relator's limited proprietary role rather than tested as though Congress had created a general prosecutor.

Morrison supplies a functional inquiry into interference with executive performance; its independent criminal office and supervisory arrangement are not identical to this assigned civil claim or a mandatory checklist for every claimant. The conditions also protect the assignee's existing procedural and financial interests and the court's adjudicative function. The arrangement does not disable the Executive's constitutional responsibility merely because it initially leaves litigation to the relator. No particular governmental dismissal, settlement, discovery or intervention request has been refused here. Whether a court may override a supported executive choice in such a later dispute requires its own record; that absent-facts question does not postpone the present decision about the statute's allocation.

### II. A case-specific relator is not a continuing federal officer

**Holding and operative rule:** A relator enforcing this partially assigned pecuniary claim under the False Claims Act is a private claimant with a defined litigation role, not the occupant of a continuing federal office requiring appointment under the Appointments Clause. The distinction turns on the position and actual functions: it is not established merely by the absence of salary, formal title, appointment or final adjudicative power, and it authorizes no private criminal prosecution, rulemaking or unrestricted sovereign coercive power.

**Authority:** The seven-Justice opinion identified above directly adopts this independent Appointments Clause holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that significant consequences for government interests do not turn every statutory civil claimant into a federal officeholder. Vermont Agency establishes a real financial assignment. The Act permits this claimant to seek a judicial remedy and a contingent personal share; it does not establish an enduring governmental position with continuing jurisdiction and general duties to adjudicate, regulate or prosecute public matters.

Freytag requires attention to significant statutory duties. Landry applies that principle to an actual continuing hearing office and rejects final-decision power as indispensable. Edmond and Hilario examine the combined hierarchy governing actual offices rather than making one factor universally decisive. Those cases do not eliminate the antecedent distinction between an office and a litigant. Morrison also shows why limited duration alone does not disprove officeholding: its independent counsel occupied a governmental office even though its jurisdiction and tenure were limited.

The dissent views litigation of the retained federal claim as governmental prosecution through a role continuing until judgment. That identifies the action's public consequence but does not establish a governmental position independent of the particular assigned claim. The relator's statutory status and party powers persist to enforce that claim, subject to the government controls described above. The office conclusion therefore rests on the entire function and structure, not on relabeling executive authority as private or on denying its importance. The two Article II inquiries are distinct; Article III standing resolves neither by itself.

### III. The rejection of the two constitutional dismissal grounds stands

**Holding and operative rule:** The Fifth Circuit's judgment rejecting the Take Care and Appointments Clause challenges is affirmed, preserving its reversal of dismissal and remand for ordinary adjudication of the false-claims action under the applicable statutory and merits requirements. The government remains a nonintervenor on the merits unless it lawfully exercises its intervention power, and Vermont Agency's sovereign-State defendant limitation remains applicable within its own scope.

**Authority:** The same seven Justices adopt the disposition and its statutory limits.

**Controlling explanation:** Both asserted constitutional grounds for dismissal fail under the assigned-claim structure presented. The allegations of health-care fraud remain allegations for ordinary litigation. The government's appellate defense of the statute does not become merits intervention, and rejection of Article II challenges does not establish statutory coverage of every defendant. Vermont Agency's distinct defendant-coverage rule survives. The appellate remand thus proceeds under the actual allocation of party powers rather than a judicially created governmental takeover.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Vermont Agency of Natural Resources v. United States ex rel. Stevens, 529 U.S. 765:** Reaffirmed for assigned-claim standing and its distinct sovereign-State defendant limit; its express Article II reservation is resolved here for the bounded FCA mechanism, not retroactively attributed to that earlier judgment.
- **Morrison v. Olson, 487 U.S. 654:** Applied for the executive-interference inquiry and the distinction between a limited-duration public office and this claimant; its independent-counsel arrangement is not made a universal control checklist.
- **Freytag v. Commissioner, 501 U.S. 868:** Applied for the actual statutory position and significant-duty inquiry; no salary or final-decision shortcut replaces it.
- **Edmond v. United States, May 19, 1997:** Its actual combined-hierarchy approach to existing offices is preserved; the present threshold inquiry distinguishes a claimant from such an office.
- **United States v. Hilario, March 19, 2001:** Its combined-control and actual-office inquiries remain in force; a judicially appointed United States Attorney is not equated with a private assignee.
- **Landry v. FDIC, June 15, 2001:** Its continuing-office and significant-hearing-duty rule is preserved, including the rejection of final-order authority as indispensable; this civil claimant does not occupy that kind of position.

## Law After Decision

The FCA's bounded assigned-claim mechanism may constitutionally continue after initial governmental declination. Its private claimant does not occupy a continuing federal office, and the actual retained government powers, with their distinct statutory conditions, are sufficient for the arrangement presented. These Article II conclusions are separate from assigned-injury standing and statutory defendant coverage. They authorize no general private executive office and do not decide an actual judicial override of a supported government request absent from this record.

## Separate Writings

**Justice Scalia, dissenting, joined by Justice Thomas.** They would reverse on both Article II grounds and bar this nonintervened prosecution while preserving lawful governmental enforcement. A partial financial assignment does not eliminate the sovereign's retained claim, penalties, settlement interests or exposure to a binding judgment. In their view, initiation and prosecution of that public component exercise executive power through a statutory role continuing until the suit ends. The Executive neither selected this prosecutor nor possesses the ordinary chain of direction that execution of the laws requires.

Scalia's Morrison dissent supplies his executive-control premise; Vermont Agency's assignment-based standing opinion expressly reserves this distinct Article II question. His and Thomas's published Laidlaw concern about private public-law enforcement likewise distinguishes an injured claimant from uncontrolled governmental enforcement. Edmond, Hilario and Landry require attention to actual authority, appointment and supervision rather than final judgment power alone. The relator's financial incentive and title do not remove the significant public function. Early qui tam practice and a court-bound assigned claim are substantial contrary considerations, but they do not cure prosecution of the retained public interest without lawful appointment and constitutionally adequate direction.

The statutory safeguards are real but conditional. Later intervention requires good cause, dismissal requires notice and a hearing opportunity, settlement has its separate judicial standard, and discovery protection depends on its stated showing. These measures do not supply the missing selection and direction. The dissent does not demand presidential at-will removal of every inferior officer or decide every private statutory action. It would return for the procedural consequence of disallowing Riley's nonintervened prosecution, including any lawful governmental election, without invalidating section 3730(a), dismissing government claims with prejudice, presuming intervention or appointment, or treating the entire Act as inseverable.

## Procedure After Action

The Fifth Circuit's reversal of dismissal and remand stand. The action returns for ordinary adjudication of the claims and applicable defenses, subject to the actual statutory powers of the government and the relator's statutory limits. The government's appellate constitutional participation does not alter its nonintervenor merits status.
