## Event, chronology and entering law

Ticknor v. Choice Hotels International, Inc.; review of Ninth Circuit No. 00-35048, 265 F.3d 931, decided September 12, 2001. Merits decision, June 17, 2002. The Ninth Circuit affirmed denial of Choice's motion to compel arbitration. The clause requires Maryland arbitration and selects Maryland law but reserves three categories of Choice claims for court. The challenge is directed to that clause's enforceability under Montana conflicts and general contract law, not to the underlying franchise merits. The motel and principal performance were in Bozeman, Montana; the parties' out-of-state residences and the Mexico execution remain part of the record.

## Controlling holdings

### Montana law governs the challenged clause

**Holding and operative rule:** Under Montana's adopted choice-of-law rule, chosen Maryland law yields on this enforceability issue only if Montana law would govern absent the effective choice, Montana has a materially greater interest in the particular issue, and enforcement of the chosen law would contradict a fundamental Montana policy. Those requirements are met by this locally performed hotel franchise and the established conflict with Montana's general policy against the proved oppressive term.

**Authority:** Stevens's opinion of the Court, joined by the other four Justices identified in Opinion Topology; the same five adopt both holdings.

**Controlling explanation:** The relevant contacts include contracting, negotiation, performance, the location of the subject property and the parties. The motel and principal franchise performance were in Montana. Ticknor was a Colorado resident who signed in Mexico, and the guarantors lived in South Dakota; an invented common Montana domicile cannot support the result. Even with those details corrected, the local property and obligations establish Montana's materially greater interest in this issue and its law as otherwise applicable. Mere disagreement with Maryland law would be insufficient; the actual application conflicts with Montana's fundamental, generally applicable policy governing oppressive adhesion provisions. The forum's conflicts rule, not an arbitration-specific preference, supplies the basis for applying that law.

**Precedent treatment:** First Options preserves ordinary judicial decision and review of antecedent contractual enforceability; no federal choice-of-law rule replaces Montana's governing conflicts predicates.

### The FAA preserves the established general unconscionability defense

**Holding and operative rule:** FAA §2 covers this interstate commercial franchise agreement but preserves a genuinely general contract defense applied equally to arbitration and other terms. Under Montana law, adhesion alone does not establish invalidity: the provision must fall outside the weaker party's reasonable expectations or, even within them, be unduly oppressive, unconscionable or contrary to public policy; the combined nonnegotiability, imposed distant forum and reservation of Choice's principal enforcement avenues establish oppression here and sustain denial of compulsion.

**Authority:** The same five-Justice Court opinion adopts this complete general-defense application and affirmance.

**Controlling explanation:** Casarotto forbids a special disability for arbitration while preserving ordinary contract defenses. That equal treatment requires real examination of the defense, not immunity from it. The hearing-supported finding concerns nonnegotiation of this clause; later addenda prepared by Choice do not compel a contrary historical finding. Commercial experience and alternatives matter, but do not categorically defeat the State's general protection.

The agreement sends disputes to Maryland arbitration while reserving Choice's indemnification claims, collection of money owed under the agreement and injunctions against unauthorized trademark use for court. Choice must arbitrate other claims. The Court neither misdescribes the reservation as complete nor requires identical promises. It finds the practical reservation of principal enforcement tools oppressive in combination with formation and forum facts. The dissent's functional explanation for different carve-outs is substantial, but does not overcome that combined application.

Green Tree's focused cost-record inquiry does not create a universal burden or discovery entitlement here. An earlier voluntary arbitration demand did not amend the bargain. The court may decline this clause without rewriting it or deciding substantive franchise liability.
