## 65. Verizon Communications Inc. v. FCC, 535 U.S. 467 (2002)

Historical event: argued October 10, 2001; decided May 13, 2002. Dockets 00-511, 00-555, 00-587, 00-590, and 00-602. The Eighth Circuit judgment, 219 F.3d 744, was affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded. O'Connor did not participate.

|---|---|---|

| Part III, including TELRIC and the constitutional-avoidance discussion | Above five plus Scalia and Thomas | Seven join the Court's reasoning. Breyer does not join Part III. |
| Part IV, including rejection of the waiver objection and additional combination rules | Five general joiners plus Thomas | Six join. Scalia does not join Part IV. |

Do not turn the seven-Justice Part III joinder into a single undifferentiated seven-to-one vote on every proposition. Breyer expressly agrees that the Act does **not** require a historical-cost pricing system and that no taking has occurred at the present time. Those results therefore have agreement from all eight participating Justices, although Breyer does not join Souter's reasoning. The specific TELRIC methodology survives 7–1; the additional combination rules survive 6–2.

### Verified grounds and boundaries

The Court's Part III rejects a fixed historical-investment meaning of cost. Ordinary usage, regulatory usage, and the statute's just-and-reasonable-rate context leave methodological room; the prohibition of reference to a rate-of-return or other rate-based proceeding further defeats the claim that Congress clearly mandated the traditional cost base. Hope supplies methodological latitude; Duquesne shows that even historical-cost systems do not guarantee recovery of every expenditure. The Court does not hold that Congress expressly forbade every conceivable historical-cost consideration.

The Court applies Chevron to uphold the FCC's particular forward-looking methodology as reasonable. TELRIC values the most efficient currently available technology at the existing wire-center locations and includes common costs, operating expenses, depreciation, and a risk-adjusted capital return. The wire-center constraint, regulatory and development lags, current availability condition, and state commission discretion over depreciation and capital costs answer the claim that perfect-competition assumptions destroy investment incentives. The FCC reasonably rejected embedded costs, efficient-component pricing, and Ramsey alternatives; low access prices can permit entry while entrants construct other facilities. Reported investment was corroborative of reasonableness, not a standalone statutory command.

The constitutional point is a rejection of this general challenge and the requested constitutional-avoidance construction. The incumbents present no particular confiscatory TELRIC rate. Rates, rather than methodology in the abstract, ordinarily supply the constitutional issue. The Court does not establish an exceptionless rule barring any pre-rate challenge: it considers, but finds unsupported, the suggested inevitable-confiscation and opportunistic-switching grounds. Duquesne's possible concern about arbitrary switching does not control because the new wholesale market has no predecessor methodology, and no promise guaranteed historical-cost recovery. A later actual confiscatory rate remains a different question.

Part IV-A rejects the suggested waiver/mandate barrier. The appellate court itself revisited the additional rules, there was no shown tactical withholding, no obsolete isolated agency error, and no waste of years of proceedings of the sort at issue in Communist Party. A significant, passed-upon continuing issue with conflicting appellate treatment was within the Court's certiorari discretion.

Part IV-B distinguishes pricing unbundling from physical combination. Section 251(c)(3) leaves room to require the incumbent to assist in combining. The authority upheld is bounded: the requesting carrier cannot do the job itself; the incumbent performs the necessary functions, not necessarily every physical act; a reasonable cost-based fee is payable; technical feasibility includes network reliability, security, and the incumbent's retained management, control, and performance; other carriers' access must not be impaired. Rules 315(e)–(f) put the burden on the refusing incumbent to prove infeasibility or interference with others' access. Rule 315(c) covers combinations not ordinarily made, while Rule 315(d) connects leased elements with the entrant's elements. Nondiscriminatory access, the accepted nonseparation rule, and the interconnection obligation support these distinct applications.

Breyer agrees to broad agency leeway and judicial restraint but invokes State Farm's required connection between rules and statutory purposes. His objection is specific: simulating low-price perfect competition may encourage permanent sharing and discourage the independent building and innovation that the deregulatory statute seeks. He considers and rejects the FCC's responses concerning depreciation, capital returns, state and foreign experience, simulated competition, supposed flexibility, and regulatory lag. His remedy is to affirm invalidation of the pricing regulations, not to mandate historical-cost ratemaking. In Part VI, joined only by Scalia, he distinguishes preventing strategic disassembly from imposing new combinations: keeping elements apart that are ordinarily apart is not itself discrimination, and he finds no statutory authority to force such combinations.

### Verified grounds and boundaries

The five-Justice Court opinion holds only that reliance on community standards, by itself, does not render COPA substantially overbroad. It does not resolve other substantial-overbreadth grounds, vagueness, or strict scrutiny. The Government did not ask for vacatur of the preliminary injunction; that remedy also could not be ordered without addressing issues the court of appeals had not considered. Vacatur of the appellate judgment therefore does not authorize enforcement.

The common background is COPA's narrower coverage than the CDA: the Web rather than all Internet communication, commercial-purpose communications rather than all speakers, and a three-part harmful-to-minors definition rather than the CDA's broader formulation. The precise statute still matters: commercial purpose does not require actual profit or an exclusive/principal business; all three prongs of the non-obscene-material definition must be satisfied; the serious-value prong is evaluated objectively and for minors; affirmative defenses use good-faith access restrictions by specified credential methods, age-verifying digital certificate, or other reasonable measures feasible under available technology. These statutory features are described, not adjudged sufficient to make the entire law constitutional.

Thomas's Part III-B, with O'Connor but without Breyer, relies on the prurient-interest and objective serious-value restrictions to distinguish Reno and reduce geographic variation. Parts III-A/C/D, without O'Connor or Breyer, decline to decide hypothetical jury instructions; invoke Hamling and Sable to place the burden on nationwide disseminators; reject the contention that the Web's geography requires a different rule; and conclude the challengers have not shown substantial variation for covered material. Note 16 is material: this group views the absence of evidence as a failure of the facial challenge, expressly disagreeing with Kennedy's view that the community-standards issue should be revisited after fuller analysis. These grounds do not command five votes.

O'Connor joins I, II, III-B, and IV. She agrees the challengers have not supplied examples establishing substantial overbreadth due solely to community variation, given the restricted statutory category. She nevertheless regards a national standard as constitutionally permissible and necessary for reasonable Internet-obscenity regulation. Because Web speakers cannot select recipients geographically, Hamling and Sable do not settle future Internet challenges. Her judgment permits future as-applied challenges and better-supported facial challenges; it is not endorsement of local standards for every Internet restriction.

Breyer joins I, II, and IV and none of Part III. He reads the statutory community as the national adult community, using the committee report and constitutional avoidance. He regards local standards as creating a serious Internet veto problem; his agreement with some of III-B/D's reasoning is expressly limited to variation from different juries applying the same national standard. It is not a joinder in those portions or acceptance of materially differing local standards.

Kennedy, Souter, and Ginsburg agree to vacate because the appellate court treated geographic variation as dispositive without construing the statute's other dimensions. They do not agree that existing limits have already removed the community-standards problem. Scope of commercial-purpose communications, the unit of material taken as a whole, minors of differing ages, and venue bear on actual burden and on the ratio of protected speech to legitimate sweep. Internet recipients cannot readily be geographically excluded; the most restrictive locality can consequently burden national speech. The statutory construction and integrated overbreadth analysis belong to the lower courts first. They do not adopt Breyer's national construction, and they expressly leave open that community standards, considered with the statute as construed, may justify the injunction.

Stevens would affirm. Protected adult speech cannot be confined to what children may receive. Internet speakers lack the targeting options of mail and telephone distribution, and a least-tolerant community's standards can become nationally restrictive. Requiring serious value for minors still leaves protected adult expression within the statute. In his view, narrowing commercial-purpose or whole-work coverage changes absolute scope without curing the substantial relative overbreadth. His note 3 rejects reading national into the statute because community standards has an established meaning; notes 5–8 preserve the commercial-coverage and proportion-of-overbreadth qualifications. His dissent does not make all sexually explicit expression protected or disavow the compelling interest in protecting minors.

## 67. Lapides v. Board of Regents, 535 U.S. 613 (2002)

Historical event: docket 01-298; argued February 25, 2002; decided May 13, 2002. Breyer wrote for a unanimous Court. No separate opinion or nonparticipation appears. The official formal disposition is **reversed**, 251 F.3d 1372. The supplied comparator's phrase reversed and remanded should not obscure that precise wording or imply an order directing the district court to remand to state court.

The holding is confined to state-law claims for which Georgia had already waived state-court immunity. Joining removal voluntarily invokes federal jurisdiction and waives the otherwise valid federal-forum objection. Clark, Gardner, and Gunter supply the litigation-conduct principle; the rule avoids inconsistent positions and unfair tactical choices. The clarity inquiry concerns the removing act, not a State's subjective desire to waive. A benign motive to secure federal interlocutory review for individual defendants does not change the rule. Authorized removal has this federal-law consequence despite a claimed state-law limit on the attorney general's power to waive immunity.

College Savings Bank's rejection of constructive waiver is distinguished because litigation conduct is a different category. The official opinion overrules Ford to the extent it would otherwise apply, because Ford's state-authority approach is inconsistent with the voluntary-invocation line. United States and tribal-immunity cases do not resolve this Eleventh Amendment question; they involve different sovereign relationships or circumstances.

The only federal claim against the State sought money damages under section 1983. Will makes a State not a person for that claim. Counsel's assertion of declaratory relief did not match the complaint as the Court read it. The Court does not resolve removal waiver for an otherwise valid federal claim, nor for state-law claims on which underlying state-court immunity remains intact. The state claims remain pending, so the question is not moot; section 1367(c)(3) leaves the district court discretion whether to remand them to state court. That is permission/discretion, not a Supreme Court command to remand.

## 68. Voting Integrity Project, Inc. v. Keisling, 259 F.3d 1169 (9th Cir. 2001)

This is a lower-court comparator only. Kleinfeld wrote for Aldisert, Kleinfeld, and William Fletcher; the saved published opinion contains no dissent. The State prevailed on summary judgment, which the panel affirmed. This supplies neither a historical Supreme Court vote nor a Supreme Court authorship assignment.

The opinion recognizes substantial textual and legislative-history arguments for a single voting day, including Congress's rejection of a proposal to preserve multiday polling. It does not dismiss those arguments as frivolous. Foster's final-selection and consummation language favors Oregon because voting remains possible on the designated federal date, but the panel expressly observes that Foster decided a different question.

The decisive additional ground is harmonization with congressional authorization of absentee voting. The federal absentee provision requires opportunities for certain absent voters and expressly allows less restrictive state practices; the panel treats that permission as including more liberal absentee practices. Construing election as consummation reconciles both enactments. Oregon mails ballots 14–20 days before election day, permits earlier returns, requires receipt by election day, and retains election-day deposits and replacement-ballot mechanisms. The election therefore is not consummated earlier merely because many ballots are cast earlier. The panel takes no position on the policy desirability of mail voting.

The supplied comparator is accurate but compressed: Foster and the date statutes are central, yet the absentee-ballot statute is an independently material part of the panel's harmonizing explanation. Do not reduce the historical opinion to counting or announcing results on the federal date; the supplied opinion expressly relies on continuing voter activity that day. No further fact about an actual election's result or administrative tabulation was independently verified here.
