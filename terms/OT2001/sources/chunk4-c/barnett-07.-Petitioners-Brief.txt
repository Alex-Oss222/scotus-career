STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
1. Statutory Framework. 
The Americans with Disabili- 
ties Act (“ADA”), aims “to provide a clear and comprehen- 
sive national mandate for the elimination of discrimination 
against 
individuals 
with 
disabilities.” 
42 
USC. 
§ 12101(b)(1). 
This case involves Title I of the ADA, 42 
U.S.C. §§ 12111 ef. seg., which addresses discrimination in 
employment. 
The general rule in Title I states that “[njo 
covered 
entity” — 
including an “employer,” 42 U.S.C. 
§ 12111(2) — “shall discriminate against a qualified individ- 
ual with a disability because of the disability of such indi- 
vidual in regard to . . . [the] terms, conditions, and privileges 
of employment.” 42 U.S.C. § 12112(a). The question in this 
case is whether an employer unlawfully “discriminates” 
against a disabled employee within the meaning of Title I by 
refusing his reassignment request for a reason completely 
unrelated to his disability, viz., his lack of seniority with the 
company and the resulting entitlement of another, more sen- 
ior employee to hold the desired position under the terms of 
a longstanding company seniority policy. 
Title I enumerates several categories of prohibited “dis- 
crimination,” one of which is especially pertinent here: “not 
making reasonable accommodations to the known physical 
or mental limitations of an otherwise qualified individual 
with a disability who is an applicant or employee, unless [the 
employer] can demonstrate that the accommodation would 
3 
impose an undue hardship on the operation of [its] business.” 
42 U.S.C. § 12112(b)(5)(A). 
The central issues under that 
provision are whether the individual is “qualified,” whether 
the accommodation is “reasonable,” and whether providing 
the accommodation would entail an “undue hardship.” 
As to the first of those, a “qualified individual with a dis- 
ability” is “an individual with a disability who, with or with- 
out reasonable accommodation, can perform the essential 
functions of the employment position that [she] holds or de- 
sires.” Jd. § 12111(8). 
“Reasonable accommodation,” in turn, while not specifi- 
cally defined in the statute, is described as follows: 
The term ‘reasonable accommodation’ may in- 
clude — 
(A) making existing facilities used by employees 
readily accessible to and usable by individuals with 
disabilities; and 
(B) job restructuring, part-time or modified work 
schedules, reassignment to a vacant position, acquisi- 
tion or modification of equipment or devices, appro- 
priate adjustment or modifications of examinations, 
training materials or policies, the provision of quali- 
fied readers or interpreters, and other similar accom- 
modations for individuals with disabilities. 
Id. § 12111(9). 
Finally, “undue hardship” “means an action requiring 
significant difficulty or expense, when considered in light 
of” several enumerated factors. 
Jd. § 12111(10)(A). 
Those 
factors are: 
(i) the nature and cost of the accommodation 
needed under this Act; 
(ii) the overall financial resources of the facility 


PAGE 10
4 
or facilities involved in the provision of the reason- 
able accommodation; the number of persons em- 
ployed at such facility; the effect on expenses and re- 
sources, or the impact otherwise of such accommoda- 
tion upon the operation of the facility; 
(iii) the overall financial resources of the covered 
entity; the overall size of the business of a covered 
entity with respect to the number of its employees; 
the number, type, and location of its facilities; and 
(iv) the type of operation or operations of the 
covered entity, including the composition, structure, 
and functions of the workforce of such entity; the 
geographic separateness, administrative, or fiscal re- 
lationship of the facility or facilities in question to 
the covered entity. 
Id. § 12111(10)B). 
2. Factual Background. Respondent Robert Barnett was 
employed by petitioner US Airways, Inc. (“US Airways”) 
and its predecessor for ten years as a customer service agent. 
Pet. App. 2a. He injured his back in 1990 while working in a 
cargo position. Pet. App. 50a. Because he could no longer 
perform the physical requirements of handling cargo, he 
sought to transfer to another position by taking advantage of 
his seniority rights under the company’s seniority policy. /d. 
US Airways’ seniority policy has been in place for dec- 
ades, and it governs over 14,000 customer service agents. 
Pet. App. 96a. Under the terms of the policy, an employee’s 
level of seniority determines his rights concerning a number 
of important issues, including the order of furloughs and re- 
calls, displacements, bidding for shifts and days off, filling 
_of vacancies, and requests for position transfers. App. 24-27. 
Respondent used his seniority to transfer into a position in 
the mailroom, the functions of which were within his physi- 
cal capabilities. Pet. App. 2a. He held the position for two 
years. Pet. App. 80a. 
US Airways suffered significant financial losses in 1992, 
ultimately resulting in the layoffs of over 7,000 employees. 
App. 21. 
In connection with the layoffs, all of the com- 
pany’s cargo and mailroom position came open for bidding 
in August 1992. 
Pet. App. 50a. Respondent became aware 
that employees with greater seniority planned to exercise 
their nghts to transfer into the position in the mailroom. /d. 
In response, he requested that the company, as an accommo- 
dation under the ADA, make an exception to its seniority 
rules and allow him to hold the mailroom position notwith- 
standing the seniority rights of the other employees to the 
position. Jd. 
US Airways created an extra (and temporary) “limited 
duty” position in the mailroom for respondent while assess- 
ing his situation. Pet. App. 50a-Sla. While limited duty po- 
sitions usually expire after sixty days, the company permitted 
respondent to remain in the limited duty position for five 
months. Pet. App. 5la. Then, in January 1993, his supervi- 
sors informed him that they could no longer maintain the ex- 
tra, limited duty position. /d. Because respondent had insuf- 
ficient seniority for a regular position in the mailroom and 
because his back condition prevented him from performing 
the duties of the cargo position available to him under the 
seniority policy, he was placed on job injury leave. /d. The 
company decided not to override the seniority policy because 
doing so would deprive his coworkers of their legitimate 
right to hold a position in the mailroom, would trigger a rip- 
ple effect displacing the settled status of a number of addi- 
tional employees, and would involve the company in a sensi- 
tive and subjective balancing of competing claims among its 
employees. See App. 22. 
3. Proceedings Below. 
Respondent filed suit alleging 
that US Airways violated the ADA’s “reasonable accommo- 
dation” requirement by not making an exception to its sen- 


PAGE 11
6 
iority policy to permit him to hold a position in the mail- 
room.’ 
a. The district court granted summary judgment in favor 
of US Airways, concluding that modification of the seniority 
system was not a “reasonable accommodation” under the 
ADA. 
Pet. App. 97a. Such modifications, the court deter- 
mined, “could create significant disruptions in the seniority 
policy.” Jd. 
b. A panel of the Ninth Circuit affirmed. Pet. App. 41a. 
The panel was persuaded by “well-reasoned opinions from 
other circuits that have concluded that the ADA does not re- 
quire an employer to give disabled employees preference 
over non-disabled employees in hiring and reassignment de- 
cisions.” 
Pet. App. 59a. In the panel’s view, the ADA re- 
quires “equality among disabled and nondisabled employees 
in hiring and reassignment decisions,” not “discriminat{ion] 
against persons free from disability.” Pet. App. 60a (internal 
quotation marks omitted). 
c. The en banc Ninth Circuit reversed. According to the 
en banc court, “reassignment is a reasonable accommodation 
to which disabled employees should have priority over non- 
disabled employees,” Pet. App. 21a, and the existence of a 
seniority system does not affect that conclusion, Pet. App. 
27a. As a result, the court determined, an employer should 
have the burden of showing in a “case-by-case fact intensive 
analysis” that a “particular reassignment” in violation of a 
seniority policy “would constitute an undue hardship.” /d.

whether it calls for measures enabling disabled em-

Ill. REQUIRING AN EMPLOYER TO OVERRIDE 
THE TERMS OF AN ESTABLISHED SENIORITY 
POLICY WOULD IMPOSE AN UNDUE HARD- 
SHIP ON THE EMPLOYER’S OPERATIONS. 
Although we believe that respondent’s and the EEOC’s 
position concerning the relative burdens faced by an em- 
ployee and employer in a reasonable accommodation claim 
is in error, see supra § II.A, adopting their view would in no 
way determine the outcome of this case. 
Even if an em- 
ployce needs only to demonstrate that a requested accommo- 
dation would be “effective,” the consequences of the ac- 
commodation on the employer’s operations would still enter 
into the analysis as part of the employer’s effort to prove 
“undue hardship.” 
In particular, the employer would have 
the opportunity to establish that the accommodation would 
impose “significant difficulty or expense” when considered 
in light of the several factors enumerated in the statute, in- 
cluding “the impact” of the accommodation “upon the opera- 
tion of the facility.” 42 U.S.C. § 12111(10). 
For the same reasons that the accommodation sought by 
respondent is not a “reasonable” one under our understand- 
ing of the parties’ relative burdens, it would work an “undue 
hardship” under respondent’s understanding of the parties’ 
relative burdens. The undue hardship inquiry takes into ac- 
count not just the financial costs of the accommodation to 
the employer, but also the non-monetary implications of the 
accommodation for employment relations. 
See 29 C.F.R. 
§ 1630.2(p\(2)(v); id. pt. 1630 app. § 1630.2(p) (“concept of 
undue hardship is not limited to financial difficulty,” but also 
“refers to any accommodation that would be unduly costly, 
extensive, substantial, or disruptive”). 
Significantly, the 
analysis would encompass an assessment of whether “the 
provision of a particular accommodation would be unduly 
disruptive to ... other employees or to the functioning of 
[the employer’s] business.” 
Jd. pt. 1630 app. § 1630.15(d) 
43 
(emphasis added). 
An accommodation that disrupts the settled and legiti- 
mate expectations of “other employees” under a longstand- 
ing seniority policy would impose an undue hardship. 
See 
supra § I1.C. Indeed, whether this Court’s decision in Hard- 
ison and the uniform judicial interpretations of the regula- 
tions implementing § 504 of the Rehabilitation Act are con- 
sidered to fall within the “reasonable accommodation” or 
“undue hardship” prongs of the inquiry, the outcome remains 
the same: 
Congress is assumed to have incorporated those 
interpretations into the ADA’s reasonable accommodation 
requirement, with the result that employers are not obligated 
to reassign a disabled employee to a position in violation of a 
bona fide and established seniority policy. 
The Ninth Circuit below, relying on the EEOC’s En- 
forcement Guidance, took the position that displacing the 
nights and expectations of more senior employees under a 
seniority policy would not always work an undue hardship, 
and that a “case-by-case fact intensive analysis” of the ques- 
tion thus was necessary. 
Pet. App. 27a; see Enforcement 
Guidance 5463 n.115. That view is insupportable. 
First, it 
cannot be squared with the decisions adopting a clear rule 
under the Rehabilitation Act. 
Second, an employer faced 
with the prospect of having to prove undue hardship in a 
“case-by-case fact intensive analysis” would likely choose to 
avoid the risk by granting an exception to the operation of 
seniority rules; and that would encourage an increasing 
‘number of similar accommodation requests, eventually com- 
promising the integrity of the seniority system. 
Finally, the “case-by-case fact intensive analysis” pro- 
posed by the Ninth Circuit cannot be squared with Con- 
gress’s purposes in the Act. Congress specified in the text of 
the statute that it was “the purpose of” the ADA “to provide 
clear, strong, consistent, enforceable standards addressing 
discrimination against individuals with disabilities.”

The open-ended 
and indefinite

with those objectives. 
This Court instead should embrace 
the “clear,” “consistent,” and “enforceable” rule that was 
adopted by courts construing the Rehabilitation Act regula-
