operation of more than one adult entertainment business

adult arcade, invalid because the city did not study the 
negative effects of that particular combination of adult

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
A. 
The City Of Los Angeles Adult Entertainment Ordi- 
nance. 
Following this Court's decision in Young v. American 
Mini Theatres, Inc., 427 U.S. 50 (1976) (Young), the Los 
Angeles City Council instructed the Planning Department 
to undertake a study “to determine whether and to what 
extent the concentration of . 
. 
. adult businesses has a 
blighting and degrading effect upon the neighborhoods 
in which they are situated.” App. 60. The City Council 
allocated $25,000 for the study and “all other projects 
under study” were delayed for the three to four months 
the study would take to complete. JA? 170. 
In June, 1977, the Planning Department completed a 
comprehensive study entitled “Study of the Effects of the 
Concentration of Adult Entertainment Establishments in 
the City of Los Angeles”? (“1977 Study”). The 1977 Study 
“encompassed all forms of ‘adult entertainment.’ ” JA 58. 
The Police Department submitted a report* comparing 
the crime statistics for the Hollywood area to the crime 
statistics citywide for the years 1969-1975, where the 
number of adult businesses “proliferated from a mere 11 
establishments to an astonishing number of 88 such loca- 
tions.” JA 177. The Hollywood area reported significant 
increases over the citywide average for crimes (JA 175 
-176), arrests (JA 176) and deployment of police person- 
nel. JA 176-177. These “overwhelming” increases “are all 
representative of the blighting results that the clustering 
of Adult Entertainment Establishments has on the entire 
community.” JA 177. 
General questionnaires were mailed to businessmen 
and residents in the studied areas and a second question- 
naire was sent to real estate professionals. JA 99-111. Real 
estate appraisers indicated that the clusters of adult busi- 
nesses “had a direct negative impact on property values.”

SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT 
This case involves the evidentiary standard necessary 
to support a municipal zoning ordinance regulating the 
spacing of adult entertainment businesses. This is not 
“the first case in this Court in which the interests in free 
expression protected by the First and Fourteenth Amend- 
ments have been implicated by a municipality’s commer- 
cial zoning ordinance” as was Young. 427 U.S. at 76 


PAGE 12
12 
(Powell, J., concurring). It is, however, otherwise indis- 
tinguishable from that seminal decision. Moreover, the 
Ninth Circuit’s decision directly conflicts with this 
Court’s decision in Renton which, in reaffirming Young, 
rejected a rigid evidentiary standard of proof in favor of 
evidence which the municipality “reasonably believed to 
be relevant to the problem that the city addresses.” 
-475 
U.S. at 51-52. Further, Renton did not require a city to 
prove that a specific business or a combination of busi- 
nesses caused harmful secondary effects, as did the court 
of appeals, only that the “category” of adult businesses 
“produce the unwanted secondary effects.” 475 U.S. at 52. 
The often stated doctrine of this Court is that each 
forum of expression under the First Amendment is sui 
generis and properly analyzed by the case law applicable 
to that forum: 
“This Court has often faced the problem of 
applying the broad principles of the First 
Amendment to unique forums of expression. 
(Citations omitted.) Even a cursory reading of 
these opinions reveals that at times First 
Amendment values must yield to other societal 
interests. These cases support the cogency of

There is no requirement under Young and Renton that 
local officials stand by and wait until adult businesses 
become public nuisances before a City can regulate the 
undesirable impacts associated with those businesses. 
There was empirical evidence in the record before the 
City Council that concentrations of adult businesses 
cause unwanted secondary effects, and that those nega- 
tive impacts can be ameliorated by the application of 
sound planning and zoning principles. 
The case law, federal and state, is an instructive 
history of the deleterious impacts adult businesses have 
in the real world. The Los Angeles City Council, before 
and after 1978, was well versed in the problems caused 
by the concentration of adult businesses, and it was rea- 
sonable for it to conclude that restricting one adult busi- 
ness to a location responds to the needs of the City and is 
logically related to the legislative ends desired by the 
City Council. 
This Court has authorized cities to regulate adult 
businesses through their zoning ordinance. The City is 
free to disperse them as in Young, or concentrate them as 
in Renton. Los Angeles has, based upon substantial evi- 
dence, adopted a method of dispersing adult businesses 
which meets the specific needs of Los Angeles. As in 
Renton, this Court should “find no constitutional defect in 
the method chosen by [Los Angeles] to further its sub- 
stantial interests.” 475 U.S. at 52. It is not the function of 
the courts “to appraise the wisdom of [the city’s] deci- 
sion,” but rather to allow cities “a reasonable opportunity

D. 
There Is No Reason To Reformulate This Court’s 
Test In Renton For Determining When Adult Busi- 
nesses Result In Secondary Land Use Impacts. 
In Renton, supra, this Court established four factors to 
consider in analyzing the constitutionality of zoning ordi- 
nances regulating adult entertainment businesses. First, if 
the ordinance does not ban adult businesses, it must be 
considered a “time, place and manner regulation.” 475 
U.S. at 46. Second, the court must determine whether the 
challenged regulation is “content neutral.” Id. at 47. An 
ordinance is content neutral if it is “designed to combat 
the undesirable secondary effects” of adult businesses. Id. 
at 49. If so, it is “reviewed under the standards applicable 
to ‘content-neutral’ time, place and manner regulations.” 
Id. Third, the government needs to show that the “ordi- 
nance is designed to serve a substantial governmental 
interest and allows for reasonable alternative avenues of 
communication.” 
Id. at 50. The fourth Renton factor, 
whether the City’s regulation provides for reasonable 
alternative avenues of communications (id.) is not before 
the Court. 
Not content with this Court’s straightforward Renton 
test, the court of appeals saw fit to reformulate “this test 
in a slightly different and (we believe) more coherent

justification for doing so. Where, as here, “a precedent of 
this Court has direct application in a case . .

CONCLUSION 
The City zoning ordinance challenged here prohibits 
more than one adult business at the same location. In 
adopting this clarification of the City’s general rule tl.at 
adult businesses be separated from one another in order 
to avoid their proven secondary land use impacts, the 
City relied on its 1977 Study, a report this Court relied on 
to validate an adult business regulation of another city. In 
addition, the specific regulation challenged here was 
upheld by the Fourth Circuit in Hart, supra. The evidence 
relied on by the City meets the Renton standard of proof 
for zoning ordinances regulating adult businesses. 
Because the court of appeals did not properly apply that 
standard of proof and did not give proper deference to 
the City’s legislative judgment as required by this Court's
