DECLARATION OF FRANKLIN P. EBERHARD 
I, FRANKLIN P. EBERHARD, declare: 
1. The facts set forth in this declaration are person- 
ally known to me. If called as a witness, I could and 
would competently testify thereto. 
2. 
I am the Deputy Director, Department of City 
Planning for the City of Los Angeles. I have held that 
position for six years. My duties are to supervise the 
Office of Zoning Administration including the Zoning 
Administrator, Code Studies Unit, Deputy Advisory 
Agency, Environmental Section, and Public Counter. I 
also supervise the Citywide Planning Division. I am the 
Assistant General Manager of the Planning Department. 
3. 
Before being appointed to my current position I 
held the following positions in the Department of City 
Planning: Chief Zoning Administrator, Senior City Plan- 
ner, City Planning Associate and Planning Assistant. 
4. During the time that I was the supervisor of the 
Code Studies section of the Department, the City Council 
instructed the Department of Planning, with the assis- 
tance of other City Departments to conduct a study on 
the effects of the concentration of adult businesses on the 
surrounding community. As part of that study we identi- 
fied the various types of adult uses, using the various 
types of uses already regulated by the zoning code, i.e., 
retail uses and amusement uses. An example of an 
amusement type of use would be a “peep show,” motion 
picture theater, or video arcade. An amusement use gen- 
erally is a place of business devoted primarily or in some 
ee 
eee 
. 
186 
substantial degree to providing on-site public entertain- 
ment. 
5. The Los Angeles Municipal Code has historically 
regulated amusement enterprises separately from retail 
uses. Some examples of amusement enterprises include 
penny arcades, game arcades, video arcades, shooting 
galleries and the like. Amusement arcades are first per- 
mitted in the C2 Zone. They are not allowed in the CR, 
C1, C1.5, MR1, or MR2 Zones. These uses would be 
permitted by right, without discretionary land use review 
provided they were in the proper zone. 
6. Bookstores are considered to be a retail use. A 
bookstore (new) is first permitted in the Cl Zone and 
used bookstores are first permitted in the C1.5 Zone. 
Bookstores are allowed in all of the commercial zones, 
except the CR Zone. In general, a retail use involves the 
provision of goods or commodities for use or consump- 
tion off-site. 
7. The reason for treating amusement enterprises 
differently from bookstores is based on the historical 
experience of the City regarding the impacts associated 
with amusement uses. Those impacts are noise, hours of 
operation, parking and loitering. 
8. There are other examples of uses in the zoning 
code, which although commonly thought of as symbiotic 
in nature, have in fact been considered separate uses and 
are therefore regulated differently. An example of this is 
automotive repair garages and automotive service sta- 
tions. An automotive repair garage requires a 300-foot 
distance from residential uses, whereas a automotive ser- 
vice station does not.

187 
9. 
In drafting the Adult Entertainment Zoning Ordi- 
nance it was reasonable to use the existing categories of 
uses contained in the Los Angeles Municipal Code, since 
the import of the ordinance was to further control the 
location of these uses. In my opinion it was reasonable to 
use the same categories of uses already regulated because 
the ordinance merely imposed additional restrictions on 
those uses based on the adverse secondary impacts when 
those uses were engaged in the provision of sexually- 
oriented materials. 
10. 
In my many years in the Department of City 
Planning I have had the opportunity to study the effects 
of the provision of more than one type of business at a 
single location. It has been my experience that a location 
that only has one business does not have as many impacts 
on the surrounding community compared to a location 
with more than one business. As an example, the code 
has special regulations for “mini-malls.” The reason for 
the regulations is that the concentration of a number of 
businesses at one site contributes to traffic problems, 
parking problems, litter, noise and other adverse impacts 
on the surrounding community. 
11. 
Therefore, transferring this experience to the 
adult entertainment issue, it was reasonable for the City 
Council to conclude that the concentration of more than 
one adult entertainment business at one site would have 
more impacts than one business standing alone. 
12. 
The City Council decided to use the dispersal 
method to mitigate the impacts caused by the concentra- 
tion of adult entertainment businesses. The City has suc- 
cessfully used dispersal type ordinances to deal with 
188 
other land use problems. In the Westwood Village Speci- 
fic Plan the ordinance requires the dispersal of fast food 
establishments. The City had identified land use impacts 
caused by the-proliferation of fast food outlets. To combat 
these impacts the Westwood Specific Plan included spac- 
ing requirements. Another example can be found in the 
Citywide Sign regulations which require off-site signs to 
maintain a minimum distance from another off-site sign.

state of California that the foregoing is true and correct.

were new to factory work and city life. Too poorly paid to 
afford vaudeville prices and working such long hours

Once the rental system began, payment varied according 
to length of time, number of screenings, audience size, 
and box office receipts (and the exhibitor’s honesty in 
reporting these figures). Renting films necessitated set- 
ting up a new tier of organization, the film exchange, 
which handled distribution of prints to exhibitors. 
A new 
level of complexity entered the industry: with higher 
profits came a demand for increased production, an elab- 
orate system to control prints, and, for the first time, 
critical feedback from audiences, exhibitors, the expand- 
ing entertainment trade press, and concerned social and 
cultural organizations.

THE KINETOSCOPE 
Penny arcades were for the poor and the young. They 
were storerooms with wide entrances decked with circus 
posters and lurid stills, forerunners of the garish movie 
advertising of later years. Phonographs had been installed 
in them early, and now sound and picture were crudely 
harnessed together to provide such naively comic or por- 
nographic bits of life as “What the Bootblack Saw” or 
“How Bridget Served the Salad Undressed.” (A trend 
manifested itself from the beginning: the risqué and slap- 
stick were immediately popular, while “Surf at Dover” or 
“Beavers at Play” brought in meager returns.) To see these 
scraps of film, you dropped a penny in a slot, looked into 
an aperture like that of a stereoscope, and ground a crank. 
A light flashed on, and for a minute you watched Fatima 
wiggle her torso or a small boy squirt the garden hose on a 
well-dressed gentleman. You can see them today, in the 
flea circuses of the world, and now they are disarmingly 
redolent of an age of innocence. Then, they were glimpses 
of a far less innocent world than the poor and the young 
knew from their own experience. 
The Kinetoscope was as far as Edison considered it 
expedient to take his new invention. Pleased with the 
flood of pennies that flowed in from 
the peep show par- 
lors, he feared that if he were to project his images so that 
more than one person could see them at a time, the audi- 
ence would soon be exhausted. But the pull of “living 
pictures” magnetized a host of would-be Edisons here and 
abroad. By 1895, the Kinetoscope had a lively imitative 
competitor in the Mutoscope, and strenuous efforts were 
being made by a score of mechanically minded men to 
combine the Kinetoscope principle with that of the magic 
lantern, so as to throw a larger-than-life-size picture on a 
sheet or on some other white surface.

THE NICKELODEON AND ITS PATRONS 
In contrast to the small-town store shows, nickelodeons 
in New York and other big cities grew out of penny 
arcades in many cases, and they looked as dark, dirty, 
and smelly as penny arcades and flea circuses look to us 
today. Their first patrons were often tramps and drunks; 
the respectable shuddered away from them and mur- 
mured against them. It began to be said that something 
ought to be done about policing them or maybe banning 
them altogether. But the city nickelodeons thrived. Even 
more than in rural America, they were the only entertain- 
ment available to the poor. 
Especially were they the chief entertainment of the 
hordes of immigrants from southern and eastern Europe 
who poured into the metropolis in the early 1900s. Barred 
from the theater by their ignorance of English, they could 
follow the one-reel picture dramas of the nickelodeons, 
learning much about American life and values in the 
process, and piecing out their English as they gradually 
related the subtitles to the action. Observing this, some of 
the more farsighted social workers of the period began to 
think that the despised “poor man’s show” might become 
his university as well. 
By the end of the Nickelodeon Age, even the city 
nickelodeons were safe and comfortable places which the 
most fastidious might (and some surreptitiously did) 
attend without offense. Exhibitors began to clamor for 
longer films with better stories and acting, knowing that 
audiences would pay more if they got more, but their 
clamor went unheeded. The real powers of the new 
industry, the producers, refused to believe that anybody

WHEN MOVIES WERE VERY YOUNG 
Fifty years ago nickelodeons came into vogue, offering 
fifteen minutes of film for five cents. 
By BOSLEY CROWTHER 
Tue glory, if such you can call it, of the old-time nickelo- 
deon theatres ~ once as rampant in the realm of entertain- 
ment as television is today - will be fondly re-called and 
celebrated in ceremonies today. The occasion is the 
fiftieth anniversary of what legend holds to have been the 
opening of the first five-cent movie in Pittsburgh, Pa. 
The “theatre” which inspired this celebration was 
actually a vacant store which two prominent Pittsburgh 
theatre owners, State Senator John P. Harris and Harry 
Davis, his brother-in-law, converted into a novel empo- 
rium where early silent movies were shown on a fifteen- 
minute rotating schedule at a nickel the customer. 
This odd little place of entertainment, located at 433 
Smithfield Street, next door to a penny arcade, was called 
the Nickelodeon, and was no more then a long, bare 
Storeroom equipped with ninety-six folding chairs. An 
old phonograph played tinny music. The screen was a 
white linen sheet. And the films were such things as “The 
Baffled Burglar” and “Poor but Honest,” which ran no 
more than a few minutes each. 
This was the curious venture which legend holds to 
have been the first exclusively motion-picture theatre and 
the model of all the nickelodeons. 


PAGE 26
214 
Legend is slightly inaccurate, as legend frequently is. 
Motion pictures were shown in tents and backrooms, in 
public halls and amusement parks, as independent enter- 
tainments, long before the opening of the Pittsburgh 
store. Old “Pop” Rock, one of the founders of Vitagraph, 
had a little nickel movie show that he operated down in 
New Orleans as early as 1896. And Tom Talley opened his 
first Electric Theatre, in which programs of primitive 
movies were shown, as an adjunct to a Los Angeles dime- 
museum as early as 1902. But the Pittsburgh venture of 
Harris and Davis came on the threshold of a great new 
trend. And it gave a name to the whole generic group of 
converted-store “theatres.” 
Tis sudden emergence of many nickelodeons, begin- 
ning in 1905, was a phenomenon in the world of enter- 
tainment that marked the real beginning of the “motion 
picture age.” The primitive “flickers,” derived from the 
inventions of the Lathams, Edison and the Lumieres, had 
been familiar for almost a decade as items on vaudeville- 
theatre bills. Here they had become so monotonous, after 
the first flush of their novelty, that they were regularly 
used to chase customers out of the theatres at the end of 
the shows. 
Then a series of circumstances which began develop- 
ing around 1905 brought on the wave of cheap show- 
places that proved the movies to be the choice of the 
masses. 
First, the discovery of the medium as a dramatic 
storytelling device with such crude but effective little 
dramas as “The Great Train Robbery,” made in 1903, 
opened the way for the wooing of a new class of naive 
215 
customers whose emotions were easily stimulated and 
whose pocketbooks were congenial to the 5-cent price. 
Second, the great wave of immigration which hit this 
country from 1900 to 1910 was dumping into the cities 
fresh pools of potential customers for the brisk little 
picture dramas that required no language to be under- 
stood. 
Third, the popular interest rose in a type of movie 
presentation called “scenic tours,” which was nothing 
more than the showing of travel pictures in a place got up 
to look like a railroad car. 
By 1907, it was calculated that there were between 
2,500 and 3,000 nickelodeans in the United States, with 
the number increasing yearly. The “ingredients” needed 
for operation such as showplace, according to a trade 
paper, Motion Picture World, were “200-500 people (audi- 
ence), phonograph (with extra large horn), woman cash- 
ier, electric sign, machine and operator and films, canvas 
screen, piano, barker, chairs.” It was further remarked 
that the owner “needs a few brains and a little tact.” 
Immediately the popularity of the nickelodeans 
became apparent, various small business enterprisers and 
opportunists rushed into the field. Among them were 
such moving spirits as Adolph Zukor, William Fox and 
Marcus Loew, who were later to become great figures in 
the vastly expanding motion-picture industry. 
Inevitably the guardians of the public weal came to 
view with alarm these upstart emporia. Within a couple 
of years of their emergence, religious and social leaders 


PAGE 27
216 
were crying out against the perilous and corruptive influ- 
ences of the nickelodeons. 
The physically hazardous nature of many of these 
“store theatres,” which were crammed into all sorts of 
buildings that were never designed for such things, 
increased their liability to charg +s. Occasional projection- 
machine fires and consequent stampedes of customers to 
get out of the crowded rooms caused periodic disasters, 
usually with fatalities. Cities soon invoked fire laws 
against them and compelled conformance to rules. 
As a consequence of the angry question of the moral 
character of the pictures shown, the Peoples Institute in 
New York City was designated to inspect all the items 
made for the nickelodeons with an eye to stopping those 
thought morally harmful. Thus was established the prece- 
dent for the censoring of films. 
The era of the nickelodeons lasted for roughly ten 
years. In that time, their vogue had penetrated to vir- 
tually every town in the United States. New York City 
had close to 700 5- and 10-cent movie places in 1908, 
which was the year in which Mayor George B. McClellan 
attempted to crack down on them. 
But the slow passing of the nickelodeons was not 
entirely due to the objections of the moralists and the 
operation of fire and health laws. It was the consequence 
of the improvement in motion pictures, the increase in 
their length and costs and the ultimate necessity of larger 
and better theatres in which to seat a greater number of 
customers.

With the advent of the four and five-reel picture after 
1912, the doom of the storefront theatre was sealed. 
Already the more advanced exhibitors, such as Loew and 
Fox, were picking up neighborhood legitimate theatres 
and old “family burlesque” houses in which to show 
films and vaudeville. The more elaborate motion pictures, 
such as Sarah Bernhardt’s “Queen Elizabeth” and the 
Italian “Quo Vadis?”, were opened in legitimate theatres 
on Broadway. The movies were becoming refined. 
To be sure, nickelodeons lingered in certain spots for 
many years. But the expansion of the motion picture had 
made obsolete its original and novel homes.

I. Introduction 
The central issue in this case is whether the City of 
Los Angeles (the “City”) violates the First Amendment by 
enforcing a zoning ordinance that defines certain activ- 
ities as “adult entertainment businesses” and prohibits 
the operation of more than one such business in the same 
building. Non-obscene adult entertainment is protected 
by the First Amendment free speech clause. Local govern- 
ments, however, may impose special zoning restrictions 
on adult entertainment businesses without violating the 
First Amendment, provided that such restrictions are 
directed 
at harmful “secondary effects” of such busi- 
nesses, and not at suppressing the content of adult enter- 
tainment. Renton v. Playtime Theatres, Inc., 475 U.S. 41, 106 
S. Ct. 925 (1986). The plaintiffs contend that the City 
cannot demonstrate harmful secondary effects arising 
from multiple adult entertainment uses within a single 
business, and that therefore a multiple use restriction 
applicable only to adult entertainment businesses violates 
the First Amendment. The City contends that its studies 
_ 255 
demonstrate that concentrations of adult entertainment 
businesses have harmful secondary effects, and that these 
studies support the multiple use restriction. The Court 
finds that while the studies addressed concentrations of 
separate adult entertainment businesses in an urban area, 
the studies did not treat the activities conducted by the 
plaintiffs’ - video rental and on-premises video viewing - 
as separate businesses. The court therefore finds that 
there is a genuine issue of fact whether the operation of a 
combination video rental and video viewing business 
leads to the harmful secondary effects associated with a 
concentration of separate businesses in a single urban 
area. The Court therefore denies both motions for sum- 
mary adjudication of the First Amendment issues.

III. 
Abstention 
The City contends that the Court should dismiss or 
stay this action under all three theories of federal absten- 
tion: Younger v. Harris, 401 U.S. 37 (1971); Railroad Com- 
mission of Texas v. Pullman Co., 312 U.S. 496 (1941); and 
Burford v. Sun Oil Co., 319 U.S. 315 (1943). 
A. 
Younger Abstention 
Federal courts may not enjoin or otherwise interfere 
with pending state criminal prosecutions absent extraor- 
dinary circumstances. In order to maintain the proper 
constitutional relationship between federal and state 
authorities, the federal courts must allow the state courts 
te adjudicate all claims in a pending criminal proceeding, 
including federal claims. Younger, 401 U.S. 37, 54 (1971). 
Of the three doctrines of abstention, only Younger is juris- 
dictional. Where it applies, the district court must dismiss 
the action. Schwartzer, et al., California Practice Guide, 
Federal Civil Procedure Before Trial § 2:1292.1 (Rutter 1996). 
The requirements for Younger abstention are (1) a 
pending state court proceeding, (2) implication of impor- 
tant state interests, and (3) adequate opportunity to raise 
the federal claims. Id. § 2:1291.2. 


PAGE 49
260 
1. Pending State Court Proceedings 
For Younger abstention to apply, state judicial pro- 
ceedings must be pending. The imminence of proceed- 
ings in the near future is not sufficient to justify Younger 
abstention. In Agriesti v. MGM Grand Hotels, Inc., 53 F.3d 
1000 (9th Cir. 1995), the plaintiffs were arrested and cited 
for misdemeanor trespass while distributing leaflets in 
front of a Las Vegas hotel. The Las Vegas district attorney 
never filed the citations with the state court. Id. at 1001. 
The district court abstained, holding that the arrests and 
issuance of a misdemeanor citation triggered the Younger 
rule. The Ninth Circuit reversed, holding that the district 
court erred by abstaining in the absence of a state judicial 
proceeding. Id. The court held that arrest and citation are 
executive, not judicial acts. Younger abstention was, there- 
fore, neither required nor proper. Id. at 1002. 
Here, the City makes the same argument rejected in 
Agriesti. On August 16, 1995 a criminal misdemeanor 
citation was issued against Alameda Books, charging that 
Alameda Books operated two adult businesses in the 
same location in violation of LAMC § 12.70(C). The cita- 
tion did not set an appearance date, but instead provided 
that Alameda Books would be notified of the date by the 
Los Angeles City Attorney's office. (Motion at 8 n.6.) The 
city attorney’s office never initiated a criminal proceeding 
based on the citation, despite having three months to do 
so before this Court issued its TRO in November 1995. 
(Opp. at 10.) 
Because no state judicial proceeding was pending at 
the time this action was filed, the Court may not apply 
Younger abstention. 
261 
B. 
Pullman Abstention (Deferral) 
The City requests that the court apply Pullman 
abstention as to plaintiff Highland Books. Federal courts 
may abstain where a limiting construction by the state 
courts might make federal constitutional review unneces- 
sary. Pullman, 312 U.S. at 500. Pullman abstention is not 
jurisdictional. Where it applies, the court must retain 
jurisdiction while the unsettled issue is litigated in state 
court. Almodovar v. Reiner, 832 F.2d 1138, 1141 (9th Cir. 
1987) (“Failure to retain jurisdiction is reversible error.”). 
The Ninth Circuit requires the presence of the follow- 
ing factors for Pullman abstention: (1) the complaint must 
touch a sensitive area of social policy on which the fed- 
eral courts ought not to enter unless no alternative to its 
adjudication is open; (2) the case must present a state law 
issue whose resolution would terminate the constitu- 
tional controversy; and (3) the resolution of the state law 
issue must be doubtful or uncertain. Pearl Inv. Co. v. City 
& Cty. of San Francisco, 774 F.2d 1460, 1463 (9th Cir. 1985). 
1. 
Sensitive Area of Social Policy 
In First Amendment cases this factor “will almost 
never be present because the guarantee of free expression 
is always an area of particular federal concern.” Rip- 
plinger v. Collins, 868 F.2d 1043, 1048 (9th Cir. 1989). 
Abstention would aggravate the “impermissible chilling 
of the very constitutional right” the plaintiff seeks to 
protect. Id. 
On the other hand, zoning and land use planning are 
matters of “sensitive social policy” over which federal 


PAGE 50
262 
courts should not normally intervene. Kollsman v. City of 
Los Angeles, 737 F.2d 830, 833-37 (9th Cir. 1984) (holding 
that district court erred in not abstaining where constitu- 
tional issue depended on interpretation of complex statu- 
tory land-use scheme which state courts had not yet 
interpreted). 
The Ninth Circuit addressed the conflict between 
First Amendment concerns and sensitive social policy in 
Almodovar, where the plaintiffs challenged the use of state 
pandering and prostitution statutes to prohibit the pro- 
duction of sexually explicit films. The Ninth Circuit 
affirmed the district court’s decision to abstain for two 
reasons. First, the court noted that statutes regulating 
prostitution touch an area of social policy that is arguably 
more sensitive than land-use planning. Id. at 1140. Sec- 
ond, the court held that abstention would not aggravate 
any chilling effect because that state issue in question 
could be adjudicated in a single proceeding with little 
delay, after which the plaintiffs could pursue any remain- 
ing constitutional claim. Id. at 1140-41. In addition, other 
parties were presenting the same issues to the California 
Supreme Court in a case pending at the time the district 
court decided to abstain. Id. Therefore, the state issue was 
likely to be resolved without substantial delay. 
The city argues that here, as in Almodovar, there is no 
danger of a chilling effect. Plaintiff Highland Books has 
been in business since 1987. LAMC § 12.70(C) has been in 
effect during that entire time. The City argues that High- 
land Book’s failure to challenge the ordinance before now 
indicates that its expression has not been chilled. (Reply 
at 2.) This argument ignores the effect of the City’s 
renewed enforcement on the ordinance as signaled by the 
263 
misdemeanor citation issued to Alameda Books on 
August 16, 1995. In addition, here, unlike in Almodovar, 
there is no pending state court proceeding that might 
produce a limiting construction of the ordinance before a 
significant chilling effect has occurred. Therefore, it is 
appropriate for the Court to treat this case according to 
the Ripplinger rule where First Amendment freedom of 
speech is at issue. 
2. 
Existence and Uncertainty of State Law Issue

issue is fairly susceptible to a constitutional construction 
by a state court applying state law. The City argues that 
because Highland Books has never been cited 
or 
inspected under the statute, 
“in the absence of some 
administrative determination, it is not possible to know 
how LAMC § 12.70(C) would be applied to this busi- 
ness.” (Motion at 4.) The lack of enforcement, however, is 
not relevant to Pullman abstention. The relevant question 
is not whether law enforcement authorities will interpret

sion, but whether the state courts wili 
> interpret the 
statute. 
The plaintiffs argue that the case law in California 
has already narrowed the definition of “adult entertain- 
ment uses” in such a way as to foreclose the possibility of 
state court decisions that would moot the federal consti- 
tutional issue. In People v. Superior Court (Lucero). 49 Cal.

ordinances that restrict adult entertainment business 


PAGE 51
264 
within a certain distance of each other. Prior to Lucero, the

C. 
Burford Abstention (Comity) 
Burford abstention prevents interference with a state 
regulatory scheme. The Ninth circuit requires the follow- 
ing factors to be present: “(1) that the state has concen- 
trated suits involving the local issue in a particular court; 
(2) that the federal issues are not easily separable from 
complicated state law issues with which the state courts 
may have special competence; and (3) that federal review 
might disrupt state efforts to establish a coherent policy.” 
Tucker v. First Maryland Sav. & Loan, Inc., 942 F.2d 1401, 
1405 (9th Cir. 1991). Where these factors are present, 
dismissal rather than stay is appropriate. Id. 
Burford abstention 
is inappropriate where federal 
issues are controlling. “A case involving a first amend- 
ment challenge to a state statute is unlikely to implicate 
purely local issues that the state courts may be especially 
competent to deal with.” Ripplinger, 868 F.2d at 1049 n.6.

266 
Here, although the City calls LAMC § 12.70(C) a 
zoning ordinance, the City has not claimed that the 
instant dispute is the subject of a zoning variance or other 
administrative procedure that might give rise to Burford 
abstention. The only process initiated against the Plain- 
tiffs is the misdemeanor citation issued to Alameda 
Books. As discussed above, the City did not pursue a 
criminal or administrative proceeding as a result of this

Because there is no current administrative proceed- 
ing, and because the federal issues in this case are likely 
to be controlling and are easily separable from any com- 
plicated state law or regulatory issues, the Court con- 
cludes that Burford abstention is inappropriate. 
IV. 
Summary Judgment 
A. 
Standard for Summary Judgment 
Summary judgment is appropriate when there is no 
genuine issue of material fact and the moving party is 
entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Fed. R. Civ. P. 
56(c); see Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317 (1986). 
In order to defeat a motion for summary judgment, 
there must be facts in dispute that are both genuine and 
material, i.e., there must be facts upon which a fact finder 
could reasonably find for the non-moving party. See 
Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 252 (1986). 
The court does not weigh the evidence or make cred- 
ibility determinations;rather, the court only determines 
whether there are any disputed issues and, if so, whether 
those issues are both genuine and material. Id. 
267 
The initial burden of establishing that there is no 
genuine issue of material fact lies with the moving party. 
Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(c); Celotex, 477 U.S. at 323; British 
Airways Board v. Boeing Co., 585 F.2d 946, 951 (9th Cir. 
1978). Once the movant has met this burden by producing 
evidence that, if left uncontroverted, would entitle the 
moving party to a directed verdict at trial, the burden 
shifts to the non-movant to present specific facts showing 
that there is a genuine issue of material fact. See Fed. R. 
Civ. P. 56(e); Celotex, 477 U.S. at 324; Lake Nacimiento 
Ranch Co. v. San Luis Obispo, 841 F.2d 872, 876 (9th Cir. 
1987).

The City contends that plaintiffs’ claims are barred

In a facial challenge under the first Amendment,

C. 
First Amendment 
The city asserts that the multiple use ordinance is a 
valid time, place and manner restriction based not on the 
content of the plaintiffs’ speech, but on the deleterious 
secondary effects associated with adult entertainment 
businesses. Ordinarily, the government may not apply 
time, place and manner restrictions based on the content 
of speech. Renton, 475 U.S. at 46-47, 106 S. Ct. at 928. 
Although the ordinance here targets adult entertainment 
businesses based on specific definitions of adult content, 
such restrictions are not content-based if the predominate 
purpose of the ordinance’s enactment is to ameliorate 
deleterious secondary effects of such businesses. 
Id. at 48. 
A content-neutral time, place and manner restriction 
on speech is constitutional if it furthers a substantial 
government interest and is narrowly tailored to affect no 
269 
more speech than is essential to the furtherance of that 
interest. Young, 427 U.S. at 79-80, 96 S. Ct. at 2457 (Powell, 
J., concurring) (citing United States v. O’Brien, 391 US. 
367, 377, 88 S. Ct. 1673, 1679 (1968)). 
In the adult entertainment context, the Ninth Circuit 
has used a three-step analysis to determine whether an 
ordinance is a valid time, place and manner restriction. 
See Tollis, Inc. v. San Bernardino County, 827 F.2d 1329, 1332 
(9th Cir. 1987). The first step is to determine whether the 
ordinance bans the plaintiffs’ expressive conduct alto- 
gether. If it does, it is not a “time, place and manner 
restriction.” Id. The second step is to determine whether 
the ordinance should be analyzed as content-based or 
content-neutral. Where, as here, the ordinance on its face 
differentiates on the basis of content, the issue is whether 
the predominate motivation in passing the ordinance was 
to ameliorate adverse secondary effects of speech rather 
than to suppress speech itself. Id. If the restriction is 
content-neutral, the third step is to determine whether 
the restriction serves a substantial government interest, is 
narrowly tailored to serve that interest and allows for 
reasonable alternative avenues of communication. Id.; see 
also Simon & Schuster, Inc. v. Members of the New York State 
Crime Victims Bd., 502 U.S. 105, 122 n.*, 112 S. Ct. 501, 511 
n.* (noting that under Renton “regulations must be ‘nar- 
rowly tailored’ to advance the interest asserted by the 
State”). Although the regulation must be narrowly tai- 
lored to serve a substantial government interest, it need 
not be the least restrictive means of serving that interest. 
Ward v. Rock Against Racism, 491 U.S. 781 109 S. Ct. 2746 
(1989); Renton, 475 U.S. at 52, 106 S. Ct. at 931 (noting that

270 
state and local governments have broad latitude to 
choose means of ameliorating secondary effects).

Amendment. Walnut Properties, Inc. v. City of Whittier, 808

3. Whether The Multiple Use Ordinance is Nar- 
rowly Tailored to Serve a Substantial Govern-

There can be no question that a city’s interest in 
combating urban blight is substantial. Renton, 475 U.S. at 
50, 106 S. Ct. at 930; Tollis, 827 F.2d at 1332. The issue is 
whether the multiple use ordinance furthers that interest. 
In order to demonstrate that the ordinance serves 
its 
interest in combating urban blight, the City has the bur- 
den to “prove that existence of the ‘secondary effects’ it is 
seeking to prevent.” Acorn, 887 F.2d at 222. 
In Acorn, the City of Seattle imposed a licensing fee 
on video arcades in 1955. The City justified the fee as a 
means of paying the law enforcement costs of dealing 
with crimes committed in the video arcades. In 1986, 
Seattle began enforcing design restrictions that made the 
video arcades less private, and therefore less useful for 
prostitution and drug dealing. The plaintiff in Acorn con- 
tended that the resulting reduction in crime removed the 
adverse secondary effect on which Seattle based its claim 
of a substantial government interest for the licensing fee. 
The Ninth Circuit agreed with the plaintiff, holding that 
the district court’s factual finding that the arcades “cur- 
rently” fostered criminal activity was clearly erroneous. 
Id. at 224. While the court accepted the city’s argument

longer existed. Id.; see also Tollis, 827 F.2d at 1333 (holding 
that city had failed to demonstrate that single showing of 
an adult movie would have any harmful secondary 
effects on the community).

1. 
Selective Settlement 
The plaintiffs claim they have been denied equal 
protection of the laws because the City has agreed not to 
enforce the ordinance against other adult bookstore oper- 
ators in a series of stipulated permanent injunctions, but 
has sought to enforce it against the plaintiffs. The issue 
here is whether the equal protection clause requires the 
City to apply the terms of the settlements to the plaintiffs 
even though the plaintiffs were not parties to the settled 
cases. The plaintiffs argue that the City’s conduct in 
settling the previous lawsuits and refusing to settle the 
instant case involves an arbitrary classification which 


PAGE 66
294 
cannot withstand rational basis scrutiny under the stan- 
dard of City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, 473 U.S. 
432, 105 S. Ct. 3249 (1985). There, the Court applied the 
rational basis standard to invalidate the application of a 
zoning ordinance against a group home for the mentally 
retarded. The court held that the ordinance, as applied to 
the group home, treated the home differently than it 
treated similarly situated care facilities and multiple-unit 
buildings. Id. at 447, 105 S. Ct. at 3258. The Court rejected 
the municipality’s argument that negative attitudes of 
property owners toward the mentally retarded provided 
a rational basis for the distinction. Id. at 448, 105 S. Ct. at 
3259. 
The plaintiffs’ argument ignores the government's 
broad discretion to choose whom it prosecutes. See Wayte 
v. United States, 470 U.S. 598, 607, 105 S. Ct. 1524, 1530 
(1985). The-equal protection clause is not violated by a 
decision to forego prosecution of certain charges as result 
of a settlement agreement or plea bargain. See Bor- 
denkircher v. Hayes, 434 U.S. 357, 98 S. Ct. 663 (1978). 
Absent an allegation of selectivity based on impermissi- 
ble discrimination by race, gender or national origin, or 
on some other arbitrary basis, selective prosecution for 
the purpose of settling cases does not violate equal pro- 
tection. Id. at 364, 98 S. Ct. at 668-69. 
The plaintiffs’ equal protection argument fails 
because their situation is not similar to that of the parties 
to the Topanga Press, E.W.A.P., and Diamond cases. Those 
parties entered into settlement agreements with the City, 
while the present plaintiffs did not. That distinction satis- 
fies the equal protection clause. Nor does the equal pro- 
tection clause require the City to offer the present 
295 
plaintiffs the same settlement terms as offered to the 
previous plaintiffs, absent an impermissible reason for 
the distinction.®

2.

The plaintiffs contend that the multiple use ordi- 
nance treats them differently than other multiple use 
businesses — such as a Sears Roebuck with an attached 
automobile service department - based on the plaintiffs’ 
exercise of First Amendment protected speech. The plain- 
tiffs argue that the City’s interest in ameliorating the 
harmful effects of the plaintiffs’ purported separate adult 
entertainment_uses are adequately served by the zoning 
regulations applicable to each use. Using the Sears exam- 
ple, if the Sears retail store and the Sears auto repair 
department were both allowed in a given zone, then the 
City would rely upon the zoning restrictions applicable to 
each use in order to protect the public, without requiring 
297 
Sears to close one of these operations. (Plaintiffs’ EQP 
Motion at 13.) The plaintiffs assert that no other business 
is subject to multiple use restrictions added on top of the 
restrictions applicable to each single use. (Id. at 14.) 
The Supreme Court has held that municipalities may 
use the content of sexually oriented entertainments as the 
basis for placing them in a different classification from 
other businesses. Young, 427 U.S. at 71. The same reason- 
ing that supports the legal fiction that content-based reg- 
ulations of adult entertainment to alleviate secondary 
effects is actually content-neutral under the First Amend- 
ment allows the distinction between adult and non-adult 
businesses to avoid strict scrutiny review under the Four- 
teenth Amendment. Because the Renton doctrine views 
such regulation as directed toward secondary effects 
rather than toward expressive conduct, no fundamental 
right is implicated. Thus, the classification in question is 
not between businesses that engage in protected expres- 
sive conduct and those that do not, but rather between 
businesses that cause harmful deleterious effects associ- 
ated with adult entertainment and those that do not. For 
equal protection purposes, rational basis scrutiny applies. 
The City’s policy to reduce the deleterious effects is a 
rational basis for the different treatment of adult enter- 
tainment businesses. Therefore, the plaintiffs’ equal pro- 
tection claim fails as a matter of law if the regulation is 
directed toward harmful secondary effects. 
As indicated above in the First Amendment discus- 
sion, the Court finds that there is a genuine issue of fact 
as to whether the 1977 studies support the finding of 
harmful secondary effects from multiple adult entertain- 
ment uses within a single building. If the City fails to 


PAGE 68
298 
meet its burden on this issue, then the ordinance must be 
examined as a content-based speech regulation under 
First Amendment strict scrutiny. Under that circum- 
stance, the ordinance would also have to pass strict scru- 
tiny under the Fourteenth Amendment as distinction 
based on exercise of a fundamental right. 
The Court therefore denies both motions for sum- 
mary adjudication on the issue of whether the ordinance 
violates the Fourteenth Amendment by treating persons 
differently based on the exercise of fundamental rights.

1. Vagueness 
The plaintiffs argue that in the event the Court rejects 
their facial challenge to the ordinance, they would not be 
able to conform their conduct to the requirements of the 
ordinance because it is unconstitutionally vague. (Plain- 
tiffs’ DP Motion at 2.) The plaintiffs contend that by 
defining an adult bookstore as an establishment which 
has adult materials “as a substantial portion of its stock- 
in-trade,” the ordinance fails to provide fair notice cf the 
conduct regulated. The plaintiffs contend that they -an- 
not determine from the statute what ratio of adult to non-

a. 
Standing and Ripeness 
The City asserts that the plaintiffs do not have stand- 
ing to assert vagueness because the plaintiffs have admit- 
ted that the ordinance applies to their businesses as 
299 
currently configured. (Complaint { 84.) In Young and 
Renton, the Supreme court rejected similar vagueness 
claims on the grounds that the plaintiffs conceded that 
the ordinances clearly applied to them. Young, 427 U.S. at 
59; Renton, 475 U.S. at 55 n.4. The Ninth Circuit has held 
that “[a] plaintiff who engages in some conduct that is 
clearly proscribed cannot complain of the vagueness of 
the law as applied to the conduct of others.” Stoianoff v. 
State of Mont., 695 F.2d 1214, 1218 (9th Cir. 1983). Because 
there is no question as to the applicability of the ordi- 
nance to the plaintiffs, the Court rejects the vagueness 
challenge for lack of standing. 
The plaintiffs argue that they will have standing if 
the preliminary injunction is lifted and they are faced 
with the task of bringing their businesses into compliance 
with the ordinance, or bringing their businesses out from 
under the definition of “adult entertainment.” The fact 
that the plaintiffs have these choices in the event that 
they lose this lawsuit demonstrates that their vagueness 
challenge under this theory is not ripe for judicial resolu- 
tion. 
b. 
Merits 
A statute is not impermissibly vague if it defines the 
offense with sufficient definiteness that ordinary people 
can understand what is prohibited, and it establishes 
standards to permit enforcement the law in a non-arbi- 
trary, non-discriminatory manner. Nunez v. City of San 
Diego, 114 F.3d 935 (9th Cir. 1997). The plaintiffs cite 
declarations of zoning officers, made in the Topanga Press 
case, in which the officers testified that they did not 


PAGE 69
300 
apply a numerical formula to determine when a portion 
of adult material was “substantial.” (Garrou Decl. Exs. A, 
B and C.) The gist of these declarations is that the officers 
know an adult business when they see one. 
There is no Ninth Circuit authority for the proposi- 
tion that a zoning ordinance that requires case-by-case 
construction of a term like “substantial portion” is imper- 
missibly vague. On the contrary, the Ninth Circuit in 
Stoianoff held that a drug paraphernalia statute was not 
impermissibly vague merely because its application 
depended on case-by-case determination of whether a 
particular item was “designed for use” as drug parapher- 
nalia. 695 F.2d at 1220. “A definition which casts a wide 
net is necessary to accomplish the objectives of this Act.” 
Id.; see also Lydo Enterprises, Inc. v. City of Las Vegas, 745 
F.2d 1211, 1215 (9th Cir. 1984) (rejecting vagueness argu- 
ment based on lack of “ ‘quantitative modifiers’ ” indicat- 
ing what amount of adult material brings business within 
coverage of adult entertainment zoning statute). 
Furthermore, the California courts have adopted a 
limiting construction of the “substantial portion” lan- 
guage. Lucero, 49 Cal. 3d 14, 259 Cal. Rptr. 740. In Lucero, 
the California Supreme Court rejected the California 
court of appeal’s construction of the language to include 
a preponderance standard under which a use would not 
be covered unless the stock in trade of the business 
included more than 50% adult material. Id. at 741 (over- 
ruling Pringle, 115 Cal. App. 3d 151, 171 Cal. Rptr. 251). In 
place of the Pringle preponderance standard the court 
adopted “ ‘the regular and substantial course of conduct’ 
standard.” Lucero, 49 Cal. 3d at 19. Under the standard as 
applied to the adult theaters at issue in Lucero, the zoning 
301 
restrictions would “apply to all adult entertainment thea- 
ters offering adult fare as a substantial part of their 
regular business, but would not apply to theaters show- 
ing only occasional or incidental adult movies.” Id. at 28. 
The regular and substantial course of conduct stan- 
dard can be applied to both the arcade and bookstore 
portions of the plaintiffs’ businesses. The City argues that 
the zoning officials’ declarations offered by the plaintiffs 
demonstrate that these officials have applied the regular 
and substantial conduct test in determining whether a 
business meets the ordinance’s definition of adult enter- 
tainment. (Def. Opp. at 31.) Zoning official Eberhard tes- 
tified that after the Lucero decision the department 
applied a number of objective factors: “One of the objec- 
tive factors might be the amount of material available for 
sale. Another objective factor would be the placement 
and display of those materials. Another objective factor 
might be whether or not the establishment 
— how it 
represents itself in terms of advertising signs, or pieces of 
information.” (Id. Ex. 26.) Based on this testimony, the 
Court concludes that the City has applied the Lucero 
standard to limit the meaning of “substantial portion” so 
that the language is not unconstitutionally vague. 
2. Overbreadth 
Overbreadth doctrine allows a person to whom a 
statute may be constitutionally applied to challenge the 
Statute on the basis that it may unconstitutionally chill 
speech by others. R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul, 505 U.S. 377, 
411, 112 S. Ct. 2538, 2558 (1992) (White, J., concurring). 
Overbreadth doctrine is “ ‘strong medicine’ ” and should 


PAGE 70
302 
not be applied unless the statute sweeps in a substantial 
amount of protected speech and is not susceptible to a 
limiting construction. Id. 
The plaintiffs, however, have not properly raised an 
overbreadth issue. The plaintiffs argue that the statute is 
overbroad because it chills multiple uses for which the 
City does not have proper evidence of deleterious effects. 
This is not an overbreadth argument, but rather a restate- 
ment of the plaintiffs’ time, place and manner argument 
that the ordinance is not justificable as a content-neutral 
regulation of speech. Overbreadth doctrine should not be 
applied when a case can be decided on narrower 
grounds. Broadrick v. Oklahoma, 413 U.S. 601, 613, 93 S. Ct. 
2908, 2916 (1973) (noting that overbreadth doctrine is to 
be applied “sparingly and only as a last resort”). Because 
the court finds a genuine issue as to whether the ordi- 
nance serves a substantial government interest under the 
secondary effects doctrine, the Court need not analyze 
this ordinance for overbreadth. The ordinance’s chilling 
effect on multiple uses is not a result of overbreadth. 
Multiple uses are the precise targets of the ordinance. The 
question in this case is whether this precise targeting is 
constitutional under the secondary effects doctrine. 
The arguments which the plaintiffs mischaracterize 
as arising under the overbreadth doctrine should there- 
fore be decided only as part of the time, place and man- 
ner analysis.

V. 
Conclusion 
A. 
Abstention 
Because there is no judicial proceeding pending 
against Alameda or Highland, the Court need not abstain 
on Younger grounds. 
The court need not defer or stay the action under 
Pullman because the lawsuit involves a First Amendment 
issue which does not normally lend itself to Pullman 
abstention, and because there is no issue of state law 
whose resolution might make it unnecessary to reach the 
constitutional issues in this case. 
Because no administrative or regulatory scheme is at 
stake in this case, the Court need not dismiss on grounds 
of Burford abstention. 
B. 
Statute of Limitations 
As to the issue of the statute of limitations, the Court 
denies the City’s motion for summary judgment because 
the plaintiffs’ alleged injuries arise out of continuing 
violations of their first Amendment rights, and therefore 
cannot be said to have accrued with the enactment of the 
multiple use amendment in 1983.

D. 
Equal Protection 
1. 
Selective Prosecution 
The City has demonstrated that there is no genuine 
issue of fact as to whether the selective settlement policy 
violates equal protection. As a matter of law, the City 
may chose [sic] to settle some cases and prosecute others. 
The City’s motion for partial adjudication is granted as to 
the selective prosecution equal protection violation. 
2. Fundamental Rights 
Because there remains a triable issue of fact as to 
whether the ordinance is a valid secondary effects regula- 
tion, there also remains a triable issue as to whether the 
ordinance violates equal protection based on exercise of a 
fundamental right. Therefore both motions for summary 
adjudication are denied as to this claim.

E. 
Due Process 
1. Vagueness 
Because the plaintiffs admit that the statute applies to 
them, there is no triable issue as to vagueness. The City’s 
motion for summary adjudication is granted as to the 
vagueness issue. 
2. Overbreadth 
Because the plaintiffs’ overbreadth argument is based 
not on the chilling effect of the ordinance on those not 
covered by its provisions, but rather on those covered, 
the Court need not reach the overbreadth question. Reso- 
lution of the First Amendment issues in the case will 
dispose of the overbreadth issue. Therefore, the Court 
grants the City’s motion for summary adjudication of the 
overbreadth claims.
