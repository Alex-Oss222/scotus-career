I, David M. Brown, hereby declare as follows: 
1. 
I am an attorney, admitted to the State Bar of 
California. Except as stated otherwise, I have personal 
knowledge of all facts set forth herein. 
2. My business address is 15303 Ventura Blvd., Suite 
1400, Sherman Oaks CA, 91403. 
3. On or about October 16, 1995, I appeared at a 
status conference in the downtown civil courthouse of the 
Los Angeles Superior Court. Opposing counsel in that 
case was Los Angeles Deputy City Attorney Peter Ne!son 
King. 
4. 
While we were together there, Mr. King and I got 
into a side conversation in connection with another litiga- 
tion matter which we had in common, a federal civil 
rights lawsuit I had filed on behalf of E.W.A.P., 
Inc. 
against the City of Los Angeles in federal court, E.W.A.P., 
Inc. 
v. City of Los Angeles, U.S.Dt.Ct., C.D. Cal. No. 
CV-95-1711 AWT. The E.W.A.P. case involved a lawsuit by 
the owner of a single “adult entertainment” business 
which, in the same store, allowed its customers to view 
so-called “adult” video tapes and also conducted retail 
sales of sexually-oriented video tapes, magazines and 
related items. The City had previously taken the position 
that the operation of such a store, even in an otherwise 
properly zoned area, constituted a violation of a portion 
of Los Angeles Municipal Code § 12.70C which states that 
“[nJo person shall cause or permit the establishment or 
maintenance of more than one adult entertainment busi- 
ness 
in the same building, structure 
or portion 
thereof... .” E.W.A.P sued the City, seeking a declaration 
that that ordinance provision was unconstitutional, both 
on its face and as applied to E.W.A.P.’s store, in violation 
of applicable free speech guarantees of the First and 
Fourteenth Amendments to the United States Constitu- 
tion. 
- 
5. The E.W.A.P. store 
Th connection with which | 
brought that lawsuit was a new store which had first 
commenced operating in October of 1994. 
6. 
The E.W.A.P. lawsuit was heard together with 
another lawsuit seeking identical reliet that had been 
filed by another similar adult bookstore /arcade business. 
That case was entitled Diamond 
v. City of Los Angeles, 
U.S.Dt.Ct., C.D.Cal. No. CV-95-1534 AWT. 
7. 
Federal Judge A. Wallace Tashima issued a pre- 
liminary injunction against enforcement of that ordinance 
in those cases on June 9, 1995. 
8. Subsequent to the issuance of Judge Tashima’s 
preliminary injunction, but prior to my October 16th con- 
versation with Mr. King, the E.W.A.IP. case was settled on 
terms which permanently prevented 
the City from 
enforcing that ordinance against the plaintiffs in those 
actions, but which allowed dismissal of the actions with- 
out a judicial resolution of the question of the constitu- 
tionality of that ordinance provision. 
9. 
In any event, during my October loth conversa- 
tion with Mr. King concerning developments in that

DECLARATION OF G. RANDALL GARROU 
(28 U.S.C. § 1746) 
I, G. Randall Garrou, hereby declare as follows: 
1. Iam one of the attorneys for plaintiffs herein and 
make this declaration in support of plaintiffs’ application 
for a temporary restraining order and preliminary injunc- 
tion. 
2. 
I was the lead counsel for plaintiffs in Topanga 
Press, Inc., et al, v. City of Los Angeles, 989 F.2d 1524 (9th 
Cir. 1993), in which the court of appeals upheld the 
issuance of a preliminary injunction by the Honorable 
Richard A. Gadbois of this court preventing the City of 
Los Angeles from enforcing against the 22 businesses 
involved in that case, among other things, the very ordi- 
nance provision challenged in the present lawsuit. The 
district court case number of that case was C.D. Cal. No. 
CV 88-7838 RG. 
3. 
Following the Ninth Circuit's affirmation of the 
district court’s preliminary injunction and the Supreme 
Court's subsequent denial of the City’s petition for a writ 
of certiorari in that case, the City ultimately settled with 
the 22 business which were involved in that litigation, 
stipulating to a permanent injunction which conferred 
permanent grandfather status upon those businesses in 
return for their agreement to dismiss the lawsuit (includ- 
ing their declaratory judgment claims) without forcing it 
to a judicial conclusion which would resolve the plain- 
tiffs’ underlying constitutional challenges. 
4. 
While the settlement agreement in Topanga Press 
did not purport to preclude the City from further 
— 
| 
attempts to enforce against non-parties thereto the particu- 
lar portion of L.A.M.C. § 12.70C challenged herein, i.e., 
the portion construed by the City to prohibit, within a 
single store or business, the conducting of two or more 
adult entertainment activities, I nonetheless was then of 
the personal belief that the City would not in fact attempt 
to enforce that provision thereafter. 
5. However, in December of 1994, I learned from 
other lawyers representing two unrelated adult entertain- 
ment clients, that the City had indeed threatened enforce- 
ment of this very provision against their clients and that 
each of these two clients had brought independent 
actions in federal court challenging the constitutionality 
of this particular ordinance provision. I am informed that 
those cases were both filed in the Central District of 
California and bear the following names and numbers: 
E.W.A.P., Inc., v. City of Los Angeles, C.D. Cal. No. 95-1711 
AWT; and Diamond v. City of Los Angeles, C.D. Cal. No. 
CV-95-1534 AWT. 
6. 
I am further informed that Judge A. Wallace Tas- 
hima of this court issued a preliminary injunction on June 
9, 1995, to prevent the City from enforcing this particular 
provision of L.A.M.C. § 12.70C pending trial in each of 
those two cases. 
7. 
I am further informed and believe, and on that 
basis allege, that shortly after the issuance of those pre- 
liminary injunctions, the City entered into a settlement 
agreement in each of those cases similar to the one it had 
entered into in Topanga Press with my clients, i.e., it 
agreed to stipulate to a permanent injunction preventing 
it from enforcing the challenged provision of L.A.M.C.

missal of their declaratory judgment claims without a 
judicial resolution of their underlying constitutional chal- 
lenges to that ordinance. 
8. Iam further informed that the business involved 
in the action entitled Diamond v. City of Los Angeles first 
opened shortly after enactment of the 1983 ordinance 
(prohibiting more than one adult “business” in the same 
building) which was challenged in that action. I am 
informed that the business involved in the action entitled 
E.W.A.P., Inc. v. City of Los Angeles commenced operation 
as a brand rew business in October of 1994. 
9. 
Based upon all of these developments, 
it had 
been my impression until very recently, that the City had 
no serious intentions of attempting to enforce this partic- 
ular provision 
(i.e., the provision prohibiting a single 
adult entertainment business from conducting two or 
more adult business activities on its premises). However, 
within the law few months, I had a conversation with one 
of the deputy city attorneys in the Los Angeles City 
Attorney’s Land Use Department (the department which 
would normally defend the City in any challenge to its 
zoning ordinances) in which that deputy informed me 
that the City was still reserving the right, perhaps some- 
day, and in an ideal test case, to enforce that ordinance 
provision. I did not, however, from that conversation, 
conclude that any enforcement of the ordinance was 
imminent. 
10. 
On Wednesday, November 8, 1995, I had a tele- 
phone conversation with David M. Brown, the attorney 
who represented the plaintiff in E.W.A.P., Inc. v. City of 
11 
Los Angeles, having, called him in connection with an 
unrelated matter. However, during the course of that 
conversation, he happened to relate to me that he had 
been in a conversation with Deputy City Attorney Peter 
Nelson King within the last few weeks in which Mr. King 
told Mr. Brown that the City would probably be proceed- 
ing with an enforcement action (to enforce this same

Brown knew to be Alameda Books, located at 1901 S.

represents. 
11. 
For this reason, it is my belief that plaintiffs are 
urgently in need of a TRO which would simply preserve 
the status quo (by temporarily restraining the initiation of 
any new criminal prosecutions) pending a hearing on 
their application for a preliminary injunction, where the 
court, after being fully appraised and hearing full argu- 
ments from all parties, can more carefully evaluate the 
merits of plaintiffs’ constitutional challenges. 
12. 
It is also my belief that the City could not con- 
ceivably be prejudiced by plaintiffs’ request for a TRO in 
that the only relief being sought is a very brief preserva- 
tion of a status quo that I understand to have existed at 
both stores for years. The ordinance here challenged has 
been on the books since 1983 and the City has not, prior 
to now, ever initiated any criminal enforcement proceed- 
ings against them under this ordinance. Moreover, the

15. 
Moreover, I believe the purposes of rule 4.2.2.

because members of the Los Angeles City Attorney’s

an interpretation would defeat one of the purposes 
underlying Local Rule 4.2.2. This is particularly true 
where, as here, the City subsequently repeated this pat- 
tern by “mooting out” two more cases raising this same 
constitutional question (i.e., the Diamond and E.W.A.P 
cases) after the judge before whom such cases were pend- 
ing similarly saw fit to issue preliminary injunctions 
against the City.

SUMMARY AND RECOMMENDATION 
The existing “Adult Entertainment Zoning” regulations 
prohibit the establishment of an adult entertainment busi- 
ness within 1,000 feet of another such business or within 
500 feet of a church, school or public park. However, an 
ambiguity in the present language describing how such 
distance shall be measured has resulted in the Depart- 
ment of Building and Safety interpreting such provisions 
in a ™ nner which permits a combination of adult busi- 
nes 
» be established within the exterior structural 
wall: 
of a single building at one location. 
In accordance with instructions of the Council’s Planning 
and Environment Committee, the Planning Department, 
with the cooperation of the City Attorney, has prepared 
the attached proposed ordinance (Appendix A). This 
ordinance amends the adult entertainment zoning regula- 
tions (Section 12.70 of the Municipal Code) by expressly 
prohibiting the establishment of more than one adult 
entertainment business within the same building or por- 
tion thereof. 
A temporary moratorium ordinance provid- 
ing for a similar prohibition has been in effect since 
March 16, 1982, but is due to expire on March 11, 1983. 
The subject ordinance provides for a three-year amortiza- 
tion of nonconforming businesses (from March 10, 1982) 
in the case of two or more existing adult businesses 
located in a single building, with a maximum possible 
five years upon individual application in cases of eco- 
nomic hardship. 
The ordinance appears to be in keeping with the City 
Council’s intent of providing for the dispersion of adult

Request 
In response to a Council motion (Bernson-Stevenson- 
Wachs), the Planning and Environment Committee on 
January 26, 1982, recommended that the Pianning Depart- 
ment and City Attorney cooperatively prepare an appro- 
priate ordinance to prohibit more than one adult 
entertainment business in a single building on one site. 
The Committee recommended, further, that a six-month 
moratorium be imposed on the establishment of more 
than one new adult entertainment business at a single 
location. Such a moratorium was requested in order to 
allow sufficient time for study of the matter and the 
preparation of permanent regulations. 
Subsequently, such a moratorium ordinance was pre- 
pared by the City Attorney and approved by the Com- 
mission and City Council. The ordinance was published 
with an urgency clause on March 16, 1982 as Ordinance 
No. 156,509. On August 18, 1982 the City Council, by 
resolution, extended the provisions of this moratorium 
ordinance (then due to expire on September 12, 1982) for 
an additional 180 days, to March 11, 1983. 
As requested by the City Council, the attached proposed 
ordinance provides for the permanent prohibition of 
more than one adult entertainment business at one loca- 
tion.

Need to Amend Adult Entertainment Zoning Regulations 
Existing regulations provide that a new adult entertain- 
ment business, as defined in Section 12.70 of the Planning 
and Zoning Code, shall be located at least 1,000 feet from 
another such business or 500 feet from a religious institu- 
tion, school or public park. The Code provides that the 
measurement of this distance shall be in a straight line, 
without regard to intervening structures, from the closest 
exterior structural wall of each adult entertainment busi- 
ness (emphasis added). 
The intent of the City Council in enacting the existing 
adult entertainment zoning regulations in 1978 was to 
provide for the dispersion of all adult entertainment busi- 
ness in the City. This being the case, it is quite apparent 
that the Council in no way intended to allow more than 
one such business to be established at a single location in 
the same building. However, the above-noted language 
relating to the measurement of distances specified in the 
ordinance has resulted in an ambiguity with respect to 
the number of businesses which may be permitted within 
the “exterior structural wall” of an establishment at one 
location. The Department of Building and Safety has 
interpreted this provision in such a manner as to permit a 
combination of such businesses to be located within the 
exterior structural walls of a single building at one site. 
Thus the need has arisen to enact the aforementioned 
interim moratorium ordinance and the subject proposed 
ordinance.

Description of Proposed Ordinance 
The subject proposed ordinance (Appendix A, attached) 
would prohibit the addition of any adult entertainment 
business to any existing adult entertainment business or 
the establishment or maintenance of more than one new 
adult entertainment business in the same building, struc- 
ture or portion thereof. Upon the recommendation of the 
City Attorney, an amortization provision has been 
included in the ordinance which would permit the con- 
tinued operation of two or more adult business which 
existed in a single building on March 10, 1982, for a 
maximum period of three years after such date. (March 
10, 1982 was the date the City Council adopted the afore- 
mentioned interim moratorium ordinance.) 
Also, upon advice of the City Attorney, a related provi- 
sion has been included in the subject ordinance to pro- 
vide that in the case of two or more adult businesses 
operating in the same building on March 10, 1982, the 
business established first may be continued, but that the 
other businesses be discontinued by March 10, 1985. The 
ordinance further provides, however, that in special hard- 
ship cases a Zoning Administrator may permit more than 
one adult entertainment business to remain in the same 
building for up to five years after March 10, 1982, pro- 
vided that an individual application is filed requesting 
such an extension (no fee to be charged) and a Zoning 
Administrator determines that: 
(1) 
the adult entertainment business is subject to a 
written lease, entered into prior to March 10, 
1982, with a termination date extending beyond 
March 10, 1985; or 
31 
(2) 
the business involves the investment of money 
in real property, a leasehold, improvements or 
stock in trade such that a longer period is neces- 
sary to prevent undue financial hardship. 
The ordinance stipulates that such applications for con- 
tinuation shall be filed with the Office of Zoning Admin- 
istration but that no such application shall be approved if 
a Zoning Administrator finds that the nonconforming 
adult entertainment business is being maintained in vio- 
lation of any provision of the Municipal Code. 
Finally, the ordinance provides that the Office of Zoning 
Administration shall process applications for continua- 
tion in accordance with the existing procedure for 
appeals from Building Department Orders as set forth 
Section 12.27A of the Code, with appeals possible to the 
Board of Zoning Appeals. 
Conclusion 
Enactment of the subject ordinance is necessary to clarify, 
on a permanent basis, existing provisions of the Adult 
Entertainment Zoning regulations contained in Section 
12.70 of the Municipal Code. It is apparent that in enact- 
ing these regulations in 1978, the City Council did not 
intend to permit more than one adult business in the 
same building at one location. Staff, therefore, recom- 
mends approval of the ordinance which will correct this 
inconsistency in the regulations and which contains rea- 
sonable amortization provisions applicable to existing 
businesses.

SUMMARY AND RECOMMENDATIONS 
A. 
Types of Ordinances to Control “Adult Eritertain- 
ment” Uses 
Two methods of regulating adult entertainment busi- 
ness via land use regulations have develuped in the 
United States. They are: 1) the concentration of such 
uses in a single area of the city as in Boston; and 2) 
the dispersal of such uses, as in the City of Detroit. 
The Detroit ordinance has been challenged and 
upheld by the U.S. Supreme Court (Young vs. Ameri- 
can Mint-Theaters, 96 S. Ct. 771, 1976}.

The scope of “adult entertainment” ordinances 
encompasses a variety of adult activities. For exam-

studios, adult motels, arcades, and certain similar 
businesses. Many other ordinances studied, however, 
are less broad in their coverage. The Detroit ordi- 
nance, for instance does not regulate massage parlors 
or adult motels, nor does it provide for the closing of 
any such businesses by amortization, which would 
be necessitated by the retroactive application of such

The U.S. Supreme Court in Young vs. American 
Mini-Theaters pointed out, as one of the bases for 
upholding the Detroit ordinance, that the regulation 
did not limit the number of “adult entertainment 
businesses. Our study has indicated that the practical 
effect of literal adoption of “Detroit” language with- 
out modification in the City of Los Angeles would be 
to limit the potential locations for such businesses 
rather severely. Due to the predominance of commer- 
cial zoning in “strips” along major and secondary 
streets, an ordinance preventing “adult entertain- 
ment” business from locating within 500 feet of resi- 
dentially zoned property would, in effect, limit such 
businesses to those areas of the City where there is 
commercial zoning of greater than 500 feet in depth. 
Areas with such commercial frontage would include 
downtown Los Angeles, a small part of Hollywood, 
Westwood, and Century City. A few industrial areas 
would also afford a separation of this distance from 
residential properties. The limitation of 1,000 feet 
between establishments (as provided in the Detroit 
ordinance) would likely be inappropriate in the City 
of Los Angeles inasmuch as commercial zoning is 
located in a strip pattern along most of the City’s 
approximate 1,400 miles of major and secondary 
highways. (It is estimated that approximately 400 
miles of such “strip” commercial zoning exists in the 
City.)

A Boston-type ordinance (concentration) to control 
adult entertainment businesses would not be accept-

In the event legislation is enacted in the City of Los

ordinance (dispersion) which requires a distance of 
1000 feet between establishments and 500 feet from

~ Existing locational patterns of adult entertainment

a dispersion of establishments. (Such patterns are

If dispersion is desired in Los Angeles, an ordinance 
should be designed specifically for the City. (Direct

PURPOSE AND SCOPE 
On January 12, 1977, the Los Angeles City Council 
instructed this Department, with the assistance of other 
City agencies, to conduct a comprehensive study to deter- 
mine whether the concentration of so-called “adult enter- 
tainment” establishments has a blighting or degrading 
effect on nearby properties and/or neighborhoods. The 
term “adult entertainment” is a general term utilized by 
the Planning staff to colicctively refer to businesses which 
primarily engage in the sale of material depicting sex or 
in providing certain sexual services. These would include 
the following: adult bookstores; X-rated theaters; adult 
motels with X-rated entertainment; massage parlors; sex- 
ual therapy establishments (other than those operated by 
a licensed psychologist, psychiatrist, etc.); and nude, top- 
less or bottomless bars and restaurants. 
During the past few years, there has been increasing 
concern in Los Angeles over the proliferation of such 
sexually oriented businesses. The derivation of such con- 
cern is varied — religious, moral, sociological and eco- 
nomic. The positions advocated by the public range from 
a “laissez faire” attitude to outright moral indignation 
and demand for prohibition. 
It should be noted at this time that the topic of news- 
racks, was not dealt with in this study. The primary 
reason for not considering newsracks is that, in addition 
to the absence of a specific Council request for this 
Department to deal with that subject, this matter has been 
and continues to be a topic of litigation in our state 
courts. Additionally, other public agencies, including the 


PAGE 34
53 
City Attorney, Bureau of Street Maintenance, and Build- 
ing and Safety, are presently pursuing assignments 
regarding newsracks, and it is premature to determine 
whether newsracks could feasibly be studied as “adult 
entertainment” businesses, from a practical or constitu- 
tional standpoint. 
: 
In giving the Planning Department this assignment, the 
City Council essentially called for a fact-finding process 
to determine whether adult entertainment establishments, 
where they exist in concentration, cause blight and dete- 
rioration. When this question has been posed to the pub- 
lic, there have frequently been anguished retorts to the 
effect that “the answer is so obvious it is ridiculous to 
even ask the question,” and “what is the City waiting for 
before it takes action to eliminate these scourges of soci- 
ety?” 
On the other side of the spectrum, certain parties who are 
against the adoption of any regulations regarding “adult 
entertainment” question the legitimacy of the govern- 
ment’s interest in the subject; and they have noted that 
magazines as “scurrilous” as those sold in adult book- 
stores are also available in the markets and drugstores 
where the likelihood of perusal by youngsters is obvi- 
ously greater than within the confines of an adult book- 
store (where no person under 18 years of age is allowed). 
In completing this study, the Planning Department has 
made every effort to ensure a fair and unbiased analysis 
of “adult entertainment.” The staff has been instructed to 
objectively review information of a factual nature; and, 
although the personal feelings of organized groups and 
the public at large were forcefully expressed at the two 
54 
public meetings and in the study questionnaires, the staff 
has maintained independence from such strong emotions 
in evaluating the data gathered. 
As noted above, the staff has specifically been given the 
charge to determine whether the concentration of “adult 
entertainment” establishments has any blighting or 
degrading effect on the neighborhoods in which they 
reside. We did not consider the specific nature or content 
of the materials or services rendered, advertised or prom- 
ised, for this would have constituted a censor-like role for 
the Department which was neither desired nor requested 
by the Council. 
This study has focused on the Hollywood community as 
well as portions of Studio City and North Hollywood as 
those areas of Los Angeles having the greatest concentra- 
tion of “adult entertainment” establishments. In order to 
assess the effect of the concentration of “adult entertain- 
ment” establishments 
in these areas, the staff has 
analyzed such factors as changes in assessed property 
values, and reviewed various crime statistics as well as 
other demographic and related data as available from the 
U.S. Census. In addition, the Department has reviewed 
various established approaches to the regulation of 
“adult entertainment” business, including legislation 
already enacted by other jurisdictions, and earlier efforts 
of the City of Los Angeles to regulate such businesses. 
By means of two public meetings on the subject con- 
ducted by representatives of the City Planning Commis- 
sion, and through the use of a mail survey questionnaire, 
the Department has also attempted to provide additional 
documentation relative to the actual or perceived impact

II. 
METHODS CURRENTLY USED TO 
REGULATE “ADULT ENTERTAINMENT” BUSINESSES 
A. APPROACHES TO THE REGULATION OF ADULT 
ENTERTAINMENT BY LAND USE REGULATION 
Two primary methods of regulating “adult entertain- 
ment” businesses via land use regulations have 
developed in the United States: the concentratior. 
approach, as evidenced by the “Combat Zone” in 
Boston, and the dispersal approach, initially devel- 
oped by Detroit. 
1. 
Boston Approach 
In Boston the “Combat Zone” was officially 
established by designation of an overlay Adult 
Entertainment District in November of 1974. The 
purpose of the overlay district was to create an 
area in which additional special uses would be 
permitted in designated Commercial Zones 
which were not permitted in these zones on a 
citywide basis. 
The “Combat Zone” had existed unofficially for 
many years in Boston, as the area in question 
contained a majority of the “adult entertainment” 
facilities in the City. The ordinance was adopted 
in response to concern over the spreading of such 
uses to neighborhoods where they were deemed 
to be inappropriate. Other considerations 
56 
included facilitating the policing of such activ- 
ities and allowing those persons who do not care 
to be subjected to such businesses to avoid them. 
Under the Boston ordinance, adult bookstores 
and “commercial entertainment businesses” are 
considered conditional or forbidden uses except 
in the Business Entertainment District. Existing 
“adult entertainment” businesses are permitted 
to continue as non-conforming uses, but, if dis- 
continued for a period of two years, may not be 
re-established. Establishment of uses in areas of 
the city other than the “Combat Zone” requires a 
public hearing before the Zoning Board of 
Appeals. 
The effectiveness and appropriateness of the Bos- 
ton approach is a subject of controversy. There 
has been some indication that it has resulted in 
an increase in crime within the district and that 
there is an increased vacancy rate in the sur- 
rounding office buildings. Due to complaints of 
serious criminal incidents, law enforcement 
activities have been increased and a number of 
liquor licenses in the area have been revoked. 
Since the “Combat Zone” and most of the sur- 
rounding area are part of various redevelopment 
projects, however, the change in character of the 
area cannot be attributed solely to the existence 
of “adult entertainment” businesses. 
In Los Angeles, the Police Department has inves- 
tigated the effect of “adult entertainment” busi- 
nesses in Hollywood and found a link between 
the clustering of these establishments and an 
increase in crime. (See Section V, pages 51 to 55). 
For this reason, and due to the enforcement prob- 
lems created by such concentrations, the Police 
Department is not in favor of a concentration 
—

57 
approach in the-City of Los Angeles. Public 
testimony at hearings and through Planning 
Department questionnaires has indicated an 
overwhelming public disapproval of this 
approach for the City of Los Angeles. 
Detroit Approach 
The City of Detroit has developed a contrasting 
approach to the control of “adult entertainment” 
businesses. The Detroit Ordinance attempts to 
disperse adult bookstores and theaters by provid- 
ing that such uses cannot, without special per- 
mission, be located within 1000 feet of any other 
“regulated uses” or within 500 feet of a residen- 
tially zoned area. 
This ordinance was an amendment to an existing 
anti-skid row ordinance which attempted to pre- 
vent further neighborhood deterioration by dis- 
persing cabarets, motels, pawnshops, billiard 
halls, taxi dance halls and similar establishments 
rather than allowing them to concentrate. 
The ordinance was immediately challenged and 
eventually was upheld by the United States 
Supreme Court. (Young vs American Mini Theaters 
96 Supreme Ct. 771, 1976.) 
In response to our request, data supplied by the 
City of Detroit Police Department indicates that 
the combination of the dispersal ordinance and a 
related ordinance prohibiting the promotion of 
pornography have been an effective tool in con- 
trolling adult businesses. To date, 18 adult book- 
stores and 6 adult theaters have been closed. 
There are 51 such businesses still in operation in 
Detroit and 38 pending court cases for various 
ordinance violations.

1. Red Light Abatement Procedure 
Red light abatement is a mechanism authorized 
by state law which allows local government to 
control criminal sexual behavior by controlling 
the places in which such behavior occurs. 
Sec. 11225 of the California Penal Code generally 
provides that every building or place used for 
illegal gambling, lewdness, assignation, or pros- 
titution, or where such acts occur is a nuisance 
which shall be enjoined, abated, and prevented. 
There are three basic steps involved in the City’s 
application of the Red Light Abatement Pro- 
cedures: 
(a) A complaint is filed by the City Attorney 
based upon the declarations of police offi- 
cers of instances of prostitution taking place 
on the premises. 
(b) The City attempts to obtain a preliminary 
injunction to shut down the business until 
completion of the scheduled trial. If the City 
succeeds, the premises may only be re- 
opened as a legitimate business until the 
time of the trial. 
(c) 
At the trial, the burden is on the City to 
prove that prohibited acts occurred on the 
premises. The remedy may be closure of the 
premises for all purposes for one year, plac- 
ing the building in the custody of the court, 
or an order preventing the use of the prem- 
ises for prostitution forever.

Complaints may be filed by citizens, and Sec. 
11228 of the Code provides that in Red Light 
Abatement Actions “evidence of the general rep-

proving the existence of a nuisance”. 
This method has been used successfully by the 
City to abate adult entertainment establishments 
in Hollywood along Western Avenue. Although

sites, a Red Light Abatement conviction can affect 
the ability of an owner or operator to obtain a 
permit for a similar business at another site (see 
permit requirements supra). Due to the require- 
ment of a court proceeding, however, this method 
of control is both time consuming and expensive.

C. OTHER REGULATION OF ADULT ENTERTAIN- 
MENT BUSINESSES IN LOS ANGELES 
Regulation of adult entertainment businesses has a 
long history in Los Angeles. In 1915 the “prevalence 
of sex evils arising out of massage parlors” caused 
the City Council then to enact Section 27.03 
(L.A.M.C.) as “a safeguard against the deterioration 
of the social life of the community.”? The ordinance 
provided: 
“(a) It shall be unlawful for any person to admin- 
ister, for hire or reward, to any person of the 
opposite sex, any massage, any alcohol rub or 
similar treatment, any fomentation, any bath or 
electric or magnetic treatment, nor shall any per- 
son cause or permit in or about his place or 
business or in connection with his business, any 
agent, employee, or servant or any other person 
under his control or supervision, to administer 
any such treatment to any person of the opposite 
sex.”5, 
This provision remained in the Code, in one form or 
another, until a similar Los Angles County ordinance 
was declared invalid in 1972 due to the preemption 
of the criminal aspects of sexual activity by the 
State.4 
In reaching its conclusion, the court referred to the 
discussion of the Los Angeles City ordinance in In Re

the limiting of criminal sexual activity.

IV. 
METHODOLOGY AND ANALYSIS 
Methodology 
In complying with the City Council’s instructions, the 
Department has utilized various available data sources, 
including property assessment data, U. S. Census data, 
and obtained other information germane to the subject in 
an effort to determine on an empirical basis, the effects (if 
any) of adult entertainment 
facilities on surrounding 
business and other properties. The Department also 
reviewed sales data of commercial and residential prop- 
erty in areas containing concentrations of adult entertain- 
ment businesses and in “control areas” containing no 
such concentrations. The staff also attempted to secure 
information on the sales volume of commercial proper- 
ties, but was unable to obtain this information. 
It should be emphasized that, in conducting this study, 
every effort was made by the Department to preclude the 
introduction of subjective judgment or other bias, except 
where the opinions of other individuals or groups were 
specifically solicited.* It was the Department's intent to 
base any conclusions entirely on relevant data and other 
factual information which became available during the 
course of conducting the study. 
* 
Expert opinions were requested from realtors, realty boards, 
appraisers and lenders through letters and questionnaires. 
The Department also sent letters to local members of the 
American Sociological Association requesting their 
assistance in this study. Their replies were limited in number 
and not significant in terms of this study.

the 1970 base year and for 1976. 
Similar calculations covering the same time period 
were also prepared for “control areas” (containing no 
concentration of adult entertainment businesses) but 
which were similar, in terms of zoning and land use, 
or which were located in geographical proximity to 
the study area nodes. Four such control areas were 
selected.

Conclusion - Changes in Assessed Valuation 
On the basis of the foregoing, there would seem 
to be some basis to conclude that the assessed 
valuation of property within the study areas con- 
taining concentrations of adult entertainment 
businesses have generally tended to increase to 
lesser degree than similar areas without such 
concentrations. However, in the staff’s opinion 
there would appear to be insufficient evidence to 
support the contention that concentrations of 
sex-oriented businesses have been the primary 
cause of these patterns of change in Assessed 
valuations between 1970 and 1976. However, 
responses to the Department’s mail question- 
naires from real estate representatives and 
appraisers have indicated that in their opinion, 
concentrations of adult entertainment businesses 
have, in some cases, had a direct negative impact 
on property values.

SURVEY QUESTIONNAIRE CONDUCTED BY 
DEPARTMENT OF CITY PLANNING 
1. Description of Survey 
In order to determine additional factual data 
relating to the subject and to seek the comments 
and opinions of property owners, businessmen, 
realtors, real estate boards, real estate appraisers, 
representatives of banks, Chambers of Commerce, 
and others, the Department conducted a mail sur- 
vey. Two questionnaires were developed. One 
was designed primarily for businessmen and resi- 
dential property owners and is hereinafter 
referred to as the General Questionnaire. The sec- 
ond was designed for realtors, real estate 
appraisers and lenders and is hereinafter referred 
to as the Appraiser Questionnaire. A copy of the 
100 
two questionnaires is contained in the Appendix. 
The completed questionnaires, together with 
other letters relative to this subject, are on file in 
Room 510, Los Angeles City Hall. 
The General Questionnaire was mailed 
to all 
property owners (of other than property in single- 
family use) within a 500-foot radius of each of the 
five study areas. The questionnaire was also dis- 
tributed to various community groups (including 
local and area Chambers of Commerce) and at the 
public meeting in Hollywood and in Northridge. 
The Appraiser Questionnaire was mailed to all 
members of the American Institute of Real Estate 
Appraisers having a Los Angeles City address 
and to members of the California Association of 
Realtors whose office is located in the vicinity of 
the study areas. 
Each of the two questionnaires contained spaces 
fer a respondent to check answers to a series of 
questions relating to the overall effect (if any) of 
adult entertainment establishments on nearby 
properties. 
It should be emphasized that the 
Department intentionally structured the “objec- 
tive response” portion of the questionnaires so as 
to reduce “bias” and to solicit the maximum 
range of responses to any specific question. For 
example, a respondent could check “positive”, 
“ne sative” or “no effect” in response to the ques- 
tion 
. 
. 
. “What overall effect do you feel that 
adult entertainment establishments have on a 
neighborhood?” 
In addition to the direct response portion of the 
questionnaire, information of a more subjective 
nature was also solicited. For example, after each 
question, space was provided for a respondent to 


PAGE 67
101 
list any comments or examples which might per- 
tain to a specific question. The beginning of each 
questionnaire also invited the respondent to write 
comments in the space provided or on a separate 
sheet. 
Between February 10 and April 30, 1977, a total of 
approximately 4,000 questionnaires were mailed 
(with return envelopes provided) or otherwise 
distributed to businessmen, real estate appraisers, 
realtors, representatives of banks and savings and 
loan institutions, the owners of multiple-unit resi- 
dential property, and others. Of this number, 694 
questionnaires were completed and returned to 
the Department (an overall 17.4 percent rate of 
return). 
In addition, the Department received 197 non- 
solicited completed questionnaires from property 
owners in Studio City. These questionnaires were 
distributed in a private mailing by a private indi- 
vidual. The subject mailing included a replica of 
the Department’s appraiser questionnaire, 
together with written material alleging City intent 
to create an adult entertainment zone in Studio 
City (copy included as Appendix D-2). According 
to the subject individual's testimony at the public 
hearing on April 27, 1977, 11,000 replica question- 
naires were mailed. Due to the prejudicial nature 
of the mailing, these questionnaires are not 
included in the study. However, the staff did tab- 
ulate the subject responses and the tabulation and 
summary are included in Appendix D-3. All per-

Responses to the foregoing questions reveal that adult 
entertainment businesses are perceived by the majority of 
respondents as exerting a negative impact on surround- 
ing businesses and residential properties. 
Whether or not such negative impacts have actually 
occurred, or only perceived to have occurred, cannot be 
readily determined, empirically, on the basis of this sur- 
vey. However, in terms of the attitudes of the respondents 
toward such businesses, the conclusion must be drawn 
that the overall effect on surrounding properties is con- 
sidered to be negative. 
Among the adverse effects of adult entertainment estab- 
lishments cited by businessmen are: 
- 
Difficulty in renting office space 
- 
Difficulty in keeping desirable tenants 
- 
Difficulty in recruiting employees 
- 
Limits hours of operation (evening hours) 
- 
Deters patronage from women and families; general 
reduced patronage 
Of those businessmen indicating that they have not seri- 
ously considered moving because of nearby concentra- 
tions of adult entertainment business, the most frequent 
response was that they had been in the area a great many 
years, and to establish elsewhere would be too risky and/ 
or that their investment was too great to move. A few 
respondents indicated that it is the adult entertainment 
businesses that should move, not they. 
The few businessmen commenting that they would not 
consider expanding in their current location indicated 
that their business did not warrant expansion.

Several businessmen indicated that their businesses are 
relatively unaffected by nearly [sic] adult entertainment 
establishments. Among the businesses cited are a com- 
mercial art studio; a building trades contractor; a mail 
order business; a telephone answering service and a 
wholesaler. 
Among the few positive effects cited by businessmen is 
the increase in business for certain non-adult entertain- 
ment businesses such as tourist-serving businesses (e.g. 
car rental agencies). “The bad effect it might have is 
cancelled out by the business it does attract; x-rated

Many respondents commented on the crimes associated 
with adult entertainment establishments: prostitution, 
dope, theft, robbery, etc. A high percentage of respon- 
dents report they do not feel safe in such areas. 
A high percentage of respondents commented on their 
concern for the effects of adult entertainment environ- 
ment on the morals and safety of children. 
A high percentage of respondents commented on the

sleazy; shabby, blighted, tasteless, etc. Also, many com- 
mented on the increased incidence of litter and graffiti.

Cluster Analysis “Used by Community Analysis 
Bureau to Describe Various Parts of the City” 
The last U.S. Decennial Census was conducted 
on April 1, 1970. With the proliferation of adult 
entertainment business it would seem appropri- 
ate to include as background information a 
description of the socio-economic and physical 
characteristics of the areas under study, as 
revealed by census data. Such a description may 
provide insight as to the underlying factors con- 
tributing to the concentration of sex-oriented 
business in the areas under study. 
— 
An excellent available source providing such a 
description is a 1974 report prepared by the 
City’s Community Analysis Bureau (CAB) con- 
cerning the “State of the City”.* In this docu- 
ment, the CAB has utilized a statistical technique 
known as “cluster analysis” to identify specific 
areas within the City which have common char- 
acteristics, as revealed by census data. In con- 
ducting this study, the CAB made use of 66 
census data items (or variables) which were 
selected from the entire spectrum of socio-eco- 
nomic and physically descriptive data items 
available for all census tracts in the City. 
The U.S. Census Bureav reports data on 
numerous geographical levels, the “census tract” 
being the smallest geographical area for which 
data is maintained and reported on a regular 
basis. There are 750 such census tract areas in the 
City, each containing a population of slightly

geographical section of the City.

On the basis of the foregoing 1970 Census data, it is 
possible to develop a general description of the two 
study area modes containing adult entertainment 
businesses in the Valley. As indicated above, such a 
description must necessarily be based on data apply- 
ing to entire census tracts, even through [sic] the study 
areas may encompass only portions of tracts. 
Residents of the Studio City study area node in 1970 
were predominantly an upper middle income group, 
with a relatively high percentage of college gradu- 
ates. High School dropouts were considerably below 
the citywide norm. Eight out of ten employed persons 
were in “white collar” jobs. The percentage of fami- 
lies receiving welfare or in poverty status was consid- 
erably below 
the citywide percentage. The 
unemployment rate was slightly higher than that of 
the entire city. 
The median value of owner occupied homes in the 
Studio City area was more than $12,400 higher than 
the City median. About one-half of the housing units 
were one-unit structures. Apartment rental rates were 
also higher than the city as a whole. The percentage 
of one-unit, owner occupied housing units built 
before 1940 (24.1 percent) approached the citywide 
median of 28.5 percent. 
r 
With regard to crime statistics (as of 1970), robberies 
per 100 population in the Studio City area were below 
the rate for the city as a whole (.172 and .454, respec- 
tively), although the number of burglaries per 100 
improved parcels (13.86) was close to the citywide 
rate of 14.96. Total arrests per 100 population (4.23) 
were about one-half of the 8.26 rate which prevailed 
citywide. 
The North Hollywood study area contrasts rather 
sharply with the above described Studio City area. In 
North Hollywood, median family income was $9,471

in 1970 - lower than the citywide median of $10,535 - 
and considerably lower than the $15,672 median 
income of residents in the Studio City study area. 
Sixty-one percent of employed persons were in 
“white collar” jobs in North Hollywood, compared 
with 80 percent in Studio City and 57 percent in the 
entire city. The percentage of families in a poverty 
status in North Hollywood was considerably higher 
than in Studio city (10.0 percent and 3.7 percent, 
respectively). The percent of families in North Holly- 
wood receiving welfare was higher than in Studio 
City, but lower than in the entire city. Unemployment 
rates, however, were lower in North Hollywod [sic]

Housing values were considerably lower in North 
Hollywood than in Studio City, and slightly lower 
than average values throughout the entire city. 
Median montly [sic] rents were lower in North Holly-

Hollywood study area, compared with only 28.5 per-

This section of the report considers the number and per- 
centages of adult entertainment businesses in the City, 
changes in these businesses since 1975, and more speci- 
fically, crime rates in the Hollywood area as compared to 
crime rates, citywide. 
The following information was compiled by the Los 
Angeles Police Department and shows the incidence of 
certain adult entertainment establishments as of two dif- 
ferent time periods - November of 1975 and December 
31, 1976. The statistics show a decrease in massage par- 
lors, bookstores, arcades and theaters and a slight rise in 
adult motels. This was during the same period of time 
that there was stepped-up surveillance and deployment

During the period included in this report, the Citywide 
deployment of police personnel rose by 21.2 percent. 
However, with the surge of crime in the Hollywood Area, 
deployment there increased by 30.6 percent, 9.4 percent 
higher than the rest of the City. Included in this figure is a 
29.4 percent hike in uniformed officers and 35.6 percent 
rise in investigators to cope with the criminal elements. 
This survey reflects a seven-year span during which time 
the Adult Entertainment Establishment in the Hollywood 
Area proliferated from a mere 11 establishments to an 
astonishing number of 88 such locations. The overall 
deleterious effect to the entire community is evident in 
the statistics provided. The overwhelming increase in 
prostitution, robberies, assaults, thefts, and the propor- 
tionate growth in police personnel deployed throughout 
Hollywood, are all representative of blighting results that 
the clustering of Adult Entertainment Establishments has 
on the entire community. These adverse social effects not 
only infect the environs immediately adjacent to the par- 
lors but creates a malignant atmosphere in which crime 
spreads to epidemic proportions. 
The remaining sections of the Police Department report 
are letters and signature petitions from concerned busi- 
nessmen, clergy, merchants, citizens and police officers 
and are in the file and available for inspection upon 
request. The following paragraph summarizes this sec- 
tion of the Police Department report. 
The police officer reports can be summarized as follows: 
all officers felt the sex-oriented businesses either contrib- 
uted to or were directly responsible for the crime prob- 
lems in the Hollywood area. The officers felt the sex 


PAGE 90
130 
shops were an open invitation to undesirables and 
t} creby directly caused the deterioration of neighbor- 
hoods. Also, it was suggested that these businesses pur- 
posely cluster in order to establish 
a “strength in 
numbers” type effect, once they establish a foothold in a 
neighborhood they drive the legitimate businesses out. 
The letters from the businessmen, clubs, churches and 
concerned citizens were all in support of police efforts to 
close adult entertainment 
facilities. The letters all 
expressed the feeling that the sex shops attracted homo- 
sexuals, perverts, prostitutes and other undesirables and 
directly contributed to the decline of the Hollywood area.

ADULT ENTERTAINMENT STUDY 
We wish to thank you for your interest in the above matter. 
Recently, residents of the Studio City area have received 
erroneous information regarding the activities of this 
Department. Specifically, they have been informed that it 
is our intent to create an “adult entertainment zone” on 
Ventura Boulevard. This information is not correct. 
In January of this year, the Los Angeles City Council 
instructed the Planning Department to conduct a study to 
determine whether so-called “adult entertainment” estab- 
lishments, where they exist in concentration, tend to have 
a deteriorating or blighting effect on adjacent properties 
and areas. Since that time, the Department staff has been 
evaluating data from the public and governmental agen- 
cies to determine whether evidence of such effects exists. 
Within the next two months, the analysis of the informa- 
tion gathered will be presented to the Los Angeles City 
Council which will make a decision as to whether adop- 
tion of regulations is appropriate. 
We regret that you were sent alarming erroneous infor- 
mation; if you have any further questions, please call my 
staff at 485-3508 or 485-3868.

On July 26, 1977, the Planning Committee considered a 
comprehensive study (orange book) submitted by the

the effect of such businesses on the citizens of Los

CPC 26475 
Some cities in the United States have adopted ordinances 
generally calling for either dispersal or concentration of 
such adult entertainment establishments. Early this year, 
the Los Angeles City Council instructed the City Plan- 
ning Department and other concerned City agencies to 
study the subject of adult entertainment establishments in 
the City in order to determine whether concentration of 
such businesses have a detrimental effect on the areas in 
which they are located. 
It is the opinion of your Committee that a “Detroit” type 
ordinance could be effective in battling the proliferation 
of sex parlors and may also discourage the opening of 
new ones. In addition, a stopgap ordinance should be 
adopted to prevent new businesses until a final ordinance 
can be drafted by the City Attorney’s Office. 
Therefore, after careful consideration, the Planning Com- 
mittee feels that an ordinance similar to that adopted by 
the City of Detroit for dispersal of such businesses should 
be prepared by the City Attorney with the cooperation of 
the Planning Department; they were so instructed. In the

nance declaring a moratorium.

businesses from clustering in various areas of the City, we 
RECOMMEND that the ordinance accompanying the 
Council file, approved by the City Attorney as to form 
and legality and approved by the City Planning Commis- 
sion, declaring a four-month moratorium on the estab- 
lishment of “adult entertainment” businesses* in the City 
of Los Angeles BE PLACED UPON ITS PASSAGE; said

281 of the City Charter.

Your PLANNING Committee reports as follows: 
On April 7, 1976, the City Council adopted a Finance 
Committee report (CF 76-48) to the effect that ... no City 
department may undertake a study where the cost would 
exceed $5,000 without approval from the Council. .. 
. 
On November 30, 14,6, your Committee considered the 
following three matters: 
1. 
The Detroit decision governing adult entertain- 
ment e.g. pornography, pornographic theaters as 
well as adult book stores, etc. (CF 75-4521, S-2, 
S-2A, S-2B and S-4). 
2. 
The Planning Department Work Program 
3. 
Architectural Districts (CF 72-4167, CPC 24580) 
The foregoing was reviewed by your Committee with 
representatives of the Planning Department, Police 
Department and the City Attorney. They advised that the 
City should develop background information and statis- 
tics relative to the effects of concentration of adult enter- 
tainment establishments in order to have an adequate 
legal basis for possible future court action. Such informa- 
tion should include economic impact and blighting infiu- 
ences of such establishments. They also pointed out that 
at the present time the Code Studies Section of the Plan- 
ning Department has many planning projects in process 
which have previously been assigned by the Council. 
Therefore, if the Council desires to proceed with a study 
169 
on the adult entertainment issue, many of these project 
studies will be delayed. 
With respect to Item No. 3 above, your Committee feels 
that the report from the Director of Planning relating to 
Architectural Districts has merit and should be returned 
to the Planning Department for more study with the 
cooperation of the City Attorney and possibly the Cul- 
tural Heritage Board. We recognize the fact that work in 
this study could be delayed three to four months if the 
Council approves the pornographic study. 
| We realize that the pornographic issue is one of the major 
problems faced by our City. Citizen complaints to the 
Police Department and Council offices are never-ending. 
So called “porno operations,” particularly when they are 
concentrated, lead to the deterioration of neighborhoods. 
The Detroit ordinance which involves zoning regulations 
has been upheld by the U.S. Supreme Court. This ordi- 
nance prohibits new commercial set ventures within 1000 
feet of similar existing facilities, and the operator is 
required to obtain approval of 51% of the residents and 
owners of other businesses within 500 feet of the pro- 
posed sex location. 
In order to approach the problem in a reasonable manner, 
it is our feeling that the pornographic study should be 
given top priority and that the Planning Department 
should be authorized to proceed with the study imme- 
diately at an estimated manpower allocation cost of 
$25,000 for an approximate three to four-month period. 
In our opinion the City should make an effort to limit the 
spread of commercial sex businesses. In no way is this

zens as we realize that there are those who desire to visit 
such establishments. We do recognize that in the past few 
years the courts seem to frown upon anti-obscenity laws 
as an invasion of free expression. 
Therefore, after careful consideration, we (two members 
present) RECOMMEND by the adoption of this report as 
follows: 
1. 
In accordance with Council policy under CF

rizea to proceed with an approximate three to 
four-month study of the pornographic problem 
(estimated manpower allocation cost $25,000), 
and that other projects under study be delayed 
for such period of time. 
2. 
That other involved City departments cooperate 
in providing information and assistance to the 
Planning Department during the preparation of 
said report.

(CPC 24580) relating to Architectural Districts be trans- 
mitted to the Planning Department for further study with 
the assistance of the City Attorney and the Cultural Heri- 
tage Board (it being understood that work on such study 
may be delayed three to four months).

INTRODUCTION 
During the past several years, the City of Los Angeles has 
been confronted with the problem of proliferating sex 
oriented Adult Entertainment Establishments. This type 
of location includes, but is not limited to, massage par- 
‘ors, adult movie theaters and arcades, adult bookstores, 
and other sexually oriented enterprises. It is believed that 
these businesses may have had blighting effects on both 


PAGE 122
174 
business and residential communities, adversely impact- 
ing the general crime problem. Efforts to deal with this 
situation by the Police Department have been centered 
around criminal enforcement. However, it has become 
apparent that other methods must be utilized if the dele- 
terious effects that sex oriented Adult Entertainment 
Establishments have on the City are to be curtailed. For 
this reason, the City Planning Department has com- 
menced a survey to evaluate specifically what effect the 
clustering of these sex oriented Adult Entertainment 
Establishments has on the immediate environs, both 
socially and economically. This report is being submitted 
to the Planning Department as an addendum to the statis- 
tical data they have gathered as a legislative base for a 
City ordinance which would, by zoning, restrict the 
number of such establishments in a given area. 
Similar efforts have been found constitutionally sound in 
the City of Detroit following the American Mini Theater, 
Inc., vs. Young case. The Hollywood Area has been 
selected for this study because the problem is most preva- 
lent there due to the clustering and proliferation of the 
sex oriented Adult Entertainment Establishments during 
the past seven years. 
This report statistically depicts the link between the clus- 
tering and proliferation of sex oriented Adult Entertain- 
ment Establishments in Hollywood, and the variety of 
criminal activity which follows. There are five sections to 
this report; the first section provides crime and arrest 
data for the Hollywood Area from 1969 through 1975, 
and compares the rate of increase with the remainder of 
the City. The remaining sections are letters and signatures 
from concerned businessmen, clergy, merchants, citizens, 
175 
and police officers. These individuals have lived or 
worked in the Hollywood Area for an extended period of 
time. Their letters express their opinions and observa- 
tions relative to the blighting -ffects that the clustering of 
Adult Entertainment Establishments has upon the com- 
munity of Hollywood. 
SECTION 1 
STATISTICAL REVIEW 
HOLLYWOOD AREA 
1969 Through 1975 
This report presents an analysis of crime and arrests for 
the Hollywood Area compared with the City of Los 
Angeles for the period beginning 1969 and ending 1975. 
Comparison charts relating the 1969 statistics to those of 
1975 are attached. This period of comparison embraces 
the years during which Adult Entertainment Establish- 
ments appeared and proliferated in the Hollywood Area. 
CRIMES 
Part I crimes are those criminal acts which most severly 
effect their victims; they include homucide, rape, aggra- 
vated assault, robbery, burglary, larceny, and vehicle 
theft. During the period of 1969 through 1975, reported 
incidents of Part I crimes in the Hollywood Area 
increased 7.6 percent while the City showed a 4.2 percent 
increase. In conformance to the overall trend, every Part | 
crime committed against a person, not against property, 
increased at a higher rate in Hollywood Area than in the 
City-wide total. Street robberies and 484 (Purse Snatches), 
wherein the victim was directly accosted by their 


PAGE 123
176 
assailant, increased by 93.7 percent and 51.4 percent, 
respectively; the City-wide increase was 25.6 percent and 
36.8 percent. 
ARRESTS 
Suspects arrested for Part I criminal acts in Hollywood 
Area increased 16.2 percent while the City dropped by 5.3 
percent. This reveals that Hollywood Area was 21.5 per- 
cent over the City’s total in the apprehension of serious 
criminals during the seven year period. 
Equally alarming as the increase in Part | arrests, is the 
increase in Part II arrests (described on chart, P. 4) in 
Hollywood Area as opposed to the rest of the City. Holly- 
wood increased in this category by 45.4 percent while the 
City rose but 3.4 percent. 
Prostitution arrests in Hollywood Area increased at a rate 
15 times greater than the City average. While the City 
showed a 24.5 percent hike, Hollywood bounded to a 
372.3 percent increase in prostitution arrests. 
Similarly, pandering arrests in Hollywood Area increased 
by 475.0 percent, three and one half times the City 
increase of 133.3 percent. (See note P. 5) 
DEPLOYMENT 
During the period included in this report, the City-wide 
deployment of police personnel rose by 21.2 percent. 
However, with the surge of crime in the Hollywood Area, 
deployment there increased by 32.5 percent, 11.3 percent 
higher than the rest of the City. Included in this figure is a 
177 
29.4 percent hike in uniformed officers and 35.6 percent 
rise in investigators to cope with the criminal elements. 
COMMENTARY 
This survey reflects a seven-year span during which time 
the Adult Entertainment Establishments in the Holly- 
wood Area proliferated from a mere 11 establishments to 
an astonishing number of 88 such locations. The overall 
deleterious effect to the entire community is evident in 
the statistics provided. The overwhelming increase in 
prostitution, robberies, assaults, thefts, and the propor- 
tionate growth in police personnel deployed throughout 
Hollywood, are all representative of the blighting results 
that the clustering of Adult Entertainment Establishments 
has on the entire community. These adverse social effects 
not only infect the environs immediately adjacent to the 
parlors but creates a malignant atmosphere in which 
crime spreads to epidemic proportions.
