REASONS FOR GRANTING THE WRIT 
The opinion of the Court of Appeals deals with an 
issue of federal constitutional law in a way which directly 
conflicts with this Court’s decision in Renton and neces- 
sarily brings its decision into conflict with other circuit 
courts of appeals. The Ninth Circuit held that the City 
must first study the negative effects of each combination 
of adult businesses and then apply those findings to the 
particular problems or needs of the City. As discussed in 
Part B, infra, this reasoning and result are the same as in 
its prior decision, Playtime Theatres, Inc. v. City of Renton, 
748 F.2d 527 (9th Cir. 1984) (Playtime Theatres), which this 
Court reversed in Renton. 
Renton stands for the proposition that a City need not 
await the entry of adult businesses and the consequent 
negative effects before it can zone to regulate adult busi- 
nesses. Further, Renton does not require a city council to 
independently investigate or corroborate each potential 
problem when it acts in furtherance of the public interest. 
All that is required is that the evidence relied on need be 
“reasonably believed to be relevant to the problem that 
the city addresses.” Renton, supra, 475 U.S. at 50-51. The 
Ninth Circuit now requires that before a city can zone to 
regulate adult businesses the city must have direct, cor- 
roborating evidence that the regulated business contrib- 
utes to the already proven negative secondary effects 
11 
associated with adult businesses, an impossible burden to 
meet. 
As discussed in Part A, infra, there is, then, a substan- 
tial conflict among the circuits as to the proper standard a 
court should use in evaluating the constitutionality of an 
adult entertainment zoning ordinance. The opinion below 
conflicts with the decisions of the Courts of Appeals for 
the Third Circuit, Mitchell v. Com'n on Adult Entertainment 
Est., 10 F.3d 123 (3rd Cir. 1993), the Eighth Circuit, ILQ 
Investments, Inc. v. City of Rochester, 25 F.3d 1413 (8th Cir. 
1994), cert. denied, 513 U.S. 1017 (1994), the Tenth Circuit, 
Z.J. Gifts v. City of Aurora, 136 F.3d 683 (10th Cir. 1998), 
cert denied, 525 U.S. 868 (1998) arid the Eleventh Circuit, 
Lady ]. Lingerie, Inc. v. City of Jacksonville, 176 F.3d 1358 
(11th Cir. 1999), cert. denied, 120 S.Ct. 1554 (2000). These 
circuits look to the purpose of the ordinance as a whole 
and, if it can be shown that adult entertainment busi- 
nesses as a class or category contribute to the undesirable 
secondary effects, then the ordinance passes scrutiny 
under the First Amendment. They do not require a city to 
separately study the negative impacts of individual busi- 
nesses. Rather, if evidence supports the need for zoning 
regulations for some adult businesses, it is constitu- 
tionally permissible to :ogulate all businesses that can be 
characterized as “adult entertainment.” 
The Ninth Circuit opinion in this case also conflicts 
with a decision of the Court of Appeals for the Fourth 
Circuit, Hart Book Stores, Inc. v. Edmisten (Hart), supra, 
which held that the specific zoning tool employed by the 
City, prohibiting more than one sexually explicit business 
at a location, did not violate the First Amendment. Both 
the trial court (App. 45) and the Court of Appeals (App. 


PAGE 11
12 
19) agreed that the regulation in Hart was the same as the 
one adopted by the City. Renton permits a city’s reliance 
on opinions such as Hart to support the municipal zoning 
regulation. Renton, supra, 475 U.S. at 50-51 (Renton enti- 
tled to rely on a 1978 opinion of the Supreme Court of 
Washington). For these reasons this ca’ e warrants review 
in the form of a writ of certiorari. See Rule 10(a) of the 
Rules of the Supreme Court of the United States. 
A. 
The Decision Of The Ninth Circuit Court of 
Appeals Is In Conflict With Five Other Cir- 
cuits. 
At least four other circuit courts of appeals have 
considered and rejected the rationale used by the Ninth 
Circuit to invalidate the City’s zoning regulation. The 
Ninth Circuit determined that this City’s 1977 Study was 
insufficient to justify the City’s regulation because the 
study (1) “did not analyze an individual bookstore/ 
arcade combination as a concentration of adult businesses 
(App. 12), (2) “was not directed at determining the 
impact of individual adult entertainment business units 
(id.), and (3) did not “identify any harmful secondary 
effects resulting from bookstore/arcade combinations as 
individual business units.” App. 13. In that court’s opin- 
ion, the “Study contains no findings that an individual 
combination bookstore/arcade produces any of the 
increased crime the Study found resulting from a concen- 
tration of adult businesses.” App. 15. “Therefore,” said 
the court, “it is unreasonable 
for the City to infer that 
absent its regulations, a bookstore/arcade combination 
would have harmful secondary effects.” Id. 
13 
The Ninth Circuit’s standard for evaluating the con- 
stitutionality of an adult entertainment zoning ordinance 
has been rejected elsewhere. 
circuit has stated sim- 
ply: “That is simply not the law.” ILQ Investments, Inc. v. 
City of Rochester, supra, 25 F.3d at 1418 (ILQ). In ILQ, the 
Eighth Circuit reversed a district court ruling “that the 
City was unreasonable in relying upon other cities’ 
studies because they did not specifically address busi- 
nesses similar to Downtown Book and Video, that is, 
adult bookstores ‘that offer both sexually explicit and 
non-sexually explicit materials and allow only off-prem- 
ises consumption of those materials.” Id. at 1417. The 
Eighth Circuit rejected ILQ’s assertion that Rochester had 
to disregard that city’s studies because they did not eval- 
uate bookstores with no on-site viewing offering both 
adult and non-adult fare. Id. at 1418. 
After noting the “thorough legislative process” 
undertaken by Rochester in enacting its zoning regula- 
tions (id. at 1417), the ILQ court held that the evidence 
relied on by Rochester need not prove that the plaintiff's 
business produced “the exact same adverse effects on its 
surroundings” as other adult businesses. It was legally 
sufficient for the city to show that plaintiff’s business was 
one of the categories of businesses “reasonably believed 
to produce at least some of the unwanted secondary 
effect.” Id. at 1418. “Even if Downtown Book and Video is 
a new type of adult business, it may not avoid a time, 
studies of the secondary effects of reasonably similar 
businesses.” Id. Because Rochester's ordinance “affects 
only categories of businesses reasonably believed to pro- 
duce at least some of the unwanted secondary effects, 


PAGE 12
14 
Rochester ‘must be allowed a reasonable opportunity to 
experiment with solutions to admittedly serious prob- 
lems.’ (Citation omitted.)” Id. 
Based on the “more flexible” Eighth Circuit standard 
(App. 18), the City’s ordinance is valid under Renton 
because it seeks to regulate only categories of businesses 
reasonably believed to produce the very serious identi- 
fied secondary effects caused by concentrations of sexu- 
ally explicit businesses. A concentration of sexually 
explicit businesses in one building is, when all is said and 
done, reasonably similar to, if not identical with, a con- 
centration of adult businesses next door to or down the 
block from each other. 
In Mitchell 
v. Com’n on Adult Entertainment 
Est. 
(Mitchell), supra, an adult bookstore and video arcade 
(Mitchell, supra, 10 F.3d at 127) challenged a Delaware 
statute restricting the hours of operation for sexually 
explicit businesses. Id. at 128. The plaintiff asserted that 
there was insufficient evidence before the legislature “to 
support its conclusion that the asserted undesirable sec- 
ondary effects it seeks to regulate resulted from the pro- 
tected activity, or if it did, that the closing-hours 
amendment would reduce them.” Id. at 132. The Court of 
Appeals held that there was a substantial government 
interest in the regulation because it “was amply demon- 
strated that the amendment was passed to control the 
socially undesirable effects incidental to the operation of 
adult entertainment establishments.” Id. at 137. “The per- 
ceived effects are akin to those created by a public nui- 
sance.” Id. 
15 
The Mitchell court rejected the argument that the 
statute was not narrowly tailored because plaintiff's par- 
ticular location was not shown to contribute to the identi- 
fied adverse effects. It also rejected the argument that a 
survey of adult bookstores in Delaware was required in 
order to “determine the effect the statute or regulation 
will have on each.” Id. at 138. Rather, the court concluded 
that the state “need only show that adult entertainment 
establishments as a class cause the unwanted secondary 
effects the statute regulates.” Id., emphasis added. 
The Ninth Circuit’s decision below conflicts with yet 
a third Court of Appeals decision, Z.J. Gifts v. City of 
Aurora, supra. The City of Aurora, Colorado, enacted a 
zoning ordinance which, inter alia, regulated a bookstore 
that sold and rented adult materials for viewing off prem- 
ises. Z.J. Gifts, supra, 136 F.3d at 685. The district court 
enjoined the ordinance “because it believed ‘none of the 
material relied on by the city council shows the business 
of Christie’s bears any relationship to [harmful second- 
ary] effects.’ ” Id. at 686. The Court of Appeals reversed, 
rejecting in the Tenth Circuit the rationale used by the 
Court of Appeals here. 
The Aurora 
court held that the evidence relied on to 
justify the ordinance need only support the City’s general 
purpose in enacting the ordinance, and that “differences 
in the mode of delivery of sexually oriented materials are 
constitutionally 
insignificant for purposes of determining 
an ordinance’s content-neutrality.” Id. at 687. 
The court's 
“analysis of Aurora’s interest in regulating sexually ori- 
ented businesses thus remains unaffected by the district 
court’s distinction between off-site and on-site viewing of 
sexually explicit materials.” Id. at 688. The ordinance 


PAGE 13
16 
regulated the category of businesses producing the 
unwanted secondary effects (id. at 687) and did not 
attempt to regulate businesses that had “a minimal or 
nonexistent connection to sexually oriented entertain- 
ment.” Id. at 689. Therefore, the bookstore owner could 
not be heard to complain that the zoning regulations 
could not be constitutionally applied to it because it was 
a new or different type of sexually oriented business. Id. 
at 690. 
Here, as in Z.J. Gifts, the characterization of the com- 
bination of an adult bookstore and adult video arcade as 
a new or unique type of business (as is, by the way, any 
other conceivable combination of adult businesses within 
the City) should not affect the underlying analysis that 
these types of businesses collectively cause serious nega- 
tive impacts. Given the uncontroverted explicit sexual 
nature of these businesses, they should not be permitted 
to avoid regulation by creating an artificial distinction 
based upon the mode or manner of operation because 
such a “distinction is constitutionally irrelevant in deter- 
mining whether [Los Angeles’] interests are important or 
substantial, particularly in light of the [Supreme] Court's 
strong statements regarding the government's interest in 
regulating such businesses in Youxg and Renton.” Id. at 
688. 
The Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit 
recently rejected the rationale of the Ninth Circuit below 
in Lady J. Lingerie, Inc. v. City of Jacksonville (Lady J.), supra, 
176 F.3d at 1365. In Lady J., “ ‘lingerie shops’ that show- 
case(d) nude dancing” (id. at 1360) challenged, inter alia, 
the city’s regulation limiting their hours of operation. Id. 
at 1365. The Eleventh Circuit rejected the argument raised 
17 
by the plaintiffs, which was identical to the rationale used 
by the Ninth Circuit below. The lingerie shops argued 
that Jacksonville needed to show a substantial govern- 
ment interest to justify its regulation on an hour-by-hour

“The plaintiffs concede that ample evidence 
exists to justify requiring them to close during 
the late evening hours, so that the hours of 
operation rule as a whole indisputably serves a 
substantial government interest. Both the plain-

this rule on an hour by hour basis. There is no 
evidence, they submit, of a substantial govern- 
ment interest to justify requiring adult busi- 
nesses to close from 10:00 a.m. until noon. This 
is a clever argument, but it confuses the require- 
ment that a regulation serve a substantial gov- 
ernment interest with the requirement that it be 
narrowly tailored to that end. We look at the 
provision as a whole to decide whether it serves a 
substantial government interest. Since it does, 
we ask whether it is narrowly tailored.” Id., 
emphasis in the original. 
The court explained the practical reason for its hold- 
ing thusly: 
“If we were to side with the plaintiffs here, 
the next litigants would argue whether evidence 
of secondary effects at 6:15 in the morning justi-

or whether evidence from 9:30 justifies requiring 
them to close at 10:45. That sort of line-drawing

substantially broader than necessary.’ (Cita-

STANDARD OF REVIEW 
A grant of summary judgment is reviewed de novo. 
See, e.g., Robi v. Reed, 173 F.3d 736, 739 (9th Cir.), cert. 
denied, 120 S. Ct. 375 (1999). We must determine, viewing 
the evidence in the light most favorable to the appellants, 
whether there are any genuine issues of material fact and 
whether the district court correctly applied the substan- 
tive law. See, e.g., Berry v. Valence Tech., Inc., 175 F.3d 699, 
703 (9th Cir.), cert. denied, 120 S. Ct. 528 (1999). We do not 
weigh the evidence or determine the truth of the matter; 
rather, we only decide whether there is a genuine issue of 
material fact for trial. See Colacurcio v. City of Kent, 163 
F.3d 545, 549 (9th Cir. 1998). 
The constitutionality of a regulation is a question of 
law that is reviewed de novo. See Gonzalez v. Metropolitan 
Transp. Auth., 174 F.3d 1016, 1018 (9th Cir. 1999), cert. 
denied, 120 S. Ct. 1553 (2000).

I. 
Introduction 
The issue in this case is whether the City of Los 
Angeles (the “City”) violates the First Amendment by 
enforcing a zoning ordinance that defines certain activ- 
ities as “adult entertainment businesses” and prohibits 
the operation of more than one such business in the same 
building. Non-obscene adult entertainment is protected 
by the First Amendment. Local governments may impose 
special zoning restrictions on adult entertainment busi- 
nesses without violating the First Amendment, provided 
that such restrictions are directed at harmful “secondary 
effects” of such businesses, and not at suppressing the 
content of adult entertainment. Renton ‘v. Playtime The- 
atres, Inc., 475 U.S. 41, 106 S. Ct. 925 (1986). The plaintiffs 
contend that at the time of the ordinance’s enactment, the 
City did not have a reasonable basis to believe that multi- 
ple adult entertainment uses within a single business 
created harmful secondary effects, and that therefore a 


PAGE 31
App. 24 
multiple use restriction applicable only to adult entertain- 
ment businesses violates the First Amendment. The City 
contends that the 1977 Studies presented to the City 
Council at the time of enactment demonstrated that con- 
centrations of adult entertainment businesses have harm- 
ful secondary effects, and that these studies support a 
restriction on concentrations of multiple uses within one 
business. The Court finds that while the studies 
addressed concentrations of separate adult entertainment 
businesses in an urban area, the studies did not treat the 
activities conducted by the plaintiffs - video rental and 
on-premises video viewing - as separate businesses. The 
Court therefore finds that there is no genuine issue of fact 
whether the multiple use ordinance was justified by a 
showing of harmful secondary effects at the time of its 
enactment.

A. 
Legal Standard for Reconsideration 
Central District Local Rule 7.16 provides that 
[a] motion for reconsideration of the decision on 
any motion may be made only on the grounds of 
(a) a material difference in fact or law from that 
presented to the Court before such decision that 
in the exercise [of] reasonable diligence could 
not have been known to the party moving for 
reconsideration at the time of such decision, or 
(b) the emergence of new material facts or a 
change of law occurring after the time of such 
decision, or (c) a manifest showing of a failure 
to consider material facts presented to the Court 
before such decision. 
A manifest showing of failure to consider material facts 
includes failure to consider such facts in light of the 
applicable law. Reconsideration is thus appropriate if the 
court’s order “committed clear [legal] error.” School Dist. 
No. 1], Multnomah County v. ACandS, Inc., 5 F.3d 1255, 
1263 (9th Cir. 1993); see also, In re Cypress Semiconductor 
Sec. Litig., 836 F. Supp. 711, 713 (N.D. Cal. 1993).

1. Standard for Summary Judgment 
Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 56(c) provides that a 
motion for summary judgment shall be granted if the 
moving party establishes that there is no genuine issue as 
to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled 
App. 29 
to judgment as a matter of law. Such a showing can be 
made by an affirmative demonstration in affidavits or 
declarations by a moving party who bears the burden of 
proof on the controverted issue at trial. In a circumstance 
where the non-moving party has the burden of proof at 
trial, summary judgment is appropriate if the non-mov- 
ing party fails to come forward with any evidence which 
would create a genuine issue of material fact. Celotex 
ros “9 
Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 323-24, 106 S. Ct. 2548, 2553 
The Court must look to the substantive law at issue 
to determine which facts are “material.” See Anderson v 
Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 248, 106 S. Ct. 2505 2510 
(1986). In deciding whether a dispute is “genuine,” the 
court must determine whether a reasonable jury could 
return a verdict for the non-moving party. Id. at 248-50 
106 S. Ct. at 2510-11. It is the moving party’s burden to 
establish that there is “no genuine issue as to any mate- 
rial fact and that the moving party is entitled to a judg- 
ment as a matter of law.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(c). The as 
party, however, has no burden to negate or ee 
matters on which the opponent will have the burden of 
proof at trial. Celotex, 477 U.S. at 323, 106 S. Ct. at 2553. 
The First Amendment portion of the Summary Judg- 
ment Order requires reconsideration because the Court 
clearly erred in determining which facts were material 
under the substantive law governing this case. Under 
First Amendment secondary effects doctrine as estab- 
lished in Renton, only facts that were before the City 
Council at the time of enactment are material to the issue 
of whether the ordinance was content-neutral and justi- 
fied by a substantial government interest. Therefore, if, as

2. 
Reconsideration of Substantive First Amend- 
ment Standard 
This case is governed by the First Amendment “sec- 
ondary effects doctrine” under Renton. Ordinarily, the 
government may not apply time, place and manner 
restrictions based on the content of speech. Id., 475 U.S. at 
46-47, 106 S. Ct. at 928. Although the ordinance neve 
targets adult-entertainment businesses based on specific 
definitions of adult content, such restrictions are not con- 
tent-based if the predominant purpose of their enactment 
is to ameliorate deleterious secondary effects of such 
businesses. Id. at 48. 
A content-neutral time, place and manner restriction 
on speech is constitutional if it furthers a substantial 
government interest and is narrowly tailored to affect no 
more speech than is essential to the furtherance of that 
interest. Young v. American Mini Theatres, Inc., 427 US. 50, 
80, 96 S. Ct. 2440, 2457 (1976) (Powell, J., concurring) 
(citing United States v. O’Brien, 391 U.S. 367, 377, 88 S. Ct. 
1673, 1679 (1968)). The City has the burden of proof at 
trial to show that in enacting the multiple use ordinance 
App. 31 
it relied upon evidence permitting the reasonable infer- 
ence that multiple uses in one business would have harm- 
ful secondary effects. Tollis, Inc. v. San Bernardino County, 
827 F.2d 1329, 1333 (9th Cir. 1987). Because the City has 
the burden of proof on this issue, the plaintiffs’ burden 
on summary judgment “may be discharged by ‘showing’ 
~ that is, pointing out to the district court — that there is 
an absence of evidence” to support the City’s burden to 
show that the ordinance was enacted to advance a sub- 
stantial government interest in achieving a content-neu- 
tral purpose. Celotex, 477 U.S. at 325, 106 S. Ct. at 2554. 
In the adult entertainment cor text, the Ninth Circuit 
has used a three-step analysis to determine whether an 
ordinance is a valid time, place and manner restriction. 
See Tollis, 827 F.2d at 1332. The first Step is to determine 
whether the ordinance bans the plaintiffs’ expressive con- 
duct altogether. If it does, it is not a “time, place and 
manner restriction,” but rather a presumptively invalid 
prior restraint.? Id. The second step is to determine 
whether the ordinance should be analyzed as content- 
based or content-neutral. Where, as here, the ordinance 
on its face differentiates on the basis of content, the issue 
° Prior restraints must meet strict scrutiny. That is, they

In the First Amendment adult entertainment context, the

material that, while not obscene for adults, would be obscene 
for minors. See Crawford v. Lungren, 96 F.3d 380 (9th Cir. 1996), 
cert. denied, 117 S. Ct. 1249 (1997). Here, because the City would 
bear the burden to meet strict scrutiny, and because the City has 
not come forward with evidence to meet this burden, a 
conclusion that the ordinance must be analyzed as a prior 
restraint results in summary judgment for the plaintiffs.

speech rather than to suppress speech itself. Id. Again, if 
the purpose of the ordinance is not content-neutral, it is a 
presumptively invalid prior restraint. If the restriction is 
content-neutral, the third step is to determine whether 
the restriction serves a substantial government interest, is 
narrowly tailored to serve that interest and allows for 
reasonable alternative avenues of communication. Id.; see 
also Simon & Schuster, Inc. v. Members of the New York State 
Crime Victims Bd., 502 U.S. 105, 122 n.*, 112 S. Ct. 501, 511 
n.* (noting that under Renton “regulations must be ‘nar- 
rowly tailored’ to advance the interest asserted by the 
State”). Although the regulation must be narrowly tai- 
lored to serve a substantial government interest, it need 
not be the least restrictive means of serving that interest. 
Ward v. Rock Against Racism, 491 U.S. 781, 109 S. Ct. 2746 
(1989); Renton, 475 U.S. at 52, 106 S. Ct. at 931 (noting that 
state and local governments have broad latitude to 
choose means of ameliorating secondary effects). 
The second and third steps are linked, in that the 
legislature must have a reasonable basis to believe that 
the restriction will ameliorate harmful secondary effects 
which the government has a substantial interest in reduc- 
ing in order for the restriction to be found content-neu- 
tral. The Summary Judgment Order, however, found that 
the content-neutrality requirement of Renton was satisfied 
by the City Council’s expression in the preamble of the 
ordinance of a content-neutral purpose to ameliorate sec- 
ondary effects. (Order at 24:1-25:28.) The Summary Judg- 
ment Order reasoned that the substantial government 
interest prong should be examined separately, and that it 
App. 33 
could be satisfied by evidence of conditions arising long 
after enactment of the ordinance. (Order at 26-38.) 
Upon reconsideration, the Court concludes that this 
aspect of the Summary Judgment Order was clearly in 
error. This reasoning would allow a municipality to regu- 
late speech based on an assertion of harmful secondary 
effects without any showing that such secondary effects 
exist. Ninth Circuit precedents following Renton require a 
greater showing that secondary effects actually exist. 
“The [local government] must show that in enacting the 
particular limitations it places upon adult theaters, 
it 
relied upon evidence permitting the reasonable infesence 
that, absent such limitations, the adult theaters would 
have harmful secondary effects.” Tollis, 827 F.2d at 1333. 
The Summary Judgment Order clearly erred by 
extending a Ninth Circuit precedent in which an adult 
entertainment ordinance was invalidated because the 
harmful secondary effect no longer existed to support the 
Proposition that an adult entertainment ordinance could 
be justified as content-neutral based on secondary effects 
that emerged after its enactment. See Acorn Investments 
Inc. v. City of Seattle, 887 F.2d 219 (9th Cir. 1989). In Acorn, 
the Ninth Circuit held that a secondary effects regulation 
was no longer valid because the conditions underlying 
the substantial government interest served by the regula- 
tion no longer existed. id. at 221-22. The Summary Judg- 
ment Order extended 
this holding to support the 
Proposition that a municipality could rely on post-enact- 
ment evidence to meet its burden to show that the ordi- 
mance was content-neutral and served a substantial 
government interest. The holding in Acorn, however, can- 
not be read to relax the burden on the government to

type approved in Young would be appropriate for Los 
Angeles. (See 3 Def.’s RJN Ex. 5 at 357.) 
By categorizing certain businesses as “bookstores / 
arcades,” and examining areas where such bookstores/ 
arcades were found in close proximity to several other 
adult entertainment businesses, the studies determined 
not the impact of a booxstore/arcade on the surrounding 
area, but the impact of bookstores/arcades as part of a 
concentration with other adult businesses. The existence 
in the studies of the bookstores/arcades category does 
not support the City’s contention that it has a substantial 
interest in separating bookstores from arcades. To the 
contrary, the fact that bookstores /arcades were studied as 
unitary businesses militates in favor of a finding that the 
studies do not demonstrate that such a combination busi- 
ness is a concentration leading to the adverse secondary 
effects demonstrated by the studies. See Walnut Properties, 
Inc. v. City of Whittier, 808 F.2d 1331, 1335 (9th Cir. 1987) 
(holding that city failed to establish that restriction on 
App. 41 
Proximity to a church of the city’s only adult theater was 
justified by its substantial interest in preventing concen- 
tration of adult businesses, because one adult business 
could not be concentrated with itself). 
The facts about the operation of the plaintiffs’ busi- 
nesses are undisputed. The City does not dispute that the 
bookstore and arcade operations take place in one “rela- 
tively small” storefront of 2500-3000 square feet, with one 
entrance from the street, one counter and cash register, 
and, during most hours, one employee monitoring the 
operations. (See Def.’s Separate Statement of Genuine 
Issues, filed Aug. 18, 1997, 4] 5-10 (indicating that the 
facts regarding the operations of the stores are undis- 
puted).) The City does not dispute the fact that “[t}he 
video viewing booths and retail sales of both stores occur 
in the same commercial space within a single building.” 
(Id. { 9.) The arcade booths are used to promote rentals 
and sales from the bookstore. (Wiener Decl. {J 13, 15.) 
Based on these undisputed facts about the combined 
nature of the bookstores/arcades, the Court concludes 
that there is no genuine issue of fact as to whether the 
1977 Studies dealing with concentrations of separate 
businesses could have been reasonably relied upon to 
support application of the multiple use prohibition to the 
plaintiffs’ businesses. 
The City contends that the bookstore and arcade 
components should be treated as separate for purposes of 
secondary effects analysis because the ordinance defines 
bookstores and arcades as separate uses. See LAMC 
§ 12.70(B)(17). The City contends that the police power to 
zone uses of land includes the ability to define certain 


PAGE 40
App. 42 
activities as separate uses, even if they take place in the 
same business establishment. The City points to zoning 
regulations that define bakeries, dry cleaners, candy 
stores, drugstores, florists, grocery stores, butcher shops, 
delicatessens, automobile repair garages, and automobile 
services stations as separate uses, even though they wed 
be operated in a single location. See LAMC § 12.13. (Def.’s 
Second RJN Ex. 15.) 
The City asserts that “[t]he City’s Zoning Code 
defines many businesses, both adult and non-adult, as 
being separate uses even though they may be operated at 
a single location.” (Def.’s UF { 79 (emphasis added).) The 
City’s authority to define businesses as separate uses 
without reference to protected speech may not be ques- 
tioned by this Court if there is any rational basis for the 
classification. The classification of certain adult entertain- 
ment activities as separate businesses, however, is subject 
to a different standard of review because the City applies 
these definitions only to businesses that engage in pro- 
tected speech. See Schad v. Borough of Mt. Ephraim, 452 
U.S. 61, 68, 101 S. Ct. 2176, 2182-83 (1981) (noting that 
standard applied to judicial review of zoning power 
depends on “the nature of the right assertedly threatened 
or violated;” where property rights are affected, rational 
basis review applies; where free speech rights are 
infringed, zoning law “must be narrowly drawn and 
must further a sufficiently substantial government inter- 
est”). Because the separate business definitions in LAMC 
$ 12.70(C) apply only to adult entertainment uses, and 
therefore discriminate on the basis of protected speech, 
they are subject to a higher level of review than rational 
basis, even when, as here, they are treated as content- 
App. 43 
neutral under Renton. Thus, unlike the bakery, grocery, 
and service station definitions, the adult entertainment 
business definitions must be narrowly tailored to serve a 
substantial government interest. The City may not require 
adult bookstores/arcades to separate their businesses 
without demonstrating that the conduct of these activities 
in the same building has the adverse secondary effects 
demonstrated by the 1977 Studies. The City’s reliance on 
the zoning regulations applicable to bakeries and groc- 
eries would only be justified if the City required that all 
bookstore/arcade combinations be defined as separate 
businesses regardless of adult content. Such an across- 
the-board regulation would be subject only to rational 
basis review. But where, as here, the City applies the 
restriction only to bookstores /arcades that engage in pro- 
tected speech, the higher standard of review must be 
applied. See Christy v. City of Ann Arbor, 824 F.2d 489, 493 
(6th Cir. 1987) (holding that adult entertainment regula- 
tions under Renton require “more than a rational rela- 
tionship 
. 
. 
. between the ordinance and this government 
interest” to justify discrimination based on protected 
speech). 
In addition, the City points to the different treatment 
of bookstores and “coin-operated amusements,” such as 
arcades, in zoning regulations other than the adult enter- 
tainment ordinance. (Def.’s UF 4] 81-88; Eberhard Decl. 
11 5-9.) The City points to the declaration of Franklin 
Eberhard, the Deputy Director of the Department of City 
Planning, and one of the authors of the 1977 planning 
department study. Eberhard declares the City has “histor- 
ically regulated amusement enterprises separately from 
retail uses” because the two types of business involve 


PAGE 41
App. 44 
different types of impact on the surrounding community. 
(Eberhard Decl. {4 4-7.) A bookstore, as a retail operation 
in which the customers buy goods for consumption off- 
site, has a different impact in the form of “noise, hours of 
operation, parking and loitering,” than does a video 
arcade which provides on-site public entertainment. (Id.) 
Eberhard’s declaration would be dispositive if the 
question were whether the City had a rational basis for 
an across-the-board regulation separating all bookstores 
from all video arcades. The City’s police power clearly 
encompasses regulations meant to reduce noise, parking 
and loitering. The multiple use ordinance at issue here, 
however, applies to a set of bookstores / 
arcades defined 
by their exercise of protected speech. As discussed above, 
in order to single out businesses based on speech without 
violating the First Amendment, the City must demon- 
strate that the adult bookstore/arcade combination pro- 
duces adverse secondary effects that would not be 
produced by a non-adult bookstore / 
arcade combination. 
See Schad, 452 US. at 73-74, 101 S. Ct. at 2185 (holding 
that municipality failed to demonstrate that live enter- 
tainment restricted by ordinance would produce second- 
ary effects that would not also be produced by other, non- 
restricted commercial activity). 
In addition, Eberhard makes the more general asser- 
tion that he has “had the opportunity to study the effects 
of the provision of more than one type of business at a 
single location” and that in his experience “a location that 
only has one business does not have as many impacts on 
the surrounding community compared to a location with 
more than one business.” (Eberhard Decl. { 10.) While 
Eberhard’s declaration might be sufficient to defend an 
App. 45 
ordinance that prohibited all multiple uses, it is irrelevant 
to the issue of whether an ordinance that prohibits multi- 
ple uses based on content violates the First Amendment. 
The argument that more than one business in a location 
has harmful secondary effects does not justify prohibiting 
multiple uses only for adult entertainment business and 
not for other types of businesses. See City of Cincinnati v. 
Discovery Network, Inc., 507 U.S. 410, 430-31, 113 S. Ct. 
1505, 1517 (1993) (rejecting application of secondary 
effects doctrine to ban on use of newsracks to dispense 
commercial handbills because city made no showing that 
secondary effects were unique to commercial newsracks 
and not also caused by newspaper racks which were not 
regulated); 
FW/PBS, Inc. v. Dallas, 493 U.S. 215, 225, 110S. 
Ct. 596, 604 (1990) (noting that where generally applied 
licensing scheme is more onerous with respect to sexually 
oriented businesses than with respect to the majority of 
other businesses, First Amendment rights are implicated). 
Even if the plaintiffs’ operations are considered sin- 
gle businesses, the City contends that a finding of second- 
ary effects from concentration of adult businesses should 
be applied broadly to include concentrations of activities 
within a single business. The only circuit court holding 
on multiple use adult entertainment regulations supports 
the City’s contention. In Hart Book Stores, Inc. v. Edmisten 
612 F.2d 821 (4th Cir. 1979), the Fourth Circuit upheld on 
ordinance similar to the one at issue here. The ordinance 
defined an “adult establishment” as any adult bookstore 
adult motion picture theater, adult mini motion picture 
theater, or massage business, and prohibited the opera- 
tion of more than one establishment in one building. id. at 
823. The only evidence offered to show the secondary 


PAGE 42
App. 46 
effect of multiple uses was a report from the county 
health department on the unsanitary conditions found on 
a visit to five adult arcades. Id. at 829 n.9. The Fourth 
Circuit held that the health department report supported 
regulation of the secondary impact of adult arcades by 
prohibiting their operation in conjunction with another 
adult entertainment business. 
Hart Book Stores, decided seven years before Renton, 
is inconsistent with controlling Ninth Circuit authority 
decided after Renton. The Ninth Circuit has required local 
governments to justify secondary effects regulations by 
showing that the conduct regulated actually produces the 
secondary effects that the government invokes to justify 
the regulation. Acorn, 887 F.2d at 224. If, as here, the City 
justifies the regulation based on secondary effects pro- 
duced by concentration of businesses, it must demon- 
strate that the conduct restricted relates to concentration. 
Id. It may not point to other secondary effects, such as 
unsanitary conditions within the businesses, that have 
nothing to do with the concentration of such businesses 
within a given area. The Court respectfully concludes 
that the Fourth Circuit in Hart Book Stores erred by treat- 
ing testimony regarding unsanitary conditions inside a 
video arcade as related to secondary effects of concentra- 
tions of adult entertainment businesses. 
The City is correct in asserting that this case is like 
Hart Book Stores. There, as here, the government failed to 
demonstrate that the combination of adult entertainment 
businesses in a single building is the equivalent of a 
concentration of separate adult businesses in a neighbor- 
hood. While such a failure passed constitutional muster 
in Hart Book Stores, it is fatal under the secondary effects 
App. 47 
doctrine as applied by the Ninth Circuit. Under Acorn, in 
order for the City to benefit from the content-neutral 
Renton analysis, the City must prove the City Council 
relied on evidence that would allow it to conclude that 
multiple uses in a single building created the specific 
secondary effects asserted in justifying the regulation as 
content-neutral. Acorn, 887 F.2d at 224. 
The 1977 Studies on which the City relies to prove a 
substantial government interest demonstrate the need for 
a distance requirement for separate adult entertainment 
businesses. The plaaning department study did not 
address the secondary impact of single adult entertain- 
ment businesses. The undisputed facts regarding the 
nature of the plaintiffs’ businesses demonstrate that they 
are not separate in the sense that the businesses 
addressed in the 1977 Studies were separate. 
The evidence produced by the City does not create a 
genuine issue which if decided in the City’s favor would 
meet its burden to show that the City Council relied on 
evidence supporting a reasonable belief that combination 
businesses, such as those of the plaintiffs, produced 
harmful secondary effects of the type asserted as the 
content-neutral justification for the ordinance. The Court 
therefore grants the plaintiffs’ motion for summary adju- 
dication of the First Amendment issue.

Roe does not change the equal protection principies

2. 
Classification Based on Exercise of Funda- 
mental Right 
The Summary Judgment order denied both the City’s 
and the plaintiffs’ motions for summary adjudication of 
the equal protection issue under this theory. The Supreme 
Court has held that municipalities may use the content of 
sexually oriented entertainment businesses as the basis 
for placing them in a different classification from other 
businesses, provided that the regulation is directed at 
harmful secondary effects. Young, 427 U.S. at 71. Because 
the Renton doctrine views such regulation as directed 
toward secondary effects rather than toward expressive 
conduct, no fundamental right is implicated. Thus, the 
classification in question is not between businesses that 
engage in protected expressive conduct and those that do 
App. 51 
not, but rather between businesses that cause harmful 
deleterious effects associated with adult entertainment 
and those that do not. 
As discussed above, the Court finds that the City has 
failed to show a genuine issue of fact in support of the 
contention that the multiple use amendment serves a 
content-neutral purpose. Therefore, the ordinance’s clas- 
sification of multiple use businesses based on adult con- 
tent is subject to the strict scrutiny standard applicable to 
classifications based on exercise of a fundamental right. 
Under strict scrutiny, the classification must be neces- 
sary to promote a compelling or overriding government 
interest. This is the same test that applies to prior 
restraints of free speech under th. First Amendment. As 
discussed above, the City’s failure to demonstrate a genu- 
ine issue of fact as to content-neutral justification of the 
multipie use ordinance requires analysis of the ordinance 
as a presumptively invalid prior restraint. Because the 
City has not come forward with evide) -e at summary 
judgment to show that the multiple use ordinance is 
necessary to serve a compelling government interest, the 
Court must grant the plaintiffs’ motion for summary 
judgment on the first cause of action based on both the 
First Amendment and Equal Protection issues. 
D. 
Reconsideration of Vagueness Issue in Third 
Cause of Action 
The plaintiffs contend that the Court improperly 
reached the merits of the vagueness challenge after con- 
cluding that the plaintiffs lacked standing to advance it. 
The plaintiffs argued at summary judgment that if their 


PAGE 45
App. 52 
facial challenge failed, they would be unable to conform 
their conduct with the ordinance because the definition 0’ 
“adult” bookstore and arcade is too ambiguous to allow 
the plaintiffs to reduce their stock of adult material by an 
amount certain to bring them under the definition. (See 
Order at 45.) 
The Court first concluded that the plaintiffs lacked 
standing to assert the vagueness claim because the plain- 
tiffs conceded that the ordinance applied to their busi- 
nesses as currently constituted. (Order at 45:15-27.) The 
Court reasoned that the plaintiffs might have standing in 
the future, if they reduced their percentage of adult mate- 
rial in an attempt to remove their businesses from the 
coverage of the ordinance. Until then, however, the Court 
concluded that the vagueness challenge was unripe. 
The Court, however, then proceeded to analyze the 
merits of the vagueness claim and concluded, in what 
would have to be dicta, that the California courts had 
construed similar adult entertainment ordinances in such 
a manner as to make the definition of “substantial por- 
tion” sufficiently clear to provide notice as to which 
businesses are affected by the statute. 
The plaintiffs correctly point out that the Supreme 
Court and the Ninth Circuit precedents on which the 
Summary Judgment Order relied did not reach the merits 
of the vagueness claims after concluding that the plain- 
tiffs lacked standing. See Young, 427 U.S. at 61, 96S. Ct. at 
2448; Lydo Enterprises, Inc. v. City of Las Vegas, 745 F.2d 
1211, 1215 (9th Cir. 1984). The plaintiffs also point to a 
App. 53 
recent Supreme Court opinion, in which the Court disap- 
proved an innovation known as “hypothetical jurisdic- 
tion,” whereby a court would be able to determine the 
merits of an action in the absence of Standing, 
if the 
merits determination would have the same result as the 
standing determination. Steel Co. v. Citizens for A Better 
Environment, 118 S. Ct. 1003, 1012 (1998). 
Under the principle announced in Steel Co. it was 
error for the Summary Judgment Order to reach the 
merits of the vagueness issue after determining that the 
plaintiffs lacked standing. 
In light of the foregoing, the Court grants the motion 
to alter or amend the Summary Judgment Order. 
The Court here reiterates its conclusion of law that 
the plaintiffs do not have standing to assert a vagueness 
claim In Young and Renton, the Supreme Court rejected 
similar vagueness claims on the grounds that the plain- 
tiffs conceded that the ordinances clearly applied to 
them. Young, 427 U.S. at 59; Renton, 475 U.S. at 55 n.4. The 
Ninth Circuit has held that “[a] plaintiff who engages in 
some conduct that is clearly Proscribed cannot complain 
of the vagueness of the law as applied to the ain of 
others.” Stoianoff v. State of Montana, 695 F.2d 1214, 1218 
(9th Cir. 1983). Because there is no question as to the 
applicability of the ordinance to the plaintiffs, the Court 
rejects the vagueness challenge for lack of standing. 
The plaintiffs argue that they will have Standing if 
the preliminary injunction is lifted and they are faced 
with the task of bringing their businesses into compliance 
with the ordinance, or bringing their businesses out from 
under the definition of “adult entertainment.” The fact

App. 54 
that the plaintiffs have these choices in the event that 
they lose this lawsuit demonstrates that their vagueness 
challenge under this theory is not ripe for judicial resolu- 
tion.

Conclusion 
Because the plaintiffs correctly contend that the Sum-

Judgment Order correctly applied the standards ae ona?

determining that the plaintiffs lack standing to assert ne 
challenge, the Court grants the motion to alter or amen 
the Summary Judgment Order to vacate the merits dis-

The previous Summary Judgment Order is vacated.

Sec. 6. 
Statement of Purpose 
There is concern on the part of public officials and 
residents of the City of Los Angeles regarding the 
adverse impacts which result from the continued concen- 
tration of adult entertainment business. Based upon this 
concern, the Los Angeles City Council in 1977 instructed 
the City Planning Department to conduct a comprehen- 
sive study, with the assistance of other City agencies, to 
determine whether and to what extent the concentration 
of such adult entertainment businesses has a blighting 
and degrading effect upon the neighborhoods in which 
they are situated. 
The findings of this study, which included testimony 
from public hearings, indicated that the concentration of 
such adult entertainment businesses tends to result in the 
blighting and degrading of the areas of such concentra- 
tion. In order to prevent the further concentration of 
these adult entertainment businesses and to protect the 
character of the City’s neighborhoods, the City Council 
enacted Section 12.70 of the Los Angeles Municipal Code 
known as the “Adult Entertainment Ordinance” which 
prohibits the establishment of an adult entertainment 
business, as defined therein, within 1,000 feet of another 
such business, or within 500 feet of any religious institu- 
tion, school, or public park. 
Nevertheless, there have been a number of instances 
in which two or more adult entertainment business [sic] 
have been established or maintained at the same location 
or operated as a single commercial enterprise. This con- 
centration of adult entertainment businesses tends to 
have an adverse impact on the neighborhood in which 
App. 75 
they are located and is contrary to the dispersal approach 
taken by the City in enacting the Adult Entertainment 
Ordinance. 
The purpose of this ordinance is to clarify existing 
provisions of the Adult Entertainment Ordinance. In 
enacting this ordinance in 1978, the City Council did not 
intend to permit the establishment of more than one .dult 
business in the same building or at the same location. 
This ordinance will clarify Council's intent in that regard 
and provide reasonable amortization provisions applica- 
ble to businesses established after September 1, 1978 in a 
manner inconsistent with Council's intent.

Severability

nance is held to be unconstitutional or otherwise invalid, 
such decision shall not affect the validity of the remaining 
provisions of this Ordinance.

Severability. 
If any provision or clause of this ordinance or the 
application thereof to any person or circumstance is held 
to be unconstitutional or otherwise invalid by any court 
of competent jurisdiction, such invalidity shall not affect 
other section provisions, clauses or applications thereof 
which can be implemented without the invalid provi- 
sions, clause or application thereof, and to this end the 
provisions and clauses of this section are declared to be 
serverable [sic].
