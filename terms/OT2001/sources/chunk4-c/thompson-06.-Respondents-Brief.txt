STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
A. 
Introduction 
Petitioners’ brief raises issues unrelated to the First 
Amendment and underscores what this case is not about. 
It is not about dispensing unsafe, ineffective or “unrea- 
sonable” quantities of compounded drugs, false or mis- 
leading advertisement, or illegal activity. This case is 
about absolute restrictions on truthful, non-misleading 
advertisement and solicitation contained in the Food and 
Drug Administration Modernization Act of 1997 
(“FDAMA”), at 21 U.S.C. §§ 353a(a) and (c) (Supp. V 
1999), that violate the First Amendment. 
This case is before the Court after rulings on cross- 
motions for summary judgment in the district court and 
affirmed (in part) by the Ninth Circuit. Petitioners offered 
no evidence establishing that the distribution of com- 
pounded drugs poses a health risk or that Respondents’ 
advertising creates demand for compounds. There is no 
evidence that Respondents ever practiced pharmacy 
other than in the regular course and in conformity with 
federal and State laws. Petitioners’ sole “evidence” that 
the dispensing of compounded prescription drugs caused 
any alleged health risk was their reference to a double 
hearsay anecdote in their own Compliance Policy Guide 
7132.16 (“CPG”). (Pets.” App. 73a.)! Petitioners now 
attempt to shore-up their barren record with hearsay 
from websites (Pets.’ Br. 27, 34) and journal articles (Pets.’

B. 
Statement of Facts 
Respondents are seven licensed pharmacies, located 
in seven states, specializing in working with physicians 
to compound drugs for individual patient needs. Respon- 
dents advertise, promote and educate physicians and 
patients about the availability of particular compounded 
drugs, classes of drugs, and types of drugs. (Verified 
Compl., { 27; Grp. Exh. 2 to Verified Compl.) 
Compounding is the process whereby a pharmacist, 
pursuant to a physician’s prescription, combines, mixes, 
or alters ingredients to create a customized medication 
for a patient for whom a manufactured drug is unavail- 
able. The tripartite relationship under which this occurs, 
i.e., the interplay between physician, patient and pharma- 
cist, is referred to as the “triad.” (Verified Compl., { 29.) 
This triad is in contrast to manufacturers who have no 
relationship with either physician or patient, but manu- 
facture for distribution to wholesalers and distributors, 
do not customize for a patient’s unique medical needs, 
and receive no physician prescriptions. Compounded 
drugs are prescribed by physicians to address many med- 
ical problems including cancer, infertility, PMS, meno- 
pause, and pain. Pharmacy compounding is a customary 
and required part of pharmacy practice, 
a mandatory 
subject in most pharmacy schools, and most, if not all, 
State laws require that pharmacists be proficient in com- 
pounding and maintain compounding equipment in their 
pharmacies. (Verified Compl., {{ 19, 20, 31.) 
Pharmacists compound a drug product when: manu- 
factured drugs are unavailable; there is no commercially 
available product to accomplish a desired medical objec- 
tive (Verified Compl., { 21(a)); a patient is allergic to a 
manufactured ingredient (Verified Compl., { 21(b)); a 
manufactured drug is unavailable in an appropriate dose 
(Verified Compl., { 21(c)); there is a need for carefully 
calibrated hormone treatments for infertility, cancer, etc. 
(Verified Compl., { 21(d)); and the patient requires an 
alternative delivery system, e.g., injection, dermal patch, 
suppository, etc. (Verified Compl., { 21(e).) Compounding 
pharmacists do not compete with drug manufacturers but 
provide drugs in the interstitial spaces where a manufac- 
tured drug does not exist or is unsuitable. 
Because of the specialization among and co-depen- 
dence between compounding pharmacists and physi- 
cians, it is vital that they establish contact and dialogue. 
This is accomplished by Respondents’ advertisement and 
solicitation, including presentations at medical conven- 
tions. (Verified Compl., {1 28, 30.) Frequently, but for the 
pharmacist’s consultation, neither physician nor patient 
would be aware of the availability of dosages, drug deliv- 
ery systems, or the ability to compound certain particular 
drugs. (Verified Compl., { 23.) This consultation is a vital 
component of the pharmacist’s role as a professional. 
(Verified Compl., { 4.) 
Compounding is not illegal under State or federal 
law. “[A]ll States include compounding as a core compo- 
nent of the profession of pharmacy.” Sen. Rep. No. 
105-43, at 67 (1997). Before FDAMA (and to this day), 
pharmacy practice and its component, compounding,

The fundamental and express differences between 
manufacturing and compounding are set forth in State 
pharmacy laws and the FDCA, i.e., 21 U.S.C. §§ 360(a)( 
(1994), 360(g)(1) (1994), amd 374(a)(2)(A) (1994). For 
example, under § 360(a)(1), pharmacy compounding is 
specifically excluded as it refers to drugs distributed 
“ ... from the original place of manufacture to the person 
who makes final delivery or sale to the ultimate con- 
sumer... .” 21 U.S.C. § 360(a)(1). “Distribution” is 
delivery by a manufacturer to a middleman, while “dis- 
pensing” is the act of a pharmacist transferring the drug 
directly to the patient pursuant to a prescription. Phar- 
macy compounding results in dispensing. Manufacturing 
and compounding are not defined in the FDCA, except to 
the extent that §§ 360(g)(1) and 374(a)(2)(A) provide 
benchmark characteristics and even then, for definitional 
purposes, revert back to and rely on State laws.

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 
FDAMA’s advertisement and solicitation restrictions 
fail this Court’s test for commercial speech restrictions 
established in Central Hudson Gas & Elec. Corp. v. Public 
Serv. Comm'n, 447 U.S. 557 (1980) and, therefore, violate 
the First Amendment. The parties agree, here and in the 
courts below, that Respondents’ advertisements and 
solicitations are truthful and non-misleading. In the court 
below, Petitioners agreed that the introduction of com- 
pounds interstate was legal, a position they no longer 
maintain in this Court. 
Petitioners do not have a substantial interest in “pre- 
venting manufacturing under the guise of compounding” 
because Petitioners’ purported interest is based on a false 
premise. The false premise is that pharmacy compound- 
ing in compliance with State laws can ever be manufac- 
turing or that they can be differentiated by volume. 
Petitioners’ erroneous position disregards the differences 
between manufacturing and compounding and falsely 
attributes advertising and soliciting solely to manufactur- 
ing. 
In an attempt to achieve that goal, Section 353a 
restricts truthful, non-misleading speech; however, 
Respondents’ adve:ising and soliciting do not drive 
demand for compounded drugs; patients’ needs as deter- 
mined by physicians’ prescriptions drive that demand.

A. 
Petitioners Have The Burden Of Satisfying The 
Central Hudson Criteria 
The parties agree, and the courts below held, that 
Respondents’ advertisements and solicitations constitute 
commercial speech and that the First Amendment issues 
in this case are controlled by Central Hudson and its 
progeny. Petitioners also concede that Respondents’ 
advertisements and solicitations are truthful and not mis- 
leading.® (Pets.’ Br. 18.) For the restrictions on advertise- 
ment and solicitation in §§ 353a(a) and (c) to pass First 
Amendment scrutiny, they must satisfy the four Central 
Hudson criteria: (1) the speech at issue must concern 
lawful activity and not be inaccurate or misleading; (2) 
the asserted governmental interest must be substantial; 
(3) the law challenged must directly and materially 
advance the asserted governmental interest; and (4) the 
law must accomplish the asserted ends in a manner that 
is not more extensive than necessary. 447 U.S. at 566. See 
also 44 Liquormart, Inc. v. Rhode Island, 517 U.S. 484, 
499-500, 529 (1996). 
The parties also agree that “[t]he government has the 
burden of justifying a commercial speech restriction.”

Although Petitioners did not so argue in the Ninth

compounded drugs are “new drugs” and, hence, illegal to

expressly waived in the Ninth Circuit. Petitioners should 
be held to that waiver, as they prevented the lower court 
from deciding that issue. See Lorillard Tobacco Co. v. Reilly, 
121 S. Ct. 2404, 2419 (2001) (“[W]e decline to reach an 
issue that was not decided below”); United States v. United 
Foods, Inc., 121 S. Ct. 2334, 2340 (2001) (declining to reach

States v. Ortiz, 422 U.S. 891, 896 (1975). 
Petitioners’ original position was that Respondents’ 
speech was inherently untruthful and misleading. (See

pursuant to 21 U.S.C. § 321(p)(1) and, therefore, illegal to

introduce into interstate commerce without having satis- 
fied the FDA’s new drug approval process. Alas, com- 
pounding was never illegal under the FDCA and was not 
treated as illegal by the FDA before the CPG or in the

Petitioners’ jurisdiction to address compounds as 
new drugs, if existent at all, would have to derive from 
the “new drug” definition in 21 U.S.C. § 321(p)(1). This is

A. 
The Inapplicability Of 21 U.S.C. § 321(p)(1) 
And The Impossibility Of Clinical Trials For 
Compounds 
Compounds are drugs, but not “new drugs.” The 
determination of what is a new drug starts with an anal- 
ysis of 21 U.S.C. § 321(p)(1), which requires expert eval- 
uation for safety and effectiveness - an evaluation 
Petitioners represent requires in excess of $200 million. 
(Pets.’ Br. 26.) Petitioners point out that this would be 
“uneconomical.” (Pets.’ Br. 27.) It would not only be 
uneconomical but impossible. To satisfy the clinical trial 
component of the approval process requires not only 
untold millions of dollars, but a statistically valid sample. 
Weinberger v. Hynson, Wescott & Dunning, Inc., 412 US. 
609 (1973), demonstrates the impossibility of applying 
§ 321(p)(1) to compounds because of the need to present 


PAGE 25
36 
“substantial evidence of effectiveness” for a new drug, 
which this Court held included “evidence consisting of 
adequate and well controlled investigations, including 
clinical investigations.” Hynson, 412 U.S. at 614, 617. See 
also United States v. 9/1 KG. Container, 854 F.2d 173, 177 
(7th Cir. 1988) (discussing veterinary drugs, the court 
held that a new drug “ 
. 
. 
. requires data from large 
populations of animals and the application of powerful 
statistical techniques.”) (emphasis added). The unavoid- 
able dilemma, revealing these provisions incapable of 
application to compounded drugs, is that compounded 
drugs are patient-specific, typically varying in dose 
levels, composition, and even delivery form from patient 
to patient. 
Petitioners’ references to § 321(p)(1) always omit crit- 
ical, operative words and phrases. Petitioners always 
only recite the “not generally recognized [] among 
experts” portion, while always omitting the “to evaluate 
the safety and effectiveness” portion. A key element of 
§ 321(p)(1) is evaluation, and as Petitioners themselves 
report, this takes not just vast sums of money, but a vast 
patient database. (Pets.’ Br. 26-27.) This database will 
never exist for compounds. Section 321(p)(1) requires 
that, to qualify as a new drug, the expert recognition 
comes after intense evaluation and, hence, must be capa- 
ble of being evaluated. This “evaluation” is impossible 
for compounds because compounds fill extemporaneous 
needs not met by manufactured drugs and, consequentty, 
would never generate a sufficiently sizeable statistical 
base upon which to determine safety and efficacy to the 
degree required for a new drug. This standard has never 
37 
been applied to compounds and, if it ever were, com- 
pounds would be eliminated, other than on a purely 
intrastate basis. 
Petitioners’ own cases establish that the new drug 
approval requirements cannot be applied to compounds. 
See, e.g., Hynson, 412 U.S. at 618 (a test drug must be 
“standardized as to identity, strength, quality, purity and 
dosage form to give significance to the results of the 
investigation”) (citation omitted); United States v. Ruther- 
ford, 442 U.S. 544, 553 (1979) (effectiveness of new drug 
must be “formed on substantial evidence”); Weinberger v. 
Bentex Pharm., Inc., 412 U.S. 645, 652 (1973) (whether a 
drug is a new drug “

. 
. depends in part on expert 
knowledge and experience . 
. 
. based on controlled clini-

These cases establish that compounds are categorically 
incapable of being subjected to the intense investigation 
required of new drugs. Compounds are not standardized, 
as required by Hynson and, by definition, do not exist in 
sufficient quantities for clinical trials.

III. PETITIONERS CANNOT CONDITION EXEMP-

PROCESS ON RELINQUISHMENT OF FIRST 
AMENDMENT RIGHTS 
Respondents maintain compounds are not new 
drugs. In the event this Court disagrees with that conten- 
tion, Respondents further maintain an exemption cannot 
be conditioned on restrictions of First Amendment rights. 
Petitioners assert that FDAMA’s exemption of com- 
pounds from the FDCA’s new drug approval require- 
ments is contingent upon pharmacies’ surrender of their 
First Amendment right to advertise and solicit. (Pets.’ Br. 
7-8, 18-19, 42-43.) Petitioners claim that the “government 
may condition a benefit on their relinquishment of a 
constitutional right if waiver of the right is reasonably 
related to the benefit.” (Pets.’ Br. 42-43, citing Dolan v. City 
of Tigard, 512 U.S. 374, 385, 391 (1994) and Pickering v.

Board of Educ., 391 U.S. 563, 568 (1968).) Petitioners did 
not raise this argument anywhere below and, hence, this

Tobacco Co. v. Reilly, 121 S. Ct. 2404, 2420 (2001) (“We 
decline to reach an issue that was not decided below.”); 
United States v. United Foods, Inc., 121 S. Ct. 2334, 2340 
(2001); United States v. Ortiz, 422 U.S. 891, 896 (1975). 
The claim is based on false assumption as well as a 
syllogism previously rejected by this Court. Petitioners’ 
purported “exemption” of compounds is illusory. As dis- 
cussed above (Part II, supra), by statute and FDA conduct, 
compounds are not and have never been treated as “new 
drugs.” Petitioners have attempted to create a hollow 
bargain - that Respondents must forego their First 
Amendment rights to advertise and solicit in exchange 
for an exemption from regulation that is inapplicable in 
the first place. To dispense compounds, Respondents 
need not relinquish their First Amendment rights. 
If, hypothetically, the Court were to find that com- 
pounds are new drugs, Petitioners nonetheless cannot 
condition an exemption upon Respondents’ relinquish- 
ment of First Amendment rights. See 44 Liquormart Inc. v. 
Rhode Island, 517 U.S. 484, 513 (1996) (Stevens, J., joined

Court has since rejected Petitioners’ position as a false 
syllogism. See 44 Liquormart, 517 U.S. at 510-14. 44 Liquor- 
mart distinguished and disavowed that “greater-includes- 
the-lesser” argument because “it is inconsistent with both

ment makes clear that the Constitution presumes that 
attempts to regulate speech are more dangerous than 
attempts to regulate conduct. 
. 
. 
. As a result, the First 
Amendment directs that government may not suppress

. ”). 
Recognizing that this Court rejected its argument in 
44 Liquormart, Petitioners rework their words and try 
again. Instead of asserting that the greater power to regu-

restrictions are a condition on an exemption from regulat- 
ing the conduct. This gets Petitioners no farther. 
FDAMA’s restriction on advertisement and solicitation as 
a condition upon the alleged “exemption” violates the 
First Amendment.
