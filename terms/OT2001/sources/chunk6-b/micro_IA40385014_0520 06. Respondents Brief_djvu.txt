STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


The sole issue properly presented in this case is whether 
punitive damages are ever available to plaintiffs under Title 
Il of the ADA and Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 
1973. Whether the facts of this or any other case merit such 
an award is not before the Court. Nonetheless, while 
purporting to present a summary of the facts in the light most 
favorable to respondent and to the jury verdict in the district 
court, petitioners have loaded their factual statement with 
irrelevant descriptions of their own evidence, which the jury 
did not credit. 


A. Facts 


The facts established at trial and credited by the jury can 
be quickly summarized. Respondent Jeffrey Gorman is para- 
plegic as a result of a spinal cord injury and uses a wheel- 
chair for mobility. Tr. 50-51. On the night of May 30, 1992, 
he visited a bar in Kansas City, Missouri, where he hoped to 
meet an acquaintance. Shortly after his arrival, a bouncer 
ejected him from the premises because the bar did not allow 
wheelchairs on the dance floor. Tr. 58-63. 


Believing that he had been discriminated against, Mr. 
Gorman sought the assistance of nearby uniformed police 
officers. Tr. 64-65. The officers, who turned out to be moon- 
lighting as private security, instead arrested Mr. Gorman for 
trespassing and summoned a Kansas City police van to take 
him to the station. Tr. 70, 65. 


When the van arrived, Mr. Gorman saw that it had no 
wheelchair lift, no wheelchair restraints, and no alternative 
means of securely transporting a person with a spinal cord 
injury. Mr. Gorman told the police officers that he would not 
be able to support himself in the van’s seats and could not 
safely be trans, rted in the van. Instead of heeding Mr. Gor- 
man’s warnings, the officers lifted him from his wheelchair 
and placed him on a bench in the back of the van. Because he 
could not sit upright on the bench, the officers attempted to 


use his belt to tie him to a wire mesh wall behind the bench. 
J.A. 5-10. 


When the van started moving, Mr. Gorman could not 
hold himself upright. During the trip, his belt broke and he 
was thrown to the floor of the van, where he remained the 
rest of the way to the station. His full urine bag (which the 
police officers had not allowed him to empty before loading 
him onto the van) broke when he fell, soaking him. Tr. 79- 
83. He suffered severe injuries to his shoulder and back from 
the fall as well as an infection that resulted from urine being 
forced back into his body by pressure on the bag before it 
burst. Mr. Gorman’s injuries required extensive medical 
treatment including shoulder surgery. Unfortunately, the back 
injuries Mr. Gorman suffered in the van cannot be surgically 
repaired because of his preexisting spinal injury. As a result, 
Mr. Gorman continues to suffer intense pain and spasms in 
his lower extremities—conditions that are likely to be per- 
manent and that have prevented him from continuing to work 
full-time. J.A. 12-20.. The actual damages Mr. Gorman in- 
curred from his treatment at the hands of the police included 
lost wages, medical bills, pain and suffering, and humiliation. 


Mr. Gorman’s injuries are not attributable to an isolated 
incident of police misconduct. The evidence established that 
the officers who arrested and transported Mr. Gorman had no 
training in the safe transportation of disabled persons, and 
that the Kansas City Police Department had no vans that 
were accessible to passengers in wheelchairs and no policies 
concerning the transportation of people with disabilities. J.A. 
25-29, 42. Moreover, petitioners stipulated that although they 
had repeatedly filed written assurances with the federal gov- 
ernment that their practices and policies complied with the 
Rehabilitation Act of 1973, those assurances were false. J.A. 


' Petitioners’ outlandish speculation that Mr. Gorman suffered these 
injuries while careening down a hill with a woman on his lap was contra- 
dicted by Mr. Gorman’s evidence at trial and rejected by the jury. 


69. Petitioners also stipulated that they did not begin to bring 
themselves into compliance with the Rehabilitation Act nor 
to train their officers about the requirements of the ADA and 
the Rehabilitation Act until after Mr. Gorman was injured. 
J.A. 69-70. 


B. Procedural History 


The procedural history of this case is convoluted, but its 
relevant features are straightforward. Mr. Gorman sued peti- 
tioners, Kansas City’s Police Commissioners, in the United 
States District Court for the Western District of Missouri, 
seeking actual and punitive damages under Title II of the 
ADA and Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act. After two 
appeals, the case was tried to a jury, which rendered a verdict 
for Mr. Gorman and awarded over $1 million in compensa- 
tory damages and $1.2 million in punitive damages. 


Petitioners moved for judgment as a matter of law, argu- 
ing that punitive damages are never available against any de- 
fendant for violations of Title [I or Section 504. Petitioners 
did not assert that their status as a governmental entity ren- 
dered them immune from punitive damages under the ration- 
ale of City of Newport v. Fact Concerts, Inc., 453 U.S. 247 
(1981). The district court accepted petitioners’ argument and 
set aside the jury’s award of punitive damages. 


The parties filed cross-appeals. Mr. Gorman’s appeal ar- 
gued that punitive damages are available under Title II of the 
ADA and Section 504, while petitioners’ appeal rested pri- 
marily on their argument that they were an instrumentality of 
the State of Missouri and were entitled to Eleventh Amend- 
ment immunity from any action for damages (compensatory 
or punitive) under Title [1 or Section 504. In opposing Mr. 
Gorman’s appeal, petitioners again did not argue that puni- 
tive damages were unavailable under City of Newport. 

The Eighth Circuit reversed the district court’s holding 
that punitive damages are unavailable under Title II and Sec- 
tion 504, but affirmed in all other respects. The court of ap- 


4 


peals rejected petitioners’ argument that the Kansas City 
Board of Police Commissioners is an arm of the State entitled 
‘o Eleventh Amendment immunity from suit in federal court. 
With respect to the award of punitive damages, the court 
ruled that because both Title II and Section 504 create private 
rights of action without specifying the precise relief avail- 
able, any appropriate relief is presumptively available under 
this Court’s decision in Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public 
Schools, 503 U.S. 60 (1992). The court held that punitive 
damages fall within the scope of the relief available under 
Title If and Section 504 in appropriate circumstances. Ac- 
cordingly, the Eighth Circuit remanded the case to the district 
court for “an independent review of the evidence to deter- 
mine whether it supports punitive damages.” Pet. App. 16a. 


SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


Title I of the Americans with Disabilities Act, enacted in 
1990, broadly outlaws discrimination against people with 
disabilities in the provision of public services by public enti- 
ties. Title II expands and extends rights previously granted 
only against recipients of federal funds under Section 504 of 
the Rehabilitation Act of 1973. Consistent with the broad 
purposes of Title II, Congress adopted a sweeping set of 
remedies. In contrast with other provisions of the ADA, 
which set forth express limits on remedies, Title II adopted 
the remedial provisions applicable to Section 504 of the Re- 
habilitation Act (as well as other federal civil rights statutes 
such as Title VI and Title [X)—remedies that had already 


been held by this Court to include private rights of action . 


with no expressly stated limits on the relief available. 

In adopting the unfettered remedial provisions of Title II, 
Congress unambiguously provided plaintiffs with a right of 
action for damages, and, indeed, with the full panoply of 
remedies generally available at law and in equity. Congress’ 
stated intent to provide the full array of legal and equitable 
relief corresponds precisely with the generally applicable 
presumption that a federal court may award any appropriate 


5 


relief when Congress provides a private right of action and 
does not clearly limit the relief available. Franklin v. Gwin- 
nett County Public Schools, 503 U.S. 60 (1992). 


Because punitive damages have long been recognized by 
this Court as an important tool in the arsenal of relief avail- 
able in the federal courts under appropriate circumstances, 
their availability under Title II of the ADA, as well as under 
Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act, is dictated both by 
Congress’ affirmative desire to make available the full pano- 
ply of remedies and by the Franklin presumption that all such 
remedies should be provided unless Congress directs other- 
wise. Moreover, punitive damages serve the purposes of both 
Title II of the ADA and Section 504 by supplementing other 
enforcement mechanisms and adding the deterrence neces- 
sary to achieve Congress’ goal of eliminating discrimination 
against people with disabilities. 

Petitioners’ status as a municipality does nothing to alter 
these conclusions. The argument that petitioners’ govern- 
mental status bars punitive damages under City of Newport v. 
Fact Concerts, Inc., 453 U.S. 247 (1981), was never pre- 
sented or decided below and is thus not before the Court. 
Even if it were, any presumption that punitive damages are 
not available against government entities would be overcome 
by clear evidence of congressional intent that governmental 
bodies be subject to the same rules of liability under the 
ADA and the Rehabilitation Act as private persons. More- 
over, the policy considerations dispositive in Newport are 
absent here. In particular, the alternative deterrent mecha- 
nism of punitive damages against individual officials who 
violate the law is unavailable under the ADA and Reha- 
bilitation Act, which have not been held to create rights of 
action against individuals. In addition, punitive damages 
awards will at times be necessary to prevent local govern- 
ments and their taxpayers from reaping financial windfalls 
through noncompliance with the ADA.

ARGUMENT 


I. TITLE If OF THE ADA CREATES A PRIVATE 
RIGHT OF ACTION WITH A FULL PANOPLY OF 
REMEDIES. 


Underlying all the arguments of petitioners and the So- 
licitor General is the notion that the right of action at issue in 
this case is an “implied” one. But unlike other cases where 
the “implication” of a private right of action is a matter of 
debate and inference, here it is manifest that Congress ex- 
plicitly created a private right of action in Title Il of the ADA 
when it expressly incorporated remedial provisions under 
which the existence of a private right of action had already 
been established both by judicial decision and by direct leg- 
islation. Equally apparent is that the right of action Congress 
created carries with it a panoply of remedies specifically in- 
cluding damages. Because an understanding of these points is 
critical to the ultimate determination of the relief available 
under Title II, a detailed explanation of the remedial provi- 
sions of Title II and their relationship with other parts of the 
ADA—and other civil rights statutes—is necessary. 


A. Congress Deliberately Created a Private Right of 
Action to Enforce Title II of the ADA. 


The enactment of the Americans with Disabilities Act re- 
flected Congress’ determination to eradicate discrimination 
against pecple with disabilities and to mandate the steps 
needed to provide them with equal access to employment, 
government services, and public accommodations. To 
achieve these ends, Congress invoked all of its powers, “in- 
cluding the power to enforce the fourteenth amendment and 
to regulate commerce, in order to address the major areas of 
discrimination faced day-to-day by people with disabilities.” 
42 U.S.C. § 12101(b)(4). The resulting legislation, as this 
Court has recognized, is a “broad mandate,” “sweeping” in 
its scope and “expansive” in its purposes—‘“a milestone on 
the path to a more decent, tolerant, progressive society.” PGA 


7 


Tour, Inc. v. Martin, 532 U.S. 661, 675, 680 (2001) (quoting 
Board of Trustees of University of Alabama vy. Garrett, 531 
U.S. 356, 375 (2001) (Kennedy, J., concurring)). 


The ADA’s substantive provisions are divided into three 
titles addressing different aspects of the problem of unequal 
treatment of people with disabilities and providing remedies 
of very different scope. Title I of the Act generally forbids 
employment discrimination against people with disabilities 
by both governmental and private entities. ADA § 102, 42 
U.S.C. § 12112. As originally enacted, Title I incorporated 
the “circumscribed remedies” (Landgraf v. USI Film Prod- 
ucts, 511 U.S. 251, 285 n.38 (1994)) provided by Title VII of 
the Civil Rights Act of 1964—remedies that, as of 1990, did 
not include damages. ADA § 107(a), 42 U.S.C. § 12117(a) 
(incorporating remedies of 42 U.S.C. §§ 2000e-4, -5, -6, -8, 
and -9); see, e.g., Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 252 (“Before the 
enactment of the 1991 [Civil Rights] Act, Title VII afforded 
only ‘equitable’ remedies.”); United States v. Burke, 504 
U.S. 229, 238 (1992) (before passage of the Civil Rights Act 
of 1991, Title VII did “not allow awards of compensatory or 
punitive damages; instead, it limit[ed] available remedies to 
backpay, injunctions, and other equitable relief”). 


Similarly, Title II of the ADA, which forbids discrimi- 
nation in the provision of “public accommodations” by pri- 
vate entities (see ADA § 302, 42 U.S.C. § 12182), incorpo-

A. Any Appropriate Relief—Including Punitive 
Damages—Is Presumptively Available. 

As the Eighth Circuit correctly perceived, this Court’s 
most authoritative pronouncement on the issue is Franklin v. 
Gwinnett County Public Schools. There, the issue was 
whether the private right of action under Title IX, which the 
Court had previously recognized in Cannon, encompassed a 
damages remedy in cases involving intentional violations. 
Franklin’ s holding that damages were available was based on 
the principle “that if a right of action exists to enforce a fed- 
eral right and Congress is silent on the question of remedies, 
a federal court may order any appropriate relief,” 503 US. at 
69—a principle that, the Court stated, “has deep roots in our 
jurisprudence.” Id. at 66. 


Accordingly, the Court held, “although we examine the 
text and history of a statute to determine whether Congress 
intended to create a right of action, ... we presume the avail- 
ability of all appropriate remedies unless Congress has ex- 
pressly indicated otherwise.” /d. Elsewhere in the opinion, 
the Court stated the rule as follows: “[A]ll appropriate relief 
is available in an action brought to vindicate a federal right 
when Congress has given no indication of its purpose with 
respect to remedies,” id. at 68; “[T]he ‘existence of a statu- 
tory right implies the existence of all necessary and appropri- 
ate remedies,” id. at 69 (quoting Sullivan v. Little Hunting 
Park, Inc., 396 U.S. 229, 239 (1969)); “[TJhe traditional pre- 
sumption [is] in favor of a federal court’s power to award 

iate relief in a cognizable cause of action,” id. at 70; 
“The general rule, therefore, is that absent clear direction to 
the contrary by Congress, the federal courts have the power 
to award any appropriate relief in a cognizable cause of ac-

tion brought pursuant to a federal statute,” id. at 70-71; 
“(T]he traditional presumption [is] in favor of any appropri- 
ate relief for violation of a federal right,” id. at 73. The Court 
emphasized the importance of “our historic judicial authority 
to award appropriate relief in cases brought in our court sys- 
tem,” and declined to engage in “selective abdication” of this 
authority by ruling out particular remedies absent clear con- 
gressional directives foreclosing their use. /d. at 74. 


Franklin rested on a long line of decisions of this Court 
holding that “where legal rights have been invaded, and a 
federal statute provides for a general right to sue for such in- 
vasion, federal courts may use any available remedy to make 
good the wrong done.” Bell v. Hood, 327 U.S. 678, 684 
(1946); see Franklin, 503 U.S. at 66-68 (citing case law un- 
derlying Bell v. Hood). The opinion in Franklin pointed out 
that this Court had consistently adhered to this principle in 
cases such as Sullivan v. Little Hunting Park, 396 U.S. 229 
(1969), Carey v. Piphus, 435 U.S. 247 (1978), and Merrill 
Lynch, Pierce, Fenner & Smith, Inc. v. Curran, 456 U.S. 353 
(1982). More pertinent here, the Court observed that the ac- 
ceptance of a damages remedy under Title VI by the majority 
of the Justices in Guardians, as well as the Court’s recogni- 
tion of a.retrospective monetary remedy for Section 504 vio- 
lations in Consolidated Rail Corp. v. Darrone, 465 U.S. at 
631, reflected applications of the presumption in favor of all 
appropriate remedies in cases where a right of action exists. 


The Franklin presumption is fully applicable to punitive 
damages. This Court has repeatedly recognized that punitive 
damages are a part of the ordinary remedial arsenal of the 
courts and have long been “a firmly established feature of 
American law.” Pacific Mutual Life Ins. Co. v. Haslip, 499 
U.S. 1, 26 (1991) (Scalia, J., concurring in the judgment). In 
Curtis v. Loether, 415 U.S. 189 (1974), the Court held that 
the Seventh Amendment afforded a right to trial by jury in 
actions seeking damages under Title VIII of the Civil Rights 
Act of 1968 (42 U.S.C. § 3613), and observed that “the relief

Corp., 464 U.S. 238, 255 (1984), Browning-Ferris Industries 
v. Kelco Disposal, Inc., 492 U.S. 257, 274, 278 & n.24 
(1989), and Haslip, 499 U.S. at 15-17. As the Court stated in 
Molzof v. United States, 502 U.S. 301, 306 (1992): 


Although the precise nature and use of punitive dam- 
ages may have evolved over time, and the size and fre- 
quency of such awards may have increased, this Court’s 
decisions make clear that the concept of “punitive dam- 
ages” has a long pedigree in the law. “It is a well- 
established principle of the common law, that in actions 
for trespass and all actions on the case for torts, a jury 
may inflict what are called exemplary, punitive, or vin- 
dictive damages upon a defendant, having in view the 
enormity of his offense rather than the measure of com-

363, 371 (1852). 


Or, as the Court put it more succinctly in United States v. 
Burke: “[(P]unitive or exemplary damages are generally

that punitive damages are potentially available in actions to

Congress has foreclosed them. Thus, the Court has held that 
punitive damages are available in actions under 42 U.S.C.

C. The Punitive Damages Remedy Serves the 
Policies of Title II and Section 504. 


A punitive damages remedy in appropriate cases is fully 
consistent with the policies underlying both Title II of the 
ADA and Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act. The ADA 
reflects an ambitious and far-reaching policy “to provide a 
clear and comprehensive national mandate for the elimina-

and, to achieve that purpose, “to provide clear, strong, con-

achieve that end. The Committee Reports on the Act ob-

B. The Franklin Presumption Applies to Section 
505 of the Rehabilitation Act. 


Notwithstanding that Franklin’s presumption in favor of 
any appropriate relief is generally applicable to punitive 
damages, petitioners (but not the Solicitor General) argue 
that the presumption is specifically inapplicable to the right 
of action created by Section 505(a) of the Rehabilitation Act, 
and, by extension, to Title I of the ADA. Petitioners contend 
that this Court so held in Lane v. Pefia, 518 U.S. 187 (1996), 
but their argument tears language from that opinion out of its 
context and distorts the holding of the Court. 


The issue in Lane was whether, in creating a right of ac- 
tion for monetary damages for violations of Section 504 of 
the Rehabilitation Act, Section 505(a)(2) constituted a suffi- 
ciently unambiguous waiver of the sovereign immunity of the 
United States to permit actions for damages against federal 
agencies alleged to have violated Section 504 directly in the 
conduct of their activities. The Court held that such actions 
were barred by sovereign immunity because Section 
505(a)(2) expressly provided for remedies only against fed- 
eral agencies acting in their capacities as “provider[s] of 
[Federal financial] assistance” and did not explicitly create a 
right of action against federal agencies that directly con- 
ducted programs or activities. See 518 U.S. at 192-93. Lane’s 
holding that Section 505(a)(2) did not provide a cause of ac- 
tion against a federal agency acting as a direct provider of 
government services has no bearing on the issue here, as it is 
undisputed that both the ADA and the Rehabilitation Act 
provide a cause of action against petitioners. 


To be sure, in arriving at its holding, Lane rejected the 
argument that the Franklin presumption supported a damages 
claim against the federal government under Section 
505(a)(2), for two reasons. First, the requirement that waivers 
of the sovereign immunity of the United States be express 
precluded resort to Franklin’s presumption about the avail- 
ability of remedies not expressly provided. 518 U.S. at 196. 


35 


Second, the Court noted that Section 505(a)(2) expressly ad- 
dresses the issue of what entities it authorizes remedies 
against, yet omits federal agencies except when acting as 
funding providers. Jd. at 196. By expressly addressing that 
particular issue, the Court held, Section 505(a)(2) rendered 
the Franklin principle inapplicable to the specific question 
presented in Lane: whether damages were available against 
federal agencies not acting as funding providers. /d. 


In holding that Section 505(a)(2)’s specificity about who 
was amenable to suit took “this case outside the ‘general 
rule’ we discussed in Franklin,” id. (emphasis added), the 
Court did not, as petitioners suggest, hold that the Franklin 
presumption was altogether inapplicable to remedies under 
Section 505(a)(2). Such a holding would have made no 
sense, for although Section 505(a)(2) specifically addresses 
who is subject to the remedies it authorizes, it does not spe- 
cifically delineate the scope of the relief available against 
those entities. Instead, it incorporates the remedies that are 
impliedly available under Title VI, to which the Franklin pre- 
sumption applies. See Franklin, 503 U.S. at 70.'* Thus, the 
Franklin presumption also necessarily controls the scope of 
the relief available under Section 505(a), and, therefore, 
under Title Il of the ADA.

C. Public Policy Supports Awards of Punitive 
Damages Against Municipalities under Title 
II and Section 504. 

Even where the Newport presumption would otherwise 
apply, it may be overcome if the public policy concerns un- 
derlying it are outweighed by other policy considerations in- 
dicating a need for a punitive damages remedy under the 
particular statutory scheme at issue. See Newport, 453 U.S. at 
266. Here, those policy concerns strongly support the avail- 
ability of punitive damages against municipal governments. 

Newport recognized the important role that punitive dam- 
ages under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 play in deterring official mis- 
conduct. 453 U.S. at 268. Critical to the Court’s decision not 
to permit such damages against governmental entities was its 
view that the “more effective” deterrent was the availability 
under Section 1983 of punitive damages directly against the 
particular officials who engaged in misconduct. Jd. at 270. As 
the Court stated, “By allowing juries and courts to assess 
punitive damages in appropriate circumstances against the 
offending official, based on his personal financial resources, 
the statute directly advances the public’s interest in prevent- 
ing repeated constitutional deprivations.” Jd. at 269; see also 
Smith v. Wade, 461 U.S. at 36 n.5 (emphasizing the impor- 
tance of this rationale to the holding in Newport). 


Given that both the ADA and the Rehabilitation Act are 
aimed at eliminating discrimination against people with dis- 
abilities in the provision of public services by State and local 
governments, the importance of deterrence under those stat- 
utes is at least as critical as it is under Section 1983. Unlike 
Section 1983, however, Title II of the ADA and Section 
505(a)(2) of the Rehabilitation Act have been held not to 
provide remedies directly against offending officials in their 
personal capacities. See Garcia v. §.U.N.Y. Health Sciences 


48 


Center, 280 F.3d at 107 (“Insofar as Garcia is suing the indi- 
vidual defendants in their individual capacities, neither Title 
II nor § 504 of the Rehabilitation Act provides for individual 
capacity suits against state officials.”) (collecting cases). Be- 
cause the ostensibly “more effective” deterrent is apparently 
unavailable under Title [1 and Section 504, these statutes 
(unlike Section 1983, which was at issue in Newport) must 
depend for their deterrent effect on the remedies they provide 
directly against the entities to which they apply. Foreclosing 
punitive damages would materially interfere with the stat- 
utes’ goal of making violation more costly than compliance. 


Moreover, the countervailing policy concern that the 
Court found decisive in City of Newport—namely, the unde- 
sirability of penalizing innocent taxpayers for acts of mis- 
conduct by public officials from which the taxpayers have 
derived no benefit, see 453 U.S. at 266, 270—is largely ab- 
sent here. The affirmative obligations imposed on public en- 
tities by Title II and Section 504 often have costs attached: 
modifying facilities, acquiring appropriate vehicles and other 
equipment, and training personnel so that people with dis- 
abilities are not denied the full benefits of public programs 
and services.”’ Unlike other civil rights statutes, violations of 
Title II will frequently stand to benefit taxpayers (at least in 
the short term) to some degree. The cost of compliance will 
likely exceed the costs that may be imposed in the few in- 
stances when a plaintiff sues for a violation and is able to es- 
tablish that he has suffered actual damages.”'
