STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


In May 1998, Traffic Stream (BVI) Infrastructure Limited 
(“Traffic Stream”), a corporation organized under the laws 
of the British Virgin Islands, raised $119,000,000 in the 
United States debt markets by entering into an Indenture, 
pursuant to which Traffic Stream issued its 14'4% Senior 


‘Secured Notes due 2006, to holders of the Notes. The Chase 


Manhattan Bank (“Chase”), a corporation existing under and 
by the laws of the State of New York and authorized 
to exercise trust powers, entered into the Indenture with 
Traffic Stream and agreed to serve as Trustee. (JA 20-144)' 
The Indenture stated that “(Traffic Stream] irrevocably 
submits to the jurisdiction of any court of the State of New

Beginning in January 1999, Traffic Stream committed

due in May 1999. (JA 12-15) Chase commenced this action on

and a deficiency judgment. (JA 18) Traffic Stream, in its 
amended answer, admitted the Events of Default, but asserted 
that its performance to pay any amount under the Indenture 
was excused based on the impossibility defense. (JA 165-75) 
Chase moved for summary judgment. (JA 196-97) 


Summary judgment was granted by the district court in 
an opinion and order dated January 31, 2000. Chase 
Manhattan Bank v. Traffic Stream (BVI) Infrastructure Ltd.,

Traffic Stream appealed to the Second Circuit, raising 
only the issues regarding the impossibility defense in its brief. 
At the oral argument before the Second Circuit Panel on 
December 1, 2000, the Panel raised the issue of whether 


5 


subject matter jurisdiction existed under 28 U.S.C. 
§ 1332(a)(2) in light of the fact that the defendant Traffic 
Stream was incorporated in a United Kingdom Overseas 
Territory, namely the British Virgin Islands. The Panel 
referred the parties to three recent cases in which the Second 
Circuit found that residents and corporations from other 
United Kingdom Overseas Territories, namely Hong Kong 
(prior to its transfer to the People’s Republic of China) and 
Bermuda, were not “citizens or subjects of a foreign state” 
for purposes of alienage jurisdiction. 


After the parties submitted supplemental briefing on this 
issue, the Second Circuit, on May 24, 2001, held that there 
was no alienage jurisdiction for residents or corporations 
from the British Virgin Islands because such residents or 
entities were “stateless” due to the fact that the United States 
does not regard the British Virgin Islands as an independent, 
sovereign political entity. The Second Circuit rejected the 
argument that the residents and individuals from the British 
Virgin Islands were citizens or subjects of the United 
Kingdom. The Second Circuit did not address materials that 
were submitted by Chase, which demonstrated the United 
Kingdom’s and the United States Department of State’s 
position that the residents and corporations from the British 
Overseas Territories were citizens or subjects of the United 
Kingdom, and should be recognized as such for purposes of 
alienage jurisdiction. The Second Circuit also did not address 
its overruling of its 1996 decision in Peregrine Myanmar 
Ltd. v. Segal, 89 F.3d 41, 45 n.4 (2d Cir. 1996), which had 
recognized alienage jurisdiction in a case involving a resident 
of the British Virgin Islands.

A. The Ruling in the Second Circuit Has Caused a Split in 
the Circuits and Destroyed the Uniform Application of 
Alienage Jurisdiction

corporations from the British Overseas Territories are “stateless” 
has been rejected by every other Circuit of the Court of Appeals 
that has addressed this issue. The Third, Fourth, and Seventh 
Circuits have uniformly held that residents and corporations 
from these Overseas Territories are “citizens or subjects” of the 
United Kingdom for purposes of alienage jurisdiction.

the district court had alienage jurisdiction over the dispute. The 
Third Circuit stated:

B. The Second Circuit’s Opinion is Contrary to the 
Founding Fathers’ Intent to Ensure Aliens Access to 
the Federal Courts 


The Supreme Court should also review the Second 
Circuit’s decision because it vitiates the very reasons the 
Founding Fathers included alienage jurisdiction in the 
Constitution. After the Revolutionary War and prior to the 
adoption of the Constitution, the state courts, fueled by a 
mixture of anti-British and anticreditor sentiment, made it 
difficult for British creditors to collect debts from local 
debtors. See Kevin R. Johnson, Why Alienage Jurisdiction? 
Historical Foundations and Modern Justifications For 
Federal Jurisdiction Over Disputes Involving Noncitizens, 
21 Yale J. Int’) L. 1, 6 (1996). Alienage jurisdiction was seen 
as a means of addressing this problem by providing foreign 
litigants with a neutral federal forum. See id. The writings 
of the Founding Fathers indicate that two predominant 
reasons were advanced as the basis for alienage jurisdiction. 


12 


The first of these reasons was to prevent entanglements 
with foreign governments that became upset when 
their citizens were unfairly treated by a less-than-neutral 
state court. Alexander Hamilton set forth this concern in 
The Federalist No. 80: 


13 


discrimination between the cases of one 
complexion and those of the other. So great a 
proportion of the cases in which foreigners are 
parties involve national questions that it is by far 
most safe and most expedient to refer all those in

the disposal of a PART. The Union will 
undoubtedly be answerable to foreign powers for 
the conduct of its members. And the responsibility 
for an injury ought ever to be accompanied with 
the faculty of preventing it. As the denial or 
perversion of justice by the sentences of courts, 
as well as in any other manner, is with reason 
classed among the just causes of war, it will follow 
that the federal judiciary ought to have cognizance 
of all causes in which the citizens of other 
countries are concerned. This is not less essential

security of the public tranquility. A distinction may

amended such that the term “alien” was replaced by the 
current “citizens or subjects” language. This change, 
however, was not made in order to narrow the meaning of 
“alien”, but rather was intended to have the statute mirror 
the Constitution in order to make it clear that the federal 
courts did not have jurisdiction over disputes between aliens. 
See Johnson, supra, at 21.

“It is axiomatic that federal courts are courts of limited 
jurisdiction and may not decide cases over which they lack 
subject matter jurisdiction.” Lyndonville Sav. Bank & Trust 
Co. v. Lussier, 211 F.3d 697, 700 (2d Cir. 2000). “[FJailure

raised at any time by a party or by the court sua sponte. If 
subject matter jurisdiction is lacking, the action must be 
dismissed.” Jd. at 700-01 (citing Bender v. Williamsport Area 
Sch. Dist., 475 U.S. 534, 541 (1986); United Food & 
Commercial Workers Union, Local 919 v. CenterMark Props. 
Meriden Square, Inc., 30 F.3d 298, 301 (2d Cir. 1994)).

Chase’s claims against Traffic Stream arise under state 
law. The federal courts have subject matter jurisdiction over 
such claims if, inter alia, the-parties are “(1) citizens of 
different States; (2) citizens of a State and citizens or subjects 
of a foreign state; [or] (3) citizens of different States and in 
which citizens or subjects of a foreign state are additional 
parties.” 28 U.S.C. § 1332(a). The district court in the case 
at bar proceeded under § 1332(a)(2) on the apparent, 
unchallenged assumption that Traffic Stream is a “citizen[ ] 
or subject[ ] of a foreign state” — the United Kingdom or 
the British Virgin Islands. 


In Matimak Trading Co. v. Khalily, 118 F.3d 76 (2d Cir. 
1997), cert. denied, 522 U.S. 1091 (1998), we held that a 
plaintiff corporation organized under the laws of Hong Kong 
did not qualify as a“‘citizen[ ] or subject[ ] of a foreign state” 
under § 1332(a)(2). At the time of the suit, Hong Kong was 
a British Dependent Territory. We concluded that “the United 
States does not regard Hong Kong as an independent, 
sovereign political entity,” and the plaintiff was therefore 
not a “citizen{ ] or subject[ ] of a foreign state” by virtue of 
its incorporation in Hong Kong. /d. at 82. Under British law, 
we further held, the plaintiff was not a citizen or subject of 
the United Kingdom. /d. at 85, 86. We therefore decided that 
the plaintiff Hong Kong corporation was technically 
“stateless” and that “a stateless person — the proverbial man 
without a country — cannot sue a United States citizen under 
alienage jurisdiction.” Jd. at 86. 


Similarly, in Koehler v. Bank of Bermuda (New York) 
Lid., 209 F.3d 130, rehearing in banc denied, 229 F.3d 187 
(2d Cir. 2000), the defendants were Bermuda corporations

In assessing the record to determine whether genuine

2. No Showing of “Impossibility” 


Assuming arguendo that section 501 does not itself bar 
defendant’s claim of impossibility, defendant’s claim must 
still fail because defendant cannot, as a matter of law, meet 
the requirements of an impossibility defense. I note at the 
outset, that parties attempting to invoke impossibility of 
performance have an uphill battle. New York courts have 
been consistently hostile to the defense, allowing it to excuse 
performance only in the most extreme circumstances. 


a. Objectively Impossible 


As set forth above, the first element of an impossibility 
defense is a showing by defendant that performance has been 
rendered objectively impossible: “Impossibility excuses a 
party’s performance only when the destruction of the subject 
matter of the contract or the means of performance makes 
performance objectively impossible.” Ke/ Kim, 70 N.Y.2d at 
902; see also 407 East 61st Garage, Inc. v. Savoy Fifth Avenue 
Corp., 23 N.Y.2d 275, 281 (1968) (“[I)mpossibility of 
performance is limited to the destruction of the means of 
performance by an act of God, vis major, or by law.”). 


Of particular relevance to this case is the well-settled 
rule in New York that “ ‘where impossibility or difficulty of 
performance is occasioned only by financial difficulty or
