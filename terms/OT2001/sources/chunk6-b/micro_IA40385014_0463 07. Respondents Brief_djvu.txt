QUESTIONS PRESENTED 


Where unlawful discrimination allegedly occurs within the 
180/300 day charge filing period, may an employer also be 
held liable for discrimination occurring prior to the charge 
filing period if ‘he earlier discrimination was sufficiently like 
or related to the discrimination occurring with the period that 
the employer was on notice of the need to investigate those 
earlier events? 


Where a hostile work environment is claimed to exist 
within the 180/300 day chargevfiiling period, must the trier of 
fact consider acts of harassment and/or other discrimination 
occurring outside of the charge filing period which may have 
helped create the hostile environment in order to determine 
the employer’s liability? 


In a discrimination case, is the trier of fact permitted to 
consider evidence of the employer’s conduct or acts of har- 
assment occurring prior to the 180/300 day charge filing pe- 
riod in order to determine the employer’s motive and intent, 
or the existence of a hostile work environment with the 
charge filing period?

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


Abner Morgan worked for Amtrak from August 1990 un- 
til March 1995. Amended Declaration of Abner Morgan 
(“Morgan Decl.”), Civil Docket (“CD”) No. 106 at 2, 6, 7.' 
In August 1990, Amtrak was taking over the Oakland Yard 
from Southern Pacific Railroad. Declaration (“Decl.”) of Joe 
George, CD No. 98 at 1-2. Most of Morgan’s co_workers 
were or had been Southern Pacific Railroad employees, in- 
cluding most of his managers. /d. 


' Whenever a declaration or deposition is referenced, it refers to a 

Declaration or deposition filed in the District Court in Opposition 

to Amtrak's Motion for Summary Judgment. The initial numbers 
refer to the Civil Docket. Page numbers are to the actual pages in 

the document. 


2 


On February 25, 1991, Morgan was asked to come to 
a meeting with his supervisor, Jerry Denton, and the facilities 
manager, Robert Vandenburg. Morgan Decl., CD No. 106 at 
2. When Morgan refused to attend the meeting without union 
representation, he was terminated for insubordination in vio- 
lation of Rule L. /d.. After filing a union grievance, Morgan 
was reinstated and the termination was reduced to a 10-day 
suspension. Jd at 2-3. This was the harshest discipline im- 
posed on any employee at the Yard for a Rule L violation 
during the period between 1989 and 1992. Trial Tr. at 1651- 
52. 


During his tenure at Amtrak, Morgan was subjected to a 
barrage of disciplinary charges, even though white employ- 
ees were rarely, if ever, disciplined for comparable behavior. 
CD No.93, Deposition (“Dep.”) of Gary A. Biemborn at 147- 
50; CD No. 93, Dep. of Elwood Conley at 20-24, 28-29; CD 
No. 93, Dep. of Glenn O. Fisher at 44-46, 50-53; CD No. 93, 
Dep. of Greg Hourigan at 56-59; Decl. of Robert Ramirez, 
CD No. 100 at 1-3. Racial epithets and disparate treatment 
of African American employees were common, particular'y 
of Morgan. Decl. of Michael Williams, CD No. 91 at 2; 
Decl. of Meriola Gotthardt, CD No. 99 at 2; Decl. of Arthur 
Elwood Conley, CD No. 92 at 2; Decl. of Joe George, CD 
No. 98 at 2; CD No. 93, Conley Dep. at 20-24, 28-29. 


Morgan was repeatedly denied access to training and 
training opportunities. Morgan Decl, CD No. 106 at 3-5. 
When he asked about receiving further training, he was told 
he “stood a snowball’s chance in hell of becoming an electri- 
cian in the Oaklana Yard.” Jd, at 3. He was also assigned 
demeaning tasks outside of his craft that no white electrician 
was ever assigned. /d.; Ramirez Decl., CD No. 100 at 1-2. 


3 


During Morgan’s tenure, the Yard was managed by a 
small group of managers. Vandenburg, the top official at the 
Yard, was heard to state that he didn’t want too many blacks 
as supervisors because of “too many problems.” CD No. 93, 
Conley Dep. at 23-24. His second-in command, Ray Borge, 
was referred to by an African-American manager as “the 
Mark Fuhrman of Oakland.” CD No. 93, Dep. of Raymond 
P. Borge at 136-37. Borge had a habit of calling African- 
Americans “shuffle-butt ni-ers” and even performed ‘the 
shuffle-butt ni-er” dance for his co-supervisors, including 
Vandenburg. CD No. 93, Conley Dep. at 21, 28-29. Other 
managers, including Robert Bordenave, Joe Deely and Jerry 
Denton were overheard using the “N” word at various times. 
Williams Decl., CD No. 99 at 2; CD No. 93, Conley Dep. at

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


This case presents three distinct issues: (1) whether and 
under what circumstances an employer who engages in a 
continuing pattern of discrimination which continues into the 
charge filing period is immune for liability for acts occurring 
prior to the charge filing period; (2) whether harassment oc- 
curring prior to the charge filing period may be relied on by 
a plaintiff to demonstrate the existence of a hostile environ- 
ment during the charge filing period; and (3) whether proof 
of intentional discrimination, including retaliation and/or an 
actionable hostile environment during a charge filing period 
can be based in part on evidence of discriminatory conduct 
which occurred prior to the charge filing period. 


In Havens Realty Corp. v. Coleman, 455 U.S. 363 (1982), 
a unanimous Court recognized that ongoing violations of 
federal anti-discrimination laws must be treated differently 
from discrete, isolated unlawful acts. The Court recognized 
that where the plaintiff alleges a continuing violation, con- 
cerns regarding staleness or prejudice to the defendant are 
absent or diminished. Simply put, an employer that main- 
tains a continuing violation neither deserves nor obtains the 
repose afforded by the statute of limitations. Furthermore, 
the “trigger” for the statute of limitations must be the last 
occurrence of the unlawful practice. 


The 180/300 day charge filing period in Section 706(e)(1) 
does not limit the scope of recovery in an otherwise timely 
Title VII action. 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-5(e)(1). The charge fil- 


8 


ing requirement is only a part of the administrative exhaus- 
tion process. Congress’ concern in enacting the procedural 
steps to secure enforcement of Title VII was to ensure 
prompt notification to the employer. Although § 706(e)(1) 
addresses the time limits for filing a charge with the EEOC, 
it does not purport to delineate the scope of the relief that a 
court might subsequently provide, or the temporal or sub- 
stantive range of violations which a court might ultimately 
address. Where an ongoing violation begins before and con- 
tinues into the charge filing period, a charge filed within 
180/300 days of the last occurrence constitutes adequate ex- 
haustion for the entire violation. 


Section 706(g)(1) of Title VII, which allows back pay 
awards up to two years decisively demonstrates that the 
charge filing period does not limit the period for which relief 
can be obtained. This two year back pay rule would be 
meaningless if the employer’s liability was to be determined 
solely by reference to the shorter 180/300 day period estab- 
lished by § 706(e)(1). Basing the amount of back pay on the 
length of the charge filing period-would have peculiar and 
inconsistent consequences in the variety of situations where 
Congress has mandated relief pursuant to the remedial provi- 
sions of Title VII. 


The explicit language in Title VII referring to an “unlaw- 
ful employment practice” provides a statutory basis for the 
continuing violation doctrine. Section 706(e)(1) requires 
that a charge be filed within a specified number of days of 
the unlawful "practice." Unlike a discrete act of discrimina- 
tion, like a dismissal, a practice can endure or recur over a 
period of time. Petitioner’s contention that § 706(e)(1) must 
always be construed to require the filing of a charge within 


9 


180/300 days of each “discrete act" without regard 
to continuing violations is inconsistent with the wording, 
purpose and intent of the statute. 


The EEOC, having assumed the role once played by the 
Civil Service Commission regarding administrative com- 
plaints of discrimination by federal employee, has followed 
and applied the continuing violation doctrine for at least 
thirty (30) years. The EEOC’s federal sector regulation, 29 
C.F.R. § 1614.105(c)(1), as well as Management Directive 
110, which both codify the doctrine, are entitled to defer- 
ence under Chevron, U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Resources De- 
fense Council, Inc., 467 U.S. 837 (1984). 


Past interpretations of other statutes are of-little value in 
construing Title VII’s particular language. The Clayton Act, 
for example, provides that "[a]ny action . . . shall be forever 
barred unless commenced within four years after the cause of 
action accrued." 15 U.S.C. § 15(b). Title VII contains no 
such express accrual rule. Similarly, the types of recurring 
violations that occur under Title VII are virtually unknown in 
NLRA cases and its provisions are materially different with 
respect to the exhaustion requirements, limiting its preceden- 
tial value. 


The proper formulation of the continuing violation doc- 
trine derives from the text of Title VII itself and from the 
purposes of a statute of limitations. Thus, discrimination oc- 
curring prior to the charge filing period may be redressed 
where that earlier discrimination is sufficiently similar in 
method or purpose to the discrimination alleged by the 
charge to have occurred within the charge filing period that 
the employer can fairly be said to have been put on notice of 
the need to inquire into the relevant pre-charge filing period 


10 


events. This formulation strikes an appropriate 
balance between the broad remedial-purpose of Title VII and 
the notice purpose of the filing requirement. Zipes v. Trans 
World Airlines Inc., 455 U.S. 385, 398 (1982). 


Imposing a notice requirement on Title VII would focus 
exclusively on the desire to encourage prompt filing of 
claims and conflict with harassment victims’ entitlement to 
the maximum benefit of the law. A desire to encourage 
prompt filing of claims cannot undermine the broad remedial 
purpose of the anti-discrimination law and destroy the bal- 
ance created by this Court in Zipes. 


There is a natural affinity between hostile environment 
claims and continuing violations. In a hostile environment 
case, it is the environment itself that constitutes the violation, 
not the individual acts of harassment which cause that envi- 
ronment. Pre-charge filing period harassment normally con- 
tributes to establishing an unlawful hostile environment and 
must be considered by the trier of fact in every circumstance. 
The district court erred in holding that a plaintiff, in attempt- 
ing to show the existence of a hostile environment within 
300 days of his charge, could not rely on earlier acts of har- 
assment. This error alone mandates affirmance of the Ninth 
Circuit’s decision reversing the judgment in favor of Amtrak. 


The district court also erred in forbidding the plaintiff to 
present evidence of, or even to assert during trial that inten- 
tional discrimination had occurred more than 300 days prior 
to his Title VII charge. This Court has repeatedly held that 
iatentional discrimination prior to the charge filing period 
may be utilized to prove an employer's motive during that 
period. See McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 
792 (1973). This error also mandates affirmance of the 


11 


Ninth Circuit’s decision reversing the judgment in 
favor of Amtrak.

A. The 180/300 Day Charge Filing Period Is an 
Exhaustion Rule Not a Limitation on the 
Scope of the Employer’s Liability 


Title VII establishes two deadlines which must be met as a 
precondition of maintaining an action. First, the plaintiff must 
have filed a charge within the requisite period--here 300 days-- 
of the unlawful practice; the filing of that charge commences 
the Title VII exhaustion process. Second, the plaintiff must file 
suit within 90 days of receiving a right to sue letter from the 
EEOC. Neither of these rules delineates the period of time for 
which relief can be awarded to redress a continuing unlawful 
practice. 


“(A] charge of employment discrimination is not the equiva- 
lent of a complaint initiating a lawsuit.” EEOC v. Shell Oil Co., 
466 U.S. 54, 68 (1984). The filing of a charge does not com- 
mence a formal adjudicatory process. Rather, the charge is the 
first step of an informal administrative process. The charge 
calls the existence of an alleged violation to the attention of the 
EEOC, and sets in motion an investigation. Where the investi- 
gation leads the Commission to hold that reasonable cause ex- 
ists to conclude a violation has occurred, Title VII directs the 
Commission to attempt to conciliate the dispute. The overarch- 
ing purpose of this administrative process is to resolve employ- 
ment discrimination claims without the necessity of litigation. 
See West v. Gibson, 527 U.S. 212, 218-19 (1999). 


16 


The strictures that might be applied to a complaint in a civil 
action are not imposed on a Title VII charge. The statutory re- 
quirements regarding a Title VII charge are liberally construed, 
in light of Congress’ awareness that these charges would ordi- 
narily be filed by laymen without the assistance of counsel. 
Love v. Pullman Co., 404 U.S. 522, 527 (1972). The scope of 
the investigation the EEOC may pursue is not strictly limited by 
the terms of the charge. EEOC v. Shell Oil Co., 466 U.S. at 68- 
74. This Court has declined to require Title VII class members 
to file any charges at all, reasoning that doing so would impose 
an undue burden on discrimination victims. Zipes, 455 U.S. at 
397; cf. Bazemore v. Friday, 478 U.S. 385, 405-06 (1986). 


In Occidental Life Insurance Co. v. EEOC, 432 U.S. 355, 
371-72 n.n.29 & 30 (1977), the Court recognized that Congress’ 
concern for the need of time limitations in enacting the proce- 
dural steps to secure enforcement of Title VII was “directed en- 
tirely to the initial filing of a charge with the EEOC and prompt 
notification thereafter to the alleged violator.” Title VII re- 
quires the EEOC to give the employer prompt notice so “that he 
would have the opportunity to gather and preserve the evidence 
with which to sustain himself when formal charges are filed and 
subsequent enforcement proceedings are instituted.” Jd. at 371 
n.30 (quoting 117 Cong. Rec. 31972 (1971)). 


Thus, although § 706(e)(1) addresses the time limits for filing 
a charge with the EEOC, it does not purport to delineate the 
scope of the relief that a court might subsequently provide, or 
the temporal or substantive range of violations which a court 
might ultimately address. Even with regard to the exhaustion 
process itself, § 706(e)(1) does not purport to establish a stan-

B. The Existence of the Two Year Back Pay Rule in 


Section 706(g) Demonstrates That the Charge Filing

Section 706(e)(1) provides that "[a] charge under this section 
shall be filed within" a specified number of days “after the al- 
leged unlawful employment practice occurred." 42 U.S.C. § 
2000e-5(e)(1). Section 706(g)(1) provides that “[bJjack pay li- 
ability shall not accrue from a date more than two years prior to 
the filing of a charge with the Commission." 42 U.S.C. 


§ 2000e-5(g)(1). Since § 706(g)(1) limits back pay awards to 
no more than two years prior to the filing of the charge, it is 
clear that the 300 day charge filing period does not limit the pe- 
riod for which liability can be established. 

1. The Text of Section 706(g)(1) Compels this 
Interpretation of Title Vii 


The temporal scope of the claims for which a plaintiff can 
obtain back pay in a Title VII action turns on the interrelation- 
ship between § 706(e)(1) and § 706(g)(1). On the face of Title 
VIL, it is § 706(g)(1), not § 706(e)(1), which delineates the point 
in time for which monetary relief in the form of back pay can be 
provided. It would have been nonsensical for Congress to cre- 
ate a back pay provision (§ 706(g)(1)) that permits plaintiffs to 
recover damages from their employer for a time period for 
which the employer could not be held liable. 


Section 706(g)({1) recognizes that back pay losses caused by 
an unlawful practice could have been occurring for several years

appropriate cases, a plaintiff can seek to establish liability be-

only “accrue” if it were caused by an actionable violation. For 
example, back pay could only be awarded for wages lost one 
year prior to the charge filing if the plaintiff showed that the 
discriminatory practice was occurring on or before that date.

relevance. Section 706(g)(1) delineates which injuries caused 
by that ongoing practice can be redressed, limiting back pay to 
amounts accruing later than two years before the charge. Read

4. Neither the 180/300 Day Charge Filing Period Nor the 
Two Year Back Pay Rule Limit the Period for Which 
Compensatory And/or Punitive Damages Can Be 
Awarded 


Congress’ treatment of compensatory and punitive damages 
awards under Title VII is expressly, and deliberately, different 
than that of the back pay awards. Congress did not impose a 
temporal limit on compensatory and punitive awards in the 1991 
amendments, despite its knowledge of the two-year time limita- 
tions on back pay awards. Cannon v. Univ. of Chi., 441 U.S. 
677, 696-98 (1979) (stating that Congress is presumed to know 
the existing laws and their interpretation while it is drafting new 
legislation); see also Vimar Seguros y Reaseguros, S.A. v. M/V 
Sky Reefer, 515 U.S. 528, 554 (1995). 


The legislative history of § 706(g)(1) makes clear that the 
primary concern of Congress in adopting the two year rule was 
to protect employers from liability for back pay accruing over 
many years or even decades. When Congress authorized 
awards of compensatory and punitive damages in 1991, it rec- 
ognized that such damages posed a similar risk of large awards. 
With regard to compensatory and punitive damages, Congress 
opted to solve the problem in a different manner, by imposing 
caps on the total amount of such awards. 


A temporal limitation on compensatory and punitive awards 
would often be impracticable as well as ineffective. The pain 


25 


and suffering inflicted, for example, by sexual harassment can- 
not in any meaningful manner be allocated to particular months 
or weeks, and thus, could not effectively be limited by a rule 
such as § 706(g)(1) or § 706(e)(1). Clearly, the courts are not at 
liberty to add the very limitation which Congress declined to 
utilize. 


C. The Continuing Violation Doctrine Is Consistent 
with the Statutory Language in Section 706(e)(1) 


Petitioner urges this Court to hold that Title VII’s charge-° 
filing requirement is triggered by each separate occurrence of 
any “discrete act” of discrimination.'® Section 706(e)(1), how-

This Court has repeatedly recognized that “[t}he starting

827, 835 (1990) (quoting Consumer Prod. Safety Comm'n vy. 
GTE Sylvania, Inc., 447 U.S. 102, 108 (1980)). When looking 
to the explicit language of a federal statute, the words therein 
should be “interpreted as taking their ordinary, contemporary, 
common meaning,” unless those words are otherwise defined. 
Perrin v. United States, 444 U.S. 37, 42 (1979). When constru-

CONCLUSION 


Continuous violations of Title VII are especially harmful to 
public policy. They involve an ongoing pattern of discrimina- 
tion rather than a single isolated act of discrimination. As such, 
they are a matter of special concern, and some flexibility in in- 
terpreting the charge filing requirement is warranted to stamp 
them out. 


Amtrak’s assertions that Title VII violations are less complex 
than other unlawful practices or “easy to detect” are ludicrous. 
Its assertion that hostile environment cases are “rare” is equally 
ridiculous. To allow Amtrak to escape responsibility for the 
three years of blatantly racist and unlawful conduct by its top 
managers which occurred outside the 300 day limitations period 
would be intolerable. It would severely compromise the goal of 
Title VII to root out and eradicate entrenched and ingrained 
forms of discrimination in the workplace. 


For all of the reasons stated herein, the Ninth Circuit decision 
should be affirmed.
