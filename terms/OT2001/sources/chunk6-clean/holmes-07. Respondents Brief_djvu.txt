QUESTIONS PRESENTED 
The questions presented are: 


1. Does 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1) divest regional Circuits of 
jurisdiction to decide appeals of final decisions of district 
courts in cases wherein the well-pleaded complaint of the 
prevailing plaintiff does not allege any claim arising 
under federal patent law? 


2. Did the Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit err in 
concluding that this action “arises under” federal patent 
law for purposes of 28 U.S.C. §§ 1295(a)(1) and 
1338(a)?

INTRODUCTION 


This is a case about an effort to skirt the unifying 
jurisdictional reach of the United States Court of Appeals for 
the Federal Circuit. Faced with respondent’s patent and 
trade dress complaint pending before the United States 
International Trade Commission, petitioner filed a 
declaratory judgment action in federal district court solely on 
the trade dress claim. Under the Federal Rules of Civil 
Procedure, respondent was obliged to assert its related 
patent claim as a compulsory counterclaim in that second 
proceeding. Applying its long-established Aerojet rule, the 
Federal Circuit recognized that it had jurisdiction over this 
case. See Aerojet-General Corp. v. Machine Tool Works, 
Oerlikon-Buehrle Ltd, 895 F.2d 736 (Fed. Cir. 1990). In 
doing so, the court acted consistently with both the letter of 
the relevant jurisdictional statutes and with Congress’ 
salutary intent to bring about greater uniformity in the body 
of patent law and curtail the then-growing sport of patent 
law forum-shopping. 


Petitioner now wants to resurrect that long- 
discredited practice and, in the process, undo over a decade 
of settled procedure in the Federal Circuit. Nothing has 
changed since the en banc Federal Circuit, speaking 
unanimously through the voice of then-Chief Judge Howard 
Markey, thoughtfully examined this Court’s decision in 
Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp., 486 U.S. 800 
(1988). In his Aerojet opinion, Chief Judge Markey 
obediently examined this Court's opinion, delivered by 
Justice Brennan, and carefully distinguished from the 
Christianson setting the circumstance where, as here, the 
jurisdictional issue arises in connection with a compulsory 
counterclaim. That marked difference in contexts, 
particularly in the present setting of a declaratory judgment 
action, makes all the difference in the world. 

Not only is the Aerojet rule well-settled in Federal 
Circuit practice and administration, the en banc court 


l 


emphasized that its pre-Christianson analytic approach 
dovetailed with this Court’s eventual decision in Christianson 
itself. This consistent interpretation of the relevant 
jurisdictional provisions was first set forth by the Federal 
Circuit in 1986, in In re Innotron Diagnostics, 800 F.2d 1077 
(Fed. Cir. 1986). The Aerojet rule was also followed, for 
example, by the First Circuit in Xeta, Inc. v. Atex, Inc., 825 
F.2d 604 (lst Cir. 1987). The correctness of the Federal 
Circuit's approach in the Christianson context, where 
counterclaims were not at issue, was ratified by this Court in 
Christianson itself. In short, not only did this Court not 
disapprove the Federal Circuit’s already-settled 
interpretation, it affirmed the Federal Circuit’s approach as 
to jurisdiction in Christianson itself. Upon careful analysis, it 
will thus be seen that petitioner has sown latter-day seeds of 
confusion over Federal Circuit procedures in the service of its 
grand litigation strategy. But the disruption that would 
attend the acceptance of petitioner's vast expansion of 
Christianson’s limited rule tugs strongly at Congress’ 
determination to bring greater coherence and consistency to 
the body of patent law through the unifying substantive 
reach of the Federal Circuit’s mandate. 

Aerojet, in short, stands fully consistent with 
Christianson and with the rules governing the orderly 
litigation of patent claims in federal courts. It is much too 
late in the day to disrupt the Aerojet framework, grounded as 
it is in both the text of the relevant jurisdictional provisions 
and in the policy animating Congress’ creation of the Federal 
Circuit.

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


Respondent Vornado manufactures and sells 
distinctive household fans, to which it obtained a patent, 
U.S. Patent No. Re. 34,551 (“the ‘551 patent”). These 
unique fans include a front grill design that incorporates a 
series of spiral (arcuate) vanes. Petitioner Holmes is a 
competitor of respondent in the household fan business, and 
imports and distributes fans covered by Vornado’s ‘551 
patent and trade dress. 


Vornado discovered that petitioner Holmes had been 
importing from China and distributing fans having the 
aforementioned spiral vanes. On November 26, 1999, 
Vornado filed a complaint with the United States

stop the illegal importation of petitioner’s fans.’ The ITC

SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT 


Based on its carefully-reasoned en banc decision in 
Aerojet-General Corp. v. Machine Tool Works, Oerlikon-Buehrle 
Ltd., 895 F.2d 736 (Fed. Cir. 1990), the Federal Circuit 
correctly held that it had jurisdiction over this patent law 
case. Read together, 28 U.S.C. §§ 1295 and 1338 confer 
appellate jurisdiction upon the Federal Circuit over any civil 
action arising” “in whole or in part” “under any Act of 
Congress relating to patents.” As a matter of plain language, 
the present action arose in part under patent law because 
one of the claims in this case - the compulsory counterclaim 
- is a patent law claim. While petitioner stresses that a “civil 
action” is “commenced by filing a complaint,” FED. R. Civ. P. 
3, the action continues with the filing of counterclaims. 
Thus, this Court explained in Cardinal Chemical Co. v. 
Morton Intl, 508 U.S. 83 (1993), that a defendant who 
raised a counterclaim arising under the patent laws had 
“properly invoked the original jurisdiction of the District 
Court” under § 1338. Id. at 98 (emphasis added).

Even outside the present context, this Court has never

in the unique context of Federal Circuit jurisdiction, 
counterclaims must be considered to effectuate Congress’ 
intent to achieve uniformity in patent law and eliminate 
forum shopping by declaratory judgment plaintiffs.

petitioner relies heavily on cases involving federal trial court

As such, there can be no question that this case arises 
under federal patent law for purposes of 28 U.S.C. §§ 1295 
and 1338. Respondent’s answer does not merely assert a 
patent law defense. To the contrary, respondent’s answer 
pleads a nonfrivolous, compulsory counterclaim for patent

ARGUMENT 


1. THE FEDERAL CIRCUIT HAS EXCLUSIVE 
APPELLATE JURISDICTION OVER SUITS IN 
WHICH ANY PLEADING - BE IT A COMPLAINT 
OR COUNTERCLAIM — ALLEGES A CLAIM 
ARISING UNDER FEDERAL PATENT LAW. 


A. 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1) Grants the Federal 
Circuit Exclusive Jurisdiction Over All Suits 
Involving Patent Law Claims, Whether Those 
Claims Are Set Forth in the Complaint or 
Pleaded as Counterclaims. 


In 1982, Congress created the Court of Appeals for 
the Federal Circuit and vested in it exclusive jurisdiction over 
appeals from the federal district courts when “the jurisdiction 
of that [district] court was based, in whole or in part, on 
Section 1338 of this title.” 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1)(emphasis 
added). Section 1338 confers original jurisdiction upon the 
federal district courts over “any civil action arising under any 
Act of Congress relating to patents.” 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a). 
JA104-105. Read together, these two sections grant 
appellate jurisdiction to the Federal Circuit over “any civil 


action arising,” “in whole or in part,” “under any Act of 


Congress relating to patents.” 

As a matter of plain language, the present action 
arose in part under the patent law because one of the claims 
in this case - the counterclaim - is a patent law claim. 
Petitioner stresses that “[a] civil action is commenced by filing 
a complaint.” FED. R. Civ. P. 3. Nothing in that rule nor this 
Court’s precedent suggests, however, that a complaint 
defines the metes and bounds of a civil action. Instead, a 
civil action continues with the filing of counterclaims. See, 
e.g., Fogg v. Ashcroft, 254 F.3d 103, 107 (D.C. Cir. 2001) 


(noting the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure define a civil 
action as “all claims for relief alleged in a single lawsuit”). 


This Court recognized as much in Cardinal Chem. Co. 
v. Morton Int'l, Inc., 508 U.S. 83, 98 (1993). There the Court 
observed that the counterclaim in the declaratory judgment 
action “properly invoked the original jurisdiction of the 
District Court” under §1338. (Emphasis added). This Court 
stated: 

In this case, therefore, it is perfectly clear that 

the District Court had jurisdiction to entertain 

Cardinal's counterclaim for a declaratory 

judgment of invalidity. It is equally clear that 

the Federal Circuit, even after affirming the 

finding of noninfringement, had jurisdiction to 

consider Morton’s appeal from the declaratory 

judgment of invalidity. A party seeking a 

declaratory judgment of invalidity presents a 

claim independent of the patentee's charge of 


infringement) to consider that claim, so does 
(barring any intervening events) the Federal 
Circuit. 


Id. at 96.

In similar contexts, courts have long recognized that 
the resolution of jurisdictional issues requires consideration 
of the entire action, not just the plaintiffs claims. In 
declaratory judgment actions (like this one), courts have 
examined the “character of the threatened action,” not just 
the declaratory judgment complaint. Public Service Comm’n 
of Utah v. Wycoff Co., Inc., 344 U.S. 237, 248 (1952). As this 
Court has explained, “[fjederal courts have regularly taken 
original jurisdiction over declaratory judgment suits in 
which, if the declaratory judgment defendant brought a 
coercive action to enforce its rights, that suit would 
necessarily present a federal question.” Franchise Tax Bd. v. 
Construction Laborers Vacation Trust, 463 U.S. 1, 19 (1983).” 
In this case, examining a compulsory counterclaim for 
allegations of patent infringement is logical, since “in many 
actions for declaratory judgment, the realistic position of the 
parties is reversed,” Public Service Comm’n, 344 U.S. at 248, 
and there is no question that in an original action respondent 
would have pleaded its patent law claims, which it was then 
pursuing before the ITC.

B. The Federal Circuit’s Jurisdiction Over a 
Patent Counterclaim Conforms with the Well- 
Pleaded Complaint Rule. 


Petitioner argues at length that the Federal Circuit 
failed to comply with the well-pleaded complaint rule, as 
articulated in Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp., 486 
U.S. 800 (1988). Petitioner is doubly wrong. As explained 
above, respondent’s well-pleaded counterclaim confers 
jurisdiction on the Federal Circuit. In addition, Christianson 
had no occasion to address this issue and did not do so. 


In Christianson, this Court looked to its cases 
interpreting the “arising under” language found in other 
jurisdictional provisions and held that a case “arises under” 
patent law in the context of § 1338 when a patentee “set[s] 
up some right, title or interest under the patent laws.” In 
doing so, this Court recognized that “arising under” 
principles must be “appropriately adapted to § 1338(a).” Id. 
at 807, 809. This interpretation parallels the “well-pleaded 
complaint rule” used to define the scope of federal-question 
jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1331. Id. at 808. 


14 


The well-pleaded complaint rule was “laid down” in 
Tennessee v. Union & Planters’ Bank, 152 U.S. 454, 459 
(1894), which held a court may act on a question of United 
States law “only when the subject is submitted to it by a 
party who asserts his rights in the form prescribed by law.” 
As this Court subsequently explained: 

“It would be wholly unnecessary and 

improper, in order to prove complainant's 

cause of action, to go into any matters of 

defense which the defendants might possibly 

set up, and then attempt to reply to such 

defense, and thus, if possible, to show that a 

Federal question might or probably would 

arise in the course of the trial of the case.” 


Boston & Montana Consol. Copper & Silver Min. Co. v. 
Montana Ore Purchasing Co., 188 U.S. 632, 638-639 (1903). 
An important purpose of the well-pleaded complaint 
rule is to provide’ a “quick rule of thumb” in resolving 
federal-state jurisdictional conflicts. See Franchise Tax Bd. v. 
Construction Laborers Vacation Trust, 463 U.S. 1, 11 (1983). 
The rule allows courts to avoid having “to go to any matters 
of defense which the defendants might possibly set up.” 
Louisville & Nashville Ry. Co. v. Mottley, 211 U.S. 149, 153 
(1908), quoting Boston & Montana Consol. Copper & Silver 
Min. Co. v. Montana Ore Purchasing Co., 188 U.S. 632 
(1903). It also allows an early determination of proper 
jurisdiction at the pleading stage of a case, and prevents 
uncertainties regarding whether a party will raise an issue 
later in litigation that could implicate jurisdiction. See 
Christianson, 486 U.S. at 814 (holding jurisdiction is not 
determined by the “well-tried case”). Thus, the scope of the 
well-pleaded complaint doctrine and, concomitantly, the 
power of a court to hear a case is defined by two principles: 
first, that the right to sue in a federal court “cannot depend 
on the defense which the defendant may choose to set up, 
and second, that the jurisdiction of the court must be invoked 


15 


at the time when the claim is brought. See Tennessee v. 
Union & Planters’ Bank, 152 U.S. at 459. 

This Court continued to employ these principles to 
divine when an action arises under the Constitution or the 
laws of the United States. In light of these defining 
principles, the well-pleaded complaint rule has been applied 
in three ways: first, to bar federal jurisdiction that is based 
solely on a defense raised in an answer; second, to bar 
federal jurisdiction based upon a plaintiffs anticipation of a 
defense that could possibly be raised by the defendant; and 
third, to bar federal jurisdiction based upon allegations to 
support a case “that are not required by nice pleading rules.” 
13B C. WRIGHT, A. MILLER & E. COOPER, FEDERAL PRACTICE 
AND PROCEDURE § 3566, at 83-87 (2™ ed.). 


These applications of the well-pleaded complaint 
rule, however, are a far cry from the situation here. The 
well-pleaded complaint rule allows federal jurisdiction over a 
claim raising a federal question properly invoked at the time 
and in the form prescribed by law. See Franchise Tax, 463 
U.S. at 22, n.24 (recognizing that a properly filed 
counterclaim might provide an independent jurisdictional 
basis). See also Aerojet-General Corp. v. Machine Tool Works, 
Oerlikon-Buehrle Ltd., 895 F.2d 736, 740 (Fed. Cir. 1990); 
Xeta, Inc. v. Atex, Inc., 825 F.2d 604, 606 (1st Cir. 1987). 


Thus, a clear and simple test to determine whether a 
case arises under patent law for purposes of § 1295(a)(1) is 
to look to the patentee’s claim of infringement at the time it 
is pled, whether it is found as a claim in a complaint or as a 
counterclaim in an answer. The critical question under 
Union & Planters’ Bank through Christianson is whether the 
patentee has asserted a claim to “some right, title or interest 
under the patent laws.” Christianson, 486 U.S. at 807. If so, 
the jurisdiction of the Federal Circuit is properly invoked 
under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1). 


Our position fully supports the defining principles of 
the well-pleaded complaint rule as appropriately adapted to 
§§ 1338(a) and 1295(a)(1). By looking to the pleadings, 


16 


including whether a patentee asserts a counterclaim of 
infringement, the appellate jurisdiction of the Federal Circuit 
is invoked without reference tu anticipated defenses and at 
the time the case is filed. Indeed, this test conforms with the 
well-pleaded complaint rule because it focuses on claims 
rather than the uncertainty associated with issues that might 
arise during litigation.* Christianson, 486 U.S. at 809; 
Christianson, 486 U.S. at 820, n.1 (Stevens, J., 
concurring)(noting that the rule “ferret({s] out claims from 
issues”); Louisville & Nashville Ry. Co., 211 U.S. at 153. The 
test also satisfies the purpose of the well-pleaded complaint 
rule. Indeed, “the ‘well-pleaded complaint rule. . . says 
nothing about whether [separating claims from issues] 
should be made only on the basis of the original complaint.” 
Christianson, 486 U.S. at 820, n.1 (Stevens, J., concurring). 
Holding otherwise would confer jurisdiction under §1295 
only when an assertion of patent infringement is found on 
the face of a complaint, but not when the same claim is 
posed as a counterclaim.’ Nothing in law or — suggests 
such an artificial approach. 

Despite petitioner's assertion to the contrary, the 
exercise of appellate jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295 over 
a case asserting a patent counterclaim is in no sense 
inconsistent with the holding in Christianson. This Court in 
Christianson ruled only on the narrow issue of whether the

C. Federal Circuit Jurisdiction Over Declaratory 
Judgment Actions with Patent Counterclaims 
is Necessary to Effectuate Congress’ Intent to 
Secure Uniformity and Eliminate Forum 


Shopping. 


The Federal Circuit further recognized that its 
construction of the jurisdictional statutes is necessary to 
fulfill Congress’ intent “to foster uniformity and preclude 
forum shopping.” Aerojet, 895 F.2d at 744. In Christianson, 
this Court had recognized that “arising under” principles 
must be “appropriately adapted to § 1338(a).” 486 U.S. at 
809. The Aerojet court likewise recognized that 
“determinations about federal jurisdiction require sensitive 
judgments about congressional intent, judicial power, and 
the federal system.” 895 F.2d at 741 (quoting Merrell Dow 
Pharm. Inc. v. Thompson, 478 U.S. 804, 810 (1986)). “[A] 
construction that undermines a statute’s goal cannot stand.” 
Id. at 745 (quoting Community for Creative Non-Violence v. 
Reid, 490 U.S. 730 (1989)). 


Prior to the formation of the Federal Circuit in 1982, 
Congress recognized that the process of appealing patent 
cases to the regional circuits promoted “mad and 
undignified races’ between alleged infringers and patent 
holders to be the first to institute proceedings in the forum 
they consider most favorable.” H.R. REP. No. 312, 97TH 
CONG., 1ST SESS. 21 (1981). This forum-shopping, Congress 
concluded, “demean[ed] the entire judicial process and the 
patent system as well.” Id. (citation omitted). 


20 


In creating the Federal Circuit, Congress wanted “to 
reduce the widespread lack of uniformity and uncertainty of 
legal doctrine that exist in the administration of patent law.” 
H.R. REP. No. 97-312 at 23. Congress found inconsistency 
among the circuits in both the patent law itself and the 
application of the law to the facts of individual cases. Id. at 
21; see also S. REP. NO. 275, 97TH CONG., 1sT SEss. 13 
(1981). This variability lead to forum shopping as patentees 
would scramble to get into the “pro-patent” circuits and 
alleged infringers would run to “anti-patent” circuits. H.R. 
REP. NO. 97-312 at 20-21 (citing the Commission on Revision 
of the Federal Court Appellate System, Structure and 
Internal Procedures: Recommendations for Change, 15, 144- 
57, 67 F.R.D. 195, 214, 361-76 (1975)). The resultant 
unpredictable outcome of contemplated litigation hindered 
industrial innovation and effective business planning. H.R. 
REP. NO. 97-312 at 20, 22 (citing the Industrial Research 
Instituie’s poll showing that leading industrial companies 
overwhelmingly favored a centralized patent appeals court.) 
Congress concluded that creation of the Federal Circuit 
would strengthen the United States patent system by 
providing consistent and uniform law and eliminating the 
widespread forum-shopping that existed in the adjudication 
of patent cases. 


Respondent’s position adheres to Congressional 
intent of promoting the uniformity of patent law and 
discouraging forum-shopping. As the Federal Circuit 
reasoned in Aerojet, “Congressional goals are facilitated 
when appeals in cases involving nonfrivolous claims arising 
under the patent laws, whether found in complaints or in 
compulsory counterclaims, are directed to this court.” 
Aerojet-General Corp. v. Machine Tool Works, Oerlikon-Buehrle 
Ltd., 895 F.2d 736, 744 (Fed. Cir. 1990). Moreover, there is 
no fear that a patentee might manipulate appellate 
jurisdiction as he is bound by rule to file his compulsory 
counterclaim at the pleading stage. See FED. R. CIV. P. 
13(a). 


21 


On the other hand, the potential for manipulation of 
appellate jurisdiction by infringers and patentees alike will 
again surface under the scheme proposed by petitioners. 


Congress’ goal of ensuring that appeals of 
patent-law claims go to the Federal Circuit 
would be thwarted by determining that 
court's appellate jurisdiction only through an 
examination of the complaint as initially filed. 
That approach would enable an unscrupulous 
plaintiff to manipulate appellate court 
jurisdiction by the timing of the amendments 
in its complaint. 
Christianson, 486 U.S. at 823-824 (Stevens, J., concurring). 
Likewise, “[djirecting appeals involving compulsory 
counterclaims for patent infringement to the twelve regional 
circuits could frustrate Congress’ desire to foster uniformity 
and preclude forum shopping.” Aerojet, 895 F.2d at 744. 


Il. THE FEDERAL CIRCUIT DID NOT ERR IN 
EXERCISING JURISDICTION OVER THIS 
APPEAL. 


The Federal Circuit properly asserted jurisdiction over 
this appeal, following the law of this Court in Christianson 
and respecting the intent of Congress to promote a uniform 
body of patent law. Briefly stated, it is undisputed that 
respondent Vornado timely plead a _ non-frivolous 
counterclaim of patent infringement - a claim which 
unquestionably arose under, indeed was created by, the 
patent laws of the United States. Thus, in every sense, the 
Federal Circuit followed the defining principles, as adapted 
to 28 U.S.C. § 1338, of the well-pleaded complaint rule, and 
thereby properly asserted jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 
1295(a)(1). As Justice Cardozo advised in Gully v. First Nat'l 
Bank in Meridian, 299 U.S. 109, 117 (1936), the Federal 


22 


Circuit applied “common-sense accommodation of judgment 
to [a] kaleidoscopic situation[}.” 

Here, as we chronicled earlier, respondent asserted 
first its claim of patent infringement in the International 
Trade Commission. In that action, Vornado fully apprised 
petitioner of the details of its patent claim including 
providing a detailed element-by-element analysis of the 
alleged patent infringement. As a result of Vornado’s 
complaint and corresponding detailed infringement analysis, 
the ITC commenced an investigation of Vornado’s claim. In 
instituting an investigation, the ITC recognized that Vornado 
had asserted a non-frivolous claim of patent infringement. 
See 19 C.F.R. § 210.10. Ignoring what it knew to be a patent 
dispute between the parties, however, petitioner filed its 
district court action in Kansas, claiming only that its fans did 
not infringe Vornado’s trade dress. In response, Vornado 
asserted a compulsory counterclaim of infringement that 
was, just two weeks earlier, set forth in a complaint filed in 
the ITC. 

In light of this procedural framework and as 
illuminated by Christianson, the pleadings in this case 
established “some right, title or interest under the patent 
laws” by way of respondent Vornado’s non-frivolous 
counterclaim of patent infringement. See Christianson, 486 
U.S. at 807. The principles of the well-pleaded complaint 
rule have thus been abundantly satisfied: the Federal 
Circuit’s jurisdiction was not based upon a defense or issue 
that might have been asserted in the case, and the timing of 
the Federal Circuit's jurisdiction was set at the pleading stage 
of the case. Indeed, had petitione: not raced to the forum of 
its choosing, there would have been no dispute that the 
appeal of this case would properly be laid at the Federal 
Circuit. As the Federal Circuit recognized, “[i]t would seem 
at best incongruous to hold that [the Federal Circuit would] 
have appellate jurisdiction when a well-pleaded patent 
infringement claim is the basis of a pleading labeled 
‘complaint’ but not when the identical well-pleaded claim is 


23 


the basis of a pleading labeled ‘counterclaim.” Aerojet- 
General Corp. v. Machine Tool Works, Oerlikon-Buehrle Ltd., 
895 F.2d 736, 742 (Fed. Cir. 1990). 


Petitioner’s conduct below, on the other hand, was 


jurisdictional rules by bringing a declaratory judgment action 
that conveniently omitted a patent law claim integral to the 
case. To reward petitioners conduct by sustaining its 
position, this Court must overrule a decade-long procedure 
of the Federal Circuit grounding its jurisdiction upon well- 
pleaded claims. To do so would profoundly disserve the 
intent of Congress to curtail forum-shopping and promote a 
uniform body of patent law.
