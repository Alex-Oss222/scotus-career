QUESTIONS PRESENTED 


Petitioner commenced this civil action in the U.S. District 
Court for the District of Kansas. Petitioner’s complaint sought 
injunctive, monetary, and declaratory relief under 15 U.S.C. 
§ 1125(a) and applicable state law. In March 2000, the District 
Court granted summary judgment in favor of Petitioner on its 
First Cause of Action, holding that Petitioner’s “sale of its fan 
and heater products does not infringe any valid or enforceable 
‘trade dress’ claimed by [Respondent] under 15 U.S.C. §§ 1051 
et seq.” Final judgment was then entered in favor of Petitioner 
pursuant to Rule 54(b) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure. 


Respondent timely filed a Notice of Appeal from the 
District Court’s judgment; however, instead of appealing to the 
Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit, Respondent directed its 
appeal to the Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit. 
Respondent contended that the Federal Circuit had appellate 
jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1), not based on anything 
contained or pleaded in Petitioner’s complaint or decided by 
the District Court, but based on Respondent’s having included, 
in its Answer, a counterclaim against Petitioner for alleged 
patent infringement. The Respondent’s counterclaim was 
extraneous to the District Court’s jurisdiction to hear Petitioner’s 
suit and formed no part of the judgment from which Respondent 
had appealed. In its brief filed with the Court of Appeals for the 
Federal Circuit, Petitioner urged that Respondent’s appeal be 
dismissed for lack of appellate jurisdiction or, alternatively, that 
the appeal be transferred to the Tenth Circuit pursuant to 
28 U.S.C. § 1631. 


On June 5, 2001, the Court of Appeals for the Federal 
Circuit vacated the District Court’s judgment and remanded 
for further proceedings. In direct conflict with decisions of this 
Court, the Federal Circuit has taken the position that a plaintiff's 
well-pleaded complaint does not determine whether an action 


il 


“arises under” federal patent law for purposes of 28 U.S.C. 
§ 1295(a)(1) and 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a). Rather, in the Federal 
Circuit’s view, the answer of a defendant must be consulted to 
determine whether an action “arises under” federal patent law; 
and if a defendant’s answer includes a patent law counterclaim 
against the plaintiff, then according to the Federal Circuit, the 
defendant’s pleading purportedly operates to change the basis 
of a district court’s jurisdiction and to cut off regional Circuit 
jurisdiction over the plaintiff's suit. 


The Federal Circuit’s interpretation of 28 U.S.C. 
§ 1295(a)(1) purports to displace vast sectors of regional Circuit 
jurisdiction and to vest in defendants, rather than plaintiffs, 
control over what appellate court will determine, and what law 
may govern, broad categories of claims arising under copyright, 
trademark, antitrust, contract, defamation, and other non-patent 
laws. 


The questions presented are: 


1. Does 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1) divest regional Circuits 
of jurisdiction to decide appeals of final decisions of 
district courts in cases wherein the well-pleaded 
complaint of the prevailing plaintiff does not allege 
any claim arising under federal patent law? 


2. Does 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1) divest regional Circuits 
of jurisdiction to decide appeals of final decisions of 
non-patent claims, where judgment is entered pursuant 
to Fed. R. Civ. P. 54(b) and is not based, in whole or 
in part, on 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a)? 


3. Did the Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit err in 
concluding that this action “arises under” federal patent 
law for purposes of 28 U.S.C. §§ 1295(a)(1) and 
1338(a)?

The judgment of the Court of Appeals was entered on 
June 5, 2001. No petition for rehearing was filed. This

The District Court had jurisdiction to hear Petitioner’s

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


Petitioner manufactures and sells HOLMES® brand 
consumer household products, including household fan and 
heater fan products. Respondent is a competitor of Petitioner 
in the U.S. fan and heater business. Both parties manufacture 
and sell axial-flow fan and heater fan products whose outlet 
grill structures incorporate a series of arcuate or curved vanes 
for directing air flow. 


In the case of Vornado Air Circulation Sys., Inc. v. 
Duracraft Corp., 58 F.3d 1498 (10th Cir. 1995), cert. denied, 
516 U.S. 1067 (1996) (“Vornado I”), the Court of Appeals for

The jurisdictional questions presented by this case are 
of broad and general significance. The decision below rests 
on a jurisdictional principle which affects every antitrust 
plaintiff, every copyright plaintiff, every contract plaintiff, 
every trademark plaintiff, and indeed, every federal court 
plaintiff whose adversary may assert a counterclaim for 
alleged patent infringement of any nature.‘ Under the Federal 
Circuit's interpretation of the “well-pleaded complaint rule”

validity. While “[i}t is possible to conceive of a rational 
jurisdictional system in which the answer as well as the 
complaint would be consulted before a determination is made 
whether the case ‘arose under’ federal law,” that simply is 
not the law. Franchise Tax Board v. Construction Laborers 
Vacation Trust, 463 U.S. 1, 10 0.9 (1983).

In Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp., 486 U.S. 
800 (1988), this Court vacated a judgment of the Federal 
Circuit because the plaintiff's complaint did not allege any 
claim “arising under” federal patent law. In reaching this 
result, this Court held that 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1) extended 


only to those cases in which a well-pleaded 
complaint establishes either that federal patent law 
creates the cause of action or that the plaintiff's 
right to relief necessarily depends on resolution 
of a substantial question of federal patent law, in 
that patent law is a necessary element of one of 
the well-pleaded claims. 


486 U.S. at 808-809. 


The “ ‘well-pleaded complaint rule,’ . . . provides that 
federal jurisdiction exists only when a federal question is 
presented on the face of the plaintiff's properly pleaded 
complaint. . . . The rule makes the plaintiff the master of the 
claim. . . .” Caterpillar Inc. v. Williams, 482 U.S. 386, 392 
(1987). 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1) expressly incorporates by 
reference the “arising under” jurisprudence of 28 U.S.C. 
§ 1338(a), under which “the District court’s jurisdiction is 
determined by reference to the well-pleaded complaint, not 
the well-tried case.” Christianson, 486 U.S. at 814. 


As its name indicates, the “well-pleaded complaint rule” 
looks to the contents of a plaintiff's complaint, and not to a 
defendant’s answer, to determine if a civil action “arises 
under” federal patent law, or not. The rule embodies a basic 
principle of federal court jurisdiction. 


The party who brings a suit is master to decide 
what law he will rely upon and therefore does 


11 


determine whether he will bring a suit arising 
under the patent or other law of the United States 
by his declaration or bill. 


The Fair v. Kohler Die & Specialty Co., 228 U.S. 22, 25 
(1913). See Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals Inc. v. Thompson, 
478 U.S. 804, 809 n.6 (1986) (“jurisdiction may not be 
sustained on a theory that the plaintiff has not advanced”); 
Healy v. Sea Gull Specialty Co., 237 U.S. 479, 480 (1915) 
(“the plaintiff is absolute master of what jurisdiction he will 


appeal to”). 


In the present case, it is undisputed that the complaint 
filed by Petitioner in the District Court does not plead any 
claim which “arises under” federal patent law within the 
meaning of 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a). The complaint pleads seven 
causes of action arising under 15 U.S.C. §§ 1051 et seq., 
Mass. Gen. L. ch. 93A §§ 2, 11, and the common law. The 
complaint invokes the District Court’s subject matter 
jurisdiction “under 15 U.S.C. § 1121, 28 U.S.C. §§ 1331 
and 1332(a) and the principles of supplemental jurisdiction 
codified in 28 U.S.C. § 1367”. Yet according to the Federal 
Circuit, this action “arises under” federal patent law and so 
is subject to Federal Circuit appellate jurisdiction under 
28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1). 


The Federal Circuit’s aberrant interpretation of the “well- 
pleaded complaint rule” traces to the case of Aerojet-General 
Corp. v. Machine Tool Works, Oerlikon-Buehrle Lid., 895 
F.2d 736 (Fed. Cir. 1990) (en banc). In Aerojet, the Federal 
Circuit held that the “well-pleaded complaint rule” was 
“merely the name of the rule, not a statement of a principle 
of law,” 895 F.2d at 743, with the purported result that a 
case could “arise under” federal patent law for jurisdictional

jurisdiction.

permitted a litigant not a party to a federal case to assert the 
doctrine “offensively” in a new federal suit against the party 
who lost on the issue in the first case. Jd. at 94-95.
