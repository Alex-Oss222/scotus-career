QUESTIONS PRESENTED 


1. Whether a breach of contract claim accrues for 
purposes of 28 U.S.C. § 2501 when Congress enacts a statute 
alleged to abridge a contractual right to freedom from 
regulatory covenants upon prepayment of government 
mortgage loans. 


2. Whether a Fifth Amendment takings claim accrues 
for purposes of 28 U.S.C. § 2501 when Congress enacts a 
statute alleged to abridge a contractual right to freedom 


from regulatory covenants upon prepayment of government 
mortgage loans.

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


Petitioners are owners of properties located throughout 
rural areas of the United States that currently are operated as 
affordable housing projects financed by mortgage loans 


issued by the Farmer's Home Administration (“FmHA”)! 
pursuant to Section 515 of the Housing Act of 1949 (the 
“Section 515 program”). This case involves an attempt by 
Congress to alter, by statute, the terms of petitioners’ 
agreements in order to limit the FmHA’s ability to permit 
petitioners to terminate their participation in the program. 
The narrow questions presented are when petitioners’ 
contract and takings claims accrued for purposes of the six- 
year statute of limitations set forth at 28 U.S.C. § 2501. The 
answers to these questions require an understanding of the 
nature of the program, the terms of the parties’ contracts, 
and the impact of the subsequent legislation on petitioners. 


The Section 515 program was intended by the 
government to develop rental housing in rural populations 
not normally served by conventional lenders. Designed to 
be used as affordable housing only during that period of 
time that the housing is financed by a government mortgage 
loan, the government by contract required each owner to 
prepay its loan when commercial financing became 
available. In addition, each owner had the contractual 
option to terminate the use of its property as affordable 
housing by prepaying its loan at a time of its own choosing 
during the term of its mortgage. In either case, prepayment 
enabled the owners to use their properties as conventional 
housing. The monies from prepayment could then be used 
by the government to finance alternative affordable housing 
needs in rural communities. Thus, prepayment helped the 
government achieve the ultimate goal of developing both 
affordable and conventional housing in rural America. 


Petitioners are aggrieved by the FmHA’s application of 
subsequent legislation that authorizes the agency to permit 
owners to terminate their participation in the program only

A. The Section 515 Program

programs encouraging private citizens to construct, own, 
and manage federally assisted affordable housing units. See 
Belden & Wiener, HOUSING IN RURAL AMERICA 114 (1999). 
The Section 515 program at issue here was one such 
program. Section 515 of the Housing Act of 1949 (codified at 
42 U.S.C. § 1485), added to the Act in 1962, authorized the 
FmHA to enter into contracts with private owners to make 
loans for the purpose of developing housing for low- and 
moderate-income and elderly tenants in rural areas.3

1. Early Congressional Attempts to Restrict the 

Agency’s Acceptance of Prepayment Requests 
To reduce this potential loss of housing, Congress 
initially responded by passing a statute in 1979 that 
specifically prohibited the FmHA from accepting certain 
prepayments of Section 515 loans. Pub. L. No. 96-153, § 503, 
93 Stat. 1134 (1979). During the debates over the 1979 
legislation, however, some in Congress “argued that owners’ 
property rights were paramount - that retroactivity was a 
breach of contract and unconstitutional.” Belden & Wiener 
at 117. In response to the political uproar caused by this 
repudiation of the owners’ rights, Congress repealed the 
statute in 1980 and restored the contractual termination 
option for loans issued prior to 1979. The 1980 legislation 
also required new owners to agree to operate their 
properties as low-income housing for a minimum of 15 or 20 

years. Pub. L. No. 96-399, § 514, 94 Stat. 1671 (1980).13

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


These cases come to this Court as the result of the 
Federal Circuit's determination that petitioners’ claims are 
time-barred, a conclusion that it reached in total disregard of 
two of the most well-established precepts of law pertaining 
to actions for damages against the United States: (1) that the 
government is bound by the law governing contracts 
between private citizens when it enters into contracts, and 
(2) that a regulatory takings claim based on the application 
of a statute that allows for an agency decision on the scope 
of permissible use of the property in question is not ripe for 
judicial review until the agency renders such a decision. By 
ignoring these bedrock principles, the Federal Circuit 
permitted the government to obtain dismissal of petitioners’ 
claims on statute of limitations grounds, depriving them of a 
hearing on the merits. The proper application of the law, 
however, demonstrates that petitioners’ claims were filed in 
a timely manner. 


Accrual of Petitioners’ Contract Claims. There is no 
question that if this were a contract dispute between two 
private parties, petitioners’ claims would be deemed timely 
and heard on the merits. Under the doctrine of anticipatory 
repudiation, petitioners’ contract claims did not accrue upon 
the enactment of the legislation by Congress, but rather 
when the agency, applying the new restrictions set forth in 
the statute, breached its contractual obligation by refusing to 
accept petitioners’ prepayment requests. This comports

2. Congress Avoided Unnecessary Disputes And 
Constitutional Challenges By Refraining From 
Retroactively Eliminating Petitioners’ Rights. 


Faced with the task of limiting the loss of housing in the 
Section 515 program, Congress of course had a number of 
options before it. For example, it could have taken title to 
the properties through its power of eminent domain, after 
which the housing could have been operated and managed 
by the federal government. Congress also could have 
-revoked the authority of the FmHA to consider any 
prepayment requests in the future, directing that any 
requests submitted be denied outright. It also might have 
declared the prepayment language in the owners’ contracts 
null and void, or passed a law making it unlawful for 
owners even to submit prepayment requests to the agency. 


Instead of taking any of these more extreme measures, 
which would have resulted in the immediate conscription of 
petitioners’ properties or the abolition of their contract 
rights, Congress chose to set up a scheme that continues to 
permit owners to submit requests to terminate their 
participation in the program and authorizes the agency to 
grant such requests in certain circumstances. In this manner, 
Congress avoided potential disputes where: (1) the agency 
no longer needs the housing at the time the owner requests 
to terminate, or (2) the owner never decides to leave the 
program for some reason, and thus never submits a request. 


By stopping short of eliminating petitioners’ contract 
rights, Congress also avoided certain constitutional 
challenges. For example, under the circumstances here, an 
absolute and wholesale destruction of preexisting contract 
rights likely would have been invalidated as an 
impermissibly retroactive impairment in violation of either 
the Takings Clause or substantive due process. See Easiern

Statutes of limitation are designed to promote judicial 
efficiency and fairness. See Lampf v. Gilbertson, 501 U.S. 350, 
357 (1991) (“limitations periods in general promote 
important considerations of fairness”); Board of Regents v. 
Tomanio, 446 U.S. 478, 487-88 (1980) (statutes of limitation 
“have long been respected as fundamental to a well-ordered 
judicial system”). Limitations rules that are logical and 
predictable create a level playing field in which plaintiffs can 
fairly obtain relief in the courts and putative defendants can 
be aware of when they may be subject to suit. Here, neither 
the government nor the petitioners could know whether the 
FmHA would permit any given owner to terminate its 
participation in the Section 515 program until that date upon 
which each owner decided to submit a prepayment request 
and either (1) learned that the agency had denied the 
request, or (2) concluded that, under the circumstances at 
issue, the agency had no authority to grant the request. 
Because every petitioner filed suit within six years of that 
date, fundamental considerations of fairness require that 
their claims be deemed timely and heard on the merits.
