MOTION FOR LEAVE TO FILE 
POST-ARGUMENT BRIEF 


Pursuant to S. Ct. Rule 25.6, respondent Illinois Bell 
Telephone Company d/b/a Ameritech Illinois (“Ameritech”) 
respectfully moves for leave to file the accompanying post- 
argument brief. As the Court knows, there are multiple 
respondents in this case. As a result of a coin toss, counsel for 
respondents WorldCom Technologies, Inc., MCI WorldCom 
Network Services, Inc., MCIMetro Access Transmission 
Services LLC, and Focal Communications Corp. (collectively, 
“WorldCom”) was allotted the oral argument time for the 
private respondents in this case. As WorldCom's counsel 
acknowledged at oral argument yesterday, his client's position 
is in direct conflict with Ameritech’s position on the critical 
issue in the case: whether the federal courts have jurisdiction 
to adjudicate a claim that a state commission has violated 
federal law by nullifying provisions in commission-approved 
section 252 interconnection agreements that expressly limit the 
obligations imposed by those agreements to the requirements of 
the 1996 Act. Ameritech vigorously disagrees with the position 
on this issue espoused by WorldCom’s counsel at oral 
argument. Ameritech respectfully submits that the accom- 
panying post-argument brief is necessary to make clear the 
extent of and bases for Ameritech’s disagreement with the 


views advocated by WorldCom and that the brief would assist 


the Court in resolving this case. 


For the foregoing reasons, the motion for leave to file the 
accompanying post-argument brief should be granted.

POST-ARGUMENT BRIEF FOR RESPONDENT 
ILLINOIS BELL TELEPHONE COMPANY d/b/a 
AMERITECH ILLINOIS 


Pursuant to Rule 25.6, respondent Illinois Bell Telephone 
Company d/b/a Ameritech Illinois (“Ameritech”) respectfully 
submits the following post-argument brief to address — and 
formally to disavow — certain statements made by counsel for 
respondent WorldCom during oral argument yesterday. See 
Sutton v. United Air Lines, Inc., 527 U.S. 471, 490 (1999) 
(citing post-argument briefs addressing points made during oral 
argument). 

WorldCom’s counsel conceded at oral argument that 
WorldCom does not speak for Ameritech. WorldCom's 
counsel acknowledged that his client’s position is in direct 
conflict with Ameritech’s position on the critical issue in the 
case: whether the federal courts have jurisdiction to adjudicate 
a claim that a state commission has violated federal law by 
nullifying provisions in commission-approved section 252 
imposed by those agreements to the requirements of the 1996 
Act. Ameritech vigorously disagrees with the position 
espoused by WorldCom’s counsel at oral argument, and seeks 
through this supplemental brief to make clear how its position 
differs from that advocated by WorldCom. 

The dispute between the private carriers in this case (and in 
the companion case) involves provisions of section 252 


Ameritech’s agreements specifically provide that the parties’ 
reciprocal compensation obligations are “As Described in the 
Act” (J.A. 8a, 14a, 20a, 30a, 38a), which is in turn defined in 
each agreement to mean “as described in or required by the 
Act” (J.A. 8a, 13a, 19a, 30a, 37a). Ameritech’s claim in this 
case is that the state commission violated the 1996 Act by 


nullifying those express statutory-based limitations on its >

reciprocal compensation obligations, and it did so in an Order 
suffused with a discussion of federal law. L 

Contrary to the arguments of WorldComi, Ameritech 
contends that, at a minimum, the federal courts have 
jurisdiction to adjudicate Ameritech’s claim that the state 
compensation on Internet traffic is inconsistent with the 
reciprocal compensation requirements of the 1996 Act that were 
expressly incorporated into the parties’ approved section 252 
agreements. WorldCom's suggestion that this is not a federal 
claim defies common sense. The Act establishes a specific 
process for the formation of interconnection agreements and 
makes the agreements approved as a result of that process 
“binding” on the parties. Permitting a state commission to 
substitute its “interpretation” for unambiguous Act-based terms 
and conditions would render the agreement formation 
provisions of the Act a nullity. 


The federal jurisdiction advocated by Ameritech would not 
“open the floodgates.” Since 1996, telecommunications carriers 
have entered into thousands of interconnection agreements, yet 
there are only a few dozen reported cases in federal court 
regarding interpretative disputes. Overwhelmingly, those cases 
have involved claims, like the claim asserted here by 
Ameritech, that a state commission has disregarded provisions 
of an approved agreement that expressly limit the parties’ 
obligations to the requirements of federal law, and thus hinge 
on a proper construction of the Act. Within that narrow 
category of disputes, the vast majority have arisen in precisely 
the factual context presented here — disputes arising out of a 
State commission’s alleged failure to respect Congress’s 
judgment that the reciprocal compensation requirements of the 
Act do not apply to Internet traffic. Trivial disputes have not 
given rise to federal lawsuits. Many section 252 agreements 
provide for ADR proceedings, which weed out smaller disputes. 
Only the most substantial disputes are taken to the state

Once the basic federal issues have been sorted out, these 
disputes will decline still further. In an analogous context, there 
have been few federal court suits arising out of disputes over

WorldCom's contention that Congress intended that some 
claims arising out of the interpretation of negotiated 
interconnection agreements belong in federal court and others 
(like Ameritech’s claim in this case) belong in state court is 
absurd. Interpretive disputes arising out of federally- 
mandated interconnection agreements that implement federal 
statutory obligations unquestionably are federal in nature and, 
like federal tariffs, should be construed under federal law. 
Such disputes — which require application of the criteria of 
the 1996 Act (see J.A. 114a-119a) — are a proper subject of 
federal court review. WorldCom’s (and the Seventh 
Circuit’s) suggestion that such disputes should be 
“bifurcated” between federal court and state court would 
subvert and undermine Congress's objective of resolving 
contested issues expeditiously. 


Congress simply could not have meant for 
interconnection disputes arising from the same agreement to 
run the gauntlet of two separate judicial systems, as 
WorldCom contends, with some issues litigated first in the 
federal system and other issues litigated years later in the 
fall on one side or another of “the federal-state divide,” given 
“myriad possible fact patterns.” World Com Reply Br. in 
00-1531 & 00-1711 at 14. It should come as no surprise, 
therefore, that there is no support whatsoever in the text, 
structure, or legislative history of the Act for such a byzantine
