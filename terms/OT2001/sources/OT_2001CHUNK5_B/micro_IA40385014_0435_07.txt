QUESTIONS PRESENTED 


Whether a state commission’s action related to the en- 
forcement of a previously approved section 252 interconnec- 
tion agreement is a “determination under [section 252]” and 
thus reviewable in federal court under 47 U.S.C. §252(e)(6). 


Whether a state commission’s acceptance of Congress’ 
invitation to participate in implementing a federal regulatory 
scheme that provides that state commission determinations 
are reviewable in federal court constitutes a waiver of Elev- 
enth Amendment immunity. 


Whether an official capacity action seeking prospective 
relief against state public utility commissioners for alleged 
ongoing violations of federal law in performing federal 
regulatory functions under the federal Telecommunications 
Act of 1996 can be maintained under the Ex parte Young 
doctrine.

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


This case involves a suit brought in the United States Dis- 
trict Court for the Northern District of Illinois by Respondent 
Ameritech Illinois (“Ameritech”). Included as defendants, 
along with other Respondent telecommunications carriers, 
were the five members of the Illinois Commerce Commis- 
sion (“ICC”), who were named “in their official capacities 
and not as individuals.” Jt. App. 125a. The suit sought re- 
view of an order of the ICC that had disposed of complaints 
the other carriers had filed against Ameritech. In these com- 
plaints the carriers charged that Ameritech had breached in- 
terconnection agreements with them by failing to make 
certain payments. The ICC agreed with the carriers and di- 
rected Ameritech to make the disputed payments. Ameritech 
sought review of the ICC decision in federal district court 
pursuant to 47 U.S.C. § 252(e)(6), part of the federal Tele- 
communications Act of 1996. - 


A. The Illinois Commerce Commission. The ICC is an 
agency of the State of Illinois, and was created in 1913 as the 
State Public Utilities Commission, American Nat'l Bank & 
Trust Co. v. Pennsylvania R. R. Co., 35 Ill. 2d 145, 148 
(1966). Its conduct is governed by the Illinois Public Utilities 
Act (“IPUA”), 220 ILCS 5/1-101 et seg. The ICC consists of 
five members appointed by the Governor. 220 ILCS 5/2-101. 
“Every finding, order or decision approved by a majority of 
the members of [the ICC] shall be deemed to be the finding, 
order or decision of the Commission.” /d. The ICC is 
charged with the “general supervision of all public utilities,” 
220 ILCS 5/4-101, as well as the enforcement of “the provi- 
sions of the Constitution and statutes of this State affecting 
public utilities, the enforcement of which is not specifically 
vested in some other officer or tribunal,” 220 ILCS 5/4-201. 


The ICC’s duties include the adjudication of complaints, 
which may be brought by the ICC itself or by, inter alia, 
“any person or corporation.” The Commission may hold 


hearings on complaints, at which the complainants and re- 
spondents may be heard and evidence received. After the 
hearing the ICC is required to make findings of fact and en- 
ter an order based on those findings. 220 ILCS 5/10-108, 10- 
110. In the event the ICC fails to enter an order within a 
specified time, any party may seek an order of mandamus 
from a state trial court compelling the ICC to act within sixty 
days. 220 ILCS 5/10-108.' ICC decisions are subject to ju- 
dicial review in the Illinois Appellate Court and the illinois 
Supreme Court. 220 ILCS 5/10-201 and 202. 


B. The Telecommunications Act of 1996. On February 
1, 1996, Congress passed the Telecommunications Act of 
1996, Pub. L. No. 104-104, 110 Stat. 56 (codified as 
amended in scattered sections of 47 U.S.C.) (“the 1996 Act” 
or “the Act”). This extensive legislation amended, but did 
not repeal, the Federal Communications Act of 1934 (“the 
1934 Act”). Under the 1934 Act (and even before its pas- 
sage), regulation of telephone service had been divided be- 
tween the federal and state governments. The federal 
government, acting through the Federal Communications 
Commission (“FCC”), had regulated interstate, long-distance 
service, and state governments, acting primarily through 
state public utilities commissions like the ICC, had regulated 
local (intrastate) service. 47 U.S.C. § 152(a) & (b); Louisi-

AT&T Corp. v. Iowa Util. Bd., 525 U.S. 366, 403-04 (1999)

new sections, and amended existing ones, in a number of ar- 
eas, including telecommunications (Title I). 


Before the 1996 Act, local telephone service was consid- 
ered a natural monopoly and regulated as such. Jowa Util. 
Bd., 525 U.S. at 371. One of the principal changes the Act 
made was to introduce competition into local telephone mar- 
kets. In order to accomplish this goal, the Act imposes cer- 
tain general duties on all local exchange carriers (“LECs”) 
and particular obligations on existing or incumbent LECs. 47 
U.S.C. § 251. All LECs are required to avoid unreasonable 
or discriminatory conditions on the resale of telecommuni- 
cations services (§ 251(b)(1)), to maintain elements of those 
services in ways that facilitate access among networks by 
other providers (§ 251(b)(2)-(4)), and to establish arrange- 
ments for reciprocal compensation to one another for trans- 
porting and terminating calls placed by each others’ 
customers (§ 251(b)(5)). 


Competition in the local telephone markets would require 
new entrants. Recognizing that new providers could not 
rapidly and efficiently duplicate the local networks that had 
been developed over time by incumbent LECs, Congress 
required each incumbent LEC “to share its network with 
competitors.” Jowa Util. Bd., 525 U.S. at 371. As a general 
obligation, all telecommunications carriers were required 
to interconnect with each others’ facilities. 47 U.S.C. 
§ 251(a)(1). Section 251(c) imposed additional obligations 
on incumbent LECs, including Ameritech, to allow intercon- 
nection with their existing networks by any carrier request- 
ing it, to negotiate in good faith agreements providing for 
interconnection, to let other carriers gain unbundled access to 
discrete elements or features of their networks, and to allow 
purchase of their services at wholesale prices for resale.

The vehicles for achieving network interconnection under 
§ 251(c) are agreements negotiated in accordance with § 252 
of the Act. Section 252 establishes procedures for negotiat- 
ing, arbitrating, and approving agreements for interconnec- 
tion, services, or network elements between incumbent LECs 
and requesting carriers.” It creates a four-step process for ac- 
complishing this task and designates state utilities commis- 
sions as the primary regulatory actors. 


First, the Act allows incumbent LECs and requesting car- 
riers to negotiate their own agreements privately. If they do, 
§ 252(aX(1) relieves them of the obligations of subsections 
251(b) and (c) and allows them simply to submit the agree- 
ments to the state commission for approval under § 252(e), 
as discussed below. The Act also provides that a party to ne- 
gotiations can ask the state commission to mediate any dif- 
ferences that may arise (§ 252(a)(2)). 


Second, if private or mediated negotiations prove unsuc- 
cessful after a specified time, § 252(b) provides for compul- 
sory arbitration of disputed terms by the state commission. 
The commission must resolve all unresolved issues within 
nine months from the time the incumbent LEC receives the 
initial request for negotiation. See § 252(b)(4)(A) & (C). 


Third, § 252(e)(1) requires that all interconnection agree-

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


This case presents three questions. The first is whether a 
provision that, by its terms, restricts federal jurisdiction to 
decisions made “under” a section governing the formation 
and approval of interconnection agreements should also be 
read, despite that plain limitation, to authorize review of de- 
cisions interpreting and enforcing those agreements after 
they have gone into effect. The answer to this question is 
that such decisions are not made “under” 47 U.S.C. § 252, 
and therefore review of them falls outside the jurisdiction 
conferred by § 252(e)(6). Section 252 prescribes in detail 
procedures relating to the formation and approval of inter- 
connection agreements in accordance with the obligation to 
enter into them imposed by § 251, but says nothing at all 
about any subsequent commission action. Under the unam- 
biguous language of the statute, then, cases involving the in- 
terpretation or enforcement of agreements already in place 
do not involve “determinations under [§ 252]”, a necessary 
qualifier for review under § 252(e)(6). 


This plain-language reading of the statute is consistent 
with the overall scheme of the 1996 Act, which introduced 
competition in local telephone markets by mandating that 
carriers provide access to each others’ networks. That goal 
is accomplished through the making of interconnection con- 
tracts and does not further require micromanagement of the 
ensuing business relationships among carriers by federal 
courts. This reading of § 252(e)(6) is further consistent with 
the principle that federal review statutes should be consid- 
ered strictly, as well as with the Act’s own provision that 
state law is preempted only where expressly stated. It also 
reflects the behavior of the Respondents in this case, who 
included provisions in their voluntarily-negotiated agree- 
ments invoking Illinois law, and especially the actions of the 
competing carrier Respondents, who invoked state law and 
complained of breaches of contract in their complaints to the 


14 


ICC. Finally, even if this reading is only one acceptable con- 
struction of § 252(e)(6), the Court should adopt it to avoid 
the difficult constitutional issues raised by the second and 
third questions presented in this case. 


The second and third questions relate to the State of Illi- 


nois’ sovereign immunity. The second question asks whethe: — 


Illinois has waived its Eleventh Amendment immunity by. 
the act of participating in the regulatory scheme prescribed 
by Congress in § 252. As argued above, the ICC was not op- 
erating under the 1996 Act at all by interpreting and enforc- 
ing interconnection agreements. Even if the Court disagrees, 
there has been no waiver; indeed, Congress has not with suf- 
ficient clarity expressed its intent that state commission par- 
ticipation be conditioned on waiver. (As with jurisdiction 
itself, as described in Part I of our argument, almost every- 
thing involving § 252(e)(6) must, if it is to be found at all, be 
inferred from some source other than text.) For the State’s 
own part, no express waiver of immunity is claimed, and the 
State has made no unequivocal declaration of its willingness 
to be sued. 


Moreover, no constructive waiver occurred. After the 
Court’s decision in College Sav. Bank v. Florida Prepaid 
Postsecondary Educ. Expense Bd., 527 U.S. 666 (1999), no 
constructive waiver is possible when, as in the Act, Congress 
has exercised regulatory powers. In this case, rather than 
voluntarily waiving its immunity, the State has been uncon- 
stitutionally coerced into federal court by the threat of the 
exercise of federal power, and not induced to assent to suit 
by the bestowing of any “gratuity” by Congress. The threat 
made by Congress in § 252 was to exclude the State from 
regulatory activity that it has been conducting for decades. 
Although the Act represents a significant exercise of Com- 
merce Clause power by Congress, even in the area of local 
telephone service, Congress has not preempted State regula- 
tion. Instead, it has explicitly authorized the FCC to preempt 


15 


state commission jurisdiction only when the state commis- 
sion fails to act. The ICC’s adjudication of these complaints 
was therefore “lawful”; indeed, it was core state govern- 
mental activity that the ICC was required to perform under 
both state and federal law. To decline to participate in the 


—proceedings contemplated by § 252 was neither a practical 


nor legal option for the ICC. Absent a valid waiver, the dis- 
trict court was without jurisdiction to entertain this suit 
against Petitioners. 


The third question involves whether, despite the State’s 
immunity, federal court review of these decisions under 
§ 252(e)(6) can nonetheless be achieved by means of an offi- 
cial-capacity action against individual commissioners under 
Ex parte Young. This suit, which in fact named the members 
of the ICC as defendants, was in reality a suit against the 
ICC itself, and therefore against the State of Illinois. The 
claimed legal errors, even errors of federal law, made by the 
ICC members acting within their jurisdiction were not viola- 
tions of federal law and therefore did not strip the commis- 
sioners of their lawful authority simply because a reviewing 
federal court might later see the law differently. 


Exposing individual commissioners to the full range of a 
federal court’s powers would also be inconsistent with 
§ 252(e)(6) itself. That subsection provides only for review 
of an order of a state commission, not for any action against 
an individual commissioner. The commissioners did nothing 
but consider and vote; the order they issued, and of which 
review was sought, was that of the ICC. Accordingly a suit 
against individual commissioners would exceed the limits of 
the remedial scheme provided for by Congress in 
§ 252(e)(6), a detailed scheme that restricts the relief avail- 
able to review of whether an agreement approved by a state 
commission complies with federal law. At most then, 
§ 252(e)(6) contemplates relief analogous to customary judi- 
cial review of decisions of administrative bodies like the

FCC, and not lawsuits that would subject individual commis- 
sioners to a federal court’s equitable powers. 


ARGUMENT 


I. STATE COMMISSION DECISIONS INTER- 
PRETING AND ENFORCING INTERCONNEC- 
TION AGREEMENTS ARE NOT REVIEWABLE 
IN FEDERAL COURT UNDER § 252(e)(6). 


The court of appeals held that the jurisdiction granted un- 
der 47 U.S.C. § 252(e)(6) encompassed the review of deci- 
sions of state utilities commissions that interpreted and 
enforced interconnection agreements, as well as those ap- 
proving them or arbitrating their terms. In so holding, the 
court ignored language that plainly limits application of 
§ 252(e)(6) to determinations made “under” § 252 itself. 
That provision, in turn, addresses only the formation and ap- 
proval of agreements. The court’s decision violated princi- 
ples of statutory construction, some long-settled and some 
specifically set forth in the 1996 Act, by interpreting the 
statute according to its perceived general “scheme.” Even if 
appropriate, moreover, an examination of the plan reflected 
in the Act, as well as the actions of the parties in this case, 
shows that the Act left responsibility for interpreting and en- 
forcing agreements among local telephone providers, and the 
review of those actions, where they have always been: in the 
hands of the States. 


A. The Plain Language of § 252(e)(6) Limits 
Federal Court Review of State Commission 
Actions to Those Involving the Arbitration or 
Approval of Interconnection Agreements. 


In holding that § 252(e)(6) allowed the district court to 
review the decision of the ICC in this case, the court of ap- 
peals ignored the primary limitation of that provision, 
namely that the “determination” of which review is sought

be made “under” § 252 itself. The Court should reject the 
court of appeals’ interpretation in favor of one giving effect 
to that plain and unambiguous restriction. 


The starting point for the construction of any statute is the 
language of the statute itself. Hallstrom v. Tillamook County, 
493 U.S. 20, 25 (1989); Consumer Product Safety Comm'n 
v. GTE Sylvania, Inc., 447 U.S. 102, 108 (1980). Where the 
language of the statute is plain, it is regarded as conclusive, 
and any inquiry as to its meaning should ordinarily end. Jn- 
galls Shipbuilding, Inc. v. Director, Office of Workers’ Com- 
pensation Programs, 519 U.S. 248, 261 (1997); GTE 
Sylvania, 447 U.S. at 108. In addition, the nature of the pro- 
vision at issue in this case also calls for a narrow reading. 
Because federal courts are limited in their jurisdiction, Kok- 
konen v. Guardian Life Ins. Co. of America, 511 U.S. 375, 
377 (1994), statutes conferring federal jurisdiction, including 
the power to review other bodies’ decisions, are strictly con- 
strued. Stone v. INS, 514 U.S. 386, 405 (1995) (“Judicial re- 
view provisions... are jurisdictional in nature and must be 
construed with strict fidelity to their terms.”); Shamrock Oi 
Co. v. Sheets, 313 U.S. 100, 108-09 (1941). 


In this case, the court of appeals held that the district court 
had jurisdiction to entertain Respondent Ameritech’s suit 
under § 252(e)(6), in which Ameritech sought review of a 
decision of the ICC interpreting and enforcing provisions of 
its interconnection agreements with the remaining Respon-

follows: 


In any case in which a State commission makes a 
determination under this section, any party ag- 
grieved by such determination may bring an action 
in an appropriate Federal district court to determine 
whether the agreement or statement meets the re-

state commission determination reviewable in federal court 
to one involving the formation of an interconnection agree-

enforcement. The requirement that the determination to be 
reviewed be one made “under this section,” i.e. under § 252, 
is announced in the initial clause as a necessary prerequisite 
to going any further. Section 252 sets out procedures only for 
the negotiation, arbitration and approval of agreements. Al- 
though it addresses in some detail the process of formation 
of interconnection agreements--including negotiation that 
leads to voluntary agreement, arbitration if the parties cannot 
agree on any issues, and the requirement that the state com- 
mission approve the agreement before it goes into effect and 
the grounds on which a commission may reject an agree- 
ment--that section is utterly silent regarding how, or by 
whom, an agreement is to be interpreted and enforced once it 
has gone into effect.® This limitation is unambiguous; a de- 
termination must be made “under” § 252, that is, made in 
connection with the listed procedures for formation or ap-

under § 252(e)(6). See Ardestani v. INS, 502 U.S. 129, 135

B. State Court Review of Decisions Interpreting 
and Enforcing Interconnection Agreements is 
Consistent with the 1996 Act and with the 
Conduct of the Parties in This Case. 


Reading 47 U.S.C. § 252(e)(6) to extend only to state 
tion agreements properly leaves review of decisions inter- 
preting and enforcing those agreements to state courts. This 
is precisely where cases involving the relationships among 
local telephone providers have been for decades and remain 
under the 1996 Act. Nothing in either the Act’s design or its 
language suggests that, once the contracts providing for in- 
terconnection are in place and have been adjudged to con- 
form to the Act, decisions resolving disputes over the 
meaning of their terms must necessarily pass through the 
federal courts in order to accomplish the Act’s goals. Indeed, 
this case demonstrates that federal court review is not essen- 
tial to the Act. 


The Court has acknowledged that in exceptional cases 
even plain language will be so manifestly inconsistent with 
legislative intent as to require a different construction. These 
instances are “rare,” United States v. Ron Pair Enter., 489 
U.S. 235, 241 (1989), and the Court has set the bar high, see 
Ingalls Shipbuilding, 519 U.S. at 261 (plain language con- 
trols unless result is “so absurd or glaringly unjust” as to 
warrant departure); Ron Pair Enter., 489 U.S. at 242 (result 
“demonstrably at odds with the intentions of the drafters”). 
Nothing in the 1996 Act approaches grounds for such a con- 
clusion in this case. It is fully consistent with the Act’s local 
telephone provisions to leave review of the interpretation and 
enforcement of contracts between carriers to state courts, 
even where that task involves the application of federal law. 
This is apparent from the Act’s language as well as the de- 
sign and purpose of its local telephone provisions.

Before the passage of the 1996 Act, the 1934 Act had di- 
vided responsibility for telecommunications regulation be- 
tween the federal government (long-distance) and the States 
(local). Although it made substantial changes in local tele- 
communications regulation, the 1996 Act did not repeal the 
1934 Act and in particular did not repeal 47 U.S.C. § 152, 
which codified that division of regulatory responsibility. See 
Iowa Util. Bd., 525 U.S. at 381 n.8 (“Congress, by extending 
[the 1996 Act] into local competition, has removed a signifi- 
cant area from the States’ exclusive control. Insofar as Con- 
gress has remained silent, however, § 152(b) continues to 
function.”) And although the obligations to interconnect im- 
posed in § 251 represent a significant departure from the 
monopolistic model under which local telephone service had 
been regulated in the past, it is equally apparent that Con- 
gress did not intend to replace all state regulatory authority, 
including the authority of state courts. Instead, the 1996 Act 
left much of the States’ regulatory systems in place, includ- 
ing the roles of state commissions as the primary regulators 
of local telephone carriers. There is no reason to conclude 
that Congress intended to displace the function of state re- 
viewing courts except where such displacement was pro- 
vided for by specific language. 


Congress exercised its Commerce Clause powers upon 
state regulation to varying degrees in the Act, ranging from 
requiring state utilities commissions to apply federal law, to 
requiring them to conduct inquiries on certain issues, to pre- 
scribing procedures to be followed by state (and even local) 
bodies, and in some areas to the complete preemption of 
state regulatory authority. Bell Atlantic Maryland, Inc. v. 
MCI WorldCom, Inc., 240 F.3d 279, 300 (4" Cir.), petition 
for cert. filed, 69 U.S.L.W. 3673 (U.S. April 5, 2001) (No. 
00-1531). Under the Act itself, however, explicit preemption 
is required to displace state law. Section 601(c)(1) of the Act 
provides:

The Act’s local telephone provisions contain no overt

Leaving the task of interpreting and enforcing intercon-

The change in local telephone regulation imposed by the Act

provide for market entry without requiring duplication of

the interconnection agreement provided for by § 252. These

the court of appeals characterized the actions of the ICC in 
this case as acceptance of a clearly conditional gift or gratu- 
ity bestowed by Congress. Because Congress could have 
preempted the ICC from all involvement in the regulation of 
telecommunications activity, the court reasoned, that activity 
was no longer “permissible” or “lawful” for the ICC. There- 
fore, “inviting” it to participate was the offer of something to 
which it was not entitled, a gift that could be conditioned on 
the waiver of sovereign immunity without amounting to co- 
ercion. MCI I], 222 F.3d at 342-44. Whether or not Dole and 
Petty represent forms of waiver that have been “endorsed,” 
MCI II, 222 F.3d at 342, or merely distinguished, they have 
no application here. 


The federal power underlying the 1996 Act was not the 
authority to confer a “gift” or “gratuity” such as approval or 
funds. Rather, Congress regulated under its Commerce 
Clause power and its power under the Supremacy Clause to 
displace state laws. Congress exercised those powers to in- 
fluence, to varying degrees, the States’ own exercise of their 
pre-existing authority over local telecommunications, see 
Bell Atlantic, 240 F.3d at 300, not to offer the States amy 
benefit. The court of appeals relied on the broad sweep of tthe 
Commerce Clause power, MC/ //, 222 F.3d at 343, but the 
fact that Congress possesses preemptive power under the 
Commerce Clause does not relax the requirement that a State 
consent to waive its sovereign immunity. Seminole Tribe, 
517 U.S. at 72 (“Even where the Constitution vests in Con- 
gress complete lawmaking authority over a particular area, 


36 


the Eleventh Amendment prevents congressional authoriza- 
tion of suits by private parties against unconsenting States.”’); 
see also FERC v. Mississippi, 456 U.S. 742, 785-86 (1982) 
(O’Connor, J., dissenting) (Despite Congress’ ability to pre- 
empt “the entire field of utility regulation,” “(t]he Constitu- 
tion permits Congress to govern only through certain 
channels.... [I]t is no answerthat Congress could have 
reached the same destination by a different route”). In order 
to obtain a valid waiver of sovereign immunity under the 
Commerce Clause, then, Congress still had to obtain from 
the State an unequivocal and voluntary declaration of waiver. 


To conclude that regulation of local telephone service by 
state commissions was no longer a “lawful” activity after the 
passage of the 1996 Act, the court of appeals essentially 
treated § 252 as if it had in fact preempted state regulation of 
local telecommunications and then “invited” the States back 
in. See MCI II, 222 F.3d at 343-44. The Act itself belies this 
notion. As noted supra p. 23, § 601(c) of the Act preserves 
preexisting federal and state law absent explicit preemption. 
Although § 252 prescribes rules and requires state commis- 
sions to apply them, it does not purport to preempt the States 
from exercising regulatory jurisdiction over local telecom- 
munications. Indeed, § 252(e)(5) specifically provides that 
state jurisdiction is “preempt[ed]” only by FCC order and 
only when the state commission fails to act. Similarly, 47 
U.S.C. § 152, which assigned regulatory jurisdiction over 
local telecommunications to the States as a matter of federal 
law, was not repealed by the Act. See lowa Util. Bd., 525 
U.S. at 381 n.8. 


Absent actual (and therefore explicit) preemption of state 
regulation, the ICC’s resolution of the competing carriers’ 
complaints was “lawful” or “otherwise permitted” activity, 
and waiver extracted under the threat of exclusion from that 
activity was “automatically” involuntary, see College Sav. 
Bank, 527 U.S. at 687. Indeec', under both federal and state 


37 


law, the ICC’s proceedings in this case were required. The 
provisions of § 252 are not “invitations” to state commis- 
sions. They prescribe what a state commission “shall” do, 
see, e.g., § 252(b)(4)(C) (state commission “shall resolve 
each issue” in arbitration); § 252(c)(1) (state commission 
“shall ensure that [arbitrated] resolutions and conditions 
meet the requirements of” § 251); § 252(e) (state commis- 
sion “shall approve or reject the agreement”). Section 
252(e)(S) speaks in similar terms, directing FCC preemption 
when a state commission “fails to act to carry out its respon- 
sibility” under § 252. These provisions reveal the real terms 
of the one-sided “invitation” extended by Congress: the 
States must incorporate federal rules and procedures into 
their regulation of local telecommunications and implicitly 
submit te suit in federal court by any “aggrieved” private 
party when they follow them. 


Illinois law similarly bound the ICC. The provisions of the 
Illinois Public_Utilities Act, in place well before the 1996 
Act and invoked by the competing carrier Respondents in 
their complaints (Jt. App. 52a, 79a, 90a), require the ICC to 
dispose of complaints brought before it and even provide for 
the issuance of writs of mandamus if the ICC does not issue 
orders resolving the complaints within a specified time. See 
220 ILCS 5/10-108, 10-109 and 10-110. Those state statutes, 
not explicitly preempted by any provision of the 1996 Act, 
do not allow the ICC to decline to resolve complaints for 
reasons unrelated to the complaints themselves, including a 
desire not to consent to suit in federal court. Accordingly the 
“invitation” from Congress to participate in local telephone 
regulation conditioned on willingness to waive sovereign 
immunity was, under both federal and state law, an offer that 
the ICC literally could not refuse. 


In concluding that state regulation of agreements among 
local telephone service providers was no longer an “other- 
wise permissible activity” for state commissions, MC/ //, 


38 


222 F.3d at 343, the court of appeals ignored the fact that 
federal regulatory power created this precondition for 
waiver. Petitioners’ presence in federal court resulted not 
from a choice made and expressed by the State of Illinois, 
but from the unilateral action of Congress. Because they in- 
volve congressional powers to offer inducements, cases like 
Petty and Dole may not present the danger of coercion that 
arises where, as here, the “gift” may not be declined. Under 
the 1996 Act, however, the consequence of refusal to waive 
instead is “not the denial of a gift or gratuity, but a sanction: 
exclusion of the State from otherwise permissible activity.” 
College Sav. Bank, 527 U.S. at 687; see also Edelman, 415 
U.S. at 688 (Marshall, J., dissenting) (distinguishing between 
laws that “impose federal standards and liability upon... of- 
ten-unwilling state agencies” and those that “induce” state 
immunity waiver by providing funds). As the College Sav. 
Bank Court feared, this type of one-sided “forced waiver” is 
merely abrogation by another name—“not even different sides 
of the same coin, [but] the same side of the same coin.” Jd. 
at 683. | 


In College Sav. Bank, the Court observed that: 


All congressional creations of private rights of ac- 
tion attach recovery to the defendant’s commission 
of some act, or possession of some status, in a field 
where Congress has the authority to regulate con- 
duct. Thus, a// federal prescriptions are, insofar as 
their prospective application is concerned, in a 
sense conditional, and—to the extent that the objects 
of the prescriptions consciously engage in the ac- 
tivity or hold the status that produces liability—can 
be redescribed as invitations to “waiver.” 


527 U.S. at 683, quoting Union Gas, 491 U.S. at 43 (Scalia, 
J., dissenting) (emphasis in original). The court of appeals in 
this case did just what that passage condemned: it “rede- 
scribed” a unilaterally-imposed federal prescription attached

39 


to “conscious{ }” ICC activity that fell within Congress’ 
regulatory field as an “invitation to “waiver.’” The more ac- 
curate description of that action by Congress is neither a 
“gift” nor a “gratuity,” but an abrogation. 


In evaluating the voluntariness of the ICC’s purported 
waiver of sovereign immunity, finally, the Court should con- 
sider the character of the activity from which the ICC would 
be excluded under the Act absent its consent to suit in federal 
court. Utilities regulation is a long-standing function of state 
government in Illinois, and the right and power to undertake 
it is not a gratuity Congress offered to the State. The ICC has 
been regulating public utilities in Illinois for almost 90 years. 
Moreover, federal law has been applied in the regulation of 
utilities in Illinois since before the ICC was created. See, 
e.g., Bhd. of R. R. Trainmen v. Terminal Ry. Ass'n., 379 Ill 
403, 411-12 (1942); People ex rel. Stead v. Chicago, Indian- 
apolis & Louisville Ry. Co., 223 Ill. 581, 594 (1906) (pre- 
dating existence of ICC). Although the Court in College Sav. 
Bank found that the distinction between inherently govern- 
mental conduct and that customarily engaged in by private 
parties (like operating the railroad in Parden) was not illu- 
minating in that commercial-activity case, it does demon- 
strate in this case the gravity of offense to state sovereignty 
posed by § 252(e)(6). If waivers of sovereign immunity can- 
not be obtained by threats of exclusion of a State from the 
“lawful” operation of a railroad or a college savings plan, it 
would be anomalous to hold that they could be based on 
threats to restrict the operation of a State’s utilities commis- 
sion, even where its proceedings involved consideration of 
federal law. The State of Illinois should not have to choose 
between giving up the “vital public service” performed by 
the ICC and “‘consenting’” to federal suit.” See Employees 
of the Dep't of Public Health & Welfare of Missouri v. Dep't 
of Health & Welfare of Missouri, 411 U.S. 279, 296-97 
(1973) (Marshall, J., concurring) (internal quotations in 
original). 


40 


Left undisturbed, the court of appeals’ effort to apply the 
constructive waiver doctrine to § 252(e)(6) would carry far- 
reaching implications. If a State, simply by acting in an area 
that falls within Congress’ commerce power, can be deemed 
to have implicitly waived its sovereign immunity as a condi- 
tion of continued activity in that area, Congress can easily 
“circumvent the antiabrobgation holding of Seminole Tribe.” 
College Sav. Bank, 527 U.S. at 683. College Sav. Bank reaf- 
firmed the principle that a State’s waiver of sovereign im- 
munity must, like any other waiver of a constitutional right, 
be both unequivocally expressed by the State, 527 U.S. at 
680-81, and “altogether voluntary,” id. at 676 (citing Beers v. 
Arkansas, 61 U.S. 527 (1858) (internal quotations omitted). 
The court of appeals’ interpretation of the 1996 Act allowed 
Congress to impose waiver unilaterally, under the threat of 
exclusion of the State irom a “lawful activity,” a condition 
that bestowed no gift; rather, it “automatically” passed the 
“point of coercion” and “destroyed” the voluntariness of any 
waiver. College Sav. Bank, 527 U.S. at 687. For these rea- 
sons, the decision below should be reversed. 


Ili. THE ICC’S MEMBERS CANNOT BE SUED 
UNDER THE DOCTRINE OF Ex parte Young. 


The third question on which the Court granted certiorari 
involves whether Respondents can evade Illinois’ sovereign 
immunity by means of a suit against the individual members 
of the ICC under the Ex parte Young doctrine. The court of 
appeals did not address the question in this case, but it did 
allow the case to proceed against the ICC’s individual mem- 
bers, Pet. App. 14a, and it answered the question in the af- 
firmative in MCI IJ, 222 F.3d at 345-48. Contrary to the 
court of appeals’ view, however, “(t]he situation presented 
here... is sufficiently different from that giving rise to the 
traditional Ex parte Young action so as to preclude the avail- 
ability of that doctrine.” Seminole Tribe, 517 U.S. at 73. 


4) 


Under Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123, 159-60 (1908), the 
Eleventh Amendment is generally not a bar to actions by 
private citizens in federal court to restrain state officials from 
committing ongoing violations of federal law. See also Idaho 
v. Coeur D'Alene Tribe of Idaho, 521 U.S. 261, 276-77 
(1997). The Young doctrine has been thought necessary to 
ensure the supremacy of federal law, see Green v. Mansour, 
474 U.S. 64, 68 (1985), and its continued validity is not in 
doubt, see Coeur D'Alene, 521 U.S. at 269. But “[t}he Court 
has also recognized... that the need to promote the suprem- 
acy of federal law must be accommodated to the constitu- 
tional immunity of the States.” Pennhurst II, 465 U.S. at 105. 
Accordingly, the Court has been careful not to expand the 
doctrine in ways that “would upset the balance of federal and 
state interests that [Young] embodies.” Papasan v. Allain, 
478 U.S. 265, 277 (1986); see also Coeur D'Alene, 521 U.S. 
at 281 (Young-type relief may not be available where a 
State’s “special sovereignty interests” are at stake); 
Pennhurst II, 465 U.S. at 104-06 (state officials may not 
be coerced under Ex parte Young to conform their conduct 
to state law); Edelman, 415 U.S. at 665-66 (Young 
action will not lie to vindicate past violations of 
federal law). 


The careful balancing of state and federal interests re- 
quired under the Court’s Ex parte Young jurisprudence 
weighs decidedly against the doctrine’s application in this 
case. First, there is no need to vindicate the supremacy of 
federal law because state commissions performing their du- 
ties under the Act do not commit “violations” of that law by 
deciding federal law issues within their jurisdiction. Second, 
by providing a specific mechanism for judicial review of 
state commission decisions rendered under the Act, Congress 
signaled its intent not to subject individual commission 
members to judicially-created remedies of the sort authorized 
under Ex parte Young. See Seminole Tribe, 517 U.S. at 73- 


42 


76. For these reasons, the Court should reverse the judgment 
of the court of appeals. 


A. State Commissioners Do Not Lose 
Their Official Character by Making 
Errors of Federal Law Within their 
Jurisdiction. 


Ex parte Young only applies if a state officer is alleged to 
be committing an ongoing violation of federal law. Papasan, 
478 U.S. at 277-78. Members of state public utility commis- 
sions are hardly open to this charge in the present context. 
They cannot be said to violate federal law, to be acting 
“without the authority of... the state in its sovereign or gov- 
ernmental capacity,” and therefore be “stripped of [their] of- 
ficial and representative character,” Young, 209 U.S. at 159- 
60, simply by resolving disputes within their jurisdiction, 
even if their decisions are based on views of federal law that, 
upon later review, are rejected. Because Respondents cannot 
overcome this traditional limitation on Young’s application, 
the Court should hold the doctrine inapplicable. 


The court of appeals framed the issue in this case as 
“whether the ICC, in determining that under the agreements 
the parties intended that calls to Internet service providers 
would be subject to reciprocal compensation, violated fed- 
eral law.” (Pet. App. 15a). This statement implies that the 
ICC and like commissions “violate federal law” when, in the 
course of resolving disputes within their jurisdiction, they 
misapply substantive federal law or rules that govern inter- 
connection agreements. This is wrong, at least as far as the 
sovereign immunity question is concerned. State commis- 
sions do not violate federal law, and thereby lose their bond 
to the State that employs them, by exercising that jurisdic- 
tion. See Papasan, 478 U.S. at 277 (“In accordance with its 
original rationale, Young applies only where the underlying 
authorization upon which the named official acts is asserted 
to be illegal.”). This is true even if, in the exercise of their 


43 


authority, commissions err in applying governing law. See 
generally Pennhurst Il, 465 U.S. at 113 (“[A]n error of law 
by state officers acting in their official capacities will not 
suffice to override the sovereign immunity of the State where 
the relief is effectively against it.”) (citation omitted); //lini 
Coach Co. v. Illinois Commerce Comm'n, 408 Ill. 104, 109- 
10, 96 N.E. 2d 518, 521 (1951) (ICC orders are not rendered 
void on the ground that they are erroneous)."® 


This result follows from Larson v. Domestic & Foreign 
Commerce Corp., 337 U.S. 682 (1949), in which the Court 
barred an action for specific performance of a contract based 
upon the federal government’s sovereign immunity, even 
though the named defendant was an official rather than the 
government agency itself. The plaintiff had named a federal 
agency’s administrator as the defendant, but made no allega- 
tion that the administrator had acted outside his statutory 
authority. /d. at 691. Holding the action was a suit against 
the government even though an individual officer was the 
named defendant, the Court laid down the general rule: 


[T]he action of an officer of the sovereign... can be 
regarded as so “illegal” as to permit a suit for a 
specific relief against the officer as an individual 
only if it is not within the officer’s statutory pow- 
ers or, if within those powers, only if the powers, 


'© There can be no dispute that the relief sought in this case is “ef- 
fectively against” the ICC. The ICC acts only as a body, 220 ILCS 
5/2-101 (“[E]very finding, order or decision approved by a major- 
ity of the members of the Commission shall be deemed the find- 
ing, order or decision of the Commission.”), the complaint filed by 
Respondent Ameritech specifically sought an injunction “against 
enforcement of the ICC’s Order,” Jt. App. 145a, and even the 
court of appeals agreed that, in the context of an action seeking 
review of the ICC’s determinations, its commissioners “are the 
Commission,” Pet. App. 14a.

or their exercise in the particular case, are consti- 
tutionally void. 


Id. at 701-02. “A claim of error in the exercise of that 
power,” the Court added, “is . . . not sufficient.” Jd. at 690. 


Larson \eads to the conclusion that state utilities commis- 
sioners do not violate federal law and therefore do not lose 
their official character when they interpret interconnection 
agreements, even if their interpretation contains errors of 
federal law. Indeed, the very action claimed to have violated 
federal law in Larson was the construction of a contract, a 
construction the Court assumed was wrong. /d. at 703. 
Having jurisdiction to decide the matters raised in the com- 
plaints in this case, the ICC was required to resolve them in 
accordance with the best judgment of the commissioners and 
the mandates of due process. But those commissioners can- 
not be sued because they render “incorrect” decisions: “We 
have heretofore rejected the argument that official action is 
invalid if based upon an incorrect decision as to law or fact, 
if the officer making the decision was empowered to do so.” 
Larson, 337 U.S. at 695; see also Pennhurst II, 465 U.S. at 
112-13 & n.22. 


To be sure, there are instances where state officials per- 
forming adjudicatory or quasi-adjudicatory functions are said 
to be in violation of federal law. See, e.g., Bush v. Gore, 531 
U.S. 98, 121 S. Ct. 525, 530-33 (2000) (per curiam) (state 
court violated voters’ equal protection rights by ordering 
manual recount of ballots without providing uniform stan- 
dards for what constitutes a legal vote); Shelley v. Kramer, 
334 U.S. 1, 20 (1948) (state court’s enforcement of racially- 
restrictive covenant held to be in violation of the Fourteenth 
Amendment). But cases like these do not support the propo- 
sition that state adjudicators violate federal law whenever 
they misapply governing federal standards. These cases only 
establish that judicial officers can be state actors within the 
meaning of the Fourteenth Amendment, see Kramer, 334 


45 


U.S. at 14, a rule that stands in acknowledged tension with 
the fiction upon which Young is based, i.e., that officials 
acting in contravention of federal law no longer act on behalf 
of the State for purposes of the Eleventh Amendment. See 
generally Florida Dep't of State v. Treasure Salvors, Inc., 
458 U.S. 670, 685 (1982) (“There is a well-recognized irony 
in Ex parte Young; unconstitutional conduct by a state officer 
may be state action for purposes of the Fourteenth Amend- 
ment yet not attributable to the State for purposes of the 
Eleventh.”) (plurality opinion). The state action cases do not 
address the scope of a State’s sovereign immunity and thus 
do not diminish the rule in Larson. Under that rule, state ad- 
judicators cannot be said to violate federal law by resolving 
disputes within their lawful jurisdiction. 


B. Limiting Relief to Judicial Review of 
State Commission Decisions Evinces 
Congressional Intent to Preclude Ex parte 
Young Actions. 


Even if state commissions can be said to violate federal 
law when they perform functions assigned to them under the 
Act, their individual members are not amenable to suit under 
Ex parte Young. Congress envisioned one limited and com- 
monplace safeguard against agency error: the availability of 
judicial review. See 47 U.S.C. § 252(e)(6). Under Seminole 
Tribe, 517 U.S. at 73-76, this limited remedy forecloses the 
availability of Young-type relief. 


“(Where Congress has prescribed a detailed remedial 
scheme for the enforcement against a State of a statutorily- 
created right, a court should hesitate before casting aside 
those limitations and permitting an action against a state of- 
ficer based upon Ex parte Young.” Seminole Tribe, 517 U.S. 
at 74. The remedial scheme supplied by § 252(e)(6) is not 
detailed in the sense that it is elaborate, but it “define[s]” and 
also “limit[s] significantly” the relief available. See id. In this 
case, of course, Ameritech brought suit not to enforce a 


46 


statutory right against Illinois, but rather to obtain some form 
of relief from the ICC’s order. Still, the result was the same: 
state officers sued in federal court. The same principle of re- 
straint should therefore apply. More specifically, just as 
courts should recoil from creating remedies in addition to 
those prescribed by Congress for the enforcement of federal 
rights, see Seminole Tribe, 517 U.S. at 74 (citing Schweiker 
v. Chilicky, 487 U.S. 412, 423 (1988)), so too should they 
avoid creating additional or different federal review mecha- 
nisms when Congress has specifically provided one, see, 
e.g., Whitney Nat'l Bank v. Bank of New Orleans & Trust 
Co., 379 U.S. 411, 420 (1965); Maxine Marine, Inc. v. Di- 
rector, Officer of Workers’ Compensation Programs, 39 
F.3d 144, 146 (7th Cir. 1994). Because Congress limited the 
remedy available to “aggrieved parties” under § 252(e)(6) to 
review of state commission decisions, and narrowed it even 
further to consideration of whether the agreement under con- 
sideration meets the requirements of specified federal laws, 
the additional remedies provided by the broad equitable 
powers of federal courts as applied to individual state offi- 
cials should not be available. 


In § 252(e)(6) Congress specifically provided for a famil- 
iar method of judicial review: “In any case in which a State 
commission makes a determination under this section, any 
party aggrieved by such determination may bring an action 
in an appropriate Federal district court to determine whether 
the agreement or statement meets the requirements of section 
251 and this section.” To the extent this language at all con- 
templates a State’s participation in review proceedings, it 
undoubtedly envisions participation by the state commission 
itself, not its individual members. See AT&T Communica- 
tions v. BellSouth Telecommunications, Inc., 238 F.3d 636, 
653 (Sth Cir. 2001) (Smith, J., dissenting). In addition, it 
clearly contemplates traditional judicial review of adminis- 
trative agency action, as would be available in analogous 
cases where review of an FCC order is sought. In those cases 


47 


the FCC is the appropriate defendant, not its individual 
members. See generally 28 U.S.C. § 2342(1); 47 U.S.C. 
§ 402; see also GTE South, Inc. v. Morrison, 199 F.3d 733, 
743 (4th Cir. 1999) (FCC is proper defendant in review pro- 
ceeding challenging its orders); City of Peoria v. General 
Elec. Cablevision Corp., 690 F.2d 116, 119 (7th Cir. 1982) 
(same). And when FCC orders are overturned, the courts do 
not issue injunctions against the FCC’s individual members, 
as Ameritech sought here (Jt. App. 145a). Instead, cases are 
remanded to the agency, and the FCC is duty-bound to com- 
ply with the court’s mandate. See 47 U.S.C. § 402(h); FCC 
v. Pottsville Broadcasting Co., 309 US. 134, 145 (1940) 
(“On review the court may thus correct errors of law and on 
remand the [FCC] is bound to act upon the correction.”). 
Section 252(e)(6)’s language suggests no intention to depart 
from this scheme where review of a state commission’s order 
is sought. 


The Act thus contemplates nothing more than straightfor- 
ward judicial review of state commission decisions. See Bell 
Atlantic, 240 F.3d at 295 (“The limit of the stated interest 
{under section 252(e)(6)] is the ‘review’ of certain State 
commission decisions.”). There is no text-based evidence 
that Congress meant to supplement this mechanism by 
authorizing district courts to enjoin state commission mem- 
bers in contravention of the States’ sovereign immunity. Cf 
5 U.S.C. § 702 (specifically waiving federal government’s 
immunity from actions for injunctions against federal offi- 
cers). To the contrary, it is doubtful that Congress, assuming 
it has in fact entrusted state commissions with the task of re- 
solving disputes over the meaning of federally-mandated in- 
terconnection agreements, wanted commissioners to be 
hauled into federal court to answer for their alleged errors in 
applying federal law. As the Fourth Circuit explained in Bell 
Atlantic: 


48 


To bring these State officers before a federal court 
to answer to the assertion of a private citizen that 
in acting within the scope of their authority they 
made the wrong decision would be an affront to the 
sovereignty of the State . . . and would undermine 
the mutual respect that characterizes the relation- 
ship between the federal and State sovereigns. 


240 F.3d at 298. 


The court of appeals rejected this argument on the ground 
that the Act does not explicitly limit the relief district courts 
may award when reviewing agency action under section 
252(e(6). MCI II, 222 F.3d at 347. The statute’s silence on 
available remedies, however, cannot be construed as a con- 
gressional imprimatur on any kind of remedy that might be 
devised to enforce judgments under § 252(e)(6). No one 
doubts the district court’s ability to enforce its judgments by 
injunction against persons who are proper parties to the 
court’s proceedings. See AT&T Communications, 238 F.3d at 
653 n.20 (Smith, J., dissenting). It is quite another matter, 
however, to say that state commission members—who are 
not mentioned in the text of § 252(e)(6), as proper parties or 
otherwise—may be named as defendants and thereby “ex- 
pose[d]... to the full remedial powers of a federal court, in- 
cluding, presumably, contempt sanctions.” Seminole Tribe, 
517 U.S. at 75. Young actions against the individual mem- 
bers of the ICC in the present context would “supplement” 
the scheme created by Congress “with one created by the 
judiciary.” Seminole Tribe, 517 U.S. at 74. As the Court has 
made clear, it is for Congress and not the courts to choose 
which remedies are sufficient for the enforcement of statu- 
tory rights. See id. at 75-76. To allow this action to proceed 
under a Young theory would contradict the intent of Con- 
gress as reflected in the specific remedial scheme described 
in § 252(e)(6). 


CONCLUSION 
The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.

(1) to interconnect directly or indirectly with the fa- 
cilities and equipment of other telecommunications 
carriers; and 


(2) not to install network features, functions, or capa- 
bilities that do not comply with the guidelines and 
standards established pursuant to section 255 or 256 
[, 47 U.S.C.]. 


(b) Obligations of all local exchange carriers 


Each local exchange carrier has the following duties: 


(1) Resale 


The duty not to prohibit, and not to impose unrea- 
sonable or discriminatory conditions or limitations 
on, the resale of its telecommunications services. 


(2) Number portability 


The duty to provide, to the extent technically feasi- 
ble, number portability in accordance with require- 
ments prescribed by the [FCC]. 


(3) Dialing parity 


The duty to provide dialing parity to competing pro- 
viders of telephone exchange service and telephone 
toll service, and the duty to permit all such providers 
to have nondiscriminatory access to telephone num- 
bers, operator services, directory assistance, and di- 
rectory listing, with no unreasonable dialing delays. 


2a 


(4) Access to rights-of-way 


The duty to afford access to the poles, ducts, con- 
duits, and rights-of-way of such carrier to competing 
providers of telecommunications services on rates, 
terms, and conditions that are consistent with section 
224 [, 47 U.S.C.]. 


(5) Reciprocal compensation 


The duty to establish reciprocal compensation ar- 
rangements for the transport and termination of tele- 
communications. 


(c) Additional obligations of incumbent local exchange 
carriers 


In addition to the duties contained in subsection (b), each 
incumbent local exchange carrier has the following duties: 


(1) Duty to negotiate 


The duty to negotiate in good faith in accordance 
with section 252 [, 47 U.S.C.] the particular terms 
and conditions of agreements to fulfill the duties de- 
scribed in paragraphs (1) through (5) of subsection 
(b) and this subsection. The requesting telecommuni- 
cations carrier also has the duty to negotiate in good 
faith the terms and conditions of such agreements. 


(2) Interconnection 


The duty to provide, for the facilities and equipment 
of any requesting telecommunications carrier, inter- 
connection with the local exchange carrier's network— 


(A) for the transmission and routing of tele- 
phone exchange service and exchange access; 


3a 


(B) at any technically feasible point within 
the carrier's network; 


(C) that is at least equal in quality to that 
provided by the local exchange carrier to it- 
seif or to any subsidiary, affiliate, or any other 
party to which the carrier provides intercon- 
nection; and 


(D) on rates, terms, and conditions that are 
just, reasonable, and nondiscriminatory, in 
accordance with the terms and conditions of 
the agreement and the requirements of this 
section and section 252 [, 47 U.S.C.]. 


(3) Unbundled access 


The duty to provide, to any requesting telecommuni- 
cations carrier for the provision of a telecommunica- 
tions service, nondiscriminatory access to network 
elements on an unbundled basis at any technically 
feasible point on rates, terms, and conditions that are 
just, reasonable, and nondiscriminatory in accordance 
with the terms and conditions of the agreement and 
the requirements of this section and section 25? [, 47 
U.S.C.]. An incumbent local exchange carrier shall 
provide such unbundled network elements in a man- 
ner that allows requesting carriers to combine such 
elements in order to provide such telecommunica- 
tions service. 


(4) Resale 
The duty— 


(A) to offer for resale at wholesale rates any 
telecommunications service that the carrier 


4a 


provides at retail to subscribers who are not 
telecommunications carriers; and 


(B) not to prohibit, and not to impose unrea- 
sonable or discriminatory conditions or limi- 
tations on, the resale of — such 
telecommunications service, except that a 
State commission may, consistent with regu- 
lations prescribed by the [FCC] under this 
section, prohibit a reseller that obtains at 
wholesale rates a telecommunications service 
that is available at retail only to a category of 
subscribers from offering such service to a 
different category of subscribers. 


(5) Notice of changes 


The duty to provide reasonable public notice of 
changes in the information necessary for the trans- 
mission and routing of services using that local ex- 
change carrier's facilities or networks, as well as of 
any other changes that would affect the 
interoperability of those facilities and networks. 


(6) Collocation 


The duty to provide, on rates, terms, and conditions 
that are just, reasonable, and nondiscriminatory, for 
physical collocation of equipment necessary for in- 
terconnection or access to unbundled network ele- 
ments at the premises of the local exchange carrier, 
except that the carrier may provide for virtual collo- 
cation if the local exchange carrier demonstrates to 
the State commission that physical collocation is not 
practical for technical reasons or because of space 
limitations.

§ 252. Procedures for negotiation, arbitration, and ap- 
proval of agreements 


(a) Agreements arrived at through negotiation 


(1) Voluntary negotiations 


Upon receiving a request for interconnection, serv- 
ices, or network elements pursuant to section 251[, 
47 U.S.C.], an incumbent local exchange carrier may 
negotiate and enter into a binding agreement with the 
requesting telecommunications carrier or carriers 
without regard to the standards set forth in subsec- 
tions (b) and (c) of section 251 [, 47 U.S.C.]. The 
agreement shall include a detailed schedule of item- 
ized charges for interconnection and each service or 
network element included in the agreement. The 
agreement, including any interconnection agreement 
negotiated before the date of enactment of the Tele- 
communications Act of 1996 [Feb. 8, 1996], shall be 
submitted to the State commission under subsection 
(e) of this section. 


(2) Mediation 


Any party negotiating an agreement under this sec- 
tion may, at any point in the negotiation, ask a State 
commission to participate in the negotiation and to 
mediate any differences arising in the course of the 


negotiation.
