STATEMENT OF JURISDICTION 


The district court had jurisdiction pursuant to 28 U.S.C. 
§ 1331 and 47 U.S.C. § 252(e)(6) to review the determination 
of the Illinois Commerce Commission (“ICC”) for compliance 
with federal law. 


The judgment of the court of appeals was entered June 18, 
1999, and amended August 9, 1999. The petition for rehearing 
was denied on August 9, 2000. By order of a single Justice of 
this Court, Petitioners’ time for filing a petition for a writ of 
certiorari was extended to November 28, 2000. The petition 
was filed on November 28, 2000, and was granted on March 5, 
2001. This Court has jurisdiction pursuant to 28 U.S.C. 


§ 1254(1). 
STATEMENT 


Illinois Bell Telephone Company d/b/a Ameritech IIlinois 
(“Ameritech”) filed this action in federal court against the 
Commissioners of the ICC (“ICC Commissioners”) and several 
Competitors, challenging the ICC’s March 11, 1998, Order 
enforcing various “interconnection agreements” that the 
Competitors and Ameritech had executed under the 
Telecommunications Act of 1996 (“1996 Act” or “Act”), Pub. 
L. No. 104-104, 110 Stat. 56, codified at 47 U.S.C. §§ 151 et 


3 


seq.” Ameritech’s complaint alleged, inter alia, that the ICC’s 
Order violates the Act and applicable regulations of the Federal 
Communications Commission (“FCC”). The ICC 
Commissioners contend that those federal claims fall outside 
the statutory jurisdiction of the district court and, in any event, 
are excluded from adjudication in federal court by the Eleventh 
Amendment. 


1. The Statutory Scheme 


The 1996 Act fundamentally changed the regulation of the 
local telephone industry throughout the country. In the 
Communications Act of 1934, Congress left regulation of 
intrastate telecommunications primarily to the states. See 47 
U.S.C. § 152(b); Louisiana Pub. Serv. Comm'n v. FCC, 476 
U.S. 355 (1986). The states, in turn, historically granted 
exclusive franchises to incumbent local telephone companies 
like Ameritech. See AT&T Corp. v. lowa Utils. Bd., 525 U.S. 
366, 371 (1999); H.R. Rep. No. 104-204, at 49 (1995). 


Congress enacted the 1996 Act for the express purpose of 
ending this monopoly regime and promoting a competitive 
market for local telephone service. See AT&T Corp., 525 U.S. 
at 371. Toward that end, the 1996 Act partially supplants state 
regulation and imposes a new set of substantive federal 
standards and procedures governing interconnection between 
incumbent local companies and potential competitors. /d. at 
371-73. Congress thus altered the prior balance of federal and

state regulatory authority in the area of local 
telecommunications. /d. at 371. 


Under the Act, a new entrant that wishes to enter a local 
telephone market must contact the incumbent carrier to 
negotiate the terms of interconnection. See 47 U.S.C. 
§ 252(a)(1). If the two carriers cannot reach an agreement, they 
must ask the governing state commission to conduct an 
“arbitration” to resolve disputed issues. /d. § 252(b)(1). The 
results of the negotiation and arbitration process are 
memorialized in an “interconnection agreement” between the 
carriers, which is then submitted for approval to the state 
commission. /d. § 252(e). 


Although the Act supplies minimum requirements that 
state commissions must follow in arbitrating provisions about 
which the parties cannot reach a negotiated agreement, parties 
may negotiate terms “without regard” to these requirements. 
Id. § 252(a)(1). When parties voluntarily agree to terms, a state 
commission may reject the terms only if they are discriminatory 
or against the public interest, convenience, or necessity. /d. 
§ 252(e)(2)(A); see also AT&T Corp., 525 U.S. at 371-73. 


Thus, in the spirit of cooperative federalism, Congress 
vested the FCC with the primary authority to implement the 
federal standards created by the Act, AT&T Corp., 525 US. at 
377-84 & n.5; 47 U.S.C. §§ 201(b), 251(d)(1), while at the 
same time, carving out a new, voluntary role for states in 
overseeing interconnection agreements. The Act in no way 
requires state commissions to help implement the new federal 
scheme. Ifa state commission declines to participate, the FCC 
will assume that function. 47 U.S.C. § 252(e)(5). 


5 

Whether rendered by a state commission or the FCC, 
agency determinations regarding the terms of interconnection 
agreements are subject to review in federal court for 
compliance with the Act. If a state commission elects to 
participate in the Act’s implementation, “any party 
aggrieved . . . may bring an action in an appropriate Federal 
district court to determine whether the agreement or statement 
meets the requirements of section{[s] 251 . . . and [252].” 47 
U.S.C. § 252(e)(6); see also AT&T Corp., 525 U.S. at 378 n.6 
(“there is no doubt . . . that if the federal courts believe a state 
commission is not regulating in accordance with federal policy 
they may bring it to heel”). If the FCC has acted in lieu of a 
state commission, its determinations are reviewed by a federal 
court of appeals under the Hobbs Act. See 28 U.S.C. 
§ 2342(1). 


2. Nature of the Dispute 


This case concerns one aspect of the parties’ 
interconnection agreements, “reciprocal compensation,” which- 
is the payment that a local carrier receives for completing a call 
for another local carrier. With local competition, customers of 
one local carrier will call customers of another. Carriers must 
therefore assist each other in delivering calls. When a customer 
of one local carrier makes a call to a customer of another local 
carrier, only the caller pays its carrier for the telephone 
services, leaving the other carrier uncompensated. The caller’s 
local carrier therefore must compensate the other carrier whose 
facilities are used to complete the call. See 47 U.S.C. 


§ 251(b)(5); Pet. App. 8a-9a. 


The parties to this case agreed in their interconnection 


agreements to pay reciprocal compensation for the exchange of 
“Local traffic.” Local traffic is defined in the parties’ 


6 


interconnection agreements as a call of less than fifteen miles 
to another local telephone number. Pet. App. 18a; JA 8a, 14a, 
38a. The dispute in this case was whether calls by local 
customers of one carrier that are routed by another carrier to its 
local customers operating as “Internet Service Providers” 
(“ISPs”) constitute “Local traffic” under the interconnection 
agreements. These calls were treated for purposes of billing 
- customers like any other local call. Pet. App. 18a-19a; JA 
113a-114a, 117a. Ameritech and the Competitors also 
routinely charged each other reciprocal compensation for 
delivering ISP calls placed by each others’ customers. JA 
118a-119a. 


In July 1997, however, Ameritech announced that it was 
unilaterally stopping reciprocal compensation payments for 
calls to local ISPs, claiming for the first time that such calls are 
not “Local Traffic.” Pet. App. 9a. In response, the 
Competitors asked the ICC to enforce the agreements by 
requiring Ameritech to resume payments of reciprocal 
compensation for ISP calls. /d. at 9a-10a. The ICC held an 
evidentiary hearing on November 21, 1997. JA 98a-99a. On 
March 11, 1998, the ICC ruled that the plain language of the 
agreements, industry custom and practice, and Ameritech’s 
own conduct mandated payment of reciprocal compensation for 
calls to ISPs. Pet. App. 10a; JA 112a-119a.”

3. The District Court Proceedings 


On March 27, 1998, Ameritech filed suit in the United 
States District Court for the Northern District of Illinois against 
the Competitors and the ICC Commissioners, in their official 
capacities, to challenge the ICC’s determination.” Ameritech 
sought declaratory and injunctive relief under 47 U.S.C. 
§ 252(e)(6), 28 U.S.C. § 1331, and 28 U.S.C. §§ 2201 and 
2202. JA 126a-127a. Three of the five counts of Ameritech’s 
complaint alleged continuing violations of federal law. JA at 
139a-145a. Those counts alleged that the ICC’s determination 
violates the express requirements of sections 251(b)(5), 
252(d)(2) and 251(g) of the 1996 Act (Count IV); that the 
ICC’s determination violates the 1996 Act in that the Act

SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT 


The first question presented is whether a state commission 
action enforcing an interconnection agreement is “a 
determination under section 252” reviewable in federal court 
under section 252(e)(6). As the Fifth, Seventh, Eighth, and 
Tenth Circuits have agreed, the answer to that question is yes. 
The language and structure of section 252 indicate that 
Congress included section 252(e)(6) in order to make 
unequivocal that federal courts would play a role in reviewing 
the actions of state commissions that choose to participate in 
the new federal scheme. Section 252(e)(6) can and should be 
construed to accomplish that purpose with respect to all state 
commission “determinations,” including enforcement 
decisions. 


The Court should not assume, however, _ that 
section 252(e)(6) is the only source of federal jurisdiction to 
review enforcement actions for compliance with federal law. 
It is not. Even if section 252(e)(6) were inapplicable, general 
federal question jurisdiction would exist under 28 U.S.C. 
§ 1331 to address claims that state officials have violated the 
1996 Act in enforcement determinations. In this case, 


13 


Ameritech has raised a series of claims to the effect that federal 
law controls over state requirements. While the courts below 
found — correctly, in our view — that Ameritech’s federal claims 
lacked merit, the courts nonetheless had the power to consider 
them under section 1331 as well as section 252(e)(6). The ICC 
Commissioners take too narrow a view of the federal courts’ 
role to enforce the 1996 Act. 


On the other hand, Ameritech takes too broad a view of 
federal jurisdiction. Nothing in the 1996 Act suggests that 
Congress intended to federalize the contract law question 
whether a state commission has correctly interpreted an 
interconnection agreement. The 1996 Act is replete with anti- 
preemption provisions and savings clauses expressly preserving 
state law. The question whether a state commission has 
correctly interpreted a contract thus arises under state law, as 
each circuit court to have expressly considered the question has 


agreed. 


The Seventh Circuit’s holding that the ICC Commissioners 
are not immune from suit here also should be affirmed for two 
independent reasons. First, consistent with the well-settled 
doctrine of Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908), the 1996 Act 
permits aggrieved parties to seek review of state commission 
determinations in federal court. Here, Ameritech brought suit 
in federal court for prospective injunctive and declaratory relief 
to stop the Commissioners’ alleged ongoing violations of 
federal law — precisely the type of action that Ex parte Young 
permits. Moreover, that the alleged violations of federal law 
occurred in a formal decision issued by the ICC, and that 
judicial review would also have been available in state court, in 
no way undermine the applicability of Ex parte Young. 


14 


By its terms, the 1996 Act imposes no limits on the 
equitable powers that federal courts have at their disposal to 
ensure that state commissioners’ actions comply with the Act’s 
requirements. Thus, an Ex parte Young suit does not expand 
the remedy that Congress provided in the 1996 Act. To the 
contrary, the remedies available under each are perfectly 
coterminous. It follows that this Court’s admonition in 
Seminole Tribe — that courts must not allow suits under Ex 
parte Young to eviscerate detailed and limited remedial 
schemes prescribed by Congress — has no application here. 


Although the analysis can stop there, the Commission 
defendants also consented to federal court jurisdiction and 
thereby waived any Eleventh Amendment immunity by 
voluntarily choosing to participate in the administration of the 
1996 Act. In the spirit of cooperative federalism, Congress 
offered states the benefit of participating in the administration 
of the Act. It lay solely within Congress’s discretion whether 
to offer states that opportunity; administration of a new federal 
Act is not an otherwise permissible activity for a state. Further, 
each state is free to decline any role in administering the Act if 
it so chooses. Accordingly, the ICC Commissioner’s 
participation in the administration of the Act is a benefit or 
“gratuity” that can be conditioned on waiver of Eleventh 
Amendment immunity under this Court’s decision in College 
Savings.

ARGUMENT 


I. FEDERAL COURTS HAVE JURISDICTION TO 
DETERMINE WHETHER STATE COMMISSION 
“ENFORCEMENT” DETERMINATIONS VIOLATE 
THE 1996 ACT. 


The first question presented is “[w]hether a state 
commission’s action relating to the enforcement of a previously 
approved section 252 interconnection agreement is a 
‘determination under [section 252]’ and thus is reviewable in 
federal court under 47 U.S.C. § 252(e)(6).” The short answer 
to that question is yes. A state commission action enforcing an 
interconnection agreement is “a determination under 
section 252” and is reviewable in federal court under 


section 252(e)(6). 


- We respectfully submit, however, that the Court’s inquiry 
should more properly begin with the general grant of federal 
question jurisdiction in 28 U.S.C. § 1331. The question, as we 
see it, should be whether anything in section 252 suffices to 
withdraw federal question jurisdiction as to this one category 
of federal claims. When the question is reformulated in this 
manner, the answer becomes very clear. There is no language 
in section 252(e)(6) — or any other portion of the 1996 Act -— 
even hinting at a congressional intent to bar federal jurisdiction 
over claims that state commission enforcement determinations 
have violated the Act. 


16 


A. Ameritech Raised Federal Questions That 
Federal Courts Have Jurisdiction to Answer 


under 28 U.S.C. § 1331. 


The ICC and its supporting amici proceed from the false 
assumption that the existence of federal jurisdiction to review 
claims that state officials violated federal law in enforcement 
decisions turns on whether those decisions are “determinations 
under section 252” within the meaning of section 252(e)(6). 
See, e.g., Pet. Br. 17-18; Br. of New Jersey et al. as Amici 
Curiae, at 1-2 (“N.J. Br.”); Br. of Amicus Curiae Nat’! Ass’n 
of Reg. Util. Comm’rs, et a/., at 9-17 (“NARUC Br.”); Br. of 
Council of State Gov’ts, et al. as Amici Curiae, at 5-6 
(“Council Br.”). Regardless of how the Court rules on that 
issue, the district court plainly had jurisdiction to consider 
Ameritech’s federal claims. A civil action “arises under” 
federal law for purposes of section 1331 when a “right or 
immunity created by the Constitution or laws of the United 
States [is] an element, and an essential one, of the plaintiff's 
cause of action.” Gully v. First Nat'l Bank, 299 U.S. 109, 112 
(1936); accord City of Chicago v. International College of 
Surgeons, 522 U.S. 156, 163-64 (1997). 


To determine whether it has federal question jurisdiction, 
the court “must look to the way the complaint is drawn to see 
if it is drawn so as to claim a mght to recover under the 
Constitution and laws of the United States.” Bell v. Hood, 327 
U.S. 678, 681 (1946); see Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better 
Env't, 523 U.S. 83, 89 (1998). Ameritech’s complaint easily 
satisfies this requirement. Three counts in that complaint — 
Counts II, III, and IV — directly allege a federal violation. In 
Count II, Ameritech alleged that “The ICC Order Is Contrary 
To Controlling FCC Orders.” JA 140a. In Count III, 
Ameritech alleged that “The ICC’s Order Violates Controlling 


17 


Federal Law, Which Assigns Authority Over Interstate 
Communications To The FCC.” JA 14la. In Count IV, 
Ameritech alleged that “The ICC Order Violates Sections 
251(b)(5), 252(d)(2) and 251(g) of the 1996 Act.” JA 142a. 
Each of these Counts requires the court to consider federal law 
as an “essential element” of Ameritech’s claims. Jurisdiction 
therefore exists under section 1331. see, e.g., Shaw v. Delta 
Air Lines, Inc., 463 U.S. 85, 96 n.14 (1983) (“It is beyond 
dispute that federal courts have jurisdiction [under section 
1331] over suits to enjoin state officials from interfering with 
federal rights”). 


Of course, federal statutory challenges to state commission— 
enforcement determinations as contrary to federal law need not 
be meritorious in order to fall within the jurisdiction of the 
federal courts. Here, for example, the district court and the 
Seventh Circuit properly concluded that nothing in the Act or 
relevant FCC regulations bars the ICC from enforcing 
agreements to pay reciprocal compensation on calls to ISPs. 
But “it is well settled that the failure to state a proper cause of 
action calls for a judgment on the merits and not for a dismissal 
for want of jurisdiction.” Bell v. Hood, 327 U.S. at 682; accord 
Jackson Transit Auth. v. Local Div. 1285, 457 U.S. 15, 21 n.6 
(1982). The “absence of a valid (as opposed to arguable) cause 
of action does not implicate subject-matter jurisdiction, i.e. , the 
court’s statutory or constitutional power to adjudicate the case.” 
Steel Co., 523 U.S. at 89 (emphasis in original). “Dismissal for 
lack of subject-matter jurisdiction because of the inadequacy of 
the federal claim is proper only when the claim is so 
insubstantial, implausible, foreclosed by prior decisions of this 
Court, or otherwise completely devoid of merit as not to 


involve a federal controversy.” /d. (internal quotations and 
citations omitted).* 


In sum, the district court plainly had jurisdiction over this 
case under section 1331, unless section 252(e)(6) somehow 
withdraws that jurisdiction. As we show next, no such 
withdrawal has occurred.

A. College Savings Reaffirms that States Voluntarily 
Waive Eleventh Amendment Immunity by 
Accepting a Federal Benefit Clearly Conditioned 
on Such Waiver. 


Eleventh Amendment immunity is waived by a state’s 
consent to suit. College Savings, 527 U.S. at 675-76. in 
College Savings, this Court clarified the conditions that must 
be satisfied to guarantee that such a waiver is “voluntary” and 
“unequivocal” when it is effected through the state’s 
acceptance of a federal benefit. /d. The Court held that 
Congress cannot make consent a precondition for a state to 
engage in an “otherwise permissible activity,” because 
exclusion of a state from such an activity is a “sanction” that 
“coerces” a state into consenting to suit. /d. at 687. Thus, “the 
voluntariness of waiver [is] destroyed — when what is attached 
to the refusal to waive is the exclusion of the state from 
otherwise lawful activity.” /d. For the same reason, engaging 
in “otherwise permissible activity” cannot be an unequivocal 
expression of a state’s intent to waive its immunity. /d. at 681- 
83 (overruling Parden v. Terminal Ry. Co. of Alabama State 
Docks Dep't, 377 U.S, 184 (1964)).2%

B. Congress Properly Conditioned State 
Participation in the Act on Waiver of a State’s 
Eleventh Amendment Immunity. 


The Seventh Circuit was correct that the invitation for 
states to participate in the implementation of the Act is the kind 
of benefit that Congress may condition on waiver of Eleventh 
Amendment immunity. Participation in the administration of 
the 1996 Act is an opportunity that Congress could choose to 


48 


grant or withhold at its pleasure, and the states are free to 
accept or decline the benefit of that participation. 


Under the Act, the substantive law applicable to 
interconnection between telecommunications carriers was 
substantially “federalized,” regardless of whether a state 
chooses to participate in the administration of those federal 
requirements. Congress, acting pursuant to its authority under 
the Commerce Clause to regulate entry into local telephone 
markets, see Hodel v. Virginia Surface Mining & Reclamation 
Ass'n, 452 U.S. 264, 290-91 (1981), “has taken the regulation 
of local telecommunications competition away from the states 
. .. [w]ith regard to the matters addressed by the 1996 Act,” 
AT&T Corp., 525 U.S. at 378 n.6. 


It follows that administration of the Act’s scheme is not an 
“otherwise permissible activity” in which a state could engage 
without Congress’ permission. Rather, that administration is a 
benefit conferred upon a state by Congress. As the Tenth 
Circuit reasoned: 


[W]ith the passage of the 1996 Act, Congress essentially 
transformed the regulation of local phone service from an 
otherwise permissible state activity into a federal gratuity. 
As in Petty and Dole, Congress was under no obligation to 
allow states to participate in the Act’s regulatory scheme. 
Accordingly, by conditioning a state’s ability to regulate 
local phone service on its consent to suit in federal court, 
Congress threatened the state with the denial of a gratuity 
rather than exclusion from an otherwise lawful activity. 


MCI Telecomms. Corp. v. Public Serv. Comm'n of Utah, 216 
F.3d 929, 938 (10th Cir. 2000), cert. denied, 121 S. Ct. 1167 
(2001); see MCI-II, 222 F.3d at 343-44 (“Congress could — and 


49 


did — take over regulation of a part of the telecommunications 
industry in accordance with its Commerce Power. Such 
regulation is no longer an ‘otherwise permissible activity’ for 
states”). 


For those reasons, assuming Congress was sufficiently 
clear about the fact that state participation was conditioned on 
a waiver, there is every reason to treat acceptance of that 
benefit as a valid waiver of Eleventh Amendment immunity.” 
With respect to commission decisions approving or rejecting 
interconnection agreements, Congress could not have been 
more clear. Section 252(e)(6) plainly authorizes review in 
federal court of such decisions, and section 252(e)(4) 
meanwhile precludes review in state court. As for enforcement 
activities, the condition may not be quite as clear, but for the 
reasons stated in Section I, supra, we submit that states still can 
fairly be said to be on notice that their enforcement actions will 
be reviewed in federal court if they choose to participate under 
the Act. 


To be sure, a state commission may want to participate in 
every determination under the Act, rather than leave any matter 
to the FCC, and may prefer not to waive immunity at the same 
time. But the same is true of any benefit or “gratuity” offered 
by Congress: states would prefer to have it, and often without 
the conditions Congress attaches. The desirability of the 
benefit, however, does not make Congress’ invitation coercive 
rather than voluntary. See South Dakota v. Dole, 483 U.S. 203,
