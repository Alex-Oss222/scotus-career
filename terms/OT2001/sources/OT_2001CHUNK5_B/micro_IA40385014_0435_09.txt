QUESTIONS PRESENTED 


1. Whether a state commission’s decision altering the 
terms of an interconnection agreement approved under section 
252 of the Telecommunications Act of 1996 is a 
“determination under [section 252]” and thus reviewable in 
federal court under section 252(e)(6) of the Act. 


2. Whether a state commission’s voluntary acceptance of 
Congress’s invitation under the 1996 Act to participate in 
implementing a federal regulatory program that provides that 
state commission determinations are reviewable in federal 
court constitutes a waiver of Eleventh Amendment immunity. 


3. Whether an action seeking prospective relief against 
state public utility commissioners in their official capacity for 
alleged ongoing violations of federal law in performing 
federal regulatory functions under the 1996 Act can be 
maintained under the Ex parte Young doctrine.

JURISDICTION 


The judgment of the court of appeals was entered on June 
18, 1999, and amended on August 19, 1999. Petitions for 
panel rehearing and rehearing en banc were denied on August 
9, 2000. Justice Stevens granted petitioners’ application to 
extend the date for filing their petition for a writ of certiorari 
to November 28, 2000. The petition was granted on March 5, 
2001. The jurisdiction of this Court rests on 28 U.S.C. 
§ 1254(1). Jurisdiction was proper in the district court under 
47 U.S.C. § 252(e)(6) and 28 U.S.C. §§ 1331 and 1337. 


STATEMENT 


This case will determine whether the federal courts have 
jurisdiction under section 252(e)(6) of the Telecommuni- 
cations Act of 1996 to review claims that state commissions 
have violated the procedural and substantive requirements of 
the Act by imposing new obligations on local exchange 
carriers under the guise of “interpreting” interconnection 
agreements previously approved by those commissions. The 
case arises from an Illinois commission determination that 
Ameritech is required by its interconnection agreements to 
pay tens of millions of dollars in reciprocal compensation to 
competing local carriers on Internet traffic. The intercon- 
nection agreements themselves limit Ameritech’s obligations 
to pay reciprocal compensation to the reciprocal 
compensation requirements of the Act, and the FCC has 
authoritatively determined that “ISP-bound traffic is not 
subject to the reciprocal compensation obligations of section 
251(b)(5).” ISP Remand Order, FCC 01-131 4 3 (Apr. 27, 
2001); accord id. FJ 23, 30. The Illinois Commerce 


2 


Commission nonetheless ruled that such payments are 
required under the Act and that Ameritech agreed to make 
them. 


The question is whether federal courts have jurisdiction to 
review this decision. The Seventh Circuit held that although 
the federal district court had jurisdiction over this case under 
section 252(e)(6) of the Act — because the commission 
decision was one “under” the Act — the federal court could 
not review Ameritech’s claim that the state commission 
unlawfully nullified the terms of its interconnection agree- 
ments, which the commission treated as raising an ordinary 
state law question of contractual interpretation. The effect of 
this holding is to strip federal courts of the power to review 
claims that state commissions have imposed obligations on 
local exchange carriers that are inconsistent with the binding 
terms of those agreements, contrary to the requirements of the 
Act. This raises serious dangers of inconsistent interpretations 
of the obligations of telephone service providers across the 50 
states, and of allowing state commissions to pursue regulatory 
policies in direct contravention of federal policy, without 
federal review. We urge this Court to hold — consistent with 
the language and structure of the Telecommunications Act — 
that all state commission determinations “under” section 252 
of the Act are reviewable in federal court to decide whether 
they are consistent with the congressional scheme. 


A. Federally Mandated Interconnection “Agreements” 
Under The Telecommunications Act Of 1996. The 1996 
Act establishes a “new federal regime” that “fundamentally 
restructures local telephone markets” and “profoundly affects 
a crucial segment of the economy worth tens of billions of 
dollars.” AT&T Corp. v. Iowa Utils. Bd., 525 U.S. 366, 371, 
379 n.6, 397 (1999) (emphasis in original). As Justice Scalia’s 
opinion for the Court explained, “the question” under the Act 
“is not whether the Federal Government has taken the 
regulation of local telecommunications competition away 


3 


from the States. With regard to the matters addressed by the 
1996 Act, it unquestionably has.” Id. at 379 n.6 (emphasis 
added). 


The key provisions by which Congress sought to open 
local telecommunications markets to competition are 47 
U.S.C. §§ 251 and 252. Those sections govern the formation 
and content of “interconnection agreements.” 
Notwithstanding their nomenclature, interconnection 
“agreements” are nothing like private commercial contracts. 
Private parties are free to determine with whom and on what 
terms they will deal; if they are unable to reach agreement 
after arms-length negotiations, they can simply walk away 
from the bargaining table without an agreement. Under the 
Act, however, Congress has compelled incumbent local 
exchange carriers (“LECs”) to enter into “agreements” that 
implement “a host of duties intended to facilitate market 
entry” by competing LECs. AT&T Corp., 525 U.S. at 371. See 
generally 47 U.S.C. § 251(b)-(c). 


If an incumbent LEC does not agree to include in its 
interconnection agreement what a competing carrier believes 
to be required by federal statute or regulation, that carrier has 
a right to ask a state commission (or the FCC, if the state 
commission fails to act) to compel the incumbent to include 
the disputed provision. Further, section 252(i) makes the 
terms and conditions in one interconnection agreement 
immediately available to any other requesting carrier, thereby 
setting an absolute floor for future agreements. In short, the 
Act sets forth a detailed framework for imposing a web of 
federal statutory and regulatory obligations on incumbent 
LECs. Section 252 interconnection “agreements” are not 
ordinary contracts but regulatory vehicles for implementing 
these Congressional commands. 


Because the Act’s default rules for local competition are 
explicitly set forth and easily invoked, incumbent LECs have 


4 


little incentive to give more, and competing LECs have little 
incentive to take less, than the Act requires. Accordingly, 
even “negotiated” terms in section 252 “agreements” often are 
incorporated without any give and take between the parties 
because federal law makes clear the rights of the competing 
LEC and the duties of the incumbent LEC. Although 
embodied in a “negotiated” agreement, the obligations are 
commonly framed in statutory terms and precisely track 
federal statutory requirements. See AT&T Communications of 
Southern States, Inc. v. BellSouth Telecomms., Inc., 229 F.3d 
457, 465 (4th Cir. 2000). The agreement “means” and 
“intends” nothing more nor less than to comply with the 
requirements of the Act. As explained below, the parties to the 
Agreements here said just that with respect to their federally 
mandated reciprocal compensation obligations. 


B. The Process For Entering Into, And Resolving 
Disputes Under, Section 252 Interconnection Agreements. 
Section 252 of the Act prescribes the process through which 
competing LECs arrive at agreements with incumbent LECs 
for the performance of duties imposed by section 251. A 
competing LEC initiates the section 252 negotiation process 
by requesting interconnection with an incumbent LEC. 47 
U.S.C. § 252(a). The incumbent then may “negotiate and 
enter into a binding agreement with the requesting 
telecommunications carrier or carriers,” without regard to the 
substantive requirements of the Act. /d. § 252(a)(1) (emphasis 
added). However, as noted above, the competing carrier has 
no incentive to accept less, or the incumbent to give more, 
than the statute’s default provisions prescribe. 


If the parties fail to reach full agreement through 
negotiation alone, the Act permits them to petition the state 
commission “to arbitrate any open issues.” 47 U.S.C. 
§ 252(b)(1). In conducting section 252 arbitrations, state 
regulators resolve open issues by “imposing appropriate 
conditions as required to implement [section 252(c)],” 


5 


provided that those conditions “meet the requirements of 
section 251” and the FCC’s rules implementing it. /d. 
§§ 252(b)(4(C), 252(c)(1); see also AT&T Corp., 525 U.S. at 
373 (noting that section 252 arbitrations are “subject to § 251 
and the FCC regulations promulgated thereunder”). 


After all issues have been resolved, through negotiation, 
arbitration, or both, the parties submit the agreement to the 
state commission for approval. 47 U.S.C. § 252(e). The state 
commission must then “approve or reject the agreement” (id. 
§ 252(e)(1)) in accordance with standards mandated by the 
Act. See id. §§ 252(e)(1), (2). Commission-approved 
agreements become “binding” regulatory mandates with 
which the parties must comply and which the commission has 
no authority to alter. Jd. § 252(a)(1). Further, because the 
terms of approved agreements become immediately available 
to all other requesting competitors who elect to adopt them 
(id. § 252(i)), the obligations in approved interconnection 
agreements not only define the parties’ duties to each other 
but may also determine the incumbent’s duties toward other 
carriers. 


The FCC and most state commissions have understood the 
Act to authorize state commissions to interpret and enforce

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


I. The initial question presented in this case is whether 
the federal courts have jurisdiction to review Ameritech’s 
claim that a state commission has nullified vital provisions in 
binding interconnection agreements that implement national 
telecommunications policy under the 1996 Act. Federal 
jurisdiction exists under both 28 U.S.C. § 1331 (see Brief for 
Petitioners in No. 00-1531) and section 252(e)(6) of the Act. 


Section 252(e)(6) provides in broad and inclusive terms 
that “[i]n any case in which a State commission makes a 
determination under this section, any party aggrieved by such 
determination may bring an action in an appropriate Federal 
district court to determine whether the agreement * * * meets 


12 


the requirements of section 251 of this title and this section.” 
(Emphasis added.) The FCC and numerous state commissions 
— including petitioners themselves in the Order giving rise to 
this case — have held that a state commission decision 
resolving the precise claim raised by Ameritech constitutes a 
“determination under [section 252].” Ameritech’s claim that 
petitioners violated the Act by abrogating binding terms of its 
Agreements squarely raises the question whether the 
Agreements, as modified by petitioners, “meet[] the 
requirements of” the Act. Ameritech’s claim thus fully 
satisfies the textual prerequisites for section 252(e)(6) 
jurisdiction. 

The structure and purpose of the Act confirm federal court 
jurisdiction over Ameritech’s claim. In section 252 Congress 
made clear its intent that regulatory determinations defining 
the rights and obligations in interconnection agreements 
conform to the procedural and substantive standards of the 
Act. Section 252(e)(6) provides the mechanism for ensuring 
that federal courts have authority to “draw the lines to which 
[state officials] must hew” and “bring * * * to heel” state 
officials who are “not regulating in accordance with federal 
policy.” AT&T Corp., 525 U.S. at 379 n.6. Whether a state 
commission imposes obligations on private carriers in the 
context of (i) arbitrating a disputed term before initial 
approval of an agreement or (ii) interpreting a disputed term 
in a previously approved agreement, the effect of its decision 
is the same. The federal interests in uniformity and 
consistency with federal law also are the same. Petitioners’ 
argument that the former category of cases should be resolved 
in federal court and the latter category in state court would 
substantially undermine the statutory scheme and impose 
adverse consequences that Congress could not have intended. 


Il. The second question before the Court is whether 
petitioners’ voluntary performance of federal regulatory 
functions under the Act constitutes a waiver of the state’s 


13 


Eleventh Amendment immunity. It is well established that 
Congress may solicit the states’ voluntary consent to private 
suits in federal court. The Act does precisely that. It invites 
state commissions to participate as “deputized federal 
regulator[s]” (MCI I], 222 F.3d at 344), subject to federal 
court review under section 252(e)(6). At the same time, 
however, sections 252(e)(5) and (e)(6) establish a parallel 
regulatory scheme in which the FCC performs those federal 
regulatory duties that a state declines to perform, again subject 
to federal review. 


Petitioners voluntarily accepted Congress’s offer to 
participate in this regulatory program. They recited 
specifically that they were proceeding under section 252 of 
the Act, a provision that expressly mandates federal court 
review. Nevertheless, petitioners now claim that their actions 
did not amount to a waiver of Eleventh Amendment 
immunity. Petitioners contend that Congress did not provide 
sufficient notice that federal court review was a condition of 
state participation. But section 252(e)(6) of the Act could 
hardly be clearer with respect to the consequences of making 
a “determination” defining obligations in section 252 
agreements. 


Petitioners also claim that they were unconstitutionally 
coerced by Congress into regulating under the Act. That 
argument cannot be squared with this Court’s precedents, 
holding that Congress is free to condition a grant of authority 
that a state would not otherwise possess on the state’s consent 
to suit in federal court. Petitioners do not dispute that 
Congress could have given the FCC all regulatory 
responsibilities under the 1996 Act, instead of giving the 
states the opportunity to perform those responsibilities if they 
so desired. Because Congress created the regulatory regime — 
established by the 1996 Act, it had the power to offer or 
withhold authority to act as a regulator in the implementation 
of the new federal statute. For that reason, petitioners’ 


14 


premise that their actions constitute “otherwise permissible 
activity” is mistaken. Under the Act, petitioners may 
unquestionably choose not to participate (as the Virginia 
commission repeatedly has done). However, if they elect to 
participate, they must do so according to Congress’s rules. 


III. The third question is whether petitioners are proper 
parties to this lawsuit under the Ex parte Young doctrine. As 
this Court consistently has held, that doctrine is essential to 
ensure that state officials do not use the Eleventh Amendment 
to avoid compliance with federal law. To determine whether 
an official capacity suit against a state officer may proceed, 
this Court has established a “straightforward inquiry” into 
whether the complaint alleges an ongoing violation of federal 
law and seeks prospective declaratory or injunctive relief. 


Although they concede the continued vitality of Ex parte 
Young, petitioners contend that the doctrine does not apply in 
this case for two reasons. First, relying on Larson v. Domestic 
& Foreign Commerce Corp., 337 U.S. 682 (1949), petitioners 
claim that their “discretionary” acts cannot violate federal 
law. However, this Court’s decisions consistently hold that 
state officers have no “discretion” to violate federal law and 
can be enjoined if they do so. Second, relying on Seminole 
Tribe v. Florida, 517 U.S. 44 (1996), petitioners assert that 
the remedies available under Ex parte Young are inconsistent 
with the Act’s remedial scheme. Petitioners’ argument turns 
Seminole Tribe on its head. In that case the Court refused to 
apply Ex parte Young because Congress had imposed severe 
restrictions on the remedies that the federal courts would 
otherwise have had at their disposal (including Ex parte 
Young relief). Here, Congress has imposed no such 
limitations. Congress has provided for federal court review to 
ensure the very compliance with federal law that Ex parte 
Young traditionally has guaranteed.

B. The structure and purpose of the Act confirm 
federal jurisdiction here. Contrary to petitioners’ contention 
(Pet. Br. 20), this Court has consistently stressed that “[i}n 
determining the meaning of the statute, we look not only to 
the particular statutory language, but to the design of the

1. The design of the statute as a whole. As this Court has 
noted, the Act’s division of authority between state and 
federal bodies is unusual. See AT&T Corp., 525 U.S. at 379 
n.6 (“a federal program administered by 50 independent state 
agencies is surpassing strange”). In deciding to allow state 
regulators to perform a central role in implementing federal 
policy, Congress used the mechanism of federal court review 
to ensure that the state agencies comply with federal law. 
State regulators are not “allowed to do their own thing.” /bid. 
They must adhere to the federal rules of the game. Section 
252(e)(6) serves the critical function of empowering federal 
courts to “draw the lines to which [state officials] must hew” 
and to “bring * * * to heel” state officials who are “not 
regulating in accordance with federal policy.” Jbid. This 
consideration applies to a// state commission determinations 
under section 252 — not just to initial “approvals.” 


State commission decisions to approve or reject 
interconnection agreements are indisputably subject to federal 
court review. Federal review is not less important when state 
commissions exercise federal authority to interpret and 
enforce obligations carriers have assumed under the Act. This 
case provides a textbook example. When the Agreements in 
question were negotiated, Internet use was in its infancy, and 
the parties did not specifically address how Internet traffic 
would be treated for reciprocal compensation purposes. If 
they had done so and the present dispute had arisen at that 
time, the matter would have been resolved by arbitration, and 
either party could have taken the dispute to federal court for 
an authoritative ruling. Instead, however, the parties 
negotiated and the Commission approved agreements

providing that reciprocal compensation would be made only

applicability of reciprocai compensation to Internet traffic, the 
matter was taken to the state commission, which assumed 
jurisdiction “under Section 252.” At this juncture, the 
question is the same, the federal interest is the same, and the 
private interests are the same, as they would have been in the 
context of an initial approval. Federal review serves the same 
purpose of ensuring conformity of state commission decisions 
to federal policy. 


In sum, decisions determining the substance of a carrier’s 
obligations in the context of “interpretation and enforcement” 
of an agreement are functionally identical —— in terms of the 
federal interest — to decisions determining the substance of 
a carrier’s obligation in the context of “approval” of an 
agreement. The only difference is one of timing. It would 
have made no sense for Congress to have excluded such a 
large and important class of cases — involving state 
commission determinations issued under the authority of 
federal law to implement federal law obligations under 
federally mandated agreements — from the Act’s judicial 
review scheme. Nowhere in the statute did Congress authorize 
state commissions to implement federal policy without federal 
review. There is no reason to bypass federal review here.

3. The federal nature of interconnection agreements. 
There is no merit to petitioners’ suggestion (Br. 24-26) that 
disputes over whether a state commission has nullified the 
terms of a binding section 252 interconnection agreement 
raise only issues of state contract law. As noted above, section 
252 agreements are not ordinary private contracts; they are 
federal regulatory instruments that require incumbent carriers 
to cooperate with their competitors and share facilities in 
ways that are dictated by the requirements of the 1996 Act 
and the FCC’s implementing regulations. 


It is an understatement to say that, in “negotiating” the 
terms of “agreements,” the parties bargain in the shadow of 
federal law. The Act imposes “a host of duties” on incumbent 
LECs (AT&T Corp., 525 U.S. at 371) and correspondingly 
confers on competing LECs substantive federal rights to 
obtain access to private property at cost-based rates. If an 
incumbent LEC does not “agree” to accept what a competing 
carrier believes to be statutory or regulatory requirements, that 
carrier has a right to ask a state commission or the FCC to 
compel the-incumbent to include those purported 
requirements in the “agreement.” 

An approved agreement thus is a carrier-specific federal 
regulation that implements federal law and policy. “[MJany 
so-called ‘negotiated’ provisions represent nothing more than 


28 


an attempt to comply with the requirements of the 1996 Act.” 
AT&T Communications v. BellSouth Telecomms., Inc., 229 
F.3d 457, 465 (4th Cir. 2000). In fact, carriers may adopt 
terms that simply incorporate the requirements of the Act and 
its implementing regulations — in which case a dispute over 
the obligations imposed under the agreement is essentially a 
dispute over the meaning of the Act. 


Not surprisingly, in serving as deputized federal regulators 
that interpret and enforce federal interconnection agreements, 
state commissioners are regularly called upon to make 
determinations on important issues of federal law. Here, for 
example, not only did the parties draft critical provisions of 
their Agreements (including those at issue in this dispute) to 
track the requirements of federal law, but petitioners overtly 
ruled on the scope of federal law in reaching their decision. 
J.A. 114a-116a. And that ruling — that federal law requires 
the payment of reciprocal compensation on Internet traffic — 
is at odds with the FCC’s recognition that Internet traffic is 
“not subject to the reciprocal compensation requirement in 
section 251(b).” JSP Remand Order ¥ 23. 


Moreover, section 252(i) makes the terms and conditions 
of any interconnection agreement immediately available to 
any other requesting carrier, just like the terms and conditions 
of a federally-approved tariff. That section severely restricts 
an incumbent LEC’s ability to engage in ordinary “give-and- 
take,” because “every concession * * * made (in exchange for 
some other benefit) * * * will automatically become available 
to every potential entrant.” AT&T Corp., 525 U.S. at 395. As 
a consequence of section 252(i), any regulatory determination 
by a state commission that abrogates the terms of a section 
252 “agreement” or deviates from federal law has industry- 
wide effect. The notion that such a determination simply gives 
rise to a state law contract action is untenable.
