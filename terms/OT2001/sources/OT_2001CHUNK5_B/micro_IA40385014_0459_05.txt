STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


As required by the Telecommunications Act of 1996 
(“1996 Act”), petitioner Verizon Maryland Inc. 
(“Verizon”) entered into an “interconnection agree- 
ment” with respondent MFS Intelenet of Maryland, 
Inc. (“MFS”), a competitive local exchange carrier 
(“CLEC”). The interconnection agreement provided 
that “reciprocal compensation” — the amount that one 
interconnecting carrier pays another for traffic that 
originates on one network and terminates on the other 
— would be due only to the extent required by the 1996 
Act. The Federal Communications Commission (“FCC”) 
ruled that the 1996 Act does not require reciprocal 
compensation for Internet-bound traffic. The Maryland 
Public Service Commission (“PSC”) nevertheless inter- 
preted the interconnection agreement to require Veri- 
zon to pay MFS reciprocal compensation for Internet- 
bound traffic, a ruling that forced Verizon to transfer to 
MFS and other competitors many millions of dollars 
that it had never agreed to pay and that federal law did 


3 


not require it to pay. Verizon challenged the PSC’s ; 
decision in federal district court, basing jurisdiction, in 
part, on 28 U.S.C. § 1331. 


The question presented by this case is whether a 
federal court may exercise § 1331 “arising under” 
jurisdiction in an action alleging that a state commis- 
sion has interpreted or enforced a 1996 Act intercon- 
nection agreement in violation of federal law. 


1. Interconnection Agreements and the 1996 
Act. The 1996 Act “fundamentally restructures local 
telephone markets” by subjecting incumbent local 
telephone companies to “a host of duties intended to 
facilitate market entry.” AT&T Corp. v. lowa Utils. 
Bd., 525 U.S. 366, 371 (1999). The documents detail- 
ing incumbents’ duties, though referred to as “intercon- 
nection agreements,” are not voluntary contracts in 
any ordinary sense. An incumbent local exchange 
carrier (“LEC”) has no choice about whether to enter 
into such an “agreement” when a CLEC makes a 
request. On the contrary, the 1996 Act requires 
incumbents, upon a CLEC’s request, “to negotiate in 
good faith .. . the particular terms and conditions of 
agreements to fulfill” the 1996 Act’s substantive duties. 
47 U.S.C. § 251(c)(1); see also id. § 252(a)(1). 


Although carriers are permitted to “negotiate and 
enter into a binding agreement .. . without regard 
to the standards set forth” in the 1996 Act (id. 
§ 252(a)(1)), “many so-called ‘negotiated’ provisions 
represent nothing more than an attempt to comply with 
the requirements of the 1996 Act” (AT&T Communica- 
tions of Southern States, Inc. v. BellSouth Telecomms., 
Inc., 229 F.3d 457, 465 (4th Cir. 2000)). That is so 
because the Act, as interpreted by the FCC, effectively 
discourages attempts to negotiate terms that provide 
either less or more than the Act requires. 


First, parties negotiate against the backdrop of 
statutory and regulatory requirements that dictate the 


4 


minimum terms to which an incumbent carrier will be 
held in the absence of a negotiated agreement. Section 
252(b) provides that either party to a negotiation may 
“petition the state commission . . . to arbitrate open 
issues, which arbitration is subject to § 251 and the 
FCC regulations promulgated thereunder.” AT&T, 525 
U.S. at 373; see 47 U.S.C. § 252(b), (c). Accordingly, so 
long as an issue has already been “resolved by . . . FCC 
Rules and interpretations,” a CLEC has little motiva- 
tion to accept anything less than the federal require- 
ment. AT&T Communications, 229 F.3d at 465. 


Second, an incumbent is discouraged from agreeing 
to anything that exceeds its obligations under federal 
law because “any interconnection, service, or network 
element provided under an agreement approved under” 
§ 252 must be made available to “any other requesting 
telecommunications carrier upon the same terms and 
conditions.” 47 U.S.C. § 252(i). As a consequence, an 
incumbent cannot safely engage in ordinary “give-and- 
take” because, as the FCC has interpreted § 252(i), 
“every concession . . . made (in exchange for some other 
benefit) by an incumbent LEC will automatically be- 
come available to every potential entrant.” AT&T, 525 
U.S. at 395. Together, therefore, §§ 252(b) and 252(i) 
impel the parties to match the terms of their intercon- 
nection agreement to the commands of federal law. 


Whether an agreement is arrived at by negotiation or 
arbitration, the parties must submit the agreement to 
the appropriate state public utility commission for 
approval and public filing. See 47 U.S.C. § 252(a)(1), 
(e)(1). Once an agreement has been approved, federal 
law requires the parties to abide by its terms. Section 
252(a)(1) explicitly provides that interconnection 
agreements are “binding.” Jd. § 252(a)(1). Likewise, 
several provisions in § 251 refer to the obligation of 
incumbent carriers to provide network access and 
interconnection “in accordance with the terms and 


5 


_ conditions of the agreement.” See id. § 251(c)(2)(D), 


(c)(3). In short, the obligations imposed by an intercon- 
nection agreement are not private contractual obliga- 
tions, but federal regulatory obligations that apply 
equally to all similarly situated telecommunications 
carriers. 


2. Reciprocal Compensation and the Internet. 
Section 251(b)(5) requires LECs to enter into “recipro- 
cal compensation arrangements for the transport and 
termination of telecommunications.” Id. § 251(b)(5). 
Under “reciprocal compensation,” when a customer of 
Carrier A places a call to a customer of Carrier E ‘n the 
same local calling area, Carrier A pays Carrier B for 
terminating that local call. Likewise, when a customer 
of Carrier B calls a customer of Carrier A, Carrier B 
pays Carrier A for terminating the call. 


The FCC has consistently held that § 251(b)(5)’s 
reciprocal-compensation obligation applies only to 
intra-exchange telecommunications traffic (that is, 
calls that originate and terminate within the same 
local calling area). For non-local or “interexchange” 
calls, a different set of rules applies (those governing 
long-distance “access charges”).” 


The application of this rule to traditional local voice 
telephone calls has been relatively straightforward, but 
the question whether reciprocal compensation applies 
to Internet-bound calls has given rise to disputes. A 
customer typically initiates an Internet session by

The ISP then completes the communication link by

CLECs have typically argued that, because many ISP 
access numbers are “local” — that is, associated with 
the subscriber’s local calling area — Internet-bound 
calls are subject to reciprocal compensation under 
§ 251(b)(5). Incumbents, by contrast, have typically 
maintained that Internet-bound calis are interstate 
and interexchange in nature, because such calls do not 
terminate at the ISP’s modem but continue in an 
unbroken transmission to distant websites. Before the 
FCC definitively resolved this issue, a number of state 
commissions had ruled that Internet-bound calls are 
actually two calls — a local call to the ISP and a 
subsequent communication between the ISP and the 
distant website — and that the initial call to the ISP is 
local in nature and therefore subject to reciprocal 
compensation. 


The FCC has made clear that those rulings were 
incorrect. It has repeatedly ruled that an Internet- 
bound call is a single end-to-end communication 
between the customer and the distant website, a call 
that is therefore interstate and interexchange. See /SP 
Remand Order 77 58-60. The agency has specifically 
ruled that, because Internet-bound traffic is interstate 
access traffic, it is “not subject to the reciprocal com- 
pensation requirement in section 251(b)[(5)].” Id. { 23; 
accord id. { 30. 


The FCC also explained in detail that the decisions of 
various state commissions requiring payment of recip- 
rocal compensation for Internet-bound traffic have 
directly undermined the goals of the 1996 Act. Such 
rulings have “led to classic regulatory arbitrage,” were

a “windfall” for CLECs, and “distort[ed) the develop- 
ment of competitive markets.” Jd. {4 21, 29, 70. Such

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


Section 1331 grants federal district courts “original 
jurisdiction of all civil actions arising under the Consti- 
tution, laws, or treaties of the United States.” A civil 
action “arises under” federal law for purposes of § 1331 
when “a right or immunity created by the Constitution 
or laws of the United States [is] an element, and an 
essential one, of the plaintiff's cause of action.” Gully 
v. First Nat’l Bank, 299 U.S. 109, 112 (1936); accord 
City of Chicago v. International College of Surgeons, 
522 U.S. 156, 163-64 (1997). Verizon’s claim below — 
that the PSC’s determination violated the federal 
Telecommunications Act of 1996 — plainly satisfies 
that test. Moreover, neither the 1996 Act nor this 
Court’s Rooker-Feldman doctrine divests a federal 
district court of jurisdiction over a case like this one. 


12 


I. An action seeking review of a state commission’s 
interpretation of a 1996 Act interconnection agreement 
arises under federal law. 


A. In the 1996 Act, Congress granted incumbent 
carriers the statutory right to “negotiate and enter into 
a binding agreement with the requesting telecommuni- 
cations carrier or carriers,” and to do so “without 
regard to the standards set forth in [§ 251(b) and (c)].” 
47 U.S.C. § 252(a)(1) (emphasis added). Federal law 
thus gives Verizon the right to insist that it be held 
only to the terms of the interconnection agreement to 
which it actually agreed. The statutory right granted 
under § 252(a) is accordingly “an element, and an 
essential one,” of Verizon’s claim. 


B. Any claim relating to the rights and obligations 
imposed under a 1996 Act interconnection agreement 
raises questions of federal law. Contrary to the court 
of appeals’ apparent understanding, interconnection 
agreements are not ordinary state-law contracts, but 
rather “federal contractis],” the interpretation or 
enforcement of which, under this Court’s precedents, 
raises questions of federal law subject to federal court 
jurisdiction under § 1331. International Ass’n of 
Machinists v. Central Airlines, Inc., 372 U.S. 682, 692 
(1963). Like the Railway Labor Act contracts at issue 
in Central Airlines, interconnection agreements “are 
creations of federal law and bound to the statute and 
its policy”; they are “therefore governed and enforce- 
able by federal law, in the federal courts.” Id. 


That conclusion is fortified by a separate line of this 
Court’s decisions. Because federal law mandates the 
creation of interconnection agreements, stipulates that 
they include a detailed schedule of charges, directs that 
they be published, requires a carrier to provide service 
according to their terms, and specifies that service 
must be made available on the same terms to all 
parties, these 1996 Act agreements are functionally

indistinguishable from federal tariffs. Insofar as inter- 
connection agreements possess the core characteristics 
of tariffs, this Court’s precedents strongly suggest that 
a claim involving enforcement of such an agreement, 
like a claim involving enforcement of a federal tariff, 
necessarily arises under federal law and presents 
questions reviewable in federal court. Thurston Motor 
Lines, Inc. v. Jordan K. Rand, Ltd., 460 U.S. 533, 534 
(1983) (per curiam); Louisville & N.R.R. v. Rice, 247 
U.S. 201, 202 (1918). 


II. No other law barred the district court from 
exercising the jurisdiction granted to it under § 1331. 


A. Nothing in the 1996 Act suggests that Congress 
intended to restrict the jurisdiction of the federal 
courts in cases like this one. The 1996 Act specifically 
confers jurisdiction on federal courts under § 252(e)(6) 
and withdraws jurisdiction from state courts under 
§ 252(e)(4). To the extent those sections do not reach 
this case, the statute is silent on the proper method for 
review. This Court has held that “the silence of Con- 
gress as to judicial review is . . . not to be construed as 
a denial of authority to the aggrieved person to seek 
appropriate relief in the federal courts in the exercise 
of their general jurisdiction.” Stark v. Wickard, 321 
U.S. 288, 309 (1944). This is true even if the statute in 
question specifically provides for judicial review in 
other circumstances. Id.; see also Reno v. Catholic 
Social Services, Inc., 509 U.S. 43, 56 (1993); McNary v. 
Haitian Refugee Center, Inc., 498 U.S. 479, 494 (1991). 


B. The Rooker-Feldman doctrine does not divest the 
federal court of its federal-question jurisdiction in a 
case like this one. That doctrine “interprets 28 U.S.C. 
§ 1257 as ordinarily barring direct review in the lower 
federal courts of a decision reached by the highest state 
court, for such authority is vested solely in this Court.” 
ASARCO Inc. v. Kadish, 490 U.S. 605, 622 (1989). 
The doctrine has no application to judicial review of 


14 


determinations by a state administrative agency. As 
this Court held in City of Chicago, “federal! jurisdiction 
generally encompasses judicial review of state adminis- 
trative decisions.” 522 U.S. at 169. 


That holding applies with full force here. First, the 
PSC was exercising authority granted to it under 
federal law, not merely under state law. Just as 
federal courts have jurisdiction to review action by 
federal agencies, they likewise have jurisdiction to 
review action by state agencies exercising federal 
statutory authority. Second, unlike the situation in 
City of Chicago, all of Verizon’s claims arise under 
federal law. Thus, there is no issue here concerning 
litigation of state-law issues in federal court. For the 
same reason, the concerns that animate abstention 
doctrine have no application to this case. 


ARGUMENT 


At issue in this case is whether a federal district 
court has jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1331 to deter- 
mine whether a state public utility commission has 
interpreted a 1996 Act interconnection agreement in a 
manner consistent with federal law. This Court has 
already suggested the answer to that question, having 
observed that “there is no doubt . . . that if the federal 
courts believe a state commission is not regulating in 
accordance with federal policy they may bring it to 
heel.” AT&T, 525 U.S. at 378 n.6. 


That principle applies here: it is a core function of the 
federal courts to ensure that federal administrative 
authority is exercised in accordance with governing 
federal law. “From the beginning ‘our cases [have 
established] that judicial review of a final agency 
action by an aggrieved person will not be cut off unless 
there is persuasive reason to believe that such was the 
purpose of Congress.” Bowen v. Michigan Academy of 
Family Physicians, 476 U.S. 667, 670 (1986) (quoting 


15 


Abbott Labs. v. Gardner, 387 U.S. 136, 140 (1967)) 
(alteration in original). Such review is fundamental to 
the constitutional legitimacy of the delegation of power 
by Congress. “The responsibility of determining the 
limits of statutory grants of authority . . . is a judicial 
function entrusted to the courts by Congress by the 
statutes establishing courts and marking their juris- 
diction.” Stark, 321 U.S. at 310. “{[U]nder Article III, 
Congress established courts to adjudicate cases and 
controversies as to claims of infringement of individual 
rights whether by unlawful action of private persons or 
by the exertion of unauthorized administrative power.” 
Id. 


It is of no consequence that the PSC is a state rather 
than a federal agency. See City of Chicago, 522 U.S. at 
169. In resolving the dispute between the private 
parties before it concerning the scope of Verizon’s 
duties under the interconnection agreements at issue, 
the PSC was exercising federal authority under a 
federal statute that sets forth Congress’s scheme for 
regulation of local telecommunications markets. For 
that reason, if the PSC had declined to exercise that 
authority, as it was free to do, the FCC would have had 
to step in and “assume the responsibility of the State 
commission .. . with respect to the proceeding or 
matter.” 47 U.S.C. § 252(e)(5).’ In the PSC’s view,

I. VERIZON’S CLAIM ARISES UNDER FEDERAL 


LAW 
“For purposes of determining whether jurisdiction 
exists under § 1331(a)..., it is not necessary to decide 


whether [Verizon’s] alleged cause of action . . . is in fact 
a cause of action ‘on which [Verizon] could actually 
recover.” Duke Power Co. v. Carolina Envtl. Study 
Group, Inc., 438 U.S. 59, 70 (1978) (quoting Bell v. 
Hood, 327 U.S. 678, 682 (1946)). Rather, the test is 
whether the claimed right is “so insubstantial, implau- 
sible, foreclosed by prior decisions of this Court, or 
otherwise completely devoid of merit as not to involve 
a federal controversy.” Oneida Indian Nation v. County 
of Oneida, 414 U.S. 661, 666 (1974). When a plaintiff 
claims that “federally protected rights have been 
invaded,” the “allegations are sufficient to sustain 
jurisdiction under § 1331(a).” Duke Power, 438 U.S. at 
71 (quoting Bell, 327 U.S. at 684). 


Verizon’s claim rests on at least two sources of 
federal rights that the PSC’s determination directly 
invaded. The first is the statutory right to enter into a 
“binding agreement” without regard to requirements 
that would otherwise be imposed under federal law. 
47 U.S.C. § 252(a)(1). The second is the intercon- 
nection agreements themselves; the rights and obliga- 
tions that arise thereunder are intrinsically federal 
in nature. Either source of rights establishes an 
independent and sufficient basis for federal court 
jurisdiction. 


17 


A. A Federal District Court Has Jurisdiction 
To Determine Whether the PSC’s Interpreta- 
tion Violates Verizon’s Federally Protected 
Right To Enter into “Binding Agreement/(s)” 


Verizon’s fundamental claim is that, in requiring 
Verizon to pay reciprocal compensation on Internet- 
bound traffic, the PSC acted contrary to the terms of 
the governing interconnection agreement, when read in 
light of controlling federal law. If that claim is correct 
— and it must be accepted as correct for purposes of 
determining the district court’s jurisdiction — the 
PSC’s determination violated § 252(a)(1), which pro- 
vides that incumbent LECs may “negotiate and enter 
into a binding agreement .. . without regard to the 
standards set forth in [§ 251(b) and (c)].” 47 U.S.C. 
§ 252(a)(1). Because federal law dictates that intercon- 
nection agreements, duly negotiated, approved, and 
filed in accordance with the requirements of § 252, are 
binding, a state commission is not permitted to impose 
obligations inconsistent with their terms. 


The statutory right at issue is central to the congres- 
sional scheme. The obligations imposed on incumbent 
LECs under the 1996 Act represent an unprecedented 
intrusion on their right to control their own property. 
See Goldwasser v. Ameritech Corp., 222 F.3d 390, 399- 
400 (7th Cir. 2000) (noting that the 1996 Act imposes 
“a host” of “affirmative duties to help one’s competi- 
tors”). To reduce somewhat the intrusion that this 
regulation represents, Congress permits incumbents, 
in the first instance, to establish the terms of agree- 
ments free from direct regulatory interference. See 
Iowa Utils. Bd. v. FCC, 120 F.3d 753, 793 n.9 (8th Cir. 
1997) (“an incumbent LEC is not bound by the Act’s 
substantive standards in conducting voluntary negoti- 
ations”), affd in part, rev'd in part sub nom. AT&T 
Corp. v. Iowa Utils. Bd., 525 U.S. 366 (1999). That 


18 


relative freedom from the direct imposition of regula- 
tory duties in circumstances where the parties have 
agreed to terms implementing the statute would be lost 
if a state commission were free to impose obligations 
notwithstanding the contrary terms of an interconnec- 
tion agreement. It is, in part, the commission’s lack of 
power to impose such obligations that gives meaning to 
§ 252(a)(1). 


For example, a state commission might regard an 
agreement to limit reciprocal compensation payments 
to those calls that are subject to reciprocal compensa- 
tion under the 1996 Act as unwise for policy reasons. 
It might be “concerned” that such an agreement would 
“result in CLECs receiving no compensation for termi- 
nating (Internet]-bound traffic.” Pet. App. 106a. It 
might believe that CLECs “incur costs to terminate” 
such traffic and that, absent reciprocal compensation, 
“CLECs will be forced to absorb these costs.” Id. at 
106a-107a. And it might, for this reason, labor to find 
a way around the plain language of an interconnection 
agreement, thereby rendering the parties’ agreement 
non-binding and denying the incumbent its rights 
under § 252(a). 


A state commission need not deliberately misconstrue 
an interconnection agreement to deny a party its rights 
under § 252(a). Whenever a state commission misinter- 
prets an interconnection agreement the injury to the 
rights of the aggrieved party is the same. In judging 
whether a state commission’s interpretation of an 
agreement is wrong, the district court’s scope of re- 
view is a familiar one. See, e.g., Shields v. Utah Idaho 
Cent. R.R., 305 U.S. 177, 185 (1938) (the question is 
“whether the [agency] in arriving at its determination 
departed from the applicable rules of law”). If the state 
commission’s determination cannot withstand such 
review, it is inconsistent with § 252(a).

The circumstances of this case demonstrate the 
importance of that principle. The parties’ agreement 
here provides that “Reciprocal Compensation” is “As 
Described in the Act,” a defined term that means “as 
described in or required by the [1996] Act and as from 
time to time interpreted in the duly authorized rules 
and regulations of the FCC or the [PSC].” Interconnec- 
tion Agreement §§ 1.7, 1.61 (see C.A. App. 49, 55; Pet. 
App. 10la). The agreement further provides that 
reciprocal compensation is limited to “Local Traffic,” 
defined as traffic that “is originated” and “terminates” 
within the same local calling area. Jd. § 1.44 (see C.A. 
App. 53; Pet. App. 101a). 


Verizon’s claim is that the parties’ agreement means 
what it says: reciprocal compensation is “as described 
in or required by the [1996] Act.” And Verizon further 
claims that, as a matter of federal law, payment of 
compensation for Internet-bound traffic is not recipro- 
cal compensation “as described in or required by the 
[1996] Act.” The FCC has recently confirmed that 
understanding of federal law. See ISP Remand Order 
q¥ 23 (holding that the 1996 Act does not require 
payment of reciprocal compensation for Internet-bound 
traffic); cf. Interconnection Agreement § 1.7 (recogniz- 
ing binding effect of “duly authorized rules and regula- 
tions of the FCC”) (see C.A. App. 49). 


The agreement’s express incorporation of the federal 
statutory standard governing reciprocal compensation 
accentuates the federal interest in this case. As noted 
above, parties often adopt provisions that “fulfill the 
duties described in [§ 251(b) and (c)]” (47 U.S.C. 
§ 251(c)(1)) by tracking the requirements imposed by 
those statutory provisions and the FCC’s implementing 
regulations. See AT&T Communications, 229 F.3d at 
465). A state commission’s construction of such provi- 
sions is therefore tantamount to an interpretation of 
the 1996 Act and the FCC’s regulations. Recognizing 
that ruch determinations involve matters of federal law

prevents state commissions from disturbing the bal- 
ance of interests struck by Congress and promotes 
uniformity in the application of federal law. Indeed, 
the identical dispute over the requirements of federal 
law may arise either before or after an agreement is 
approved. In both circumstances, judicial review for 
consistency with federal law ensures that Congress’s 
legislative choices will be respected. 


B. Any Claim Based on a 1996 Act Interconnec- 
tion Agreement Arises Under Federal Law 


Even if § 252(a) did not grant Verizon an express 
statutory right to be held only to those obligations 
contained in its interconnection agreement, there is no 
question that Verizon’s claims here depend on rights 
and obligations derived from the parties’ interconnec- 
tion agreement. In a regulatory scheme like the 1996 
Act, where the governing document implements under- 
lying federal statutory duties, the rights and duties 
imposed by those documents are questions of federal 
law. Two related lines of authority make that conclu- 
sion inescapable. 


1. Disputes concerning 1996 Act interconnection 
agreements satisfy the “arising under” requirement of 
§ 1331 because such documents are federal contracts 
that implement federal policy and embody federal 
standards. Unlike ordinary commercial contracts, 
which reflect voluntary undertakings by willing parties 
acting without legal compulsion, 1996 Act interconnec- 
tion agreements are the product of federal legal coer- 
cion designed to implement federal obligations. Incum- 
bent LECs such as Verizon are required by federal law 
to negotiate these agreements to discharge their 
obligations under the 1996 Act — specifically, “to fulfill 
the duties described in [§ 251(b) and (c)].” 47 U.S.C. 
§ 251(c)(1). Federal law, moreover, requires incum- 
bents to enter into good-faith negotiations at the 
behest of a requesting carrier even if they would 


21 


otherwise be unwilling to do so — indeed, even if state 
law would otherwise prohibit such inter-carrier negoti- 
ations and agreements. See id. § 261(c). 


Further, federal law enumerates the specific duties 
that are subject to negotiation, establishes the mini- 
mum terms to which an incumbent must “agree” on 
request, and subjects the resulting agreements to 
governmental approval under specified federal stan- 
dards, without which the agreement has no effect. 
See id. §§ 251(b)-(c), 252(e). Federal law further re- 
quires that these agreements be publicly filed, and it 
provides that the incumbent carrier must make the 
provisions of any approved interconnection agreement 
available to any other requesting carrier on the same 
terms and conditions. See id. § 252(h), (i). Finally, 
once such an agreement is approved, the incumbent 
has a federal-law duty to provide service “in accordance 
with the terms and conditions of the agreement.” /d. 
§ 251(c)(2)(D), (e)(3). 


One cannct fairly describe such agreements as a 
genuine product of the parties’ voluntary consent. On 
the contrary, they reflect the positive command of 
federal law. In closely analogous circumstances, this 
Court held that a dispute over the construction of a 
labor-management agreement entered into pursuant to 
§ 204 of the Railway Labor Act raised a federal ques- 
tion for purposes of § 1331. /nternational Ass'n of 
Machinists v. Central Airlines, Inc., 372 U.S. at 695- 
96. The reasoning in that case compels the same con- 
clusion here. 


Under § 204, airlines and their employees were 
required to establish a board of adjustment to settle 
employee grievances. Jd. at 686. A contract between 
an airline and a union established such a board, and 
the board ruled against the airline in a grievance 
proceeding. When the airline refused to comply, the 
union brought a federal action to enforce its award. 


22 


The court of appeals, affirming an order dismissing the 
case for lack of jurisdiction, held that the complaint 
“did not disclose ‘affirmatively a federally-created

American Surety Co. v. Schultz, 237 U.S. 159 (1915)

Airlines, it is federal law — specifically, §§ 251 and 252

are Congress’s chosen means for implementing the

federal statute is the source of the power and authority

II. NO OTHER PROVISION OF LAW DEPRIVES 
FEDERAL COURTS OF JURISDICTION OVER 
CLAIMS LIKE VERIZON’S 


Because Verizon’s claims arise under federal law, the 
requirement for district court jurisdiction under 28 
U.S.C. § 1331 is satisfied. The sole remaining ques- 
tion, therefore, is whether any other provision of law 
withdraws that federal jurisdiction. 


The Fourth Circuit invoked two possible statutory 
limitations. First, the court held that § 252(e)(6) does 
not apply to state commission decisions interpreting or 
enforcing interconnection agreements. It then inferred 
that, because “Congress created a limited federal right 
of review of State commission determinations as de- 
fined in 47 U.S.C. § 252(e)(6),” it must have “intended 
for the right of review to be exercised in State courts” 
whenever § 252(e)(6) does not apply. Pet. App. 49a. 
Second, the Fourth Circuit believed that, “in the 
absence of specific federal authorization,” it would 
“violate basic tenets of federalism” to permit a federal

court to “review a State quasi-judicial body.” Id. at 
50a." 


For the reasons set forth below, neither of these 
sources of law bars a district court’s exercise of subject 
matter jurisdiction in this case.

United States [was] an element, and an essential one, 
of the plaintiff's cause of action.” Jd. at 164 (quoting 
Gully, 299 U.S. at 112). Second, the Court held that 
the district court had supplemental jurisdiction over 
any claims that arose under state law (id. at 165-66) — 
for example, the state-law claim that the Chicago 
Landmarks Commission had misapplied its ordinances. 


The Court rejected the argument that the federal 
court was deprived of jurisdiction because an action for 
on-the-record review was not a “civil action[]” within 
the “original jurisdiction” of the district court for 
purposes of the removal statute: it held that, even if its 
“state claims” were not civil actions, its federal claims 
were. Just as important for present purposes, the 
Court made clear that an action to secure “judicial 
review of state administrative decisions” is “generally 
encompasse(d]” within federal subject matter jurisdic- 
tion under § 1331. Jd. at 169. As the Court noted, 
“district courts routinely conduct deferential review 
pursuant to their original jurisdiction over federal 
questions, including on-the-record review of federal 
administrative action.” Id. at 171. See also Shields, 


37 


305 U.S. at 184 (“respondent was entitled to resort to 
equity in order to obtain a judicial review of the ques- 
tions of the validity and effect of the [Interstate Com- 
merce] Commission’s determination”). 


City of Chicago makes clear that Rooker-Feldr in is 
inapplicable to the circumstances presented here. That 
doctrine applies to situations where a state court 
renders a final judgment; in such circumstances, 
Congress intends that the sole avenue into federal 
court should be a petition for certiorari invoking the 
explicit authority of the Supreme Court to review the 
judgments of “the highest court of a State in which a 
decision could be had.” 28 U.S.C. § 1257(a). But § 1257 
makes no mention of administrative agencies, and City 
of Chicago itself involved administrative determina- 
tions made pursuant to established standards; both the 
determinations and the standards were said to violate 
the federal constitution. Whether or not adminis- 
trative action involves an adjudicative function, and 
whether or not such action is subject to review in state 
court, a federal forum is available for claims that such 
administrative action has violated federal law. 


The conclusion that the exercise of federal jurisdic- 
tion is appropriate is even clearer in this case than it 
was in City of Chicago for two reasons. First, the 
agency action here was undertaken in the exercise of 
authority granted under federal law. Contrary to the 
Fourth Circuit’s understanding, nothing in Maryland 
law authorizes the PSC to hear a complaint against 
Verizon alleging a violation of federal law. On the 
contrary, the provision of Maryland law on which the 
Fourth Circuit relied makes clear that a person may 
file a complaint only to “allege a violation of this 
article” — i.e., the state statute, not the 1996 Act. 
Md. Code Ann., Pub. Util. Cos. § 3-102(a)(2) (1998) 
(emphasis added). Nothing in the complaint underly- 
ing this case alleged that Verizon had violated any 


38 


provision of the Maryland Public Utility Companies 
law; instead, the allegation was that Verizon had 
violated the 1996 Act and the parties’ 1996 Act inter- 
connection agreement. 


Accordingly, it was only to the extent that federal law 
authorized the administrative complaints at issue here 
that preliminary resort to the PSC was appropriate. As 
every other federal court to consider the question has 
agreed, a state commission’s authority to consider such 
complaints derives from its authority under § 252 to 
arbitrate and approve interconnection agreements. See 
Southwestern Bell Tel. Co. v. Connect Communications 
Corp., 225 F.3d 942, 947 (8th Cir. 2000) (“we can reach 
no conclusion but to agree with the FCC’s determi- 
nation that the state commissions’ power to enforce 
interconnection agreements springs from § 252”); MCI 
Telecomms. Corp. v. Illinois Bell Tel. Co., 222 F.3d 323, 
338 (7th Cir. 2000) (“[a) state commission’s authority 
to approve or reject interconnection agreements under 
the Act necessarily includes the authority to interpret 
and enforce, to the same extent, the terms of those 
agreements”), cert. denied, 121 S. Ct. 896 (2001); 
Southwestern Bell Tel. Co. v. Public Util. Comm’n, 208 
F.3d 475, 479-80 (5th Cir. 2000) (“[T]he Act’s grant to 
the state commissions of plenary authority to approve 
or disapprove... interconnection agreements necessar- 
ily carries with it the authority to interpret and enforce 
the provisions of agreements that state commissions 
have approved.”). 


The conclusion that the delegation of federal author- 
ity to state commissions includes the power to interpret 
and enforce interconnection agreements reflects the 
settled principle that the agency entrusted with mak- 
ing the policy judgments inherent in overseeing the 
approval of a federal tariff is likewise the exclusive 
original tribunal for claims that require construction of 
disputed terms. United States v. Western Pac. R.R., 


39 


352 U.S. 59, 65-66 (1956); Great Northern, 259 U.S. at 
291. In this case, the issue presented — the meaning 
of the interconnection agreement provision requiring 
reciprocal compensation “as . . . required by the Act” 
and limiting such compensation to “local traffic” as 
specifically defined in keeping with FCC regulations — 
involved the type of issue that must be resolved in the 
first instance by the agency with “the law-making 
power over certain aspects of commercial relations.” 
Western Pac., 352 U.S. at 65 (internal quotation marks 
omitted). The courts have therefore correctly concluded 
that the type of determination at issue here is one 
made under the authority delegated by federal law. 
Accordingly, the PSC was acting as a federal agency, 
not merely as a state agency, because it was exercising 
a power delegated by Congress, not the state. The rule 
of Califano v. Sanders, 430 U.S. 99 (1977), that 
“federal district courts may engage in on-the-record, 
substantial evidence review of federal agency actions 
under § 1331” (City of Chicago, 522 U.S. at 183 
(Ginsburg, J., dissenting)) fully applies here. 


Second, unlike City of Chicago, where the plaintiff 
had raised both federal and state-law challenges to 
state administrative action taken under state law, this 
case raises no issue of state law. The administrative 
scheme at issue here is exclusively federal, and the 
obligations imposed by the interconnection agreements 
at issue are likewise exclusively federal. See supra at 
20-29. Accordingly, the only legal issues presented are 
matters of federal law. Forcing Verizon to litigate 
these issues in state court — whether or not the state 
court properly has jurisdiction — “would be at war with 
the unqualified terms in which Congress, pursuant to 
constitutional authorization, has conferred specific 
categories of jurisdiction upon the federal courts.” 
England v. Louisiana State Board of Med. Examiners, 
375 U.S. 411, 415 (1964). 


40 


For the same reason, the principles underlying 
abstention doctrine could not support a federal court’s 
refusal to exercise jurisdiction in this case. Because 
the issues here are ones purely of federal law, this case 
“does not demand significant familiarity with, and will 
not disrupt state resolution of, distinctively local regu- 
latory facts or policies.” New Orleans Pub. Serv., Inc. 
v. Council of New Orleans, 491 U.S. 350, 364 (1989). In 
such circumstances, the district court must exercise the 
federal jurisdiction conferred by Congress. 


CONCLUSION 


The judgment of the court of appeals should be 
reversed.
