STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


The issue in this case is whether a reviewing court may 
correct an error when a district court sentenced a defendant 
for an offense that was neither charged in the indictment nor 
presented to the petit jury because an essential element of the 
offense was instead supplied by the sentencing judge using an 
improper standard of proof. This series of mistakes denied 
respondents their rights under the Fifth and Sixth 
Amendments to have each element of an offense presented to 
a grand jury and found by a petit jury beyond a reasonable 
doubt. Petitioner admits that it was “plain error” to omit an 
essential fact, drug quantity, from the indictment and from the 
elements submitted to the petit jury for a finding, and it 
admits that plain error occurred when the sentencing judge 
used the preponderance of the evidence standard to find this 
element. 


Petitioner nevertheless claims that this Court should ignore 
these fundamental errors. To do so would reduce the grand 


3 


jury to superfluity and defy the basic guarantees afforded to 
criminal defendants by the Constitution and protected by this 
Court’s decisions. Petitioner’s argument would require this 
Court to overrule its own precedent that a court cannot exceed 
its jurisdiction by sentencing a defendant for an offense not 
charged in the indictment. Ex parte Bain, 121 U.S. 1 (1887). 
Moreover, petitioner would have this Court disregard its 
recent holding that elements of criminal offenses must be 
presented to a petit jury and proven beyond a reasonable 
doubt. Apprendi v. New Jersey, 530 U.S. 466 (2000); see also 
In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). Finally, petitioner’s 
argument would require this Court to hold that sentencing 
respondents for an offense whose elements were not properly 
charged in the indictment, based on a finding made by the 
sentencing judge under a preponderance of the evidence 
standard, did not affect the integrity, fairness, or public 
reputation of the judicial proceeding. Such a holding would 
be untenable when the mistakes in this case undermined the 
very foundation of the criminal trial. The litany of 
constitutional errors that flowed from the initial charging 
decision in this case demonstrates the foundational 
importance of the grand jury indictment. Petitioner’s attempt 
to let these errors go uncorrected would eviscerate the 
protections embodied in the grand jury guarantee. 


A. Background 


The grand jury charged respondents with a conspiracy to 
commit a drug offense prohibited by 21 U.S.C. § 841. 
Section 841(a) makes it “unlawful for any person knowingly 
or intentionally . . . to manufacture, distribute, or dispense, or 
possess with intent to manufacture, distribute, or dispense, a 
controlled substance.” 21 U.S.C. § 841(a)(1). Section 846 
sets out the offense of conspiracy and provides that “[aJjny 
person who attempts or conspires to commit any offense 
defined in this subchapter shall be subject to the same 
penalties as those prescribed for the offense, the commission 


4 


of which was the object of the attempt or conspiracy.” /d. 
§ 846. 


Subsection 841(b), in turn, provides a gradation of drug 
amounts and penalties in controlled substance offenses and 
states that “any person who violates subsection (a) of this 
section shall be sentenced as follows.” Jd. § 841(b). The 
penalties begin with a term of imprisonment “which may not 
be less than 10 years or more than life” under § 841(b)(1)(A); 
the relevant part of this subsection applies to possession of 50 
grams or more of cocaine base. Subsection 841(b)(1)(B) 
provides a penalty “which may not be less than 5 years and 
not more than 40 years” for 5 or more grams of cocaine base. 
Subsection 841(b)(1)(C) is the “catchall” provision that 
applies to all other violations of § 841(a) involving schedule I 
or II substances; it sets a term of imprisonment capped at 20 
years without regard to drug quantity. 


While respondents’ appeal was pending, this Court decided 
Apprendi v. New Jersey, 530 U.S. 466 (June 26, 2000). In 
Apprendi, the Court held that, “[o]ther than the fact of a prior 
conviction, any fact that increases the penalty for a crime 
beyond the prescribed statutory maximum must be submitted 
to a jury, and proved beyond a reasonable doubt.” Jd. at 490. 
The Apprendi decision followed this Court’s decision in 
Jones v. United States, 526 U.S. 227 (Mar. 24, 1999) (also 
decided while respondents’ appeal was pending), which 
expressed doubt about the constitutionality of allowing 
penalty-enhancing findings to be determined by a judge by a 
preponderance of the evidence. /d. at 243 n.6. Since this 
Court’s decision in Apprendi, most courts of appeals that have 
analyzed the federal drug statute have held that the quantity of 
drugs must be alleged in the indictment and proven to a petit 
jury beyond a reasonable doubt. See United States v. 
Thomas, 274 F.3d 655, 663 (2d Cir. 2001) (en banc) (citing 
cases from seven other circuits); but see United States v. 
Bjorkman, 270 F.3d 482, 492 (7th Cir. 2001) (stating merely 


5 


that “‘a post-Apprendi indictment should specify .. . the 
events listed in § 841(b) on which the prosecutor relies to 
establish the maximum sentence’”), petition for cert. filed, 
No. 01-8356 (U.S. Jan. 25, 2002). Petitioner does not 
challenge either of these conclusions. See Pet. Br. 7-11. 


B. The District Court Proceedings 


In March 1998, a federal grand jury in the District of 
Maryland returned a _ superseding indictment charging 
respondents and others with a single count of conspiracy to 
distribute and to possess with the intent to distribute cocaine 
and cocaine base. The indictment did not allege the amount 
of drugs involved. Instead, it mentioned a “detectable amount 
of’ the controlled substances.’ The charge arose out of an 
investigation of a drug organization in Baltimore, Maryland. 
Authorities seized currency, weapons, drug paraphernalia, 
approximately 380 grams of cocaine base, and approximately 
84 grams of cocaine hydrochloride during searches of several 
houses and the arrests of respondents. Pet. App. 6a. 
Prosecutors charged the respondents in the single-count 
indictment, which cited only 21 U.S.C. § 841(a)(1) and 21 
U.S.C. § 846. J.A. 59, 62. 


Respondents pleaded not guilty, and a jury trial commenced 
on August 31, 1998. C.A J.A. 42. During the trial, the 
district court instructed the jury that “as long as you find that 
a defendant conspired to distribute or possess[] with intent to 
distribute these controlled substances, the amounts involved 
are not important.” Supp. C.A. App. 13. On November 9, 
1998, the jury acquitted one defendant but found the seven 
respondents guilty of a conspiracy to distribute and possess 
with intent to distribute the controlled substances at issue.

_ C. The Court Of Appeals Decision 


As relevant here, respondents appealed their convictions 
because their sentences were invalid under Apprendi v. New 
Jersey, 530 U.S. 466 (2000). Apprendi had been decided 
after their sentencing but while their appeal was pending. 
Petitioner conceded that there had been an Apprendi error 
because the drug quantity element had not been presented to 
the grand jury or found by the petit jury beyond a reasonable 
doubt. See Griffith v. Kentucky, 479 U.S. 314, 328 (1987) (a 
new rule for criminal prosecutions applies to all cases 
pending on direct review). Nevertheless, petitioner asserted 
that respondents had forfeited the argument by not raising it at 
trial, and the error should be reviewed under the plain-error 
standards of Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b). 
According to petitioner, the court should, under its 
discretionary authority, ignore the error because there was 
evidence that would have supported a jury determination of 
guilt for the offense for which respondents were sentenced 
but never charged.“ 


*The government’s position on these issues has wavered since 
Apprendi. In some post-Apprendi litigation, the government has argued 
that the facts referenced in subsection (b) (relating to quantities) of § 841 
are mere sentencing factors that need not be charged in the indictment, a 
position it has now abandoned. See United States v. Promise, 255 F.3d 
150, 182-84 (4th Cir. 2001) (en banc) (Luttig, J., concurring in judgment), 
petition for cert. filed, No. 01-6398 (U.S. Sept. 20, 2001). It is also 
maintaining in a separate case before this Court that “[i}f the legislature 
could provide for judicial fact-finding by a preponderance of the evidence

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed for 
three reasons. First, a district court’s jurisdiction is limited to 
the offenses charged in the indictment. Ex parte Bain, 121 
U.S. 1, 12-13 (1887); see also United States v. Miller, 471 
U.S. 130, 144 (1985). In this case, the district court exceeded 
its jurisdiction when it sentenced respondents for an offense 
that was not charged in the indictment and therefore never 
presented to the petit jury for a finding. A jurisdictional error 
can be noticed by the court at any time, and it requires 
correction. Fed. R. Crim. P. 12(b)(2). The court of appeals 
properly vacated the sentences in this case, although its 
analysis of the error under the discretionary plain-error 


11 


standard was unnecessary because the nature of the error 
mandated correction. 


Second, the court of appeals’ judgment also should be 
affirmed because the errors in this case fall into a class of 
fatal flaws known as “structural errors” that “affect[] the 
framework within which the trial proceeds.” Arizona v. 
Fulminante, 499 U.S. 279, 310 (1991). These errors “are so 
intrinsically harmful as to require automatic reversal.” Neder 
v. United States, 527 U.S. 1, 7 (1999). Specifically, this 
Court has held that reversal is necessary when a court 
proceeds against a defendant for a charge that was not in the 
indictment, even if the error has been forfeited. Silber v. 
United States, 370 U.S. 717 (1962) (per curiam). In addition, 
application of an improper standard of proof constitutes a 
separate structural error under Sullivan v. Louisiana, 508 U.S. 
275 (1993). Contrary to petitioner’s arguments, the defects in 
this proceeding are unrelated to the errors in Neder v. United 
States, 527 U.S. 1 (1999), and Johnson v. United States, 520 
U.S. 461 (1997), which involved the flawed presentation of a 
case to the petit jury, rather than errors in the indictment, 
sentencing, and standard of proof. 


Third, the errors in this case warrant correction under 
harmless-error or plain-error review. This Court has held that 
proceeding against a defendant for an offense not charged in 
the indictment can never be harmless error. Stirone v. United 
States, 361 U.S. 212 (1960). Petitioner tries to avoid the 
result of this holding by arguing that the claim of error in this 
case was forfeited and is subject to plain-error review. 
Respondents, however, objected to drug quantity at the 
earliest possible moment in the proceedings—when the 
prosecution first sought to establish the quantity element at 
sentencing. Petitioner’s argument that the objection was 
forfeited in fact supports respondents’ case. It demonstrates 
that the charging function of the grand jury had been ignored 
and the critical element, upon which respondents’ 


12 


punishments were to be determined, was not presented until 
sentencing and accordingly was decided using the wrong 
standard of proof. 


In all events, the court of appeals’ judgment should be 
affirmed because an increase in respondents’ sentences from 
20 years to life, or from 20 years to 30 years, deprived 
respondents of a significant liberty interest. Moreover, the 
integrity, fairness, and public reputation of the proceeding 
were seriously affected by the Fifth and Sixth Amendment 
violations in this case. To reach this conclusion, it is 
unnecessary to examine the quantity or quality of the 
evidence adduced at sentencing. The discretion to correct 
such systemic errors does not depend on a defendant’s 
innocence or guilt. In accordance with the carefully crafted 
structure of our criminal process, the government’s failure to 
present such evidence to the grand jury and obtain a separate, 
greater charge in an indictment warrants vacatur of 
respondents’ sentences.

sentence imposed by the district court must therefore be set 
aside. 


A formal accusation is essential for every criminal trial, and 
without it, the court has no jurisdiction to proceed. Post v. 
United States, 161 U.S. 583, 587 (1896), Albrecht v. United 
States, 273 U.S. 1, 7 (1927). In order to charge an offense, an 
indictment must allege every element of the offense. 
Hamling v. United States, 418 U.S 87, 117 (1974); Russell v. 
United States, 369 U.S. 749, 763 (1962). If the indictment 
fails to include an essential element or fact, the defendant 
cannot be convicted or sentenced for that offense. Russell, 
369 U.S. at 771-72; Ball v. United States, 140 U.S. 118, 136 
(1891). The indictment’s charges may only be broadened by 
the grand jury itself, and if the court allows an actual or 
constructive amendment to the indictment, the error is 
reversible. Stirone, 361 U.S. at 215-17. 


Although 18 U.S.C. § 3231 grants jurisdiction to district 
courts over crimes against the United States, it does not 
confer a grant of roving jurisdiction for the district court to 
convict and sentence a defendant for any crime that may be 
supported by the evidence. Jurisdiction over a particular 
offense is essential to the validity of a criminal prosecution 
and the legality of a sentence, and that jurisdiction is 
conferred and defined in a formal charging document. Bain, 
121 US. at 13-14.

B. The Violation Of The Right To Indictment By A 
Grand Jury Is A Structural Error. 


Although the Silber Court did not use the term “structural 
error,” its reversal of the indictment error demonstrates that 
defective indictments fall within a class of structural errors 
this Court has found to be so fundamental that they require 
reversal. See Sullivan v. Louisiana, 508 U.S. 275, 281-82 
(1993). A structural error is a “defect affecting the 
framework within which the trial proceeds, rather than simply 
an error in the trial process itself.” Arizona v. Fulminanite, 
499 US. 279, 310 (1991). Such errors “undermine[] the 
structural integrity of the criminal tribunal itself,” Vasquez v. 
Hillery, 474 U.S. 254, 263-64 (1986), and deprive an accused 
of “basic protections.” Rose v. Clark, 478 U.S. 570, 577-78 
(1986). The rights violated by these errors “protect important 
values that are unrelated to the truth-seeking function of the 
trial,” id. at 587 (Stevens, J., concurring in judgment), and 
their deprivation has “consequences that are necessarily 
unquantifiable and indeterminate.” Sullivan, 508 U.S. at 282. 


The violation of the right not to be sentenced for a crime 
that was not charged in an-indictment readily satisfies these 
criteria. This structural safeguard serves to protect not only 
the individual accused but also the system of justice. Its 
elimination therefore vitiates the legitimating mechanism for 
prosecution and represents a “structural defect[] in the 
constitution of the trial mechanism.” Fulminante, 499 U.S. at 
309. Indeed, there can be no question that bypassing this 


25 


grand jury requirement “affect[s] the framework within which 
the trial proceeds,” rather than being “simply an error in the 
trial process itself.” /d. at 310. 


The right to have a group of citizens determine the validity 
of charges against an accused before a criminal trial may be 
initiated is a fundamental protection undergirding the criminal 
process. 


Historically, [the grand jury] has been regarded as a

founded upon reason or was dictated by an intimidating

whether there is probable cause to believe a crime has been 
committed and the protection of citizens against unfounded

element not found by the grand jury, the results are, like those 
of other structural errors, “necessarily unquantifiable and 
indeterminate.” Sullivan, 508 U.S. at 282. 


The grand jury requirement further embodies a structural 
allocation of powers among the judiciary, the executive, and 
citizenry. The Founders chose to place the power to initiate a 
federal criminal proceeding with the citizens—not with the 
judge, not with the prosecutor. “[Twelve] ordinary citizens 
must agree upon an indictment before a defendant is tried on 
a felony charge.” Gaither v. United States, 413 F.2d 1061, 
1066 (D.C. Cir. 1969). The grand jury “is a constitutional 
fixture in its own right” that “belongs to no branch of the 
institutional Government, serving as a kind of buffer or 
referee between the Government and the people.” United

Gaither, 413 F.2d at 1066 (footnote omitted). This Court has

Sentencing a defendant for an offense that was not charged 
in the indictment or found by a jury beyond a reasonable 
doubt is an error that affects “the entire conduct of the trial 
from beginning to end” and “the framework within which the 
trial proceeds.” Fulminante, 499 U.S. at 309-10. These 
errors are as fundamental to the structure of the trial as other 
errors this Court has previously declared “structural.” For 
instance, the “violation of the guarantee of a public trial 
required reversal without any showing of prejudice even

denial constitutes structural error. Tumey v. Ohio, 273 U.S. 
510, 535 (1927); Sheppard v. Maxwell, 384 U.S. 333, 351-52

of a defendant for a crime not charged in the indictment is an 
error of such magnitude that it “‘infect[s] the entire trial 
process.” Neder v. United States, 527 U.S. 1, 8 (1999) If 
the right to have proposed charges reviewed by a group of

C. The Denial Of The Right To A Petit Jury Verdict 
Beyond A Reasonable Doubt On Each Element 
Of An Offense Is Structural Error Under 
Sullivan v. Louisiana. 


In addition to the indictment error in this case, the error in 
having a sentencing judge rather than a petit jury decide an 
element of the offense under the preponderance of the 
evidence standard rather than the reasonable doubt standard 
constitutes the same type of error that the Court determined to 
be structural in Sullivan v. Louisiana, 508 U.S. 275 (1993). 
In Sullivan, this Court considered a trial where a defendant 
was convicted under the wrong standard of proof: the judge 
gave the petit jury an erroneous reasonable doubt instruction. 
This error “unquestionably qualifie[d] as ‘structural error,’” 
according to the Court. /d at 282. 


Denial of the right to a jury verdict of guilt beyond a 
reasonable doubt is certainly [a structural error], the jury 
guarantee being a “basic protection” whose precise 
effects are unmeasurable, but without which a criminal 
trial cannot reliably serve its function. The right to trial 
by jury reflects, we have said, “a profound judgment 
about the way in which law should be enforced and 
justice administered.” 


Id. at 281 (internal citation omitted). 


Sullivan’s error was the application of the wrong standard 
of proof, and it could not be remedied by allowing the 
reviewing court to weigh the evidence under the correct 
standard of proof, for this would deny the essential guarantees 
that guilt be found by a jury beyond a reasonable doubt. /d. at 
281, 279. In the instant case, respondents suffered an 
equivalent error when an element of their sentenced offense 
was found, not under a faulty version of the beyond a 
reasonable doubt standard, but unambiguously under the 
wrong standard—by a mere preponderance of the evidence— 
and by the wrong fact-finder. This error alone qualifies as a 


36 


Sullivan structural error mandating reversal. There is an even 
more egregious injustice in this case than existed in Sullivan, 
however, since respondents suffered another error going to 
the very structure of the criminal justice process: the 
indictment did not charge the offense for which they were 
sentenced. If a flawed statement of the reasonable doubt 
standard was structural error, it defies reason to conclude that 
no structural error has occurred where there is blatant use of a 
lower standard by the wrong fact-finder and an indictment 
error this Court has already described as “far too serious” to 
shrug off as harmless error. Stirone, 361 U.S. at 217. 


D. Structural Errors Warrant Correction 
Regardless of the Evidence. 


According to this Court, when an indictment fails to allege 
an element of the crime, the fundamental nature of the error 
necessitates correction. See Stirone, 361 US. at 219 
(reversing a conviction because of a “fatal error” in the 
indictment’s failure to allege an element of the crime); Bain, 
121 US. at 13; Silber v. United States, 370 U.S. 717 (1962) 
(per curiam). While minor defects in indictments may not 
require correction,. “the substantial safeguards to those 
charged with serious crimes cannot be eradicated under the 
guise of technical departures from the rules.” Smith v. United 
States, 360 U.S. 1, 9 (1959). 


This Court has held that structural errors “require reversal 
without regard to the evidence in the particular case.” Rose, 
478 US. at 577 (citing Chapman, 386 US. at 23 n8 
(emphasis added)). They invalidate a conviction even though 
there may be no reasonable doubt that the defendant is guilty. 
See, e.g., Vazquez v. Hillery, 474 U.S. 254 (1986) (reversing 
a conviction despite overwhelming evidence of the 
defendant’s guilt because of an error in the grand jury 
composition). A structural error affects more than the 
accuracy of the result; by definition, it undermines the very 
process itself, and such an error cannot be plugged into an 


37 


evidence-quantifying formula. In 7umey v. Ohio, 273 US. 
510 (1927), the Supreme Court reversed a conviction entered 
by a biased judge, even though “evidence show/ed] clearly 
that defendant was guilty,” because “[n]o matter what the 
evidence was against him, [the defendant] had the right to 
have an impartial judge.” /d. at 535. Similarly, a defendant 
has a right to indictment by a grand jury and conviction by a 
petit jury using the beyond a reasonable doubt standard no 
matter what the evidence is against him. 


Recognizing these principles, this Court in Sullivan 
reversed a conviction when the petit jury guarantee was 
undermined by a faulty reasonable doubt instruction. The 
Court forbade a reviewing court to “engage in pure 
speculation—its view of what a reasonable jury would have 
done,” when the result was that the “‘wrong entity judge(s] 
the defendant guilty.’” Sullivan, 508 U.S. at 281 (alteration in 
Sullivan) (quoting Rose, 478 U.S. at 578). “[T]o hypothesize 
a guilty verdict that was never in fact rendered—no matter 
how inescapable the findings to support that verdict might 
be—would violate the jury-trial guarantee.” /d at 279. For 
the same reasons, a court is not permitted to speculate on a 
grand jury’s possible decision on an element of an offense 
that was never charged. “To allow... the court... . to make a 
subsequent guess as to what was in the minds of the grand 
jury at the time they returned the indictment would deprive 
the defendant of a basic protection which the guaranty of the 
intervention of a grand jury was designed to secure.” Russell, 
369 US. at 770. 


This Court has explained that structural errors are errors 
that “are so intrinsically harmful as to require automatic 
reversal (i.e., affect ‘substantial rights’) without regard to 
their effect on the outcome.” Neder, 527-0.S-at 7. Because 
such structural errors “deprive defendants of ‘basic 
protections,” id at 8, they “will always invalidate the 
conviction.” Sullivan, 508 U.S. at 279 (citing Fulminanie, 


nn eee 


38 


499 US. at 309-10). Petitioner argues that in this case plain- 
error review applies and trumps the rule of reversal. See Pet. 
Br. at 43. However, respondents did not forfeit their claim of 
error, see infra Il1.A.3, and the rule of reversal compels 
correction of the structural errors in this case. 


Ill. THE ERRORS IN THIS CASE WARRANT 
REVERSAL UNDER HARMLESS-ERROR OR 
PLAIN-ERROR REVIEW. 


A. Reversal Is Warranted Under Harmless-Error 
Review. 
1. Respondents’ Right To Liberty And Their 


Right To A Grand Jury Were Violated By 
The Errors Here. 


In all events, the errors in this case warrant reversal under 
harmless-error review. The error should be corrected unless 
the prosecution can show beyond a reasonable doubt that the 
error was harmless, i.e., that it did not affect a defendant’s 
“substantial rights.” Fed. R. Crim P. 52(a); see also 
Delaware vy. Van Arsdall, 475 US. 673, 681 (1986). 
Petitioner cannot meet its burden here because this Court 
already has determined that an indictment error like the one in 
this case can never be harmless error: “[d]eprivation of such 
a basic right [as the grand jury right] is far too serious to be 
treated as nothing more than a variance and then dismissed as 
harmless error.” Stirone vy. United States, 361 U.S. 212, 217 


(1960). 


In addition, an illegal increase in a sentence from a 
statutory maximum of 20 years to a term of life 
imprisonment, or from 20 years to 30 years, plainly affects a 
defendant’s substantial rights. The offense whose elements 
were properly alleged in the indictment here carried a 
maximum statutory sentence of 20 years. However, five of 
the respondents were illegally sentenced to life in prison, and 
two others were illegally sentenced to 30 years in prison. 


39 


This error unlawfully deprived respondents of their right to 
liberty. This Court has already explained in Apprendi that if a 
defendant is sentenced based on a fact that was not proven to 
the jury, “it is obvious that both the loss of liberty and the 
stigma attaching to the offense are heightened.” Apprendi v. 
New Jersey, 530 U.S. 466, 484 (2000). “[I]}t can hardly be 
said that the potential doubling of one’s sentence—from 10 
years to 20—has no more than a nominal effect. Both in 
terms of absolute years behind bars, and because of the more 
severe stigma attached, the differential here is unquestionably 
of constitutional significance,” found the Apprendi Court. /d. 
at 495. The increases in the sentences in this case impose 
precisely the same grievous losses as those in Apprendi. 


2. Respondents Did Not Receive The 
Constitutionally Required Notice Of The 
Crime For Which They Were Sentenced, But 
Even If They Had, Such Notice Could Not 
Cure The Defects In The Proceeding Here. 


One of the purposes of the grand jury indictment is to 
provide notice to the accused of the charges against him. 
Russell vy. United States, 369 U.S. 749, 763 (1962). The 
superseding indictment in this case did not include any 
allegations regarding the quantity of drugs, see supra, n.1, nor 
did the prosecution attempt to make up for this omission by 
later filing an information alleging the quantity, as it did in 
some cases. See, e.g., United States vy. Promise, 255 F.3d 
150, 163 (4th Cir. 2001) (en banc), petition for cert. filed, No. 
01-6398 (U.S. Sept. 20, 2001). Petitioner argues that this 
Court should ignore the errors in this case because 
respondents were not surprised by their sentences, based on 
the original indictment that had been superseded. Pet. Br. at 
47. Petitioner fails to cite any case supporting the counter- 
intuitive proposition that a superseded indictment constitutes 
the “notice” due to an accused under the Constitution. See id 
at 24. Compare United States v. Olson, 716 F.2d 850, 852-53 


rr 


40 


(11th Cir. 1983) (rejecting the district court’s conclusion that 
defendant’s lack of surprise rendered harmless the 
prosecution’s failure to file a statutorily required information 
on a recidivism enhancement). To the contrary, the only 
inference to draw is that the offenses charged in the prior 
indictment have been abandoned in favor of the charges 
included in the superseding indictment. When the 
prosecution chose to drop the quantity allegation, the message 
it sent was that it had decided that it had insufficient evidence 
to prove this element beyond a reasonable doubt. 


This Court has required an indictment to apprise the 
defendant of the accusation against him by including “such a 
statement of the facts and circumstances as will inform the 
accused of the specific offence, coming under the general 
description, with which he is charged.” United States v. Hess, 
124 U.S. 483, 487 (1888). If an indictment fails to include 
the essential facts, it “enables [a defendant’s] conviction to 
rest on one point and the affirmance of the conviction to rest 
on another. It gives the prosecution free hand on appeal to fill 
in the gaps of proof by surmise or conjecture.” Russell, 369 
U.S. at 766. That is precisely what happened here. Petitioner 
relies on evidence that may never have been shown to the 
grand jury (and, in any event, might have been ignored), and 
an accusation of drug quantity that was submitted to neither 
the grand jury nor the petit jury for a determination. 


Even had the prosecution provided notice, this post- 
indictment notice would not suffice to remedy the errors here 
because the omission of an element from the indictment 
deprives an accused of more than notice. Beyond notice is 
the separate constitutional right, protected by Fifth 
Amendment, that a defendant be tried upon charges found by 
a grand jury. /d. at 770. Notice would not make up for the 
right that was violated—a determination by fellow citizens 
that there was probable cause supporting the precise charge. 
Thus, whatever notice was provided outside of the indictment, 


41 


a court “cannot know whether the grand jury would have 
included in its indictment a charge that” was never presented 
to it. Stirone, 361 U.S. at 219. Respondents’ sentences 
cannot stand under harmless-error review because their 
substantial rights were undeniably affected. 


3. Respondents Are Not Subject To Plain-Error 
Review Because They Objected At The 
Earliest Possible Moment In The Proceeding. 


Plain-error review should not apply to this case because 
respondents objected at the earliest possible time during the 
proceeding. It was not until the prosecution first sought to 
establish the quantity element at sentencing that respondents 
could logically object to the quantity issue. See, e.g., United 
States v. Strayhorn, 250 F.3d 462, 467 (6th Cir. 2001) 
(concluding that a defendant’s objections to drug quantity on 
non-constitutional grounds at his pre-Apprendi guilty plea 
hearing and at sentencing sufficed to preserve an Apprendi 
claim for appeal). When the indictment charged an offense 
carrying a 20-year sentence, and the jury found facts 
supporting the same, respondents were not obligated to object 
to a failure to offer charges supporting a life-term sentence. 
Indeed, respondents could not object to the denial of their 
Fifth and Sixth Amendment rights until they appreciated that 
they were being sentenced for a crime for which they were 
never charged. 


Plain-error review is designed to prevent “sandbagging” 
(where a defendant stays deliberately silent about an error at 
trial so that he may obtain a reversal on the basis of the error 
on appeal). See, e.g., Johnson v. United States, 520 U.S. 461, 
465-66 (1997) (noting that preventing sandbagging is 
important in determining whether to notice plain error). 
Respondents here could hardly be accused of “sandbagging” 
when Apprendi, upon which they rely, was not decided until 
after their trial. Respondents objected to the quantity of drugs 
at the earliest logical moment, at sentencing. Accordingly, 


42 


correcting the error in this case would not allow respondents 
to obtain “any strategic benefit from [their] failure to timely 
object to the error.” Promise, 255 F.3d at 195 (Motz, J, 
dissenting). 


The requirement that a defendant object exists so that the 
district court can correct the alleged error. See United States 
v. Atkinson, 297 U.S. 157, 159 (1936). In this case, however, 
the only error the district court could correct was the increase 
in sentence based on a finding of drug quantity. Respondents 
presented that objection in timely fashion. The district court 
could not have ruled that an Apprendi-type error had 
occurred, even had respondents raised a constitutional 
argument, because neither Apprendi nor Jones had been 
decided, and existing circuit precedent would have defeated 
the argument. See, e.g., United States v. Fletcher, 74 F.3d 49, 
53 (4th Cir. 1996). In fact, this Court has acknowledged the 
futility in requiring a defense attorney to “mak[e] a long and 
virtually useless laundry list of objections to rulings that were 
plainly supported by existing precedent.” Johnson, 520 U.S. 
at 468. No purpose in promoting an orderly judicial process 
would be served by imposing the penalty of plain-error 
review in this case. See, e.g., United States v. Novey, 922 
F.2d 624, 629 (10th Cir. 1991) (“An exception to the rule 
precluding review exists where an intervening Supreme Court 
decision changes the law while an appeal is pending.”); 
United States v. Mulder, 889 F.2d 239, 240 (9th Cir. 1989) 
(defendant’s “claims are not waived because this failure [to 
raise the claims in the district court] was not due to any 
‘belated decision to change trial tactics,’ but was caused by a 
recent Supreme Court case that directly affected his claim”) 
(citation omitted). Plain-error review should not apply in this 
case.

B. Application of Plain-Error Review Warrants 
Affirmance of the Court of Appeals Decision. 


1. Respondents’ Substantial Right To Liberty 
Was Violated. 


Under plain-error review, an error must affect a defendant’s 
substantial rights. Fed. R. Crim. P. 52(b). In this case, when 
the sentencing court sentenced respondents for a crime with 
which they had not been charged, it deprived five respondents 
of their substantial liberty interest by increasing their 
incarceration from 20 years to life, and from 20 years to 30 
years in the case of two respondents. See supra, III.A.1. 


Petitioner tries to avoid this conclusion by speculating that 
had it charged respondents for the more serious offense, it 
would have obtained the same result. Pet. Br. at 48. This 
conjecture is irrelevant since the effect on respondents’ rights 
is clear. See Apprendi, 530 U.S. at 484. Moreover, it ignores 
this Court’s admonition to reviewing courts not to speculate 
on the result of grand jury proceedings: “neither this [Court] 
nor_any other court can know that the grand jury would have 
been willing to charge” an offense other than the one included 
in the actual indictment. Stirone, 361 U.S. at 217. 


In Vasquez v. Hillery, 474 U.S. 254 (1986), this Court 
forbade courts to conduct a review of the potential harm when 
the “structural integrity of the criminal tribunal itself’ was 
“undermine[d]” because the effect of the error cannot be 
ascertained, and a court “cannot know” how the grand jury 
would have decided under alternative conditions. /d. at 263- 
64. Although Hillery was addressing grand jury 
discrimination, the principle that a court cannot speculate on a 
grand jury’s decision applies equally to the error in this case. 
Indeed, Hillery explained that the grand jury “does not 
determine only that probable cause exists to believe that a 
defendant committed a crime, or that it does not,” but also has 
the power to select “a greater offense or a lesser offense.” /d. 
at 263. 


44 


Because the “‘grand jury is not bound to indict in every 
case where a conviction can be obtained,’” id., an evaluation 
of the evidence in hindsight cannot stand in for the grand 
jury’s decision on whether to indict. Cf Sullivan v. 
Louisiana, 508 U.S. 275, 281 (1993) (forbidding a reviewing 
court to “engage in pure speculation—its view of what a 
reasonable jury would have done,” when the result was that 
the “‘wrong entity judge[s] the defendant guilty’”) (alteration 
in Sullivan) (quoting Rose, 478 U.S. at 578). Moreover, 
unlike a petit jury error, where the entire record is available to 
evaluate the input of a potential mistake, there is no record of 
grand jury proceedings that could serve as a benchmark for 
making a harmless-error assessment. A reviewing court 
therefore cannot substitute itself for the grand jury to 
determine the outcome of a grand jury proceeding on a charge 
that was not presented. For this reason, the failure to present 
a charge to the grand jury necessitates a presumption of 
prejudice. See Stirone, 361 U.S. at 217. 


2. The Errors Undermined The Integrity, 
Public Reputation, And Fairness Of The 
Criminal Justice Proceeding. 


The errors in this case merit reversal under plain-error 
review because they seriously affected the integrity, public 
reputation, and fairness of the judicial proceedings. See Fed. 
R. Crim. P. 52(b); United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 735, 736 
(1993). Respondents’ sentences exceeded the statutory 
maximum for the indicted offense. The terms of their 
imprisonment were based on an element of an offense that 
was not charged in the grand jury indictment, and that was not 
found by the petit jury beyond a reasonable doubt. The errors 
deprived respondents of their Fifth and Sixth Amendment 
guarantees of an indictment by a grand jury and a petit jury 
verdict based upon the beyond a reasonable doubt standard of 
proof. This Court has exercised its own discretion under the 
plain-error standard to notice an error in an indictment that 


So ee RGN 6 Pe SL Sa Tilt it RAT ED 


45 
had not been raised either before the court of appeals or this 
Court itself. Silber, 370 U.S. at 717. There was no error 
when the court of appeals did the same. 


This Court has repeatedly affirmed that a violation of “such 
a basic right” as indictment by a grand jury can never be 
harmless error, Stirone, 361 U.S. at 217, that a defendant has 
a right not be sentenced or “convicted on the basi of facts not 
found by, and perhaps not even presented to, the grand jury 
which indicted him,” Russell, 369 U.S. at 770; and that an 
“indictment found by a grand jury [is] indispensable to the 
power of the court to try the petitioner for the crime with 
which he was charged,” Ex parte Bain, 121 US. 1, 12-13 
(1887). Petitioner’s position would require that these bedrock 
principles be rejected, thereby undermining the grand jury’s 
role as a structural safeguard for the accused. 


3. The Fourth Prong Of Plain-Error Review 
Calls For The Evaluation Of An Error’s 
Effect Independent Of A _ Defendant’s 
Innocence Or Guilt Where The Right 
Violated Protects Values Distinct From The 
Accuracy Function Of The Trial. 


Petitioner claims that the errors in this case should not be 
corrected because there was evidence against respondents. 
Correcting plain error, however, does not depend on a 
defendant’s innocence or guilt. Indeed, this Court has 
explained that an error can “seriously affect the fairness, 
integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings 
independent of the defendant's innocence.” Olano, 507 U.S. 
at 736-37 (internal quotation marks omitted) (emphasis 
added). Importantly, the Olano Court explained that a court 
“should” exercise its discretion to correct any plain error 
prejudicing a defendant’s substantial rights which meets this 
fourth prong of the plain error test. /d. at 736. 


Weighing evidence, which petitioner would have this Court 
do, is an analysis that applies to “the admission of . . . forms 


46 


of improperly admitted evidence.” Fulminante, 499 US. at 
310. It may be an appropriate exercise if the alleged error 
was minor or occurred during the presentation of the case to 
the petit jury. See Neder v. United States, 527 U.S. 1, 16-18 
(1999); Johnson, 520 U.S. at 467. The error in this case, 
however, is not about accuracy but about the core structure of 
the criminal process. In dismissing an indictment because of 
a complete lapse in the grand jury process, this Court 
explained that “[t]he injury is not limited to the 
defendant—there is injury to the jury system, to the law as an 
institution, to the community at large, and to the democratic 
ideal reflected in the processes of our courts.” Ballard v. 
United States, 329 US. 187, 195 (1946) (grand jury 
discrimination). The Court has similarly protected other 
rights that serve functions unrelated to outcome accuracy. In 
McKaskle v. Wiggins, 465 U.S. 168 (1984), for instance, the 
Court addressed a defendant’s right to represent himself at 
trial. The Court did not consider the evidence against the 
defendant (who was twice convicted by a jury, the first time 
on a defective indictment), because the right did not exist to 
promote the accuracy of the outcome but rather to protect 
dignitary values. See id at 177 n.8, 182. 


Evaluation of the evidence is similarly inapposite here, 
where the right to a grand jury protects values beyond the 
truth-seeking function of the trial. Like the right to self- 
representation, the right to a grand jury “is either respected or 
denied,” id. at 177 n.8, and its denial is not amenable to an 
analysis of the error’s effect on the outcome. Evidence- 
weighing is as useful in analyzing the harm of sentencing for 
an uncharged offense as a thermometer is in measuring the 
weight of a pile of bricks. 


Contrary to petitioner's argument, United States v. 
Mechanik, 475 U.S. 66 (1986), does not suggest a different 
result. In Mechanik, a properly constituted grand jury 
returned a complete indictment against defendants. However, 


47 


the prosecutor had allowed two witnesses to be present at the 
same time before the grand jury. /d. at 67. This was arguably 
a violation of the terms of Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 
6(d), which refers in the singular to “the [grand jury] witness 
under examination.” Without even deciding that the two- 
witness appearance breached the terms of Rule 6(d), the Court 
held that the costs of “reversal of a conviction” and retrial 
would outweigh the harm of such an error. Mechanik, 475 
U.S. at 72. The potential technical error in Mechanik did not 
go to the core functions of the grand jury (indictment) and 
instead went only to the procedural mechanism of the grand 
jury’s review of evidence and testimony. It stands in stark 
contrast to the fundamental and acknowledged errors in the 
instant case. In Mechanik, the mistake was, at most, a 
violation of a rule, whereas in the instant case, substantive, 
pervasive, and systemic errors violated the Fifth and Sixth 
Amendments and impaired the very core of the federal 
criminal justice system. 


In this case, the multiple errors went not just to the question 
of accuracy—though certainly the wrong standard of proof 
employed by the wrong fact-finder has an impact on the 
accuracy of the trial result—but also to values reaching 
beyond the correctness of verdicts. By abandoning the 
requirement that a grand jury must return a indictment 
charging an offense before a federal court can sentence a 
defendant for that offense, the district court (albeit 
unwittingly) allowed the structural integrity of the proceeding 
to be irreparably compromised. Weighing the evidence 
cannot repair the harm to the judicial process.

culpable defendants did not receive higher sentences, Pet. Br. at 49, cuts 
against petitioner. The fault for any such discrepancy lies with the 
prosecution for not formally charging the more serious offense against 
those who committed a more serious offense.

C. The Lower Court’s Choice To Reverse In A Case 
Where Judicial Integrity Was Compromised 
Preserves The Discretionary Nature Of Plain- 
Error Review. 


To sentence a defendant for an offense that was not charged 
in an indictment or found by a jury is an error that will always 
affect the fairness, integrity, and public reputation of the 
judicial process. However, the court of appeals did not, as 
petitioner suggests, institute a per se rule. Instead, the court 
chose to exercise its discretion: 


Because we believe that the “nature of the error” is 

“fundamental,” that the “plain error was committed in a 

matter so absolutely vital to defendants,” and, most 

importantly, that the error “seriously affect[s] the 

fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial 

proceedings,” we “feel ourselves at liberty to correct it.” 
Pet. App. 12a (emphasis added) (internal citations omitted) 
(alteration in original). The decision of the court of appeals 
did not limit courts’ discretion to notice or not notice errors, 
as the petitioner misstates the holding. See Pet. Br. at 12. 
Rather, the court elected to exercise its discretion because it 
found that the error injured the integrity of the proceedings. 
Rule 52(b) grants lower courts the discretion to correct such 
errors. 


When the court of appeals enjoys “discretion” to make a 
determination, this Court should not reverse that 
determination absent an abuse of that discretion. See, e.g., 
United States Nat'l Bank of Oregon v. Indep. Ins. Agents of 
Am., Inc., 508 U.S. 439, 447-48 (1993) (holding that the court 
of appeals “had discretion to consider” an issue not raised by 
either party, and its decision to consider the issue “was 
certainly no abuse of its discretion”); Weisgram v. Marley 
Co., 528 U.S. 440, 454, 456 (2000) (affirming the circuit 
court in its “exercise of ‘informed discretion” because it “did 
not abuse its discretion”). It is entirely conceivable that a 


49 


court might decline to exercise its discretion to notice an error 
in a closer case where a genuine issue arose as to whether the 
indictment was indeed fatally defective. But it is 
inconceivable that a court’s choice to notice the Fifth and 
Sixth Amendment errors in this case constitutes an improper 
exercise of its statutory discretion. 


In fact, petitioner's argument, if accepted, would do just 
what it accuses the court of appeals of doing: it would 
eliminate a court’s discretion under plain-error review. If 
petitioner is successful in promoting its newly fashioned rule, 
then a court will be unconditionally barred from correcting an 
error—no matter how seriously the error affected the 
integrity, fairness, or reputation of the proceeding—f there is 
a certain quantity of evidence against the defendant. The per 
se approach advanced by petitioner is inconsistent with the 
discretionary nature of plain-error review.

This Court has already explained the consequences of 
allowing a trial court to convict or sentence a defendant for an 
offense that was not in an indictment: 


If it lies within the province of a court to change the 
charging part of an indictment to suit its own notions of 
what it ought to have been, or what the grand jury would 
probably have made it if their attention had been called 
to suggested changes, the great importance which the 
common law attaches to an indictment by a grand jury, 
as a prerequisite to a prisoner’s trial for a crime, and 
without which the Constitution says “no person shall be 
held to answer,” may be frittered away until its value is 
almost destroyed. 
Bain, 121 US. at 10. The historic place of the grand jury in 
protecting the accused from the government underscores the 
centrality of the indictment process in a criminal proceeding. 
Similarly, when the prosecution withholds an element from 


50 


the grand jury and the court removes an element from the 
petit jury, to instead sentence the defendant for a different 
offense under the wrong standard of proof, the criminal 
justice system has been dealt a severe, if not fatal, blow. To 
ignore such errors would require this Court to discard 
centuries of precedent protecting the rights of defendants and 
the integrity of the process itself in favor of a system that 
seeks conviction at all costs. The Framers of the Constitution 
rejected that system more than 200 years ago. This Court 
should implement the Framers’ intent.

For the reasons stated above, the judgment of the court of 
appeals should be affirmed.
