STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


This case involves federa! court review of an order 
entered by the Public Service Commission of Maryland 
("Maryland Commission" or "MDPSC"). The Maryland 
Commission is an agency of the State of Maryland and it 
supervises and regulates telecommunications carriers in 
Maryland. As part of its responsibilities, the Maryland 
Commission administers and enforces the Public Utility 
Companies Article ("PUC Article") of the Maryland Code. 
Final orders or decisions of the Maryland Commission are 
subject to judicial review in Maryland circuit courts, PUC 
Article, §3-204. Parties are entitled to further review by the 
Maryland Court of Special Appeals. Jd. at §3-209. 


This case began at the Maryland Commission when 
Verizon Maryland, Inc. ("Verizon") filed a request for 
declaratory ruling asking that the Maryland Commission find 
that Verizon was not obligated under §251 of the 
Telecommunications Act of 1996 ("1996 Act") to pay 
reciprocal compensation for calls delivered to Internet 
Service Providers ("ISPs") who are served by competitive 
local exchange carriers ("CLECs"). The Maryland 
Commission issued a declaratory ruling finding that Verizon 
must continue to pay reciprocal compensation and Verizon 
sought review of this order pursuant to §252(e)(6) of the 
1996 Act, 47 U.S.C. §251 et. seq. and 28 U.S.C. §1331. 


The District Court of Maryland dismissed Verizon's 
complaint, finding that both the Maryland Commission and 
the individual commissioners were immune from suit under 
the Eleventh Amendment (Pet. App. 77a-84a);' and also

pursuant to 28 U.S.C. §1331 (Pet. App. 90a). On appeal, the 
United States Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit held 
that the Maryland Commission did not waive its Eleventh 
Amendment immunity when it implemented the 1996 Act.

A. THE TELECOMMUNICATIONS ACT OF 1996 
Congress enacted the 1996 Act, Pub. L. No. 104-104, 


110 Stat. 56 (codified at 47 U.S.C. §251 et seq.), to open the 
local telecommunications markets to competition” The

> At the time the 1996 Act was passed, 19 states had in place rules 
opening the local exchange market to competition. In seven states, 
competing firms already had begun to offer switched local service. 
These states were California, Illinois, Maryland, Massachusetts, 
Michigan, New York and Washington. Competition rules were in place 
in Arizona, Colorado, Connecticut, Florida, Georgia, Iowa, Louisiana, 
North Carolina, Ohio, Oregon, Tennessee and Virginia. See, Common 
Carrier Competition Report, CC Report No. 96-9, FCC, Common 
Carrier Bureau (Spring 1996) at page 3.

1996 Act* imposes a general duty on all telecommunications 
carriers to interconnect with other telecommunications 
carriers. 47 U.S.C. §251(a). With regard to incumbent local 
exchange carriers ("ILECs") such as Verizon, the duty to 
interconnect is even more specifically defined by §251(c)€2). 
In conjunction with these interconnection obligations, the 
1996 Act requires all local exchange carriers to establish 
reciprocal compensation arrangements for the transport and 
termination of telecommunications. 47 U.S.C. §251(b)(5). 
For purposes of compliance with this subsection, the 1996 
Act provides that such terms and conditions must provide for 
the recovery by each carrier of costs associated with the 
transport and termination on each carrier's network facilities 
of calls that originate on the network facilities of the other 
carriers. Further, such terms and conditions must determine 
costs on the basis of a reasonable approximation of the 
additional costs of terminating such calls. 47 U.S.C. 
§252(d)(2)(A). However, ILECs may negotiate and enter in 
a binding agreement with a requesting carrier without regard 
to the standards set forth in subsections (b) and (c) of §251. 


The procedures for negotiations between Competitive 
Local Exchange Carriers ("CLECs") and ILECs are 
governed by §252 of the 1996 Act. If negotiating companies 
cannot reach agreement, §252 provides that the parties may 
request that the appropriate State commission arbitrate 
unresolved issues. Each interconnection agreement must be 
submitted to the State commission for approval, regardless of 
whether the agreement was negotiated by the parties or 
arbitrated by the State commission. 47 U.S.C. §252(e)(1). 
However, the State commission may only reject a negotiated 
agreement if it finds that the agreement discriminates against 
a telecommunications carrier, or the implementation of such

B. PROCEEDINGS BEFORE THE MARYLAND 
COMMISSION 


Pursuant to the 1996 Act, MFS Intelenet of 
Maryland, Inc. ("MFS") and Verizon entered into an 
interconnection agreement on July 16, 1996. The Maryland 
Commission approved this agreement on October 9, 1996. 
On April 28, 1997, Verizon informed MFS that it would 
discontinue payment of reciprocal compensation for calls to 
ISPs, claiming that the Agreement's reciprocal compensation 
provisions did not apply to calls to ISPs. On May 21, 1997, 
MFS filed a complaint with the Maryland Commission 
alleging that the actions of Verizon violated the 1996 Act 
and the MFS Agreement. On September 11, 1997, the 
Maryland Commission found that Verizon must pay 
reciprocal compensation to MFS for calls to ISPs under the 
terms of the Agreement. The Maryland Commission's 
decision was upheld by the Circuit Court for Montgomery 
County, Maryland.° 


The Maryland Commission considered this issue a 
second time within the context of a Sprint Communications 
Company, L.P. ("Sprint") arbitration complaint. Sprint filed 
a Motion for Resolution of Disputed Issue, seeking a 
Commission ruling on how ISP-bound calls should be 
treated. The Maryland Commission concluded that ISP- 
bound calls “are classified as local in nature and are 
therefore, subject to reciprocal compensation" from Verizon. 


On February 26, 1999, the Federal Communications 
Commission ("FCC") released an Order addressing the

Cc. THE DISTRICT COURT DECISION 


Verizon sought review of the Maryland 
Commission's Order in the United States District Court for 
the District of Maryland. First, Verizon objected to the 
Maryland Commission's determination that Verizon should 
continue to pay reciprocal compensation for ISP-bound calls 
pursuant to the terms of the previously approved 
interconnection agreement. Verizon also objected to the 
Maryland Commission's determination that CLECs who do 
not have approved interconnection agreements shall receive 
the arbitrated reciprocal compensation rates contained in the 
effective SGAT as an interim compensation mechanism. 


On October 20, 1999, the District Court dismissed 
Verizon's Complaint, holding that: (1) the Maryland 
Commission and the individual commissioners were immune 
from suit under the Eleventh Amendment® (Pet. App. 77a- 
84a); (2) the Maryland Commission was an indispensable 
party under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 19(b) so that the 
case could not proceed without the Commission (Pet. App. 
84a- 86a); (3) alternatively, Verizon's challenge to that 
portion of the Commission's Order relating to the SGAT was

D. THE FOURTH CIRCUIT DECISION 


Verizon and the United States Government appealed the 
District Court's decision. On February 14, 2001, the United 
States Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit affirmed the 
District Court's decision. The Fourth Circuit reached four 
major conclusions. First, and foremost, the Court of 
Appeals found that the federal proceeding brought pursuant 
to §252(e)(6) of the 1996 Act against the Maryland 
Commission is constitutionally barred. The Fourth Circuit 
expressly found that Congress did not clearly manifest an 
intent to condition a State commission's participation in the 
regulation of interconnection agreements on a waiver of 
sovereign immunity from private suit. (Pet. App. 19a). 


Second, and equally important, the Fourth Circuit 
also found that an action brought in federal court against the 
individual commissioners was constitutionally barred. 
Among other reasons, the Court of Appeals found that a 
federal court action against the State commissioners would 
improperly expand the federal remedy selected by 
Congress. (Pet. App. 29a). 


The third determination by the Fourth Circuit was 
that §252(e)(6) does not confer jurisdiction on the federal 
courts to review a State commission's enforcement 
decisions. The Fourth Circuit found that because federal 
courts are courts of limited jurisdiction, decisions by State 
commissions that are not expressly designated for review in 
federal court are left for review by State courts. (Pet. App. 
35a). The Court of Appeals then held that State 
commission orders administering and enforcing 
interconnection agreements are not §252 determinations 
(Pet. App. 47a). Thus, according to the Court, these orders 
are reviewable only by State courts. (/d.) 


Finally, with regard to federal question jurisdiction, 
the Fourth Circuit held that 28 U.S.C. §1331 does not 
provide federal jurisdiction because Congress intended 
federal jurisdiction to be limited to the grant in 47 U.S.C. 
§252(e)(6). The Court of Appeals found that it "would be 
nonsensical to conclude that even though Congress 
carefully articulated jurisdictional responsibility in the 1996 
Act, it nonetheless intended to have 28 U.S.C. §1331 
override this effort." (Pet. App. 48a). According to the 
Fourth Circuit, "[t]he simple fact that interconnection 
agreements are "creations of federal law" does not make 
their interpretation a sufficiently substantial federal question 
to confer jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. §1331." (Pet. App. 
49a) (citations omitted). 


Judge King dissented, concluding that: (1) §252(e)(6) 
expressly confers jurisdiction on the federal courts to review 
the State's enforcement determinations; (/d. at 56a); (2) the 
States waived their sovereign immunity by participating in 
the regulation of interconnection agreements; and (3) the 
individual Commissioners could be sued for prospective 
injunctive relief. (/d. at 67a.) Judge King found it 
unnecessary to reach the 28 U.S.C. §1331 jurisdictional 
question. Id. at 58a n. 5. However, he asserted that the 
Rooker-Feldman doctrine was inapplicable. (/d.) (citations 
omitted).

II. THE ELEVENTH AMENDMENT MANDATES 
THAT THIS CASE BE DISMISSED. 
A. The Maryland Commission Has Not 
Waived Its Sovereign Immunity. 


Generally, the Eleventh Amendment divests federal 
courts of jurisdiction over suits brought by private parties 
against a state. See, Marie O. v. Edgar, 131 F.3d 610, 614 
(7™ Cir. 1997). The Eleventh Amendment presupposes that 
each state is a sovereign entity in the federal system and that 
"{ijt is inherent in the nature of sovereignty not to be 
amendable to the suit of an individual without [a state's] 
consent." Hans v. Louisiana, 134 U.S. 1 (1890). This 
Amendment has been interpreted to bar federal jurisdiction 
over suits against a state arising under the Constitution or 
Laws of the United States, whether those suits are brought by 
citizens of another state or by the state's own citizens. /d. 
The Eleventh Amendment im;oses a_ constitutional 
limitation on the jurisdiction of Article III courts. Pennhurst 
State School & Hospital v. Halderman, 465 U.S. 89, 98 
(1984). Eleventh Amendment immunity is similarly 
applicable to the Commission as an arm of the State of 
Maryland. See, Puerto Rico Aqueduct and Sewer Authority 
v. Metcalf & Eddy, Inc., 506 U.S. 139, 142-46 (1993). 


Previously, the Supreme Court identified three 
instances in which the Eleventh Amendment does not 
deprive an Article III court of jurisdiction to entertain 
allegations of state governmental wrongdoing. First, 
Congress may abrogate the States’ Eleventh Amendment 
immunity "by making its intention unmistakably clear" in the 
text of a federal statute enacted pursuant to a constitutional 
provision that empowers Congress with abrogation nights. 
Dellmuth v. Muth, 491 U.S. 223, 228 (1989). Second, a state 
may waive Eleventh Amendment immunity Parden v. 


26 


Terminal Ry. Co., 377 U.S. 184 (1964). Finally, a court may 
consider a suit against a state official to enjoin a non- 
discretionary violation of federal law. Ex parte Young, 209 
U.S. at 159-60. 


This Court has repeatedly held that the Congressional 
power to abrogate the States’ sovereign immunity can only 
be exercised by a clear legislative statement. Dellmuth v. 
Muth, 491 U.S. at 227-228 (1989). In Blatchford v. Noatak, 
501 U.S. 775 (1991), the Supreme Court expressly found 
that §1331 does not suffice to abrogate immunity for all 
federal questions. /d. at 78. As noted by the Blatchford 
Court, "the fact that Congress granted jurisdiction to hear a 
claim does not suffice to show Congress has abrogated all 
defenses to that claims." /d. at 786 n. 4 (Emphasis in 
original). 


This Court's recent Seminole decision places 
limitations on a party's ability to bypass a State's immunity 
from suit in federal court. The decision restricts the power 
of Congress to abrogate state immunity. The decision also 
narrows the scope of the Ex parte Young exception to 
Eleventh Amendment immunity. Finally, Seminole arguably 
left open the issue of whether a State may impliedly waive 
its immunity to suit in federal court. 


However, in College Savings, the Supreme Court 
repudiated the doctrine of constructive waiver. The Court 
found that the "constructive waiver experiment was ill 
conceived" and it overruled “whatever may remain of 
Parden," that doctrine's seminal decision. 


Petitioners below maintained that constructive waiver 
is possible when Congress provides unambiguously that a 
State will be subject to suit if it engages in certain federally 
regulated conduct and the State voluntarily engages in that 


27 


conduct. The Supreme Court disagreed, finding that even an 
unambiguous statement of Congressional intent cannot be 
reconciled with the requirement that a State's waiver of 
sovereign immunity be unequivocal. College Savings, 527 
U.S. at 680. (Citations omitted). Thus, a state does not 
consent to suit in Federal court simply by engaging in 
federally regulated conduct even where Congress has 
provided that the State will be subject to private suit if it 
engages in that conduct. /d. at 680-681. See also, Fla. Dept. 
of Health v. Fla. Nursing Home Asso., 450 U.S. 147, 150 
(1981) (State's participation or agreement to administer a 
federal program does not constitute a waiver of Eleventh 
Amendment immunity). 


This Court should determine that the Maryland 
Commission cannot be found to have waived its sovereign 
immunity for two reasons. First, Congress lacks the 
constitutional authority, pursuant to the Commerce Clause, 
to condition the State's participation on that state's waiver of 
its Eleventh Amendment immunity. Second, the 1996 Act is 
neither a "gift" nor a "gratuity". 


1) Congress Lacks the Constitutional 
Authority to Condition Grants Under the 
Commerce Clause. 


In light of Seminole and College Savings, even 
assuming Congress expressly conditioned state participation 
in the regulatory scheme of the 1996 Act on waiver of the 
State's immunity to suit in federal court, Congress 
nevertheless was without authority to do so under the 
Commerce Clause. Art. I §8, Cl. 3. Any argument based on 
constructive waiver presupposes that Congress has the 
authority pursuant to Article I of the Constitution to force a 
state to waive its Eleventh Amendment immunity as a 
condition of participating in a field subject to federal 


28 


regulation. However, the Seminole Court stated in no 
uncertain terms, that "the Eleventh Amendment restricts the 
judicial power under Article III, and Article I cannot be used 
to circumvent the constitutional limitations placed on federal 
jurisdiction." 517 U.S. at 72-73. Thus, as noted by the 
College Savings Court, the Seminole decision “expressly 
repudiates” the proposition that Congress has authority, 
under the Commerce Clause, to expand Federal jurisdiction. 
College Savings, 527 U.S. at 683. Therefore, allowing 
federal courts to apply the doctrine of constructive waiver 
based on Congress’ commerce powers would directly 
contravene the Supreme Court's express repudiation of this 
doctrine in Seminole. - 


The Seminole Court found that Congress cannot 
abrogate a state's Eleventh Amendment immunity pursuant 
to the Commerce Clause. 517 U.S. at 69. More 
specifically, the Seminole Court stated that "[e]ven when the 
Constitution rests in Congress complete lawmaking authority 
over a particular area, the Eleventh Amendment prevents 
Congressional authorization of suits by private parties 
against unconsenting states." 517 U.S. at 72. The impact of 
Seminole's conclusion was explicitly stated by the College 
Savings Court, which found that "[rjecognizing a 
congressional power to extract constructive waivers of 
sovereign immunity through the exercise of Article I powers 
would also, as a practical matter, permit Congress to 
circumvent the anti-abrogation holding of Seminole Tribe." 
527 US. at 683. 


Therefore, the conclusion that Congress could have 
excluded states from any role in the 1996 Act is irrelevant to 
determining whether a state has constructively waived its 
immunity. Under analysis, Congress would be permitted to 
do indirectly what it cannot do directly, namely, abrogate a 
state's immunity pursuant to the Commerce Clause by 


29 


making abrogation a "condition" of participation. See, eg. 
Abril v. Virginia, 145 F.3d 182, 190 n. 15 (4 Cir. 1998). 


Since Congress has no authority under its Article I 
Commerce Clause powers to abrogate state sovereign 
immunity, Seminole 517 U.S. at 71-72, it follows the 
Congress may not make the waiver of sovereign immunity a 
condition to the state's action in a field that Congress has 
authority to regulate under the Commerce Clause. In the 
present case, where Congress is not empowered to abrogate 
the Eleventh Amendment immunity conisrred upon the 
Commission directly, likewise it cannot do so indirectly 
through principles of constructive waiver. The Maryland 
Commission has not waived such protection by voluntarily 
participating in the regulation of telecommunications. The 
telecommunications. industry indisputably involves a 
quintessential state interest - the provision of 
telecommunications services to the citizenry at competitive 
rates - protection of which Congress cannot condition on a 
state's waiver of vital state rights. 


This Court should find that Congress lacks the 
constitutional authority to require states to waive their 
Eleventh Amendment immunity when it enacts legislation 
pursuant to the Commerce Clause. Since the Supreme Court 
has previously ruled that Congress cannot abrogate States’ 
immunity through the Commerce Clause, it would be 
illogical to permit Congress to condition any "gift" on 
accepting abrogation of Eleventh Amendment immunity 
pursuant to this very same constitutional provision.

B. This Proceeding Does Not Meet the 
Requirements of the Ex parte Young 


Exception 


Verizon attempted to avoid the bar of Eleventh 
Amendment sovereign immunity by naming the Commission 
members individually in their official capacities. See, Ex 
parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908). Seminole represents the 
first Supreme Court decision since Ex parte Young to hold 
that the Eleventh Amendment bars an action against a State 
official for purely prospective relief to prevent ongoing 
violations of federal law. 


Relief under Ex parte Young may be foreclosed if the 
statute in question provides "a detailed remedial scheme,” if 
that remedy is "significantly more limited than . . . the 
liability upon the state officer under Ex parte Young” and if 
Congress did not to "authorize federal jurisdiction under Ex 
parte Young over a causes of action with a limited remedial 
scheme." Seminole, 517 U.S. 44, 74-75 (1996). 


The Seminole Court found that "where Congress has 
prescribed a detailed remedial scheme for the enforcement 
against a state of a statutorily created right, a court should 
hesitate before casting aside those limitations and permitting 
an action against a state officer based upon Ex parte Young." 
Seminole, 517 U.S. at 74. This Court should find that the 
1996 Act constitutes a comprehensive statutory scheme and 
thus the Ex parte Young doctrine does not apply. 


In Seminole, after rejecting the tribe's abrogation 
argument, the Court turned to the tribe's alternative 
contention that Ex parte Young served as a means by which 
to obtain relief in federal court. The tribe contended that 


36 


federal court jurisdiction existed to proceed in an action 
against the Governor to obtain injunctive relief. 


However, the Seminole Court held the Ex parte 
Young doctrine inapplicable. The Court reasoned that in 
contrast to the "modest set of sanctions" Congress 
established in the IGRA, a suit under Ex parte Young would 
expose the defendant State official to the "full remedial 
powers of a federal court." Also, allowing a suit for relief 
under Ex parte Young would render ineffective the remedies 
Congress designed. The Court acknowledged that Congress 
has authority to establish federal court jurisdiction over State 
officials comparable to Ex parte Young jurisdiction, but 
concluded that Congress had not applied such authority 
when it wrote the IGRA. Of significance, the Court ruled 
that even though Congress’ remedial scheme under the IGRA 
did not work because it violated the State's Eleventh 
Amendment nights, the Court could not "rewrite the statutory 
scheme in order to approximate what we think Congress 
might have wanted." Seminole, 517 U.S. at 74-76. 


The Seminole Tribe decision places an obligation on 
the federal courts to examine Congressional intent with 
respect to the scope of statutory remedies that may be 
available in any case where the Ex parte Young doctrine is 
asserted to remove the Eleventh Amendment bar. 
Application of the Supreme Court doctrine espoused in 
Seminole must lead to the conclusion that finding jurisdiction 
under Ex parte Young in this imstance would be 
inappropriate. In Seminole, the Supreme Court found that 
when a statute implies that Congress intended an alternative, 
specific form of relief be available to enforce compliance 
with that statute, a court should infer that Congress did not 
intend the federal court to provide traditional Ex parte Young 
injunctive relief.

The Rooker-Feldman doctrine stems from two 
Supreme Court cases decided sixty years apart. In Rooker v. 
Fidelity Trust Co., 263 U.S. 413 (1923), the Supreme Court 
held that lower federal courts lack jurisdiction to entertain 
appeals from state court judgments because that power is 
reserved to the Supreme Court. In 1983, the Supreme Court 
decided District of Columbia Court of Appeals v. Feldman, 
460 U.S. 462 (1983), clarifying Rooker and giving the 
Rooker-Feidman doctrine its name. In Feldman, the 


42 


Supreme Court held that lower federal courts have no 
jurisdiction to hear "challenges to State court decisions in 
particular cases arising out of judicial proceedings", Jd. at 
486, or decide questions “inextricably intertwined" with 
State court judgments. Jd. at 483 n. 16. 


The Rooker-Feldman doctrine is founded on two 
main inferences derived from provisions of Title 28 of the 
United States Code. The first inference is that 28 U.S.C. 
§1257, which gives the Supreme Court appellate review of 
certa:1 State court decisions, is the only federal statute that 
explictly provides for federal review of State court 
decisious. Congress granted original jurisdiction to federal 
district courts under a variety of statutes, including 28 U.S.C. 
§§1331 and 1332. Under this statutory scheme, federal 
district courts are courts of orginal, not appellate 
jurisdiction. Thus, by inference, 28 U.S.C. §1257 granted 
jurisdiction exclusively to the Supreme Court to review final 
judgments or decrees from the highest court in a state. The 
second inference is that Congress intended to foreclose all 
other forms of federal court review of State decisions. 
Because Congress only gave the Supreme Court the explicit 
authority to review the decisions of a state court, Congress 
meant to deny all other federal courts that power. 


Thus, the Rooker-Feldman doctrine is an extension of 
the principle that federal courts are courts of limited 
jurisdiction. Also underlying the Rooker-Feldman doctrine 
is the idea of federalism. The doctrine is designed to 
preserve the two distinct legal systems so that each may 
proceed independently of the other with the Supreme Court 
as the final arbiter of any federal questions raised in either 
system. The policy reasons which underlies the doctrine 
apply just as readily to state agency decisions rendered in 
quasi-judicial proceedings as they do to State court 
decisions.

In reaching this conclusion, the Supreme Court analyzed the
