The Sixth Amendment provides: 


In all criminal prosecutions, the accused shall 
enjoy the right to a speedy and public trial, by an 
impartial jury of the state and district wherein the

SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT 


Although the Sixth Amendment guarantees “the 
assistance of counsel .. . [ijn all criminal prosecutions,” 
that constitutional protection extends the State's 
obligation to provide counsel only in those misdemeanor 
cases involving actual incarceration. This Court 
established that standard in Argersinger v. Hamlin, 407 
U.S. 25, 33, 38 (1972), by requiring appointed counsel 
only where prosecution “actually fled] to imprisonment” 
or “imprisonment actually occur[red].” This Court has 
twice refined that principle. First, this Court 
distinguished between “actual imprisonment” — which 
invokes the right to appointed counsel — and “fines or the 
mere threat of imprisonment” in Scott v. Illinois, 440 U.S. 
367, 373 (1979). Then, in Nichols v. United States, 511 
U.S. 738, 746 (1994), this Court declared that appointed 
counsel is needed only where a conviction “result[s] in 
imprisonment.” Shelton, who received a probationary 
sentence, remained at liberty, subject to a possibility of 
imprisonment only upon noncompliance with the terms of 
his release. Because Shelton faced only the “threat” of 
incarceration, he had no right to appointed counsel during 
the trial of his misdemeanor case. 


Notwithstanding the distinction drawn between 
“actual imprisonment” and the “mere threat” of 
incarceration in Argersinger, Scott, and Nichols, the 
Alabama Supreme Court concluded that imposition of a 
suspended or probationary sentence in a misdemeanor 
case triggers the right to counsel. This interpretation 
contravenes the construction given the Sixth Amendment 
by this Court in Scott and the majority of federal appeals 
and state supreme courts that have addressed the issue. 
Accordingly, this Court should reverse and reinstate 
Shelton’s probationary sentence.

ARGUMENT 


SHELTON’S RECEIPT OF A SUSPENDED 
SENTENCE IN HIS MISDEMEANOR CASE WAS A 
“MERE THREAT OF IMPRISONMENT” UNDER 
SCOTT V. ILLINOIS AND DID NOT INVOKE HIS 
SIXTH AMENDMENT RIGHT TO APPOINTED 
COUNSEL. 


Under the Sixth Amendment, the right to state- 
appointed counsel extends only to those cases involving 
actual incarceration. That conclusion is the product of 
three decisions. Initially, in Argersinger v. Hamlin, 407 
U.S. 25, 33, 38 (1972), this Court found that counsel was 
required only where “imprisonment actually occur[red].” 
This Court refined that principle in Scott v. Illinois, 440 
U.S. 367, 373 (1979), by differentiating between “actual 
imprisonment” and “fines or the mere threat of 
imprisonment.” Finally, this Court affirmed the 
distinction drawn in Scott by recognizing in Nichols v. 
United States, 511 U.S. 738, 746 (1994), that only 
“criminal proceedings that result{]) in imprisonment” 
invoke the right to counsel. Shelton remained at liberty 
under his probationary sentence, subject to a possibility of 
imprisonment only upon violation of the terms of his 
release. Therefore, because Shelton faced only the 
“threat” of incarceration, but was not actually imprisoned, 
he had no right to counsel during the trial of his 
misdemeanor case. 


A. This Court Has Interpreted the Sixth 
Amendment to Extend Misdemeanor 
Defendants the Right to Appointed Counsel 
Only When They Are Actually Imprisoned. 


The Sixth Amendment guarantees that “[ijJn all 
criminal prosecutions, the accused shall enjoy the right 


10 


... to have the assistance of counsel for his defense.” U.S. 
Const.. amend. VI. When “originally drafted,” this 
provision accorded only a right to retained counsel in 
federal criminal proceedings. Powell v. Alabama, 287 
U.S. 45, 68 (1932) (“Historically [a hearing] . has 
always included the right to the aid of counsel when 
desired and provided 5y the party asserting the right.”); 
Scott, 440 U.S. at 370 (the Sixth Amendment 
“contemplated [only] . . . the right of an accused in a 
criminal prosecution in a federal court to employ a lawyer 
to assist in his defense”). Despite the limited original 
purpose of the Sixth Amendment, this Court extended the 
right to include appointed counsel for indigent felony 
defendants. Powell, 287 U.S. at 71 (defendants charged 
in 1931 with capital crime who “stood in deadly peril of 
their lives” and were “unable to employ counsel” entitled 
to protection under Sixth Amendment); Johnson v. Zerbst, 
304 U.S. 458, 463 (1938) (right to appointed counsel for 
federal felony defendants facing loss of “life or liberty”); 
Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335, 340—45 (1963) (Sixth 
Amendment right to appointed counsel extended through 
Fourteenth Amendment to state felony prosecution where 
defendant faced five years in state prison). 


In the application of the Sixth Amendment to 
misdemeanors, this Court first focused on an individual's 
“deprivation of liberty” absent the advice and guidance of 
counsel in Argersinger, 407 U.S. at 32-34. Argersinger, 
charged in Florida with carrying a concealed weapon, 
faced a punishment of imprisonment up to six months, a 
$1,000 fine, or both. Jd. at 26. Following a bench trial, 
Argersinger was sentenced to serve 90 days in jail. Jd. 
Argersinger contested his uncounseled conviction im a 
state habeas corpus petition, alleging that he was 
deprived of his right to counsel. Jd. The Florida Supreme 
Court denied Argersinger relief, concluding that the right 
to appointed counsel extended only to trials involving 


11 


“non-petty offenses punishable by more than six months 
imprisonment.” Jd. After granting certiorari, this Court 
rejected the Florida “six-month” standard and ruled that, 
because Argersinger “was in fact sentenced to jail,” he 
was entitled to the assistance of appointed counsel. Jd. at 
37. 


This Court, however, rejected the broader arguments 
of Argersinger and the Legal Aid Society of New York, as 
amicus curiae, that appointment of counsel was required 
whenever “imprisonment was an authorized penalty.” 
Scott, 440 U.S. at 373 n.4. Drawing a bright line, this 
Court established that the right to appointed counsel 
applies only where prosecution “actually fled] to 
imprisonment” or “imprisonment actually occur[red].” 
Argersinger, 407 U.S. at 33, 38.

therefore know when to name a lawyer to

The run of misdemeanors will not be affected by 
today’s ruling. But in those that end up in the 
actual deprivation of a person’s liberty, the accused

C. If the Scott “Actual Imprisonment” Standard 
Was Interpreted to Require Appointment of 
Counsel When Suspended or Conditional 
Sentences Were Iinposed, it Would Divert 
Scarce Judicial Resources. 


The incremental benefit of providing counsel in 
situations where only the “threat” of imprisonment exists 
does not outweigh the strain that would be imposed on 
scarce governmental and judicial resources. Argersinger, 
407 U.S. at 49 (“[w]here the possibility of a jail sentence is 
remote ... the costs of assistance of counsel may exceed 
the benefits”) (Powell & Rehnquist, JJ., concurring). 
States and courts would incur additional expenses in 
compensating defense counsel and supplying additional 
judicial resources, such as extra courtroom time and court 


21 


reporters. Moreover, such a rule would “favor defendants 
classified as indigents over those not so classified, yet who 
are in low-income groups where engaging counsel in a 
minor petty-offense case would be a luxury the family 
could not afford.” Id. at 50. 


Concomitantly, trial courts’ abilities to adjudicate 
“minor” offenses efficiently and equitably would be 
thwarted by the escalating delay and congestion. Jd. at 
59 (pressures on obtaining counsel and diverting other 
resources would be “likely to result in the stretching out — 
of the process with consequent increased costs to the 
public and added delay and congestion in the courts”). 
Inundated with inevitable “overlawyering” of minor cases, 
the courts and the public would suffer the unnecessary 
effects of a “stretching out of the process[.]” Jd. 


Resources would be further exhausted in reviewing 
innumerable cases where an uncounseled misdemeanor 
conviction resulting in a suspended or conditional 
sentence was used, under Nichols, to increase the penalty 
for a subsequent crime. Diverting money and time to 
resolve misdemeanor offenses and reexamine the use of 
uncounseled convictions for enhancement purposes would 
drain local and state courts, thereby depleting the limited 
judicial resources available to adjudicate felony and 
especially capital cases. Finally, successful challenges 
would emasculate anti-recidivism statutes and their 
underlying policies t. safeguard the public and deter 
future crime. 


The “actual imprisonment” language employed in 
Scott and Nichols “is clear. Nowhere does [this] Court 
limit its holding to those misdemeanor convictions 
resulting in fines. Logically speaking, the ‘mere threat of 
imprisonment’ can only be read to include suspended 
sentences.” United States v. Smith, No. 94-5627, 1995 
WL 314892, at *2 (6th Cir. May 23, 1995) (unpublished 


22 


opinion). A suspended sentence, under which Shelton did 
not face a loss of liberty, fell within the “threat” category 
identified in Scott. In such circumstances, Shelton’s 
“interest in personal liberty [was] diminishjed], 
[therefore,] so [was] his right to appointed counsel.” 
Lassiter, 452 U.S. at 26. The Alabama Supreme Court 
ignored these principles and erroneously concluded that 
Shelton’s prospect of imprisonment invoked the right to 
counsel. Because that decision conflicts with this Court's 
decisions in Argersinger, Scott, and Nichols, and with the 
decisions of a majority of federal appeals and state 
supreme courts that have considered this issue, this 
Court should apply the “actual imprisonment” standard 
and rule that Shelton’s suspended jail term did not invoke 
the Sixth Amendment right to counsel.
