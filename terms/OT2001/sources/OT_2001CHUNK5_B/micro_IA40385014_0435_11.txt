QUESTIONS PRESENTED 


1. Whether a state commission’s order that interprets or 
otherwise affects a previously approved “interconnection 
agreement” is a “determination under [section 252]” and thus 
reviewable in federal court under 47 U.S.C. § 252(e)(6). 


2 Whether a state commission’s acceptance of Congress’ 
invitation to participate in implementing a federal regulatory 
scheme that provides that state commission determinations 
are reviewable in federal court constitutes a waiver of 
Eleventh Amendment immunity. 


3. Whether an official capacity action seeking prospective 
relief against state public utility commissioners for alleged 
ongoing violations of federal law in performing federal 
regulatory functions under the federal Telecommunications 
Act of 1996 can be maintained under the Ex parte Young 
doctrine.

STATEMENT OF THE CASE - 


In the Telecommunications Act of 1996, Congress enacted 
a new subchapter of the Communications Act that seeks 
rapidly to introduce competition into local telephone markets 
that heretofore had been intractable monopolies. 47 USC. 
§§ 251-261. These provisions preempt all state laws that 
could have the “effect” of preventing any firm from offering 
competitive local services, and they further impose a series of 
federal duties on the incumbent loca: exchange carriers 
(“incumbent LECs”) that require them to share certain of their 
network facilities with prospective competitors. 


These federal duties are expressed in very general terms, 
and there is nominally a three-step process for translating the 
general duties into concrete and enforceable legal rights. 
First, the Federal Communications Commission (“FCC”) 
issues regulations that define the federal duties with greater 
specificity. Second, any willing state commission (or the 
FCC) establishes “interconnection agreements” that further 
specify the details of these federal network sharing 
requirements between incumbent LECs and each carrier who 
requests interconnection. Third, in the course of the 
implementation of any one interconnection agreement, there ~ 
are proceedings in which the terms of the “approved” 
interconnection agreement will be interpreted, clarified, 
superceded, or modified and in which the incumbent LECs’ 
obligations are made still more concrete. Questions whether 
the incumbent LEC has violated the Act or the FCC’s 
regulations arise during each of the three steps in the process. 


The three stages in the definition and implementation of the 
LECs’ obligations are not discrete, but substantially overlap, 
both substantively and temporally. For example, there are 
pending proceedings in which the FCC is considering further 
modifications of its regulations, in which state commissions 
are interpreting previously approved interconnection 


2 


agreements or resolving “open issues” under them, and in 
which state commissions are arbitrating or reviewing new 
interconnection agreements that would supplement oF 
supercede the previously approved agreements For this 
reason, the interconnection agreements that are at issue In this 
case all expressly provide that the state commission retains 
jurisdiction to resolve issues arising under the agreement and 
provide for modification of the agreements in response to 
changes in the FCC regulations or other governing law. 


The Act imposes a federal duty on incumbent LECs to 
comply with both the terms of their federal interconnection 
agreements and the substantive requirements of the Act. 
§§ 251(c)(2)-(3).' The Act expressly gives injured persons a 
federal private right of action (in either federal court or at the 
FCC) against an incumbent LEC who violates these or any 
other requirements of the statute. §§ 206-209. Because 
Congress invited individual state commissions to play a 
central role in establishing the interconnection agreements 
that are a primary vehicle for implementing and enforcing the 
incumbent LECs’ new duties, the Act also provides another 
remedy that is at issue here: the provision of § 252(e) that 
allows federal court review of state commissions 
interconnection agreement determinations. 


This case presents a very narrow issue involving the scope 
of this § 252(e) remedy. As petitioners concede (Br. 18), 
§ 252(e)(6) provides for exclusive federal court jurisdiction 
when a state commission issues a decision that initially 
approves or rejects an interconnection agreement, in whole or 
in part, and requires the federal court to determine if the 
agreement meets the requirements of the Act and the FCC's 
regulations. But petitioners claim that the Act requires a 
different result when, as here, a state commission has retained 
jurisdiction to resolve open issues or to interpret or modify

the agreement and when, as here, a state commission enters 
an order that further specifies the LECs’ obligations under the 
agreement. In this event, petitioners claims that the state 
commission is not making “determinations under this section 
[252],” that the provisions of § 252(e) are inapplicable, and 
that any challenges to the state commission’s order can only 
be maintained in state courts. Petitioners further claim that 
the Eieventh Amendment would dictate this result even if the 
statute were ambiguous. 


As explained in detail below, petitioners’ claims rest on a 
wholly artificial distinction that has no support in the 
statutory text, that is inconsistent with the structure of the Act 
and the other federal remedies that it creates, and that would 
severely impede the efficient and orderly administration of 
this important federal statute. 


But to put these claims in their full context requires a more 
detailed description of (1) the new federal requirements 
adopted in 1996, (2) the rulemaking, interconnection 
agreement, and implementation proceedings that have 
occurred under this Act to date, (3) § 252(e) and the FCC’s 
construction of it, and (4) the specific controversy that gives 
rise to this case. 


1. In the 1996 Act, Congress set out to “end[] the 
longstanding regime of state-sanctioned monopolies” and 
“fundamentally restructure[] local telephone markets” so that 
they, like long distance services, could become effectively 
competitive. AT&T Corp. v. lowa Utils. Bd., 525 U.S. 366, 
371, 377, 378 n.5 (1999). The Act thus establishes a “new 
federal regime” designed to eliminate the legal and the 
economic impediments to local competition, id at 379 n.6 
(emphasis in original omitted), and “to shift monopoly 
[telephone] markets to competition as quickly as possible,” 
H.R. Rep. No. 104-204, at 89 (1995). 


Section 253 of the Act ends the legal barriers to local 
competition. It expressly preempts any state law that could 


4 


have the “effect” of preventing any firm from offering any 
telecommunications service. § 253(a). 


Sections 251 and 252 of the Act address economic barriers 
to local entry. Congress recognized that the local networks of 
the incumbent LECs are characterized by tremendous 
economies of scale and scope, and that new entrants could not 
replicate all of the facilities required to provide ubiquitous 
local calling in any geographic area. Congress thus enacted a 
number of provisions that require each incumbent LEC to 
“share its network with competitors” on rates and terms that 
are just, reasonable, and nondiscriminatory. A7&7 Corp., 
525 US. at 371. Incumbent LECs must, ier alia, allow 
potential competitors to: (i) “interconnect{]” with the 
incumbents’ networks, §251(c)(2), (ii) purchase 
“{u]nbundled access” to discrete elements of those networks 
at cost-based rates, §§ 251(c)(3), 252(d)(2), and (iii) purchase 
for resale any telecommunications services the incumbents 
provide to retail subscribers, § 251(c)(4). When competing 
and incumbent carriers interconnect, each must transport and 
terminate calls that originate on the others’ network, and the 
originating carrier compensates the terminating carrier 
through payment of “reciprocal compensation.” § 251(b)(5). 


Congress legislated these new federal obligations of

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


The Seventh Circuit correctly held that § 252(e)(6) gives 
federal courts jurisdiction over state commission orders that 
interpret previously approved interconnection agreements and 
that the exercise of this jurisdiction does not violate the 
Eleventh Amendment. There is no substance to any of the 
claims raised by petitioners. 


1. First, petitioners nave here raised an extreme and 
untenable statutory claim that rests on artificial distinctions 
that have no support in the statutory text and that is 
inconsistent with the structure of the statutory scheme as a 
whole. In particular, petitioners contend that § 252(e)(6) of 
the Act provides for federal court review of state 
determinations of the LECs’ obligations to provide 
interconnections to new entrants only when the 
determinations are made in connection with the initial 
“formation” and “approval” of an interconnection agreement. 
Petitioners claim that federal court review is not available 
under § 252(e)(6) over “subsequent commission action” in 
which the state commission enters orders that interpret, 
modify, or supercede the provisions of the interconnection 


14 


agreement after it was initially approved — and makes the 
same determinations under federal law that the commission 
could have made when it in ‘tially approved the agreement. 


Petitioners’ claims have no support in the text of 
§ 252(e)(6). That provision authorizes actions in an 
appropriate federal district court “in any case” in which a 
state commission makes “determinations under this section 
[252]” and provides that the federal court is to determine 
whether the resulting interconnection agreement or statement 
meets the requirements of §§ 251 and 252 and of the FCC’s 
implementing regulations. Under the terms of § 252, it makes 
no difference to federal court jurisdiction whether the state 
has made its “determinations” in the “proceeding” in which it 
initially approved the agreement or in connection with a 
subsequent proceeding or “matter” in which the state resolved 
open issues or otherwise interpreted or modified the 
agreement. In both events, the state commission action will 
squarely present the question whether the resulting agreement 
meets the requirements of federal law. 


Petitioners’ claims are also antithetical to the statutory 
scheme as a whole. Petitioners’ basic contention is that the 
“goals” of the Act are met by the initial approval of an 
interconnection agreement that is upheld by a federal court 
and that Congress intended thereafter to rely exclusively on 
state court proceedings to assure that the Act is implemented. 
That is simply wrong. Congress recognized that the initial 
approval of interconnection agreements that are susceptible of 
implementation in ways that meet the Act’s requirements is 
only a first step. The Act thus contains a whole panoply of 
federal remedies to assure that incumbent LECs do not 
subvert their agreements after they are approved and that the 
incumbent LECs in fact implement the agreements in a 
manner that satisfies the terms and purposes of the Act. 
Petitioners’ proposed construction of § 252(e)(6) could create 
conflicts with the Act’s other remedies.

2. Even if this suit had been a suit against the ICC, it 
waived any Eleventh Amendment claim. It has long been the 
rule that a state may waive its sovereign immunity by 
participating in a federal program that clearly conditions state 
participation on consent to federal jurisdiction. The state 
commissions had no independent right to implement the new 
federal regulatory scheme of section 252 without 
congressional authorization, for Congress could have 
delegated the administration of that scheme exclusively to the 
FCC. Instead, it granted state commissions the opportunity to 
participate, subject to their right to opt out of the scheme and 
the clear condition that parties aggrieved by determinations 
that they make under § 252 could seek judicial review in 
federal court. The ICC (which specifically declared that it 
was _ exercising section 252 jurisdiction) accepted the 
conditions imposed by section 252 in exercising its rights 
thereunder, and thus consented to federal court jurisdiction 
under section 252(e)(6). 


3. The ICC commissioners are also subject to federal court

Ex parte Young authorizes federal court jurisdiction whenever

always available if the plaintiff seeks prospective relief to 
vindicate federal statutory rights. Moreover, unlike the 
Statute at issue in Seminole Tribe v. Florida, 517 US. 44 
(1996), the Act does not prescribe a detailed remedial scheme 
inconsistent with Ex parte Young jurisdiction.

Like other courts of appeals, the Seventh Circuit held that 
state commissions orders that interpret, modify, or enforce 
previously approved interconnection agreements are subject 
to judicial review in federal district court under section 
252(e)(6). Petitioners contend that this holding is contrary to 
the plain language of section 252(e)(6) which, they claim, 
provides for federal court review only of decisions that 
initially approve agreements. They further contend that the 
doctrine of constitutional avoidance would compel this Court 
to reverse even if the matter were doubtful. 


Each of the petitioners’ arguments lacks merit. “(T]he 
meaning of statutory language, plain or not, depends on 
context,” King v. St. Vincents Hosp., 502 US. 215, 221 
(1991), and thus courts must “‘look to the provisions of the 
whole law, and to its object and policy.”” United States Nat'l 
Bank v. Independent Ins. Agents of Am., Inc., 508 US. 439, 
455 (1993). But petitioners are urging artificial distinctions 
that have no support in the text of § 252(e)(6) itself. Further, 
their proposed distinctions are inconsistent with the statutory 
scheme as a whole, would create conflicts with the Act's 
other federal remedies, and would serve no rational purpose. 


A. Section 252(e)(6) Provides For Federal Court 
Review Of All State Determinations Of The 
Lawfulness Of The Requirements That They 
Prescribe, Irrespective Of When They Are Made. 


The interconnection agreements authorized in section 252 
are a principal mechanism for implementing the new federal 
duties that Congress imposed on incumbent LECs to create 
competition in local telephone markets. See AT&T Corp. v. 


17 


lowa Utils. Bd., 525 U.S. 366, 371-73 (1999). In section 252, 
Congress granted willing state commissions the authority to 
establish the requirements of interconnection agreements, 
subject to federal district court review that would assure that 
the resulting obligations meet the requirements of sections 
251 and 252 of the Act and the FCC’s implementing 
regulations. 


In the discharge of these obligations to date, state 
commissions have not attempted definitively to resolve all of 
the issues that may arise between the parties in advance of the 
initial approval of the agreements. To the contrary, they have 
approved agreements that they believed would be susceptible 
of being implemented in a manner that would satisfy the 
standards of the Act, and by approving an agreement within 
the statutory ten month period, they advanced the statutory 
purpose of allowing the rapid implementation of network 
sharing arrangements that will allow competition to develop. 
At the same time, for practical reasons, the state commissions 
deferred the resolution of certain open issues and more 
detailed statements of the incumbent LECs’ responsibilities to 
future proceedings that the state commissions retained 
jurisdiction to conduct. For example, at the time of the initial 
approvals of agreements, state commissions had almost 
uniformly made no attempt to establish permanent rates for 
interconnections and network elements that meet the 
standards of § 252(d) of the Act. Instead, they have approved 
agreements that contain “interim rates,” but that provide that 
permanent rates would be set in future arbitration cr industry- 
wide proceedings under the Act’s standards and that those 
rates would supercede those set forth in the previously 
approved agreement. See, e.g., US West Communications v. 
MFS Intelenet, Inc., 193 F.3d 1112, 1118 (9th Cir. 1999). 


Similarly, the agreements did not specify the LECs’ 
obligations with respect to every conceivable type of facility 
or service arrangement that can be claimed to fall within the 
duties covered by the agreements. Nor did they generally 


18 


prescribe all of the specific performance standards that must 
be met to satisfy the agreements’ and the Act’s general 
requirements. Instead, they provide a mechanism for 
resolving these questions in the future by providing that when 
there are specific disputes about the interpretation of the 
agreements and their application to specific arrangements, 
those disputes may be brought to the state commissions. See, 
e.g., JA 28a; Addenda 1-2. In this regard, the parties 
recognized that fundamental questions involving the 
obligations of incumbent LECs can be resolved by orders that 
interpret previously approved agreements. Indeed, orders that 
nominally interpret agreements can constitute modifications 


of the agreements. 


In addition, the parties have recognized that interconnection 
agreements (like injunctions and consent decrees) are entered 
into in response to particular federal statutes and 
implementing regulations, and that the interconnection 
agreements must be subject to modification if particular FCC 
regulations are vacated or revised or if the legal and factual 
circumstances in existence at the time of their adoption have 
otherwise fundamentally changed. Compare System Fed'n 
No. 91, Ry. Employees Dep't v. Wright, 364 U.S. 642, 646-47

agreement provide procedures in which modifications of the 
agreements can be sought if FCC regulations are invalidated 
or revised or if other material changes in the law occur. See, 
e.g., JA 29a-30a; Addenda 3-4.

standards of §§ 251(c) and 252(d) and the pertinent FCC 
regulations §§ 252(d)(2)-(4). When arbitrated agreements 
are being interpreted or modified, the state will determine if 
the interpretation of the agreement or the new modified terms 
satisfy the substantive requirements of §§ 251 and 252 and 
the FCC regulations. § 252(e)(2)(B). 


Matters are no different when a state commission resolves a 
dispute over the proper interpretation of a term of an 
interconnection agreement that was not arbitrated but that was 
“negotiated”: that is, agreed to the parties in light of the 
requirements of the FCC’s then-existing regulations When 
negotiated agreements are interpreted, the state must 
determine—at a minimum—f the result is an agreement that 
satisfies the substantive requirements of federal law that 
govern negotiated agreements: that is, that the term is non- 
discriminatory and will be implemented consistently with the 
public interest § 252(e2\A). Further, because the FCC 
regulations establish requirements that are to appear in every 
interconnection agreement unless there is “clear mutual assent 
to different terms,” Local Competition Order 4 56, the 
resolution of disputes about the interpretation of “negotiated” 
terms will ordinarily turn on a _ state commission’s 
determinations of how the FCC’s then-existing regulations 


20 


apply to the particular facility, service, or other condition. 
That, too, is the same determination that the state commission 
would have made if the parties had foreseen the dispute 
before the agreement was finalized and had decided to 
arbitrate the question of what the incumbent LEC’s obligation 
would be under those particular conditions. 


Petitioners concede that when a state commission makes 
any of the foregoing determinations prior to approval of an 
interconnection agreement or statement, the determinations 
are reviewable in federal district court under § 252(e)(6) and 
that the Act then prohibits state court review. See 
§ 252(e)(4). The federal court and the federal court alone is 
then to “determine whether the agreement or statement meets 
the requirements of section 251 and this section [252]” 
§ 252(e)(6). 


But petitioners contend that § 252(e)(6) bars federal court 
review of these same determinations—and requires that they 
be reviewed only in state court—when the state enters an 
order modifying, interpreting, or enforcing the agreement 
after it was initially approved. In petitioners’ view, the plain 
terms of § 252(e)(6) deny federal courts jurisdiction over any 
such “subsequent commission action{s].” Br. 13. They thus 
could not review the state orders that apply the standards of 
§ 252(d) and the FCC’s pricing regulations to set permanent 
network element rates that supercede the interim rates 
contained in the agreement when it was approved. They 
similarly could not review whether interpretations of broad 
terms of the agreements violate federal law. Indeed, under 
petitioners’ position, there could be no federal court review 
even if a federal court had previously upheld the agreement 
on the ground that it is required by federal law, and the state 
commission had the next day vacated the agreement based on 


a purported change in federal law. 


While this position is contrary to the Act as a whole, the 
short answer to petitioners’ claim is that the terms of 


21 


§ 252(e(6) do not permit the distinction that petitioners 
propose, much ‘ess require it. That is so for three reasons. 


First, § 252(e)(6)’s terms expressly apply to all determin- 
ations made under the section, regardless of whether they 
occur before or after the initial approval of the agreement. In 
particular, section 252(e)(6) is entitled “Review of State 
commission actions,” and it broadly provides that “[i]n any 
case in which a State commission makes a determination 
under this section, any party aggrieved by such determination 
may bring an action in an appropriate Federal district court to 
determine whether the agreement ... meets the requirements 
of section 251 of this title and this section” § 252(e)6) 
(emphasis added). These terms do not permit a claim that a 
determination of the LECs’ responsibilities and their 
lawfulness is reviewable in federal court when made prior to 
approval, but not when the same determinations are made 
thereafter to the agreement as interpreted or modified. 


Second, petitioners’ claim would be baseless even if these 
terms applied only to determinations that are made in the 
“proceedings” in which interconnection agreement are 
approved. When parties and the state commission defer open 
issues and questions of the interpretation and modification of 
the agreements, the future proceedings that result are 
fundamentally a continuation of the proceedings in which the 
agreement or statement was approved. They occur because 
the parties and the commission determined that it was 
impractical to resolve all possible issues at initial approval, 
which is statutorily required to occur within ten months of the 
request for interconnection. Instead, the parties and the 
commission deferred the issues, and made the approval of the 
agreement subject to the outcome of the future proceedings. 


Petitioners do not argue that the state commissions lack the 
authority to defer these determinations under § 252 to future 
proceedings with the assent of the parties to the 
interconnection agreement In administering federal statutes, 
agencies inherently have the authority that is “a ‘legitimate, 


22 


reasonable, and direct adjunct to ... explicit statutory power.’” 
In re Trans Alaska Pipeline Rate Case, 436 U.S. 631, 655 
(1978). Thus, the determinations made in post-approval 
modification, interpretation, or enforcement proceedings are 
part of the same “proceeding” in which the agreement was 
“approved.” 


For the same reasons, every court of appeals to resolve the 
issue has held that “[{a] state commission’s authority to 
approve or reject interconnection agreements under the Act 
necessarily includes the authority to interpret and enforce, to 
the same extent, the terms of those agreements once they have 
been approved by that commission.” 


Third, § 252(e)(5) establishes that state commissions have 
responsibilities under § 252 not only in the “proceedings” in 
which interconnection agreements are formed and initially 
approved, but also in “other matter[s] under this section.” In 
particular, it provides a mechanism in which a state commis- 
sion may elect whether or not to discharge its responsibility in 
“any proceeding or other matter under this section [252]” and 
in which the FCC will assume jurisdiction if the state elects 
not to act. Whether or not it is a “proceeding” under § 252, it 
is assuredly a “matter” under the section when a state resolves 
open issues or interprets, modifies, and enforces an agreement 
after it had initially been approved.

B. Petitioners’ Construction Is Inconsistent With 
The Act’s Other Remedies And Its Objects. 


In addition to violating the terms of § 252(e)(6), petitioners’ 
claims are foreclosed by the Act as a whole and its objects 
and policies. This point is confirmed by petitioners’ attempts 
to argue otherwise. In particular, petitioners acknowledge 
that the object of the Act is to foster local competition by 
implementing arrangements in which incumbent LECs share 
essential and other related facilities with competitive carriers 
on terms that are nondiscriminatory and just and reasonable. 
But petitioners contend that that goal is accomplished by the 
mere initial formation and approval of interconnection 
agreements that do not violate federal law on their face. Once 
that occurs, petitioners contend that there is no longer a 
substantial federal interest in regulating the LECs’ conduct 
and that it is subject only to state commission orders 
reviewable in state courts under state contract law. 


These claims are flatly contrary to the provisions of the Act 
as a whole, which dramatically underscore why § 252(e)(6) 
cannot be construed to apply only to determinations that are 
made under § 252 at the time of the initial approval of 
interconnection agreements. First, because Congress recog- 
nized that “post-approval conduct” and orders can wholly 
defeat the Act’s objects, the Act contains federal substantive 
requirements and a panoply of federal remedies that are fully 
applicable after interconnection agreements have been 
initially approved. Second, under the Act’s terms and settled 
law, interconnection agreements are matters governed by 
federal law, not state contract law.

D. Section 252(e)(6) Grants District Courts The 
Jurisdiction To Resolve The Construction Of

Although the court of appeals correctly held that the district 
courts had jurisdiction to review the ICC’s order under 
section 252(e)(6), it has also adopted what has aptly been 
termed an “absurd jurisdictional scheme.” Southwestern Bell 
Tel. Co. v. Brooks Fiber Communications of Okla., Inc., 235 
F 3d 493, 498 n.2 (10th Cir. 2000). Under it, “every time a 
carrier complains about a state agency’s action concerning an 
agreement, it must start in federal court (to find out whether 
there has been a violation of federal law) and then may move 
to state court if the first suit yields the answer ‘no.”” Pet. 
App. 5a. This aspect of the Seventh Circuit’s decision is 
erroneous. 

The Seventh Circuit fashioned its bifurcated jurisdictional 
scheme because it believed that state law governs the 
interpretation of the agreements and the only federal question 
is whether the interpretation violates federal standards. That 
is wrong for two independent reasons. First, both “negoti- 
ated” and arbitrated agreements present federal questions 
under the act. See supra at 19-20, 25. Second, intercon- 
nection agreements are federal contracts. See supra at 33-35. 


Further, even if state law did govern those agreements, and 
even if somehow there were no supplemental jurisdiction 
under 28 U.S.C. § 1367, that would be no impediment to a 
district court’s review of the commission’s interpretation of 
the agreement. First, the district court must interpret the 
interconnection agreement in order to perform its duty of 


37 


determining whether the agreement violates the Act. Thus, 
any alleged state-law question regarding the existence and 
meaning of the agreement “is a federal question,” and the 
district court cannot “‘surrender the duty to exercise [its] own 
judgment,” although it may give weight to the state tribunal’s 
views. General Motors Corp. v. Romein, 503 U.S. 181, 187 
(1992). Second, even if agreement interpretation were not 
itself a federal question, federal courts always have power to 
resolve state-law questions that are the predicates for 
determining questions of federal law. See, e.g., Greenholtz v. 
Inmates of the Neb. Penal & Corr. Complex, 442 U.S. 1, 12- 
14 (1979). 


ll. THE ICC HAS WAIVED ITS SOVEREIGN 
IMMUNITY. 


Petitioners contend that, if section 252(e)(6) is construed to 
grant the federal district courts jurisdiction to review state 
commission orders enforcing interconnection agreements, 
such review would violate sovereign immunity, which they 
claim that the ICC did not waive. Their argument is baseless. 


It has long been the rule that “[a] State may effectuate a 
waiver of its constitutional immunity by a state statute or 
constitutional provision, or by otherwise waiving its immunity 
fo suit in the context of a particular federal program.” 
Atascadero State Hosp. v. Scanlon, 473 U.S. 234, 238 n.1 
(1985) (emphasis added). The petitioners seeks to evade this 
rule by claiming that this Court severely curtailed the power 
of Congress to elicit waivers of sovereign immunity by states 
that participate in federal programs in College Savings Bank 
v. Florida Prepaid Postsecondary Education Expense Board, 
527 U.S. 666 (1999), Br. 32-40, even though no federal 
program was at issue in that case. The petitioners misread 
College Savings Bank, which directly affirmed the power of 
Congress to elicit sovereign immunity waivers in the manner 
it did in the 1996 Act. As this Court has declared, “[t]here is 
no doubt .. that if the federal courts believe a state 
commission is not regulating in accordance with federal 


38 


policy [under the 1996 Act] they may bring it to heel.” A7&7 
Corp. v. lowa Utils. Bd., 525 U.S. 366, 379 n.6 (1999). 


In College Savings Bank, this Court reaffirmed the 
longstanding rule that a state may waive its sovereign 
immunity, but emphasized that the waiver must be “altogether 
voluntary on the part of the sovereignty.” 527 U.S. at 675 
(internal quotation marks omitted). The issue in College 
Savings Bank was whether a state’s participation in an 
activity regulated by federal law (in that case, the marketing 
in interstate commerce of prepaid college tuition plans) 
constituted voluntary waiver simply because Congress had 
authorized a private right of action in federal court against the 
state for violations of the Lanham and Trademark Remedy 
Clarification Acts. /d. at 671. 


This Court held that the state did not waive its immunity 
merely by engaging in such federally regulated activity. /d. at 
676-77. In so holding, the Court overruled Parden v. 
Terminal Railway of the Alabama State Docks Department, 
377 U.S. 184 (1964), which had held that a state waives its 
immunity by operating a railroad subject to suit in federal 
court under the Federal Employers’ Liability Act. 377 US. 
id. at 192-93. Parden-style waiver was impermissible, the 
Court held, because “there is little reason to assume actual 
consent based upon the State’s mere presence in a field 
subject to congressional regulation.” College Savings Bank, 
527 U.S. at 680. Congress cannot make consent a precon- 
dition for a state to engage in “otherwise permissible 
activity,” because exclusion of a state from such an activity is 
a “sanction” that coerces a state into consenting to suit. /d at 
687. Thus, in College Savings Bank, the Florida agency did 
not waive its immunity simply by engaging in commercial 
activity regulated by federal statute. 


In rejecting “Parden-style waiver” — a term appearing three 
times in the majority opinion, see id. at 679, 681, 683, to 
describe waiver based on state engagement in federally 
regulated conduct — this Court took pains to distinguish the 


39 


“fundamentally different” implied waivers of state sovereign 
immunity that were effective because Congress had offered a 
“gratuity” to the States conditioned on a waiver of immunity. 
Id. at 686; AT&T Communications v. BellSouth Telecomms., 
Inc., 238 F.3d 636, 644 (Sth Cir. 2001) (noting that College 
Savings “expressly overruled Parden and its ‘constructive- 
waiver experiment [as] ill conceived,” but did not forbid 
other forms of implied waiver) (quoting 527 U.S. at 680). 


First, the Court reaffirmed the validity of Petty v. 
Tennessee-Missouri Bridge Commission, 359 U.S. 275 
(1959), which “held that a bistate commission which had been 
created pursuant to an interstate compact ... had consented to 
suit by reason of a suability provision attached to the 
congressional approval of the compact.” College Savings 
Bank, 527 U.S. at 686. The implied waiver of sovereign 
immunity in Petty was valid because the states had no 
independent right to “form an interstate compact without first 
obtaining the express consent of Congress; the granting of 
such consent is a gratuity.” /d. 


Second, the Court noted that implied waiver of state 
constitutional immunities was firmly established by South 
Dakota vy. Dole, 483 U.S. 203 (1987), which held that 
“Congress may, in the exercise of its spending power, 
condition its grant of funds to the States upon their taking 
certain actions that Congress could not require them to take, 
and that acceptance of the funds entails an agreement to the 
actions.” College Savings Bank, 527 U.S. at 686. Accord- 
ingly, the Court reaffirmed the rule of Atascadero, another 
Spending Clause case, that participation in a federal program 
constitutes consent to federal court jurisdiction if the statute 
“manifest[s] a clear intent to condition participation in the 
programs ... on a State’s consent to waive its constitutional 
immunity,” 473 U.S. at 247. See College Savings Bank, 527 
U.S. at 678 n.2. 


Thus, as the Fifth Circuit has held, agreeing with the 
Seventh and Tenth Circuits, the Court’s discussion of those 


40 


cases in College Savings Bank affirmed the rule that 
“Congress may still obtain a non-verbal voluntary waiver of a 
state’s Eleventh Amendment immunity, if the waiver can be 
inferred from the state’s conduct in accepting a gratuity after 
being given clear and unambiguous statutory notice that it 
was conditioned on waiver of immunity.” AT&T 
Communications, 238 F.3d at 645, MCI I], 222 F.3d 323, 340 
(7th Cir. 2000), cert. denied, 121 S. Ct. 896 (2001), MC]! 
Telecomms. Corp. v. Public Serv. Comm'n, 216 F.3d 929, 937

sovereign immunity if the (1) the statute gives the state

waiver; (2) nonparticipation would not exclude the state from 
activities otherwise lawfully within its powers, and (3) the 
state elects to engage in the conduct or transaction after such 
legal notice has been given. A7&7 Communications, 238 F.3d 
at 644. Those three conditions have been amply met by the 
1996 Act.

state commissions on notice that their participation in the

The petitioners also make the odd claim that by authorizing 
“judicial review” under section 252(e)(6), Congress thereby 
precluded injunctive or declaratory relief. Br. 45. First, Ex 
parte Young jurisdiction is the traditional form of judicial 
review of state commission actions in federal courts, and it 
can hardly be said that Congress intended to prohibit it in a 
statutory provision entitled “Review of State commission 
action.”'® Second, the fact that under statutory review a 
district court need not invoke the nonstatutory writs, and may 
simply “remand” to the agency, is of no moment. Remands 
are no less coercive in the relevant sense, FCC v. Pottsville 
Broadcasting Co., 309 U.S. 134, 145 (1940) (the agency is 
“bound to act upon the correction” of law by the agency), but 
regardless injunctions are always available even in federal 
statutory review of agency orders. See, e.g., National Mining

such relief over the same objection”) (citation omitted).

Ass'n v. United States Army Corps of Eng’rs, 145 F.3d 1399,

Equally unfounded is the petitioners’ claim that the single 
sentence in § 252(e)(6) authorizing judicial review is “‘a 
detailed remedial scheme,”” Br. 45, preclusive of Ex parte 
Young jurisdiction, much like the statutory scheme at issue in 
Seminole Tribe v. Florida, 517 U.S. 44, 75-76 (1996). There 
is no comparability. The statute at issue in Seminole Tribe, 
the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act (“IGRA”), obligated states 
to negotiate a regulatory compact with Indian tribes wishing 
to develop gaming enterprises on tribal lands within the state, 
25 U.S.C. § 2710(d)(3), and provided a judicial right of action 
for a tribe to compel the state to negotiate in good faith, id 
§ 2710(d)(7 Ai) & (Bi). See 517 US. at 49. Once the 
jurisdiction of the district court is invoked, other provisions of 
IGRA “describe an elaborate remedial scheme designed to 
ensure the formation of a Tribal-State compact.” /d at 50. 
Because certain of the remedies authorized against the 
State—such as statutory mediation for violations of court 
orders to negotiate a compact—were inconsistent with normal 
con-tempt sanctions that would be available in a suit against 
the officers, the Court concluded that Congress intended to 
prohibit the Tribe from invoking Ex parte Young to enforce 
the statute. See id at 75-76. The Court cautioned, however, 
that its holding was limited to the statute before it, and that it 
did not hold that every limited remedial scheme in a statute 
foreclosed Ex parte Young actions. Jd at 75 n.17. 


By contrast, section 252(e)(6) does no more than provide a 
right of review in federal court. It does not speak to the issue 
of what relief a district court may order if it finds a violation, 
much less create an “intricate remedial scheme,” id at 74, of 
special post-judgment statutory relief that might conflict with 
traditional equitable relief. Judicial review is always an 
exercise of equitable jurisdiction, and, absent any express 
Statutory limitation on the relief a court may order, the full 
panoply of equitable remedies available in any judicial review

proceeding must be presumed available here. Porter v. 
Warner Holding Co., 328 U.S. 395, 398 (1946), Franklin v. 
Gwinnett County Pub. Sch, 503 US. 60, 66 (1992). 
Congress did not bar the district court from exercising Ex 
parte Young jurisdiction.
