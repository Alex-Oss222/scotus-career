STATEMENT OF JURISDICTION 


The district court had jurisdiction pursuant to 28 U.S.C. 
§ 1331 and 47 U.S.C. § 252(e)(6) to review the determination 
of the Public Service Commission of Maryland (“PSC”) for 
compliance with federal law.

1. The Statutory Scheme 


The 1996 Act fundamentally changed the regulation of the 
local telephone industry throughout the country. In the 
Communications Act of 1934, Congress left regulation of 
intrastate telecommunications primarily to the states. See 47 
U.S.C. § 152(b); Louisiana Pub. Serv. Comm'n v. FCC, 476 
U.S. 355 (1986). The states, in turn, historically granted 
exclusive franchises to incumbent local telephone companies 
like Verizon. See AT&T Corp. v. Iowa Utils. Bd., 525 US. 
366, 371 (1999); H.R. Rep. No. 104-204, at 49 (1995). 


Congress enacted the 1996 Act for the express purpose of 
ending this monopoly regime and promoting a competitive 
market for local telephone service. See AT&T Corp., 525 U.S. 
at 371. Toward that end, the 1996 Act partially supplants state 
regulation and imposes a new set of substantive federal 
standards and procedures governing interconnection between 
incumbent local companies and potential competitors. /d. at 
371-73. Congress thus altered the prior balance of federal and 
state regulatory authority in the area of local 
telecommunications. /d. at 371. 


Under the Act, a new entrant that wishes to enter a local 
telephone market must contact the incumbent carrier to 
negotiate the terms of interconnection. See 47 U.S.C. 
§ 252(a)(1). If the two carriers cannot reach an agreement, they 
must ask the governing state commission to conduct an 
“arbitration” to resolve disputed issues. Jd. § 252(b)(1). The 
results of the negotiation and arbitration process are 
memorialized in an “interconnection agreement” between the 


4 


carriers, which is then submitted for approval to the state 
commission. /d. § 252(e). 


Although the Act supplies minimum requirements that 
state commissions must follow in arbitrating provisions about 
which the parties cannot reach a negotiated agreement, parties 
may negotiate terms “without regard” to these requirements. 
Id. § 252(a)(1). When parties voluntarily agree to terms, a state 
commission may reject the terms only if they are discriminatory 
or against the public interest, convenience, or necessity. /d. 
§ 252(e)(2)(A); see also AT&T Corp., 525 U.S. at 371-73. 


Thus, in the spirit of cooperative federalism, Congress 
vested the FCC with the primary authority to implement the 
federal standards created by the Act, AT&T Corp., 525 U.S. at 
377-84 & n.5; 47 U.S.C. §§ 201(b), 251(d)(1), while at the 
same time, carving out a new, voluntary role for states in 
Overseeing interconnection agreements. The Act in no way 
requires state commissions to help implement the new federal 
scheme. If a state commission declines to participate, the FCC 
will assume that function. 47 U.S.C. § 252(e)(5). 


Whether rendered by a state commission or the FCC, 
agency determinations regarding the terms of interconnection 
agreements are subject to review in federal court for 
compliance with the Act. If a state commission elects to 
participate in the Act’s implementation, “any party 
aggrieved . . . may bring an action in an appropriate Federal 
district court to determine whether the agreement or statement 
meets the requirements of section{s] 251 . . . and [252].” 47 
U.S.C. § 252(e)(6); see also AT&T Corp., 525 U.S. at 378 n.6 
(“there is no doubt . . . that if the federal courts believe a state 


5 


commission is not regulating in accordance with federal policy 
they may bring it to heel”).” 


2. Nature of the Dispute 


This case concerns one aspect of the parties’ 
interconnection agreement, known as “reciprocal 
compensation,” which is the payment that a local carrier 
receives for completing a call for another local carrier. With 
local competition, customers of one local carrier will call 
customers of another. Carriers must therefore assist each other 
in delivering calls. When a customer of one local carrier makes 
a call to a customer of another local carrier, only the caller pays 
its carrier for the telephone services, leaving the other carrier 
uncompensated. The caller’s local carrier therefore must 
compensate the other carrier whose facilities are used to 
complete the call. See 47 U.S.C. § 251(b)(5); Bell Atlantic Tel. 
Cos. v. FCC, 206 F.3d 1, 2-3 (D.C. Cir. 2000). 


Verizon and MFS agreed in their negotiated 
interconnection agreement to pay each other reciprocal 
compensation for the exchange of “Local Traffic.” Pet. App. 
95a. Local Traffic is defined in the parties’ interconnection 
agreement as a call within a given local calling area, or 
expanded area service (“EAS”) area, to another local telephone 
number. Pet. App. 95a. The dispute in this case was whether 
calls by local customers of one carrier that are routed by another 
carrier to its local customers operating as “Internet Service 
Providers” (“ISPs”) constitute “Local Traffic” as defined in the 


interconnection agreement.

SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT 


In this case, the Court has granted review to consider only 
the question whether, if section 252(e)(6) does not apply, 
federal courts have genera! federal question jurisdiction under 
28 U.S.C. § 1331 to address claims by carriers that the decision 
of a state commission interpreting or enforcing an 
interconnection agreement violates federal law. As we explain 
in our Mathias brief, actions filed by carriers seeking review of 
state commission decisions interpreting or enforcing 
interconnection agreements may be brought under section 
252(e)(6) of the 1996 Act to determine compliance with federal 
law. If this Court holds in Mathias that section 252(e)(6) grants 
federal court jurisdiction over enforcement actions such as this, 
the question presented here will be moot.

Regardless of whether section 252(e)(6) is interpreted as an 
independent grant of jurisdiction, however, section 1331 
unquestionably provides general federal question jurisdiction in 
these circumstances. While WorldCom fully supports the 
Fourth Circuit’s conclusion that Verizon’s substantive federal 
claims are without merit, those claims were not wholly 
frivolous or insubstantial at the time they were raised and, 
therefore, “arise under” federal law. Bedrock principles of 
federal jurisprudence establish that federal courts have general 
federal question jurisdiction under section 1331 to address non- 
frivolous claims that state officials have violated federal law. 
Here, the Fourth Circuit erred by first reaching the ultimate 
conclusion on the merits (that Verizon’s federal claims lacked 
merit), and then working back from that conclusion to 
determine that the court therefore lacked jurisdiction over 
Verizon’s complaint. That analysis is precisely backward. 


Indeed,- given the general grant of federal question 
jurisdiction in section 1331, the fundamental question presented 
in both this case and Mathias is whether anything in section 
252 withdraws federal question jurisdiction as to this one 
category of federal claims. The answer to that question is clear. 
Nothing in section 252(e)(6) — or any other provision of the 
1996 Act — suggests a congressional intent to bar federal 
jurisdiction over claims that state commission enforcement 
determinations violate the Act. General federal question 
jurisdiction under section 1331 therefore exists in this case, as 
it did in Mathias.

I. FEDERAL COURTS HAVE GENERAL FEDERAL

The face of Verizon’s complaint establishes that Verizon

insubstantial or frivolous. The district court therefore had 
subject matter jurisdiction to consider them, as nothing in the

A. Verizon Raised Federal Questions That Federal 
Courts Have Jurisdiction to Answer under 28 
U.S.C. § 1331. 


Regardless of how the Court rules in Mathias on the scope 
of section 252(e)(6), the district court plainly had jurisdiction 
under section 1331 to consider Verizon’s federal claims. A 
civil action “arises under” federal law for purposes of 
section 1331 when a “right or immunity created by the 
Constitution or laws of the United States [is] an element, and an 
essential one, of the plaintiff's cause of action.” Gully v. First 
Nat'l Bank, 299 U.S. 109, 112 (1936); accord City of Chicago 
v. International College of Surgeons, 522 U.S. 156, 163-64 
(1997). 


To determine the exi. ence of federal question jurisdiction, 
the court “must look to the way the complaint is drawn to see 
if it is drawn so as to claim a right to recover under the

Constitution and laws of the United States.” Bell v. Hood, 327 
U.S. 678, 681 (1946). As this Court recently reaffirmed: 


[T]}he district court has jurisdiction if “the right of the 
petitioners to recover under their complaint will be 
sustained if the Constitution and laws of the United 
States are given one construction and will be defeated 
if they are given another.” 


Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env't, 523 U.S. 83, 89 (1998) 
(quoting Bell v. Hood, 327 U.S. at 685). 


As with Ameritech’s complaint in Mathias, Verizon’s 
complaint here easily satisfies these jurisdictional requirements. 
As noted, Verizon brought two counts in its complaint, both of 
which directly identify alleged violations of federal law. In 
each count, Verizon “claims a right to recover under the . . . 
laws of the United States. Bell v. Hood, 327 U.S. at 681. 
“(T]he right of the petitioners to recover” thus depends upon 
the construction of the “laws of the United States.” Steel Co., 
523 U.S. at 89. In Count I, Verizon alleged that “(t]he PSC’s 
decision . . . is inconsistent with federal law[.]” Compl. 4 40. 
In Count II, Verizon again alleged that “(t]he PSC’s

Shaw v. Delta Airlines, Inc., 463 U.S. 85, 96 n.14 (1983) (citing

Of course, federal claims challenging state commission

prior decisions of this Court, or otherwise completely devoid of
