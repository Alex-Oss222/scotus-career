QUESTIONS PRESENTED 


I. Whether the court of appeals erred in holding that 
section 252(e)(6) of the Telecommunications Act of 1996 
gives federal district courts jurisdiction to review orders of 
state public service commissions that resolve contract 
disputes under state law, where the express language of 
section 252(e)(6) of the Act limits federal review to 
determinations made by state commissions under section 
252, the Act leaves supervision of the agreements to the 
states, and the Act prohibits implied preemption of state law. 


Il. Whether the court of appeals erred in holding that a 
state public service commission constructively waived the 
state’s constitutional sovereign immunity by adjudicating 
complaints brought under state law calling for the 
interpretation and enforcement of privately negotiated 
provisions in contracts entered into pursuant to the 
Telecommunications Act, when after College Savings 
waivers of immunity must be deliberate, express waivers 
made by the state itself. 


Ill. Whether the court of appeals erred in finding 
jurisdiction over the ICC, through the Commissioners, 
without determining whether the requirements for 
application of the Ex parte Young exception were satisfied, 
where Congress did not authorize suits against state 
commissioners under the Act, where the conduct complained 
of was not alleged to violate the Constitution, and where the 
substance of the relief sought was reversal of the ICC’s 
order.

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


This case involves federal court review of an order entered 
by the ICC. The ICC is an agency of the State of Illinois, and 
it supervises and regulates telecommunications carriers in 
Illinois. As part of its responsibilities, the ICC administers 
and enforces the Illinois Public Utilities Act, 220 ILCS 
5/1-101 et seg. (1993). ICC orders or decisions are subject to 


3 


judicial review in the Illinois Appellate Court. Jd. § 5/10- 
201. 


The case began at the ICC when several competing 
telecommunications carriers brought complaints alleging 
breaches of their interconnection agreements with Illinois 
Bell Telephone Company d/b/a Ameritech Illinois 
("Ameritech IIlinois”) and violations of Illinois law. The ICC 
resolved those complaints in favor of the competing carriers, 
and Ameritech Illinois sought review of the order pursuant to 
section 252(e)(6) of the Telecommunications Act of 1996. 


A. Telecommunications Act of 1996 


On February 8, 1996, Congress enacted the 
Telecommunications Act of 1996, Pub. L. No. 104-104, 110 
Stat. 56 (codified as amended in scattered sections of 47 
U.S.C.) (“1996 Act” or “Act”), which amended the 
Communications Act of 1934. A keystone of the Act is the 
introduction of competition into local telephone service 
markets. The Act imposes general duties on all 
telecommunications carriers and further obligations on all 
local exchange carriers (“LECs”). 47 U.S.C. § 251(a), (b). 
Recognizing that new entrants to the local telephone market 
would not be able rapidly and efficiently to duplicate the 
facilities developed over time by incumbent LECs, Congress 
imposed additional obligations on incumbent LECs like 
Ameritech Illinois to, among other things, allow new 
entrants to “interconnect” with their networks, gain 
unbundled access to discrete elements of their networks, and 
purchase their telecommunications services for resale at 
wholesale prices. See id. § 251(c). The means of obtaining 
interconnection, unbundled access, and resale are through 


agreements negotiated in accordance with section 252 of the 
Act. 


B. Section 252 


Section 252 of the Act is titled “Procedures For 
Negotiation, Arbitration, And Approval Of Agreements.” As 
its title suggests, section 252 establishes procedures for 
negotiating, arbitrating, and approving interconnection 
agreements between incumbent LECs and requesting 
carriers. Section 252 sets forth a four-part structure for 
accomplishing this task and implementing the Act. 


First, Congress chose to rely in the first instance on 
voluntary negotiations between incumbent LECs and 
requesting carriers. If they reach a voluntary agreement, 
section 252(a)(1) relieves them of the _ substantive 
requirements of section 251(b) and (c). 


Second, if private carrier-to-carrier negotiations prove 
unsuccessful, Congress provided for compulsory arbitration 
of any open issues to reach an agreement. Section 252(b) 
allows any party to the negotiation, during the period from 
the 135th to the 160th day from the initial request for 
negotiation, to petition a state commission to arbitrate any 
open issues. The state commission must resolve all 
unresolved issues raised by the arbitration petition and 
any response within nine months from the time the 
incumbent LEC receives a request for interconnection, 
services, or network elements under section 251. 47 U.S.C. 


§ 252(b)(4)(A), (C). 


Third, Congress required that all interconnection 
agreements be submitted to a state commission for approval 


5 


before becoming effective. Jd. § 252(e)(1). A_ state 
commission may only reject an agreement (or parts thereof) 
adopted by negotiation if it finds that the agreement 
discriminates against a carrier not a party to the agreement or 
if it is not in the public interest. Jd. § 252(e)(2}(A). An 
agreement adopted by arbitration (or parts thereof) may be 
rejected if it fails to comply with section 251, regulations 
prescribed by the Federal Communications Commission 
(“FCC”) pursuant to section 251, or the pricing standards in 
section 252(d). Jd. § 252(e)(2)(B). Once a final agreement is 
submitted, a state commission must act on it within ninety 
days if it is a negotiated agreement (or negotiated portion of 
an agreement), or thirty days if it is an arbitrated agreement 
(or arbitrated portion of an agreement). If it does not, the 
agreement is deemed to be approved. Jd. § 252(e)(4). Once 
the agreement is approved, it must be filed with the state 
commission. Jd. § 252(h). 


Fourth, Congress provided a means for judicial review of 
a state commission’s determinations made under section 252. 
47 U.S.C. § 252(e)(6). 


C. The Interconnection Agreements 


In 1996 and 1997, Teleport Communications Group, Inc. 
(“TCG”), WorldCom Technologies, Inc. (“WorldCom”), 
Focal Communications Corporation of Illinois (“Focal”), 
AT&T Communications of Illinois, Inc. (“AT&T”), and 
MCI Telecommunications Corporation and MCImetro 
Access Transmission Services, Inc. (jointly “MCI”) 
(collectively “Competitive Carriers”) executed _inter- 
connection agreements with Ameritech Illinois and 
submitted those agreements to the ICC in accordance with 


6 


section 252(e)(1) of the Act. The TCG, WorldCom, and 
Focal agreements are privately negotiated agreements. The 
MCI and AT&T agreements contain privately negotiated 
portions and arbitrated portions. None of the arbitrated 
provisions in those agreements is involved in this case. The 
reciprocal compensation provisions in each agreement were 
privately negotiated by the respective carriers. Pursuant to 
section 252(e)(2), the ICC approved all of the 
interconnection agreements. 


D. Proceedings at the Illinois Commerce Commission 


In the fall of 1997, after performing under their 
agreements for some time, TCG, WorldCom, and MCI filed 
complaints against Ameritech Illinois. In their complaints, 
each carrier alleged that Ameritech Illinois breached the 
terms of its respective ICC-approved interconnection 
agreement, violated prior ICC orders, and violated various 
sections of the Illinois Public Utilities Act (“IPUA”), 220 
ILCS 5/1-101 et seg. (1993). See Pet. App. 10a. For 
example, TCG complained that Ameritech Illinois refused to 
pay TCG reciprocal compensation for local calls originated 
by end users on Ameritech Illinois’ network and terminated 
to Internet Service Providers (“ISPs”) on TCG’s network in 
violation of Section 5.6.1 of its interconnection agreement 
with Ameritech Illinois. TCG requested that the ICC find 
and declare that i) such calis are local calls subject to 
reciprocal compensation under its interconnection 
agreement, ii) Ameritech Illinois is obligated to pay TCG for 
past due amounts with interest, and iii) Ameritech Illinois 
should be reprimanded for anticompetitive and unilateral 
withholding of monies owed to TCG. WorldCom and MCI 
each requested similar relief. The ICC consolidated the three 


7 


complaints and granted petitions to intervene filed by AT&T 
and Focal. 


On March 11, 1998, the ICC entered a 17-page written 
order granting the consolidated complaints, finding that the 
disputed calls to ISPs were calls subject to reciprocal 
compensation according to the terms of the parties’ 
interconnection agreements. (“March 11 Order” or “Order”). 
The ICC found that Ameritech Illinois’ conduct in stopping 
its reciprocal compensation payments to the complainants 
had violated its interconnection agreements. Accordingly, 
the ICC ordered Ameritech Illinois to compensate the 
complainants for all past due reciprocal compensation 
amounts and ordered Ameritech and the complainants to 
resume making payments in accordance with their 
agreements. See Pet. App. 10a.

REASONS FOR GRANTING THE WRIT 


The 1996 Act is a major piece of federal legislation that 
“fundamentally restructures local telephone markets” and 
“profoundly affects a crucial segment of the economy worth 
tens of billions of dollars.” AT&T Corp. v. lowa Utilities Bd., 
119 S. Ct. 721, 726, 738 (1999) (“Jowa Utilities”). The Act's 
goals of fostering competition in the local markets and 
furthering competition in the long-distance market aim to 
bring lower prices, better quality service and technological 
innovations to all telephone subscribers. To implement its 
provisions, the Act enlists the states and the FCC in a 
cooperative effort. Thus, the issues implicated by the Act are 
of substantial local and national importance. 


Despite the cooperative federal-state effort, the Act does 
not give federal courts jurisdiction to review every state 
agreements. The Seventh Circuit’s construction of section 
252(e)(6) radically alters the federal-state balance of power 
and injects the federal courts into state administrative review 
schemes. Under the Seventh Circuit’s sweeping 
interpretation, section 252(e)(6) radically shifts judicial 
review of many state commission actions involving purely 
local matters from state courts to federal courts and allows 
every telecommunications carrier with an interconnection

I. Section 252(e)(6) Does Not Give Federal Courts 
Jurisdiction to Review Every State Commission 
Action Concerning Interconnection Agreements. 


The court of appeals fundamentally misconstrued the Act 
to allow federal district court review of every state 
commission action concerning interconnection agreements. 
In doing so, the court ignored the Act’s purpose and structure 
and the plain language of section 252(e)(6) limiting federal 
jurisdiction to those state commission determinations made 
under section 252 (i.e, determinations made in the 
arbitration and approval process). In addition, in holding that 
federal jurisdiction under section 252(e)(6) is exclusive when 
it exists, the court necessarily found, without so much as 
discussing it, that section 252(e)(6) preempted state judicial 
review laws in violation of section 601 of the Act. The 
court’s opinion contradicted the plain meaning of the Act, 
implicitly preempted state judicial review laws in violation 
of section 601 of the Act, and fundamentally altered the 
federal-state constitutional balance. 


A. Section 252(e)(6) Limits Federal District Court 
Review to State Commission Determinations 
Made Under Section 252. 


The plain language of section 252(e)(6) limits federal 
district court review to state commission determinations 
made under section 252. Section 252(e)(6) provides in 
relevant part: “In any case in which a State commission 
makes a determination under this section, any party 


13 


aggrieved by such determination may bring an action in an

251 and this section.” Pet. App. 42a-43a. In its Order 
granting the Competing Carriers’ complaints brought under 
the IPUA, however, the ICC did not make a determination 
under section 252 as required by section 252(e)(6). In fact, 
for a state commission to act “under section 252,” its 
jurisdiction must be invoked pursuant to section 252, and the 
only way for that to occur is for a carrier to i) file a petition 
requesting mediation under section 252(a)(2), ii) file a 
petition requesting arbitration under section 252(b)(1), or iii) 
submit an agreement requesting approval under section 
252(e)(1). The Competing Carriers, however, invoked the 
ICC’s authority under Illinois law, and the ICC adjudicated 
their complaints under Illinois law. None of the findings and 
conclusions in the ICC’s Order was made under section 252. 
The only state commission determinations that can be made 
under section 252 are those determinations made in the 
arbitration and approval process. 


Section 252(e)(6) cannot be read to include state 
commission decisions interpreting previously approved 
agreements without rendering parts of the statute 
meaningless. For example, the FCC’s authority to presmpt 
state commission jurisdiction under section 252(e)(5) makes 
sense only with respect to the mediation and arbitration 
responsibilities given to state commissions under § 252. See 
47 C.F.R. § 51.801 (1999). State commissions perform these 
section 252 responsibilities only upon specific request. 47 
U.S.C. § 252(a)(2); id. § 252(b(1). Similarly, the first 
sentence in § 252(e)(6) is rendered a nullity if the second

application of sections 252(¢)(4-6) to state commissions’ 
“action{s],” “responsibilit{ies],” and “determination(s}” 
under section 252. The court of appeals’ interpretation reads 
the limiting words “under this section” (i.e., section 252) 
right out of section 252(e)(6). 


That the Act denies federal district courts jurisdiction to

Congress very easily could have written: “No state court

B. Judicial Review of the ICC’s Order Lay

The court’s construction of section 252(e)(6) to require 
federal court review of every state commission action 
concerning agreements also violates section 601, the Act’s 
rule of construction prohibiting implied preemption of state 
law. Pet. App. 45a. Before and after the Act, states possess

from a state commission’s plenary authority under state law

This Court’s decision in Jowa Utilities confirms that 
review of the ICC’s Order lay exclusively in the Illinois 
Appellate Court. The Court specifically observed that: 
Where the Act “is silent, § 152(b) [the provision establishing 
the dual federal-state regulatory model] continues to 
function.” Jowa Utilities, 119 S. Ct. at 731 n.8. As mentioned

preempts section 5/10-201 of the IPUA in violation of

li. The Seventh Circuit’s Constructive Waiver Holding 
Is Contrary to College Savings and Conflicts With

Strand, 209 F.3d 909 (6th Cir. 2000). 


The court of appeal’s constructive waiver holding is 
contrary to College Savings and the Court’s cases requiring a 
deliberate, express waiver of immunity by the state itself. In 
College Savings, the Court expressly overruled Parden v. 
Terminal Ry. Co. of Alabama Docks Dep't, 377 U.S. 184 
(1964), and repudiated the doctrine of constructive waiver 
under the Commerce Clause. The Court rejected the idea of 
implied waiver of state sovereign immunity as incompatible 
with its cases requiring an express waiver of immunity by 
the state itself. College Savings, 119 S. Ct. at 2228. College 
Savings confirms that a state’s waiver must be a deliberate, 
express waiver made by the state itself and, like federal 
sovereign immunity waivers, cannot be implied. Jd. at 2228- 
29. In this case, no party claimed nor could it, that Illinois 
expressly waived the state’s immunity from suit in federal

court. Illinois has not expressly waived its Eleventh

Frances J. v. Wright, 19 F.3d 337, 342-43 (7th Cir. 1994),

authorized to do so by the state’s constitution, statutes, or

In finding that Illinois waived its immunity, the court of

III. The Decision Below Conflicts With Ex Parte Young, 
209 U.S. 123 (1908). 


Although the court of appeals asserted jurisdiction over 
the ICC, through the Commissioners, it did not determine 
whether Ameritech Illinois’ claims against the 
Commissioners fit within the narrow Ex parte Young 
exception to the Eleventh Amendment. In allowing 
Ameritech Illinois’ claims against the Commissioners, the 
court erred because 1) Congress did not intend for Ex parte 
Young actions to be brought against individual state 
commissioners, 2) Ameritech Illinois did not allege a 
constitutional violation which is a necessary predicate for 
application of Ex parte Young, and 3) Ameritech Illinois did 
not allege an ongoing violation by the Commissioners. 


A. Congress Did Not Intend for Ex parte Young 


Actions to Be Brought Against Individual State 
Commissioners. 


The threshold question in the Ex parte Young inquiry is 
whether Congress intended to authorize Ex phn in 
actions. Seminole Tribe v. Florida, 517 U.S. 44, 75 n.17. If 
Congress did not intend to authorize suits against state 
officials as individuals, then the Ex parte Young exception is 


22 


unavailable. Here, the purpose and structure of the Act and 
the plain language in section 252(e)(6), make clear that 
Congress did not intend for review of state commission 
determinations made under section 252 to be accomplished 
through Ex parte Young actions against the commissioners 
themselves. The Act does not authorize actions against 
individual state officials. The Act refers only to state 
commissions. The term “state commissioner” or 
“commissioner” is not even mentioned in the Act. More 
particularly, section 252(e)(6), the articulated basis for 
federal jurisdiction, refers only to state commissions. The 
court of appeals failed to undertake this initial inquiry and, as 
a consequence, erred in allowing Ameritech Illinois’ claims 
against the Commissioners. See Pet. App. 14a. 


The court of appeals also failed to reconcile its reading of 
the Act to require review of administrative agency actions — 
whether state commission or FCC — with a suit against the 
individual commissioners under an Ex parte Young theory. 
Id. The court construed section 252(e)(6) to require that state 
commissions be made parties to suits reviewing their actions 
“just as the FCC is a party to suits seeking review of its 
actions.” Jd. (internal quotations omitted). Thus, in the 
court’s view, section 252(e)(6) provides for judicial review 
of regulatory actions made by an administrative agency, 
whether federal or state. Under federal law, the FCC is 
named as a party to appeals from its orders. Ingalls 
Shipbuilding v. Director, OWCP, 519 U.S. 248, 267 (1997); 
see Fed. R. App. P. 15(a) (requiring that agency be named 
respondent in review of its orders). There is no indication in 
section 252(e)(6) that an aggrieved party may sue the FCC 
commissioners individually or that it may ee 


23 


injunctive, or relief other than administrative review of the 
FCC’s actions. Similarly, under Illinois law the ICC is 
named as 2 party to appeal of its orders, 220 ILCS 5/10-201; 
155 Ill.2d R.335(a), and there is no indication in section 
252(€)(6) that an aggrieved party may sue a commissioner of 
a state commission or that it may seek declaratory, 
injunctive, or relief other than administrative review of a 
state commission’s determinations made under section 252. 
Indeed, it would be anomalous if Congress had intended to 
expose state commissioners “to the full panoply of a federal 
court's remedial powers, including, presumably, contempt 
sanctions,” when the Act, as interpreted by the court, 
contemplates judicial review of administrative agency action. 
Seminole Tribe, 514 U.S. at 75. The court’s reading of the 
review provided in the Act is simply incompatible with an 
action based on an Ex parte Young theory. 


Furthermore, in simply treating the ICC and the 
Commissioners as interchangeable parties, the court failed to 
appreciate the larger principle reconfirmed in Seminole 
Tribe, that where Congress creates a remedial scheme for the 
enforcement of a particular federal right, the courts should 
not supplement that remedial scheme with additional 
remedies. Section 252 establishes a limited remedial scheme 
for review of state commission determinations made in 
approving or rejecting agreements. The review under section 
252(€)(6) does not include federal district court review of 
state commission orders interpreting previously approved 
agreements or suits against individual state commissioners to 
otherwise accomplish federal review of those state 
commission orders. In this case Ameritech Illinois brought 
an action against the Commissioners under section 252(e)(6) 


24 


to circumvent the review provided for under Illinois law and 
impermissibly enlarge its rights under the Act. The court of 
appeals rewrote the statutory scheme, rendered superfluous 
the limited statutory remedy provided under section 
252(e)(6) and permitted Ameritech Illinois to obtain relief 
against the individual commissioners that it could not 
otherwise obtain. As a consequence, the court’s opinion 
allowing Ex parte Young actions under the Act was improper 
and contrary to this Court’s precedent. 


B. The Ex Parte Young Exception Applies Only to 
Ongoing Constitutional Violations by State 
Officials. 


The basis of the Ex parte Young doctrine is that when a 
state officer acts in violation of the Constitution, he cannot 
claim to be acting on behalf of the state for purposes of the 
Eleventh Amendment and, therefore, an action against him is 
not a suit against a state barred by the Eleventh Amendment. 
Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123, 159-60 (1908); Pennhurst 
State Sch. & Hosp. v. Halderman, 465 U.S. 88, 102 (1984) 
(“Pennhurst IT’). To the ICC’s knowledge, in the time since 
Ex parte Young was decided, this Court has not expanded the 
narrow Ex parte Young -exception beyond alleged 
constitutional violations. This is not surprising since the 
Court has repeatedly instructed that the Ex parte Young 
exception is a narrow exception, not to be given an 
expansive interpretation. E.g., Pennhurst IT, 465 U.S. at 102. 


The Ex Parte Young exception is inapplicable to 
Ameritech Illinois’ claims the company did not 
allege that the individual issioners violated the 


25 


Constitution, whether by virtue of the Supremacy Clause‘ or 
more familiar constitutional provisions. In allowing the 
claims against the Commissioners the court of appeal’s 
decision is contrary to long-standing precedent of this Court 
limiting application of the Ex parte Young exception to 
alleged constitutional violations and, if there were any doubt, 
the Court’s recent sovereign immunity cases resolve it. 

This Court’s sovereign immunity decisions last Term 
confirm that the Ex parte Young exception applies only to 
ongoing constitutional violations by state officials. In 
College Savings and Alden, the Court reaffirmed that state 
sovereign immunity is constitutional, deriving from the 
structure of the Constitution itself. College Savings, 119 S. 
Ct. 2230; Alden, 119 S. Ct. at 2254. The Court also 
reaffirmed that the principle of state immunity embodied in 
the Eleventh Amendment is a constitutional limitation on the 
federal judicial power established in Article III of the 
Constitution. Alden, 119 S. Ct. at 2266. The Ex parte Young 
doctrine, however, is a judicially-created narrow exception to 
the Eleventh Amendment. It represents an attempt by the 
Supreme Court to “harmonize the principles of the Eleventh 
Amendment with the effective supremacy of rights and 
powers secured elsewhere in the Constitution.” Pennhurst II, 
465 U.S. at 105. 


4. Importantly, the Supremacy Clause is not a source of federal 
rights; rather it secures federal rights by according them priority 
when they come into conflict with state law. Golden State Transit 
v. Los Angeles, 493 U.S. 103, 107 (1989). 


26 


As Alden confirms, the Ex parte Young exception to the 
Eleventh Amendment is necessary to ensure the Constitution 
“remains the supreme law of the land.” Alden, 119 S. Ct. at 
2263. Thus, although state sovereign immunity is 
constitutional, the Court has determined that such immunity 
must yield in certain cases to protect other rights and 
privileges secured by the Constitution. Consequently, Ex 
parte Young requires a constitutional predicate for its 
application. In fact, given the constitutional origins of 
sovereign immunity and its limits on congressional power 
under Article I and federal judicial power under Article III, 
there would appear to be little difference between Congress 
using its Article I powers to abrogate a state’s Eleventh 
Amendment immunity (an action found to be without 
constitutional authority in Seminole Tribe) and a federal 
court using Ex parte Young to accomplish the same result by 
allowing private suits alleging nonconstitutional, federal 
statutory violations by state officials. In each case, the 
constitutional justification for such action is lacking. 


Here, Congress had no constitutional authority to subject 
Illinois to private suits in federal court under the Act, an 
exercise of Congress’ commerce power. The ICC’s 
adjudication of the Competing Carriers’ complaints, in 
which it resolved disputes over privately negotiated 
reciprocal compensation provisions in interconnection 
agreements, did not challenge the supremacy of the 
Constitution or violate any right protected by the 
Constitution. See Alden, 119 S. Ct. at 2263. Unlike a typical 
Ex parte Young action brought to enjoin a state official’s 
enforcement of a state law alleged to conflict with federal 
law, in this case Ameritech Illinois claimed the ICC erred in 


27 


interpreting privately negotiated portions of its agreements 
—— an action state law specifically authorizes the ICC to take. 
See Papasan v. Allain, 478 U.S. 266, 277 (1986) (“In 
accordance with its original rationale, Young applies only 
official acts is asserted to be illegal.”). 

The ICC is specifically authorized under state law to 
decide the issues raised in the Competing Carriers’ 
complaints. 220 ILCS 5/13-514(8); see id §§ 5/10-101; 5/10- 
108. No contrary claim was advanced, nor could it be. Given 
the power to make such decisions, however, an allegedly 
erroneous decision is insufficient to strip Illinois of its 
constitutional sovereign immunity through an Ex parte 
Young action against the Commissioners. As the Court stated 


in Larson v. Domestic & Foreign Commerce Corp., 337 
U.S. 682 (1949): 


It is argued that an officer given the power to make 
decisions is only given the power to make correct

Accordingly, the court erred in finding that the individual

ICC violated federal law in the past, relief not within the 
narrow £x parte Young exception. Ameritech also 
purportedly sought to enjoin enforcement of the ICC’s 
Order. Properly characterized, Ameritech sought reversal of 
the ICC’s Order. Pet. App. 14a (“Ameritech said that it 
brought this action to ‘challenge the ICC Order.””). That the 
ICC’s Order is still in place does not transform that past 
decision into a_ present, ongoing violation by the 
Commissioners. Ameritech Illinois did not claim that the 
Commissioners themselves were violating federal law when 
it filed its claims, let alone that they were continuing to 
violate federal law afterwards. In short, Ameritech Illinois 
sought relief from a past ICC decision, but the ICC’s Order 
itself does not constitute the ongoing violation for purposes 
of Ex parte Young. See Republic of Paraguay v. Allen, 134 
F.3d 622, 628 (4th Cir.), aff'd sub nom. Breard v. Greene, 
523 U.S. 371 (1998).

For the foregoing reasons, the petition for a writ of 
certiorari should be granted.

Evans, Circuit Judge. Once we determine whether we 
have jurisdiction (and the scope of that jurisdiction) under 
the Telecommunications Act of 1996, 47 U.S.C. §§ 251 and 
252, what we will have before us today is a rather narrow 
issue on the merits: whether a decision of the Illinois Com- 
merce Commission regarding reciprocal compensation for 
rect connections to Internet service providers violates 

Ww. 


Until the 1990’s local phone service was monopolistic; in 
fact, many people viewed it as a natural monopoly. Regula- 
tion was left to the states. Now, technological advances have 
taken us far beyond the sort of telephone services parodied 
by Emestine.' Today, we even have competition in local 
markets. Through the Telecommunications Act of 1996 
Congress has opened the door to competing local exchange 
Commission (FCC) and the federal courts into the previously 


| Lily Tomlin’s character on Rowan and Martin’s 1960’s TV 
show, Laugh-In who with an officious tone and a lot of nasal 
snorting sat at her switchboard and asked, “Have I reached the 


party to whom I am speaking?” 


8a 


state-regulated monopoly. Just how far into the scheme does 
the federal presence reach? is the $64,000 question. 


Illinois Bell Telephone Company, doing business as 
Ameritech Illinois (Ameritech) is the incumbent local ex- 
change carrier in Chicago and most of the rest of Illinois. 
Worldcom Technologies, Inc., Teleport Communications 
Groups, Inc., MCI Telecommunications Corp., Inc. and 
MCIMetro Access Transmission Services, Inc., AT&T 
Communications of Illinois, Inc., and Focal Communications 
Corporation have, under the Act, recently become competi- 
tors with Ameritech for local telephone business. When a 
competitor builds its own local network it interconnects its 
facilities with Ameritech so that calls can be made between 
customers of the two networks. For example, when an 
Ameritech customer makes a call, the person or entity called 
may be the customer of another of the carriers; Ameritech 
bills its customer for the call as a local call. The same is true 
if a customer of a competing carrier calls an Ameritech cus- 
tomer. If this were all that happened, the carrier whose cus- 
tomer received the call would not be compensated for its part 
in the transaction. But the 1996 Act provides for “reciprocal 
compensation” for local calls and it requires companies to 
establish agreements for intercarrier compensation for the 
calls. If the call is a local call, then, under the Act in the first 
example, Ameritech would have to pay reciprocal compensa- 
tion to the other carrier for “terminating” the call (in the 
lingo of the industry). In this way, both carriers get money. 
Ameritech and each of the other carriers have interconnec- 
tion agreements as required by the Act, 47 U.S.C. §§ 
252(b)(5) and 252(d). 


9a 


Section 252(d) sets out the procedure by which intercon- 
nection agreements are to be reached. The carriers must first 
attempt to negotiate in good faith to reach voluntary agree- 
ments. At any time in the negotiations a party may ask a state 
commission to participate as a mediator. If no agreement is 
reached, the Act provides for compulsory arbitration of unre- 
solved issues. Any party may petition a state commission to 
arbitrate the dispute. Furthermore, any agreement reached by 
negotiation or arbitration must be submitted to the state 
commission for approval. If a state commission fails to carry 
out its responsibilities, the FCC will preempt the state com- 
mission’s jurisdiction and act for the state commission. A 
state commission’s failure to approve or reject an agreement, 
however, it is not a failure to act; rather, the agreement will 
be deemed approved. 


The interconnection agreements between Ameritech and 
each of the carriers in this case are nearly identical. They be- 
came effective in late 1996 and early 1997 and have been 
approved by the Illinois Commerce Commission (ICC). For 
a time, the parties routinely paid reciprocal compensation 
under the agreement. 


Trouble arose, however, when Ameritech became con- 
cerned that it was paying out more in reciprocal compensa- 
tion than it was collecting. On July 3, 1997, it sent a letter to 
the other carriers, claiming that the imbalance was due to 
their inclusion of calls involving Internet service providers 
(ISPs) in the requests for reciprocal compensation. Ameri- 
tech said that it would no longer pay compensation for calls 
to Internet service providers because those calls were not lo- 
cal calls. It seems that the other carriers may have been con- 
centrating their marketing on ISPs. The agreements provided 


10a 


for compensation per minute of use, and calls to ISPs are, 
quite predictably, of long duration. 


That is true because Internet service providers are compa- 
nies which offer their customers connections to the Internet 
through the telephone network. ISPs are assigned a local 
telephone number and the telephone companies, including 
Ameritech, bill their customers for a local call when the cus- 
tomers call ISPs within the local calling area. However, the 
ultimate connection is usually to a website in a distant loca- 
tion, giving Ameritech a basis for claiming that the calls 
were not local calls subject to the reciprocal compensation 
agreements. For reasons we need not get into, Ameritech is 
not obligated to compensate the carrier of the ISP if the call 
is deemed to be long-distance, rather than local. In other 
words, at the present time if a call to an ISP is not subject to 
reciprocal compensation, the ISP’s carrier is not compen- 
sated. 


Not surprisingly, when Ameritech cut off payments the 
other carriers filed complaints with the ICC. On March 11, 
1998, the ICC ruled in their favor, finding that the plain lan- 
guage of the agreements mandates reciprocal compensation; 
that there is no basis for treating these calls differently from 
other local calls. The ICC, in fact, called Ameritech’s con- 
duct anti-competitive. 


Ameritech filed the present case in the District Court for 
the Northern District of Illinois, naming as defendants the 
competing carriers and the Commissioners of the ICC in 
their official capacity. Ameritech also filed a petition for re- 
view of the order in a state court. District Judge David H. 
Coar, to whom the federal case was assigned, reached a deci- 


lla 


sion, as Ameritech says, “affirming the ICC Order” on July 
21, 1998, and entered judgment in favor of the competing 
companies on August 4, 1998. In entering judgment under 
Rule 54(b) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, Judge 
Coar stated: 


This Court has currently pending motions to dis- 
miss the Illinois Commerce Commission [which, 
correctly labeled, would be a motion of the Com- 
_Missioners} from this action. Because of the nov- 
elty and urgency of the issues presented on the 
merits of the action and because the Eleventh 
Amendment issues presented by the Commission’s 
Motions to Dismiss were not related to the merits 
of the underlying claim, this Court directed the 
Commission to participate in briefing of the issues 
on the merits without prejudice to its rights to pro- 
ceed on its Eleventh Amendment motions. 


On August 25 the motions of the Commissioners to dismiss 
the action were denied. Before August 25 Ameritech had 
filed an appeal of the July 21 decision; after the decision on 
the motions to dismiss, it filed a second notice of appeal 
naming all defendants; the Commissioners filed a cross- 
appeal. All three appeal were consolidated. 


Appellate jurisdiction over the Commissioners is some- 
what problematic in that no formal judgment was entered 
against them. However, it is clear that the Commissioners 
participated in the litigation on the merits of this action. In 
entering judgment pursuant to Rule 54(b) on August 4, Judge 
Coar made it clear that his decision disposed of the merits of 
the case, but also allowed the Commissioners to continue 


12a 


with their claim that the federal court was a forum in which 
they did not belong, obviously an issue which should have 
preceded a decision on the merits. The decision on the mo- 
tions to dismiss resolved that issue, making the decision on 
the merits binding on the Commissioners (a decision, of 
course, going in their favor). It is a situation in which “for- 
mal defects in the terminating order do not prevent appeal.” 
Otis v. City of Chicago, 29 F.3d. 1159, 1165 (7th Cir. 1994). 
See also Bankers Trust Co. v. Mallis, 435 U.S. 381 (1978). 
Although we conclude that we have jurisdiction over the ap- 
peal as it involves the Commissioners, we are dismayed by 
this snag and, we might add, by the cursory marmer in which 
-Ameritech and the Commissioners treated the issue, despite 
our order to brief it. 


We proceed to the merits. The issue as Ameritech pre- 
sented it to us is whether the calls to Internet service provid- 
ers are local calls, subject to reciprocal compensation, or 
long-distance calls, often interstate. That is the issue which 
was exhaustively briefed; that, in fact, is the easy issue. But 
that is not, and cannot be, the issue we decide. 


The appeal of the Commissioners of the Illinois Com- 
merce Commission will lead us, however circuitously, to the 
real issue before us. The Commissioners contend that the 
federal courts have no jurisdiction over them. Originally they 
contended that, among other things, sovereign immunity 
prevents federal courts from exercising jurisdiction over 
them in their official capacity because in their official capac- 
ity they are the Commission, a state entity, and the State of 
_ Illinois has not waived its sovereign immunity. That argu- 
ment is no longer tenable following our recent decision in 
MCI Telecommunications Corp. v. Illinois Commerce Com- 


l3a 


mission, 168 F.3d 315 (7th Cir. 1999), in which we deter- 
mined that the participation of the Commission in the 
scheme set out in the Act is a waiver of the state’s sovereign 
immunity. 


Another of the Commissioners’ contentions, however, is 
that the Act, which admittedly provides for some federal 
court review over some actions of the state commissions, 
does not provide review in this instance. This is an attack on 
our subject matter jurisdiction. The Commissioners say that 
the scope of review under § 252(e) is limited to review of an 
“agreement”; nowhere, they say, is a “federal district court 
authorized to review and order of a state commission under 
state law... .” 


Section 252(e)(6) says in part: 


In any case in which a State commission makes a 
determination under this section, any party ag- 
grieved by such determination may bring an action 
in an appropriate Federal district court to determine 
whether the agreement or statement meets the re- 
quirements of section 251 and this section. 


The commissioners rely on the word “agreement” and ignore 
the word “determination” despite the fact that in an earlier 
order in this case --- //linois Bell Telephone Co v. World- 
Com Technologies, Inc., 157 F.3d 500, 501 (7th Cir. 1998) -- 
we said flatly, “Decisions of state agencies implementing the 
1996 Act are reviewable in federal district courts.” In addi- 
tion, although the present case arises in a slightly different 
context, our decision in MCI explains the nature of our juris- 
diction. We noted that subsection 252(e)(6) provides for ju- 


14a 


dicial review of “state commission actions,” not simply re- 
view of “interconnection agreements” and that subsection 
252(e)(4), when read in conjunction with subsection 
252(e)(6), shows that Congress contemplated suits against 
state defendants in federal court. It provides that “[n]Jo State 
court shall have jurisdiction to review the action of a State 
commission in approving or rejecting an agreement under 
this section.” We concluded that this language meant that 
Congress envisioned suits reviewing “actions” by state 
commissions and that those suits were to be brought exclu- 
sively in the federal courts. In addition, we said that Con- 
gress intended that the “state commissions be parties to the 
federal court suits reviewing their actions, just as the FCC is 
a party to suits seeking review of its actions.” At 320. 


Of course, the Commission itself is not a party to the ac- 
tion before us even though, in its briefing in this court, 
Ameritech said that it brought the action to “challenge the 
ICC Order.” Similarly, in its complaint Ameritech said that it 
was filing the action pursuant to § 252(e) to challenge de- 
terminations made by the Illinois Commerce Commission. 
Even though the Commission is not a named party, the 
Commisisoners themselves acknowledge that. In their offi- 
cial capacity, they are the Commission. We have jurisdiction 
under § 252(e)(6) to review the actions of the Commission 
and will proceed on that basis. 


But what is the scope of review under § 252(e)? Writing 
for the Court in AT&T v. Jowa Utilities Board, 119 S. Ct. 
721, 733 n.10 (1999), Justice Scalia indicated that the Act 
was broad federal occupation of territory once belonging to 
the states and that in turning some “federal policymaking . . . 
over to state administrative agencies,” Congress set out a 


15a 


scheme which “is decidedly novel.” He continued, “saying 
that the attendant legal questions, such as whether federal 
courts must defer to state agency interpretations of federal 
law, are novel as well,” or, as he put it elsewhere, it is a 
“surpassing strange” setup. At 730 n.6. 


Our view of the scope of review, however, has already 
been set out in MC/ in which we indicated that we would 
review a state commission’s action for “compliance with the 
requirements of § 251 and § 252 of the Telecommunications 
Act... .” We also said that we would not review those ac- 
tions for compliance with state law. 168 F.3d at 320. The 
issue before us today, then, is whether the ICC, in determin- 
ing that under the agreements the parties intended that calls 
to Internet seivice providers would be subject to reciprocal 
compensation, violated federal law. 


We have long given deference to the pronouncements of 
the FCC. See, e.g. FCC v. National Citizens Comm, for 
Broad., 436 U.S. 775 (1978); Illinois Bell Tel. Co. v. FCC, 
740 F.2d 465 (1984). It also appears that none of the tele- 
phone companies involved in the present case dispute that 
deference is in order; in fact, not surprisingly in this instance, 
everyone thinks the FCC is on its side. That agency is in the 
process of calling for hearings on proposed rules and will 
presumably carry out its duty to resolve ambiguities in the 
statute. Jowa Util. Bd., 119 S. Ct. at 738. But in the absence 
of a rule, the agency issued a declaratory ruling on February 
26, 1999, on the issue of whether calls to Internet service 
providers are local calls. See Declaratory Ruling in CC 
Docket No. 96-98 (February 26, 1999). The ruling was is- 
sued after the briefing in this appeal and obviously after the 
agreements at issue here were drafted. 


l6a 


The FCC said 


that the communications at issue here do not ter- 
minate at the ISP’s local server, as CLEC’s [com- 
peting local exchange carriers] and ISP’s contend, 
but continue to the ultimate destination or destina- 
tions, specifically at an Internet website that is of- 
ten located in another state. 


Declaratory Ruling at 9 4 12. The FCC declined to separate 
ISP-bound traffic into two components: an intrastate service 
going to the local ISP and a portion, more likely than not to 
be interstate, going from the ISP to the websites. The conclu- 
sion was that ISP traffic for “jurisdictional purposes [is] a 
continuous transmission from the end user to a distant Inter- 
net site.” At 11 ¥ 13. This is the part of the ruling which 
Ameritech likes. ‘ 


But having determined its jurisdiction, the FCC went on to 
a discussion of regulation of the calls in the absence of a rule 
covering compensation for these calls. It is this part of the 
ruling that the other carriers prefer. Because there is no rule 
yet promulgated on this issue, the FCC said: 


We find no reason to interfere with state commis- 
sion findings as to whether reciprocal compensa- 
tion provisions of interconnection agreements 
apply to ISP-bound traffic, pending adoption of a 
rule establishing an appropriate interstate compen- 
sation mechanism. 


At 15 4 21. So parties may 


17a 


voluntarily include this traffic within the scope of 
their interconnection agreements under sections 
251 and 252 of the Act, even if these statutory pro- 
visions do not apply as a matter of law. Where par- 
ties have agreed to include this traffic within their 
section 251 and 252 interconnection agreements, 
they are bound by those agreements, as interpreted 
and enforced by the state commissions. 


At 15 ¥ 22. Even if ISP traffic is largely interstate, parties 
may have agreed to treat it as subject to reciprocal compen- 
sation or, in fact, a state commission may impose reciprocal 
compensation in arbitration proceedings: 
A state commission’s decision to impose reciprocal 
compensation obligations in an arbitration proceed- 
ing -- or a subsequent state commission decision 
that those obligations encompass ISP-bound traffic 
-- does not conflict with any Commission rule re- 
garding ISP-bound traffic. By the same token, in 
the absence of governing federal law, state com- 
missions also are free not to require the payment of 
reciprocal compensation for this traffic and to 
adopt another compensation mechanism. 


At 18 9 26. 


Now that the FCC has issued its ruling, and noting again 
that we defer to its reasonable interpretations of the Act, our 
task is to examine the ICC order, not to determine whether 
the ICC correctly applied principles of state contract law, but 
to see whether its decision violates federal law, as set out in 
the Act or in the FCC’s interpretation. 


18a 


The short answer is that it does not. The FCC could not 
have made clearer that in the absence of a rule; a state 
agency’s interpretation of an agreement so as to require 
payment of reciprocal compensation does not necessarily 
violate federal law. 


The agreements here provide: 


Reciprocal Compensation applies for transport, 
and termination of Local Traffic billable by Ameri- 
tech or [the other carrier] that a Telephone Ex- 
change Service Customer originates on 
Ameritech’s or [the other carrier’s] network for 
termination on the other Party’s network. 


All the contracts contain the following definition, “ ‘Recip- 
rocal Compensation’ is As Described in the Act.” In the con- 
tracts with AT&T, Focal Communications Corporation, 
Worldcom Technologies, Inc., MCIMetro Access Transmis- 
sion Services, another relevant definition is “ ‘Local Traffic’ 
means a call the distance of which is fifteen (15) miles or 
less as calculated by using the V&H coordinates of the origi- 
nating NXX and the V&H coordinates of the terminating 
NXX or as otherwise determined by the FCC or Commission 
for purposes of Reciprocal Compensation . . . .” The “Com- 
mission” referred to is the Illinois Commerce Commission. 
The contract with Teleport Communications Group provides 
that “ “Local Traffic’ means local service area calls as de- 
fined by the Commission,” which again is the ICC. 


The ICC concluded that the agreement unambiguously 
provide that: 


19a 


reciprocal compensation is applicable to local traf- 
fic billable by Ameritech. Since Ameritech Illinois 
currently charges end users local service charges 
when completing calls that terminate at the com- 
plainants’ ISP customers, the plain reading of the 
interconnection agreements inevitably leads to the 
conclusion that reciprocal compensation charges 
should apply to those calls. 
ICC order dated March 18, 1998, at 11. 
Ameritech attacks this conclusion primari ily by stating that 
the Act does not require reciprocal compensation; the 
agreements precisely track the Act (reciprocal compensation 
is “as described in the Act”); therefore the agreements cannot 
require reciprocal compensation for calls to ISPs. 


The syllogism is an oversimplification. That the Act does 
not require reciprocal compensation for calls to ISPs is not 
to say that it prohibits it. The Act simply sets out the obliga- 
tions of all local exchange carriers to provide for reciprocal 
compensation: “Reciprocal compensation. The duty to estab- 
lish reciprocal compensation arrangements for the transport 
and termination of telecommunications.” § 251 (b)\(5). Then 
in § 252 (d)(2) state commissions are instructed that terms 
and conditions for reciprocal compensation are not to be 
considered reasonable unless they provide “for the mutual 
and reciprocal recovery by each carrier of costs associated 
with the transport and termination on each carrier’s network 
facilities of calls that originate on the network facilities of 
the other carrier” and that the costs be determined on the ba-

what the parties intended under the agreements. The FCC 
sets out factors which the state commissions may consider in

III. DISCUSSION 


The defendant Commissioners’ motions to dismiss raise 
four different arguments: (1) lack of subject matter jurisdic- 
tion; (2) Eleventh Amendment bar; (3) res judicata and col- 
lateral estoppel; and, (4) abstention. This court will analyze 
each argument in turn. 


A. Subject Matter Jurisdiction 

The Commissioners’ first argument is that this court lacks 
subject matter jurisdiction to proceed. Specifically, the 
_ Commissioners state that neither Section 252(e)(6) nor 28 
U.S.C. § 1331 authorizes this type of review in federal dis- 
trict court. The standard of review for a Rule 12(b)(1) motion 
to dismiss for lack of subject matter jurisdiction depends 
upon the purpose of the motion. See Freiburger v. Emery Air 
Charter, Inc., 795 F. Supp. 253, 256 (N.D. Ill. 1992); SA 
Wright & Miller, Federal Practice & Procedure: Civil.2d, 
§ 1363 at 456 (2d ed. 1990). If, as in the instant case, the mo- 
tion simply challenges the sufficiency ofthe allegations of 
subject matter jurisdiction, the court must accept as true all 
well-plead factual allegations and draw all reasonable infer- 
ences in favor of the plaintiff. United Transp. Union v. 
Gateway Western Ry. Co., 78 F.3d 1208 (7th Cir. 1996) (cit- 
ing Rueth v. U.S. E.P.A., 13 F.3d 227, 229 (7th Cir. 1993)). 


Ameritech asserts that subject matter jurisdiction is proper 
under Section 252(e)(6) of the Telecommunications Act. 
Section 252(e)(6) provides that: 


27a 


In any case in which a State commission makes a 
determination under this section, any party ag- 
grieved by such determination may bring an action 
in an appropriate Federal district court to determine 
whether the agreement or statement meets the re- 
quirements of section 251 of this title and this 
section. 


47 U.S.C.§ 252(e)(6) (1998). The Commissioners first argue 
that ICC’s order which is the subject of review in this case is 
not a “determination” within the meaning of Section 
252(e)(6). Therefore, Ameritech cannot be “aggrieved” by a 
determination by the state commission and cannot bring an 
action under the Act. Second, the Commissioners contend 
that Section 252(e)(6) does not provide for federal court re- 
view of an order of a state commission involving the inter- 
pretation and enforcement of an Interconnection Agreement 
after the Agreement has been submitted to a state commis- 


sion and approved. This court finds that neither argument has 
merit. 


The Commissioners cite recent federal district court deci- 
sions in support of their position that the ICC has not made a 
reviewable “determination.”. However, this court finds that 
the cases relied on by the Commissioners do not support 
such a position. See GTE South Inc. v. Morrison, 957 F. 
Supp. 800 (E.D. Va 1997); GTE North v. Glazer, 989 F. 
Supp. 922 (N. D. Ohio 1997); GTE North v. McCarty, 978 F. 
Supp. 827 (N.D. Ind. 1997); GTE Northwest v. Hamilton, 
971 F. Supp. 1350 (D. Or. 1997). In all of these district court 
decisions, the court found that subject matter jurisdiction was 
lacking due to the fact that the state commission had not yet 
approved or rejected an interconnection agreement by the 


28a 


parties. Therefore, the courts found that there was no “de- 
termination” that was ripe for review. See e.g., Morrison, 97 
F. Supp. at 804 (“The statute’s structure and purpose would 
be thwarted if this Court’s review commenced before the 
completion of the [state commission’s ] proceedings.”’). The 
instant case arises under a very different context. Here, 
Ameritech seeks review of a state commission decision in- 
terpreting and enforcing an already approved agreement. 
Therefore, the cases cited by the Commissioners are not con- 
trolling. 


In a recent Eighth Circuit decision interpreting the Act, 
Iowa Utilities Board v. Federal Communications Commis- 
sion, 120 F.3d 753, 804 n. 24 (8th Cir.1997), cert. denied, 
AT&T Corp. v. Iowa Utilities Bd. __ U.S. __, 118 S. Ct. 879 
(1998), the court found stated that “[w]e believe that the en- 
forcement decisions of state commissions would also be sub- 
ject to federal district court review under subsection 
252(e)(6).” This court finds that such an interpretation of the 
Act is consistent with the goals and structure of the Act. To 
hold otherwise would be to allow the state commissions to 
make critical decisions interpreting federal laws and policies, 
which was not Congress’ intent in passing a federal commu- 
nications act. See U.S. West Communications v. Hix, 986 F. 
Supp. 13, 17 (D. Colo. 1997)(“{S]}tate commissions, while 
having experience in regulating local exchange carriers in 
intrastate matters, have little or no expertise in implementing 
federal laws and policies and do not have the nationwide 
perspective characteristic of a federal agency.”). The Act 
specifically provides that federal courts have exclusive juris- 
diction to review Interconnection Agreements and to ensure 
that those agreements meet federal requirernents. See MC/ 


29a 


Telecommunications, No. 97 C 2225 et al., 1998 WL 156678 
at *14 (finding that § 252(e)(6) provides for exclusive review 
of agreements in federal court); 47 U.S.C. § 252(e)(4)(“No 
State court shall have jurisdiction to review the action of a 
State commission in approving or rejecting an agreement 
under this section.”). Allowing state court review of state 
commissions’ rulings interpreting agreements would run 
afoul of Congress’ intent. See Michigan Bell Telephone Co. 
v. MFS Intelnet of Michigan, Inc., No. 5:98 CV 18, F. 
Supp. 2d _, __, 1998 WL 413749, at *4 (W.D. Mich. July 
21, 1998) (“[C]onsideration of the statutory scheme demon- 
strates that permitting state court review of this type of Order 
would directly contravene the intent of Congress.”). In con- 
clusion, this court finds that the ICC’s order interpreting the 
meaning of the reciprocal compensation provision of the In- 
terconnection Agreements constitutes a “determination” un- 


der the meaning of the Act that is subject to review in federal 
court.

C. Res Judicata and Collateral Estoppel 


In their second motion to dismiss, the Commissioners ar- 
gue that Ameritech’s suit is barred by the doctrines of res 
judicata and collateral estoppel. Res judicata is & judicial 
doctrine designed to ensure the finality of judicial dlecisions. 
See Car Carriers, Inc. v. Ford Motor Co., 789 F.2d §89, 593 
(7th Cir. 1986). Res judicata, also called claim preclusion, 
bars the same parties or their privies from relitigating any 
issue that was raised in a prior judgment or could hiave been 
raised in the prior action. See Harper Plastics, Inc. w. Amoco 
Chemicals Corp., 657 F.2d 939, 945 (7th Cir. 198l). There 
are three threshold requirements to be considered in applying 
res judicata: (1) identity of the parties or their priivies; (2) 
identity of the causes of action; and, (3) a final judgment on 
the merits. See Lee v. City of Peoria, 685 F.2d 196, 199 (7th 
Cir. 1982). A court must find each of these threshold re- 
quirements in order to apply res judicata as a bar. The Com- 


3la 


missioners argues that the elements of claim preclusion are 
substance, and the ICC’s March 11, 1998 order constitutes a 
final judgment on the merits. 


Under the doctrine of collateral estoppel, “once a court has 
decided an issue of fact or law necessary to its judgment, that 
decision may preclude relitigation of the issue in a suit on a 
different cause of action involving a party to the first case.” 
Allen v. McCurry, 449 U.S. 90, 94, 101 S. Ct. 411, 414 
(1980). A federal court applies state collateral estoppel prin- 
ciples in determining whether a state court judicial determi- 
nation precludes relitigation of an issue. See Studio Art 
Theatre v. City of Evansville, 76 F.3d 128, 130 (7th Cir.), 
cert denied, U.S. _, 117 S. Ct. 177 (1996). In determin- 
ing the preclusive effect of a prior state court judgment, fed- 
eral courts must look to state law and determine whether 
another state court would give preclusive effect to the prior 
proceeding. Kunzelman v. Thompson, 799 F.2d 1172, 1176 
(7th Cir. 1986). A federal court must give the same preclu- 
sive effect to a state court ruling as would a court in the ren- 
dering state. Migra v. Warren City Sch. Dist. Bd. of Educ., 
465 U.S. 75, 81, 104 S. Ct. 892, 896 (1984). Under Illinois 
collateral estoppel principles, four elements must be met for 
collateral estoppel io apply: “(1) the issue sought to be pre- 
cluded must be the same as that involved in the prior litiga- 
tion, (2) the issue must have been actually litigated, (3) the 
determination of the issue must have been essential to the 
final judgment, and (4) the party against whom estoppel is 
invoked must be fully represented in the prior action.” La 
Preferida, Inc. v. Cerveceria Modelo, S. A. de C.V., 914 F.2d 
900, 906 (7th Cir. 1990). The Commissioners contend that 


32a 


all four elements have been satisfied, and that there is “no 
question” that Ameritech raised and fully litigated before the 
ICC the issues now presented in its complaint.‘ 


This court finds, however, that the doctrines of res judicata 
and collateral estoppel are inapplicable to the instant case. 
As previously stated, this court has exclusive juridiction to 
review determinations made by the ICC interpreting and en- 
forcing interconnection agreements. See supra Part III.A. 
This decision is supported by the Eighth Circuit’s recogni- 
tion that “the enforcement decisions of state commissions 
would also be subject to federal district court review under 
subsection 252(e)(6).” Jowa Utilities Board, 120 F.3d at 904 
n. 24. As an appeal from an administrative decision, this 
court has jurisdiction to review de novo the state commis- 
sions’ interpretation of law. All other issues, including fac- 
tual findings, are reviewed with substantial deference. See 
Illinois Bell Telephone Co., No. 98 C 1925, 1998 
WL419493, at *2-3. The fact that Congress gave federal 
courts jurisdiction to review such decisions negates any ar- 
gument that the state commission’s determination must be 
given preclusive effect. To hold otherwise would controvert 
the purpose of the Act. 


E. Abstention 


4 Counts I through IV of its complaint were decided before the

Finally, the Commissioners argue that this court should 
abstain from exercising its jurisdiction under the Burford and 
Younger abstention doctrines. “Federal courts have a ‘virtu- 
ally unflagging obligation to exercise the jurisdiction given 
them.’ ’’ Moses H. Cone Memorial Hosp. v. Mercury Constr. 
Corp., 460 U.S. 1, 15, 103 S. Ct. 927, 936 (1983) (quoting 
Colorado River Water Conservation Dist. v. United 
States, 424 U.S. 800, 813, 96 S. Ct. 1236, 1244 (1976)). 
Notwithstanding this obiigation, a court may abstain from 
exercising its jurisdiction in certain, narrowly construed cir- 
cumstances. The Supreme Court has identified four catego- 
nes of circumstances in which abstention may be 
appropriate. See Colorado River, 424 U.S. at 814-18, 96 S. 
Ct. at 1244-46. 


1. Burnford Doctrine 


Under what is commonly referred to as the “Burnford doc- 
tine,” where timely and adequate state-court review is avail- 
able, a federal court sitting in equity must decline to interfere 
with the proceedings or orders of state administrative agen- 
cies: (1) when there are “difficult questions of state law bear- 
ing on policy problems of substantial public import whose 
importance transcends the result in the case then at bar”; or 
(2) where the “exercise of federal review of the question in a 
case and in similar cases would be disruptive of state efforts 
to establish a coherent policy with respect to a matter of sub- 
stantial public concern.” New Orleans Public Serv., Inc. v. 
Council of City of New Orleans, 491 U.S. 350, 361, 109 S. 
Ct. 2506, 2514 (1989) (quoting Colorado River, 424 U.S. at 
814, 96 S. Ct. at 1245). 


34a 


The Commissioners argue that both of the alternate Bur- 
ford rationales for abstention are satisfied. However, this 
court finds that neither Burnford rationale applies to the in- 
stant case. First, the Commissioners have not made any con- 
vincing showing that there are difficult questions of state law 
presented. In fact, Count V is the only count that is brought 
under state law. The remaining counts were brought under 
the Telecommunications Act, a federal law. Second, the 
Commissioners have not demonstrated that federal review 
would be disruptive of state policy. The instant case involves 
the discrete question of whether calls terminated at Internet 
Service Providers are local for purposes of the interconnec- 
tion agreements. The ICC’s ruling on this issue was based 
entirely on the language of the Agreements themselves and 
federal law. See Illinois Bell Telephone, No., 98 C 1925, 
1998 WL419493, at *7-8. Therefore, no state policy that will 
be threatened by federal review. 


2. Younger Doctrine 


Under the Younger abstention doctrine, in the absence of 
extraordinary circumstances, a federal court will not exercise 


jurisdiction if such exercise would interfere with an ongoing . 


state court criminal prosecution. Younger v. Harris, 401 U.S. 
37, 46, 91 S. Ct. 746, 751 (1971). The Younger doctrine re- 
quires that a federal court exhibit “comity,” that is, a “proper 
respect for state functions.” /d. at 44, 91 S. Ct. at 750. Where 
vital state interests are involved, a federal court should ab- 
stain “unless state law clearly bars the interposition of the 
constitutional claims.” Moore v. Sims, 442 U.S. 415, 426, 99 
S. Ct. 2371, 2379 (1979). Courts considering the application 
of the Younger doctrine should consider three factors “(1) the 
judicial or judicial in nature state proceedings must be on- 


35a 


going; (2) the proceedings must implicate important state 
interests; and (3) there must be an adequate opportunity in 
the state court proceeding to raise constitutional challenges.” 
Barichello v. McDonald, 98 F.3d 948, 955 (7th Cir. 1996) 
(citing Trust & Inv. Advisers, Inc. v. Hogsett, 43 F.3d 290, 
295 (7th Cir. 1994)). The Commissioners argue that all three 
factors are satisfied because (1) Ameritech’s petition for re- 
view of the ICC’s March 11, 1998 order is currently pending 
in the Third District Appellate Court; (2) the regulation of 
telecommunications carriers is an important state interest and 
matter of substantial public concern; and, (3) Ameritech has 
an adequate opportunity to raise any federal issues in the on- 
going state proceedings. 


This court finds that Younger abstention is applicable to 
the instant case because there is no available state remedy for 
review of the ICC’s decision. As this court has explained, 
under the Telecommunications Act, federal courts provide 
exclusive review for the decisions of state commissions. See 
Michigan Bell, _ F. Supp.2d at __, 1998 WL 413749, at *8 
(finding that abstention does not apply because federal courts 
have exclusive jurisdiction over plaintiffs’ 252(e)(6) claim). 7 


IV. CONCLUSION 


For the reasons stated in this Memorandum Opinion and 
Order, the Commissioners’ first and second motions to dis- 
miss are DENIED.

5/13-514 Prohibited Actions of Telecommunications Car-

§ 13-514. Prohibited Actions of Telecommunications Car- 
riers. A telecommunications carrier shall not knowingly 
impede the development of competition in any telecommuni- 
cations service market. The following prohibited actions are 
considered per se impediments to the development- of 
competition; however, the Commission is not limited in any 
manner to these enumerated impediments and may consider 
other actions which impede competition to be prohibited: 


(1) unreasonably refusing or delaying intercon- 
nections or providing inferior connections to an- 
other telecommunications carrier; 


(2) unreasonably impairing the speed, quality, or 
efficiency of services used by another telecommu- 
nications carrier; 

(3) unreasonably denying a request of another 
provider for information regarding the technical 
design and features, geographic coverage, informa- 
tion necessary for the design of equipment and 
traffic capabilities of the local exchange network 
except for proprietary information unless such in- 
formation is subject to a proprietary agreement or 
protective order;

(4) unreasonably delaying access in connecting 
another telecommunications carrier to the local ex- 
change network whose product or service requires 
novel or specialized access requirements; 


(5) unreasonably refusing or delaying access by

(6) unreasonably acting or failing to act in a 
manner that has a substantial adverse effect on the 
ability of another telecommunications carrier to 
provide service to its customers; 

(7) unreasonably failing to offer services to cus- 
tomers in a local exchange, where a telecommuni- 
cations carrier is certificated to provide service and
