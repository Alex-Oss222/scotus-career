STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act mandates adoption 
of redistricting plans that afford Latino citizens an equal 
opportunity to participate in the political process and to 


elect representatives of their choice. 42 U.S.C. § 1973; 
Thornburg v. Gingles, 478 U.S. 30 (1986). Plaintiffs-appel- 
lants allege that the Texas congressional and state senate 
plans violate the Voting Rights Act because they dilute 
Latino voting strength. 
After the Texas Legislature failed to enact any redis- 
tricting plan in its 2001 session, the Texas Legislative 
Redistricting Board (LRB) convened and enacted state 
legislative redistricting plans, including the senate plan 
challenged here. 
Plaintiffs-appellants’ (hereafter “plaintiffs”) suit chal- 
lenging these state legislative redistricting plans and 
seeking the adoption of a congressional redistricting plan 
proceeded to trial before a three-judge panel of the 
United States District Court for the Eastern District of 
Texas in October and November, 2001. The District Court 
upheld the LRB’s senate redistricting plan, which con- 
tained the same number of Latino-majority districts as the 
previous decade’s redistricting plan. In addition, the Dis- 
trict Court crafted its own congressional plan that con- 
tained the same number of Latino-majority districts as the 
previous decade’s redistricting plan. In both the congres- 
sional and senate phases of the case, the lower court 
erroneously ruled, without explanation of its reasoning, 
that proposed districts in which Latinos comprise the 
majority of registered voters fail to satisfy the first pre- 
condition for section 2 relief, as established in Thornburg 
v. Gingles. 
Plaintiffs appeal the District Court’s decisions with 
respect to the Texas congressional and senate redistricting 
plans. The District Court failed to apply properly the 


standards for relief under section 2 of the Voting Rights 
Act. As a matter of law, plaintiffs’ showing meets and 
exceeds what the Voting Rights Act requires. In addition, 
as its lack of any findings demonstrates, the District 
Court also failed to give adequate consideration to the 
voting rights claims before it. 
A. Factual Background 
The 2000 Census revealed that the Hispanic! popula- 
tion of Texas increased by 2.3 million persons during the 
preceding decade. This rise in the Latino population, 
amounting to 60 percent of the state’s growth overall, 
contrasted sharply with an increase of only 641,000 Anglo 
persons.2 As Latinos rose to 32 percent of the state’s 
population, Texas gained two additional congressional 
seats, bringing the total number of seats in the Texas 
delegation to 32. 
Against this backdrop of Latino population growth, 
the Texas Legislature, the state judiciary and ultimately 
the federal court undertook the task of statewide redis- 
tricting. The Texas Legislature adjourned its regular ses- 
sion on May 28, 2001 without enacting any redistricting 
plans. The LRB, charged by default with the respon- 
sibility of crafting state legislative districts, convened on

June 6, 2001. On July 24, 2001, the LRB enacted plans for 
the Texas House and Texas Senate. Texas congressional 
redistricting remained unaddressed by the state legisla- 
ture when Governor Rick Perry declared that he would 
not convene a special legislative session for the purpose 
of congressional redistricting. 
B. Procedural History 
Plaintiffs filed this challenge to congressional, senate 
and house redistricting in the United States District Court 
for the Eastern District of Texas, which initially deferred 
action pending resolution of state court lawsuits. After 
parties filed suit in various state courts, the Texas 
Supreme Court declared that dominant jurisdiction lay in 
the Travis County District Court, and the state-court con- 
gressional redistricting litigation proceeded to trial in 
September 2001. After trial, on October 19, 2001, the 
Texas Supreme Court vacated the redistricting plan 
crafted by the Travis County court. Noting that, as a 
result, there was no state plan in place for congressional 
redistricting, the United States District Court commenced 
trial in the congressional phase of this litigation on Octo- 
ber 22, 2001. The United States District Court subse- 
quently tried the senate phase of the case on November 5 
and 6, 2001. 
The District Court issued its own congressional plan 
on November 14, 2001 and, on November 28, 2001, 
upheld the LRB’s senate plan. Plaintiffs filed their notice 
of appeal on December 14, 2001 and filed their amended

C. District Court’s Orders 
1. Congressional Redistricting Plan 
The District Court’s adopted congressional redistrict- 
ing plan did not include a single additional Latino-major- 
ity congressional district, and instead placed the state’s 
two new congressional districts in majority-Anglo subur- 
ban areas. New Congressional District 31 connects the 
suburbs of Houston and Austin and is more than 70 
percent Anglo. New Congressional District 32 is located 
in the Dallas suburbs and is more than 60 percent Anglo. 
The District Court’s plan did maintain pre-existing 
Latino-majority districts, but these districts had, with the 
dramatic growth of the Texas Latino population, become 
more densely populated with Latino residents over the 
past decade. Rejecting plaintiffs’ request to redistrict the 
South Texas region to include seven districts with a Lat- 
ino voter majority, the District Court created in that 
region only six districts, with an average 72 percent Lat- 
ino population. 
Although much of the evidence at trial focused on 
the question of minority voting rights, the lower court 
made no findings of fact regarding Latino population 
increase, geographic concentration, cohesive voting 
among Latinos, or Anglo bloc voting. The District Court 
examined none of the factors identified in the Senate 
Report accompanying the 1982 amendments to the Voting 
Rights Act as relevant to a vote dilution inquiry. 


Concluding that “the evidence shows that the Latino 
population is not sufficiently compact or numerous to 
support another, effective majority Latino citizenship -dis- 
trict in Texas,” the District Court failed to consider any of 
the factual evidence that plaintiffs presented in satisfac- 
tion of the first Gingles precondition, including, most 
importantly, plaintiffs’ proposed seven Latino registered- 
voter majority districts in South Texas. J.S. App. at 43. 
Within a 16-page opinion, the District Court’s entire 
discussion of Latino vote dilution is confined to four 
sentences: 
We find that the plaintiffs have failed to prove 
that vote dilution will occur in violation of sec- 
tion 2 of the Voting Rights Act in the absence of 
seven Latino citizenship majority congressional 
districts. . . . The evidence did not persuade us 
that... Latino . . . voting age populations are 
sufficiently numerous to form voting age popu- 
lation majorities in effective districts. . .. As for 
the proposed Latino opportunity districts, the 
evidence shows that the Latino population is not 
sufficiently compact or numerous to support 
another, effective majority Latino citizenship 
district in Texas, in Dallas County or in South 
Texas. We find that, under the totality of the 
circumstances, the failure to create seven such 
districts will not prevent full and equal Latino 
participation in the political process. 
J.S. App. at 12, 14-15. 
2. Senate Redistricting Plan 
Two weeks after issuing its decision in the congres- 
sional phase of the case, the District Court upheld the 


31-seat senate redistricting plan that the LRB created. 
This senate plan created only six Latino majority districts 
in South Texas. In this phase of the litigation, plaintiffs 
again proposed seven Latino voter majority districts in 
the region (J.S. App. at 46), and the District Court again 
concluded that plaintiffs had failed to satisfy the require- 
ments of the first Gingles precondition. 
The District Court adopted its earlier conclusions 
from the congressional phase of the case and did not 
consider facts and evidence relevant to plaintiffs’ pro- 
posed senate districts, despite the fact that plaintiffs’ 
proposed senate districts were geographically different 
from plaintiffs’ proposed districts in the congressional 
phase. 
The court’s entire relevant discussion is as follows: 
Looking first to the Balderas . . . plaintiffs’ 
claims, we must reject these claims for reasons 
substantially similar to those given in our deci- 
sion in the congressional redistricting case. The 
record before us demonstrates that the Latino 
population is not sufficiently compact or 
numerous to support another, effective majority 
Latino citizenship district in Dallas and Tarrant 
Counties or in South Texas. We further find that, 
under the totality of the circumstances, the fail- 
ure to create a seventh Latino-majority district 
will not prevent full and equal Latino participa- 
tion in the political process. Rather, creating 
such a district would risk giving rise to retro- 
gression in the existing Latino-majority districts. 
We conclude that these plaintiffs have failed to 
prove that section 2 of the Voting Rights Act 


requires any correction to the LRB Senate plan 
based on allegations of Latino vote dilution. 
J.S. App. at 26.

ARGUMENT - THE QUESTIONS PRESENTED 
IS SUBSTANTIAL 
With growth in the Latino population largely respon- 
sible for the increase in Texas’ population in the 1990s 
and for the concomitant state gain of two congressional 
seats following the 2000 Census, plaintiffs invoked the 
jurisdiction of the federal court to draw new congres- 
sional and state legislative district lines cognizant of the 
need to prevent Latino vote dilution. Even though these 
Voting Rights Act claims are what led to the convening of 
a three-judge District Court, the court paid scant atten- 
tion to the claims in subsequently designing a congres- 
sional redistricting plan and ratifying a Texas state senate 
plan. While the panel described at some length how it 
balanced the interests and arguments of the myriad parti- 
san political interests that intervened in plaintiffs’ action, 
it rejected plaintiffs’ serious civil rights claims with a few 
conclusory lines and no findings of fact or clear conclu- 
sions of law whatsoever. Although the panel invoked the 
proper phrase, “totality of the circumstances,” in rejecting 
plaintiffs’ claims, its cursory decision demonstrates a fail- 
ure to evaluate that totality and a failure to provide the 
parties and this Court an adequate basis from which to 
assess or understand the panel’s conclusions. This error 
sets a dangerous precedent for the subordination of vot- 
ing rights claims in litigation following future decennial 
redistricting throughout the nation. 


Even more troubling and important than the District 
Court’s cursory overall evaluation are two erroneous 
legal conclusions underlying its sparely-worded rejection 
of plaintiffs’ claims. First, the court held that Latinos 
were not numerous enough to constitute a “majority” in 
an additional congressional district or an additional sen- 
ate district even though plaintiffs proved that Latinos 
could constitute over 50 percent of registered voters in 
such districts. In an abundance of caution made possible 
by the substantial numbers of Latinos residing in South 
Texas, plaintiffs presented and requested only the cre- 
ation of Latino voter-registration majority districts in 
South Texas even though no federal court had ever before 
required such a high threshold — previous courts having 
opted instead for population, voting age, or citizen voting 
age majority standards. 
Although Fifth Circuit precedent itself firmly estab- 
lishes a lesser standard, the District Court nonetheless 
_ rejected plaintiffs’ satisfaction of the unprecedentedly dif- 
ficult voter-registration standard. The District Court thus 
imposed, without fully articulating it, an even higher 
standard still - a standard that is at once both difficult to 
conceive and nigh impossible to achieve. The boundless 
nature of the court’s implied standard — exceeding a voter 
majority by an immeasurable amount - is contradictory 
to Gingles, which crafted the majority-minority standard 
as a measure of opportunity to elect in a district where a 
minority group’s voting strength is not overridden 
because of its size. The District Court’s error sets a stan- 
dard that could render the Voting Rights Act a dead 
letter; such a precedent requires correction. 
a 


10 
Matching this unprecedented standard for numer- 
osity under the Voting Rights Act and Gingles, the District 
Court also imposed an impossible - and again wholly 
unarticulated - measure of compactness. Without ever 
explaining why, the court concluded that the Latino pop- 
ulation of South Texas is not sufficiently compact to war- 
rant an additional senatorial or congressional district. Yet, 
in other parts of the Texas maps, the court simultaneously 
adopted or ratified districts of lesser compactness. More- 
over, plaintiffs’ proposed Latino-majority districts were 
as compact or more compact than court-reviewed dis- 
tricts that applied in Texas throughout the 1990s. Thus, 
despite precedent establishing the appropriateness of a 
relaxed compactness standard for Voting Rights Act 
remedies, the court actually imposed a stricter standard 
than it applied to other districts. This error, like its com- 
panion described above, sets a precedent whose ill- 
defined but nonetheless apparent effect is to render Vot- 
ing Rights Act claims nearly impossible ever to prove. 
The District Court’s errors threaten to subject plain- 
tiffs and other Latino voters in Texas to a decade of 
elections in districts that dilute their voting strength. 
Moreover, the insult to this ongoing injury lies in a 
largely unexplained federal court review whose only 
obvious instruction for future challenges is to establish 
largely unachievable standards under section 2 of the 
Voting Rights Act for “majority” and compactness. These 
erroneous precedents, together with the District Court's

I. Precedent Establishes Clear Requirements to Evalu- 
ate Claims of Dilutive “Packing” Under Section 2 of 
the Voting Rights Act. 
Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act prohibits any state 
or political subdivision from imposing a “voting quali- 
fication or prerequisite to voting or standard, practice or 
procedure . . . in a manner which results in the denial or 
abridgement of the right to vote on account of race or 
color.” 42 U.S.C. § 1973(a). 
In enacting the 1982 amendments to the Voting 
Rights Act, Congress explicitly intended the act to protect 
minority voters from mechanisms which had the effect of 
diminishing their voting strength. “[D]iscriminatory elec- 
tion systems or practices which operate, designedly or 
otherwise, to minimize or cancel out the voting strength 
and political effectiveness of minority groups, are an 
impermissible denial of the right to have one’s vote fully 
count, just as much as outright denial of access to the 
ballot box.” S. Rep. No. 97-417, at 28 (1982). 
This Court’s opinion in Thornburg v. Gingles provides 
the framework for determining the existence of vote dilu- 
tion after the 1982 amendments. Under Gingles, plaintiffs 
first must demonstrate: 
1. that the minority community is sufficiently 
large and compact to constitute a majority in 
a single-member district; 
Nm that minorities are politically cohesive; and, 
_ 


12 
3. that the white majority votes as a bloc gener- 
ally to defeat the minority’s preferred candi- 
date. 
Gingles, 478 U.S. at 50-51. 
After this Gingles three-part threshold is met, plain- 
tiffs must also show that, under the “totality of the cir- 
cumstances,” they do not possess the same opportunities 
to participate in the political process and elect representa- 
tives of their choice enjoyed by other voters. 
The Gingles framework, including the three-part 
threshold test, was developed in litigation over multi- 
member electoral districts, but it also applies in single- 
member district cases challenging the failure to draw a 
sufficient number of majority-minority districts. Growe v. 
Emison, 507 U.S. 25, 40 (1993). Thus, the framework 
applies to cases, like this one, challenging redistricting 
after the decennial census. 
“Packing” minority voters is a well-recognized dilu- 
tive mechanism. “How such concentration or ‘packing’ 
may dilute minority voting strength is not difficult to 
conceptualize. A minority group, for example, might 
have sufficient numbers to constitute a majority in three 
districts. So apportioned, the group inevitably will elect 
three candidates of its choice, assuming the group is 
sufficiently cohesive. But if the group is packed into two 
districts in which it constitutes a super-majority, it will be 
assured only two candidates.” Voinovich v. Quilter, 507 
U.S. 146, 153-154 (1993); see also Gingles, 478 U.S. at 46 n. 
11 (“Dilution of racial minority group voting strength 
may be caused [either] by the dispersal of blacks into 
districts in which they constitute an ineffective minority 


13 
of voters or from the concentration of blacks into districts 
where they constitute an excessive majority.”). 
Proving dilutive packing in the redistricting context 
requires plaintiffs to show, in satisfaction of the first 
Gingles precondition, that creating additional majority- 
minority districts is possible.> Johnson v. De Grandy, 512 
U.S. 997, 1007 (1994). 
Had the District Court properly applied this well- 
established framework for evaluating a claim of dilutive 
packing, it would have concluded that Latino population 
growth in Texas over the past decade has resulted in a 
sufficient number of Latino voters to comprise the major- 
ity in seven, as opposed to the pre-existing six, senate 
and congressional districts in the South Texas region — the 
pre-existing districts having become “packed” over the 
course of the 1990s. Instead, without ever explaining the 
new standards it would apply, the District Court 
departed from the established framework and applied 
unprecedented and amorphous standards under the first 
Gingles precondition to deny the addition of Latino voter 
majority seats in Congress and the Texas Senate. Almost 
as an afterthought, the court also stated its conclusion on 
the “totality of the circumstances” without ever consider- 
ing or determining the effect of evidence presented on 
that issue.

IV. The District Court Erred in Failing to Make Find- 
ings in Support of its Conclusions Sufficient to 
Allow Proper Appellate Review. 
This Court has long held that cases under the Voting 
Rights Act require findings that are “a blend of history 
and an intensely local appraisal of the design and impact 
of the [challenged practice] in the light of past and pre- 
sent reality, political and otherwise.” White v. Regester, 412 
U.S. 755, 769-770 (1973). The legislative history of the 
Voting Rights Act reiterates the reasoning of White and 
affirms that “the question whether the political processes 
are ‘equally open’ depends on a searching practical eval- 
uation of the ‘past and present reality.’” S. Rep. No. 
97-417, at 30. Detailed fact finding is required in voting 
cases because the “determination [of a violation] is pecu- 
liarly dependent upon the facts of each case.” Rogers v. 
Lodge, 458 U.S. 613, 621 (1982), quoted in Gingles, 478 U.S. 
at 79. 
Here, the District Court’s failure to evaluate facts 
related to plaintiffs’ vote dilution claim, and to make

Voting Rights Act case. Because of its expansive view of 
what is required for equitable adjudication of voting 
rights claims, this Court has remanded Voting Rights Act 
cases that do not include adequate findings of fact. See, 
e.g., Statewide Reapportionment Advisory Comm. v. Theodore, 
508 U.S. 968 (1993) (vacating judgment and remanding 
for further consideration in light of Solicitor General's

CONCLUSION 
According to the 2000 Census, Latinos are the largest 
minority group in the United States, and have become an 
increasingly significant presence in every region of the 
country. Therefore, it is increasingly important to ensure 
that courts apply the proper standards in assessing claims 
that lawmakers have responded to growth in the Latino 
community by unlawfully limiting the number of districts 
in which that community can effect electoral outcomes. 
In this case, the District Court failed to apply the 
proper standards, and, in doing so, established new, ill- 


27 
defined, and unprecedented standards that would make 
section 2 inappropriately difficult to enforce. The court 
should have recognized that a Latino community is nec- 
essarily — as a matter of law - sufficiently numerous to 
constitute the “majority” in proposed additional districts 
if it would constitute more than half of all registered 
voters in those districts. Instead, the court faulted the 
plaintiffs for failing to meet its own unrealistic, and much 
higher, threshold for “majority” status. The court also 
should have determined that the Latino community is 
compact enough to merit additional districts when those 
districts would be as compact or more compact than other 
districts in the state. The District Court instead applied 
some unknown - again higher - standard to hold Latinos 
in South Texas insufficiently compact to satisfy section 2. 
Finally, the District Court should have carefully analyzed 
all of the evidence and provided findings of fact before 
drawing any conclusion about the “totality of the circum- 
stances” as defined in Gingles. Instead, the court oblig- 
atorily invoked “totality,” without ever really applying it, 
in denying plaintiffs’ claims. 
These errors set regrettable precedents in adjudicat- 
ing the claims of all Voting Rights Act plaintiffs, not only 
in redistricting but in at-large challenges as well. More 
important, these are precedents that many courts could 
follow in addressing claims by the growing Latino

This phase of the redistricting case involves the Texas 
congressional districts following the 2000 census. After a 
period of deferral to the State of Texas as mandated in 
Growe v. Emison} and the failure of the State to produce a 
congressional redistricting plan, we are left with the 
“unwelceme obligation of performing in the legislature’s 
stead.”2 We will describe the course of this litigation and 
explain the process by which we drew the congressional 
redistricting plan which we order.?

Federal courts have a limited role in crafting a con- 
gressional redistricting plan where the State has failed to 
implement a plan. The limits are not to be found in the 
traces of the unconstitutional plan being replaced. 
“Although a court must defer to legislative judgments on 
reapportionment as much as possible, it is forbidden to 
do so when the legislative plan would not meet the 
special standards of population equality and racial fair- 
ness that are applicable to court-ordered plans.”§ Rather,

IV 
Finally, to state directly what is implicit in all that we 
have said: political gerrymandering, a purely partisan 
exercise, is inappropriate for a federal court drawing a 
congressional redistricting map.’® Even at the hands of a 
legislative body, political gerrymandering is much a 
bloodfeud, in which revenge is exacted by the majority 
against its rival. We have left it to the political arena, as 
we must and wisely should. We do so because our role is 
limited and not because we see gerrymandering as other 
than what it is: an abuse of power that, at its core, evinces 
a fundamental distrust of voters, serving the self-interest 
of the political parties at the expense of the public good. 
V 
The parties represented competing plans for redis- 
tricting the Congressional seats. We have passed by the 
approach by which these plans were created in favor of 
the approach we have described, which we found to be 
mandated by our position as a federal court engaging in 
our “delicate task with limited legislative guidance.”?

the Texas State Senate following the 2000 census. Follow- 
ing the failure of the state legislature to implement a 
redistricting plan for the Senate, the Legislative Redis- 
tricting Board adopted a plan on July 24, 2001, to redis- 
trict the 31 Senate seats.1 Because Texas is a “covered” 
jurisdiction under section 5 of the Voting Rights Act, the 
State then submitted this plan to the United States 
Department of Justice for preclearance.2 On October 15, 
2001, the United States Department of Justice informed 
the Texas Secretary of State that the LRB Senate plan, 
known as 1188S, had been precleared pursuant to section 
5 of the Voting Rights Act. This three-judge court then 
heard evidence on November 5 and 6, 2001, supplement- 
ing the evidence taken at the congressional redistricting

challenges to precleared legislatively-adopted redistrict-

State’s apportionment plan only insofar as it is deficient 
as a matter of federal law and may only act remedially to 
correct any such deficiency.}?

IV 
We conclude that there is no meritorious challenge, 
based on federal constitutional or statutory law to the 
LRB Senate plan, 1188S, nor any state law challenge over 
which this three-judge court has jurisdiction. As such, we 
hold that 1188S, as enacted into law by the LRB, governs 
State Senate apportionment for the State of Texas follow- 
ing the 2000 census. There be.ng no reason for delay, we 
direct entry of final judgment in this case pursuant to 
Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 54(b).

JOHN HANNAH, JR., Chief District Judge, having con- 
curred in the judgment, writes further. 
I have concurred with the legal conclusion reached 
by my brethren in regard to the House and Senate Redis- 
tricting Plan. I write separately to express my shock at the 
drawing of districts by the State of Texas. The dominant 
political party treated all members of the opposing party 
as if they were “enemies of the State” instead of respected 
state leaders, many with a great wealth of governmental 
knowledge and ability that has and could inure to the 
benefit of Texas. 
The State has obviously ignored traditional redistrict- 
ing principles in favor of political expediency. The State’s 
lack of respect for political subdivisions is evident. Two 
examples from the State’s Senate Plan will suffice 
although many examples from the House and Senate Plan 
could be cited. 
E) Paso County’s population according to the 2000 
Census is 679,622. The population figure for an ideal 
senate district is 672,639. A respect for political subdivi- 
sions would suggest making El Paso County into a single 


App. 34 
senatorial district. However, the State unnecessarily splits 
El Paso County into Districts 29 and 19. It places the 
residents of southern El Paso County in District 19 along 
with 335,367 persons from Bexar County, hundreds of 
miles away and of a different community of interest. 
Even more offensive is Senate District 17, a district 
described even by the State’s own expert witness as 
weird, odd and bizarre. Stretching over 180 miles from 
University Park in Harris County to the Louisiana border, 
it is barely a mile wide in spots, and requires travel by 
boat to meet the standards of contiguity. Starting in Uni- 
versity Park and Bellaire, in Harris County, Anglo 
enclaves within the borders of the City of Houston, it 
snakes west through a narrow corridor to another Anglo 
enclave of middle and upper middle class homes in the 
western suburbs of Houston, out to the edge of Anglo 
Katy. It wends its way south into another exclusive Anglo 
stronghold in the First Colony area of Fort Bend County, 
carefully avoiding the sections of Black population in 
bordering Missouri City while retaining the Anglo popu- 
lations of Missouri City. After wending its way south 
through the Anglo portions of Fort Bend County, it 
reaches into Brazoria County. It then veers northeast 
along a narrow strip of Galveston County, taking in the 
Anglo community of Jamaica Beach and a narrow part of 
the City of Galveston which borders the beach, but draws 
its line at the neighborhood of Galveston which contain a 
large Black population. The district continues its north- 
east direction along the coast of Chambers County, where 
its main inhabitants are the birds in the Anahuac 
National Wildlife Refuge, the population maps showing 
zero for human inhabitants in District 17’s section of 


App. 35 
Chambers County. Travel from the Galveston portion of 
this district into the Chambers County portion of this 
district, if eschewing a route into District 4, requires a trip 
on the Bolivar Ferry onto the Bolivar Peninsula. The 
District ends in Jefferson County, at the border of Louisi- 
ana. In Jefferson County, it splits the community of Port 
Arthur from its suburbs and, more importantly from 
Beaumont. The district lines show a careful encompass- 
ment of the small black community within Port Arthur 
while rejecting the larger Black bordering community of 
Beaumont. 
Examining District 17, referred to in the trial of this 
case as the “fish hook,” we note its extremely irregularity 
on its face and deplore the State’s rejection of traditional 
redistricting principles such as compactness, respect for 
political subdivisions, and community of interests. 6 
county lines are split by this district and no whole county 
is included. Further, District 17 splits 17 or 18 towns and 
cities in its meander through the coastal plane of south- 
eastern Texas. 
The evidence concerning the lack of regard for tradi- 
tional communities of interest was compelling. Jefferson 
County residents testified that they are often adverse to 
residents of Harris County on issues of air pollution and 
water rights and that the working class communities of 
Jefferson County have nothing in common with the mid- 
dle and upper middie class suburbs of Houston or with 
exclusive University Park. There was testimony that 
Jefferson County has been wholly within one senate dis- 
trict for the last 168 years.

Further, evidence showed that District 17 as it now 
stands, was introduced by a last minute amendment, and 
voted on by the Legislative Redristricting Board over the 
strong objection of Lieutenant Governor Ratliff who 
deplored the lack of fairness inherent in voting on an 
amendment which the public had not have [sic] a chance 
to see. 
All this being said, the federal courts have a limited 
role in considering challenges to precleared, legislatively- 
adopted redistricting plans. We may not substitute our 
judgment for that of the Legislature, in this case, the 
Legislative Redristricting Board, unless we find that the 
plan runs afoul of federal law. The State may engage in 
political gerrymandering even if it “so happens that the 
most loyal Democrats happen to be black Democrats and 
even if the State were conscious of that fact.”1 However, I 
concur with the legal conclusion that plaintiffs have 
failed to meet their evidentiary burden of showing that 
the Texas Senate plan violates the Constitution or laws of 
the United States, as distasteful as portions of it may be.
