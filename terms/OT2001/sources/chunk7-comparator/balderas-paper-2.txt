MOTION TO AFFIRM 
Pursuant to Supreme Court Rule 18.6, Appellee Brian 
Manley urges this Court to affirm the decision of a federal 
three-judge court establishing, after a full trial, the district 
lines for the 32 congressional districts allocated to the State 
of Texas. The district court properly rejected the argument 
that the Voting Rights Act required adoption of the 
Appellants’ proposed configuration of districts in South 
Texas, which sought to squeeze a seventh majority-Latino 
district into that region by drawing districts stretching from 
the Mexican border far to the north and lowering the Latino 
share of the population in each district. The court recognized 
that Appellants’ proposed districts were not sufficiently 
compact to trigger the requirements of section 2 of the 


_% 
Voting Rights Act, and that they would have the effect of 
fracturing the Latino population to the point where its 
electoral power would be reduced rather than enhanced. 
Appellants have identified no significant legal issue raised by 
this case. Nor can they plausibly claim that the district 
court’s conclusions lacked a strong basis in the evidence 
presented at trial. 
STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
The 2000 census revealed substantial population growth 
in the State of Texas. Much of the growth was fueled by an 
increase in the Latino population in all parts of the state, but 
the principal areas of growth were the Dallas/Fort Worth 
Metroplex and the greater Houston area. As a result of 
population growth, Texas gained two additional 
congressional seats, increasing its total from 30 to 32. 
Congressional redistricting in Texas in 2001 followed a 
difficult procedural path. The Texas Legislature was unable 
to agree on a congressional redistricting plan during its 
regular session, ending on May 28, 2001. Even before the 
end of the legislative session, multiple lawsuits had been 
filed in both state and federal courts. Appellants (the 
Balderas plaintiffs) and Appellee (plaintiff Brian Manley) 
each filed suit alleging that the old 30-district congressional 
plan was unconstitutional. Following the teachings of this 
Court in Growe v. Emison, 507 U.S. 25 (1993), a three-judge 
federal court in the United States District Court for the 
Eastern District of Texas (Fifth Circuit Judge Higginbotham 
and District Judges Hannah and Ward) entered an order 
deferring the federal court litigation in order to allow the 
State, whether through a special legislative session or state 
court litigation, to construct a congressional redistricting 


ie 
plan. The federal panel set a deadline of October 1, 2001, for 
the completion of the State’s efforts, with a trial in federal 
court on any remaining federal law issues to commence on 
October 15, 2001. The October deadlines recognized that a 
final congressional redistricting plan needed to be in place by 
no later than November in order to avoid delaying any of the 
statutory deadlines in the State’s election process. See Texas 
Election Code § 14.001 (requiring counties to distribute 
voter registration cards identifying each voter’s precinct 
between November 15 and December 6). 
Pretrial wrangling in state court ultimately required the 
Texas Supreme Court to determine which of two competing 
state trial courts properly had jurisdiction over congressional 
redistricting. See Perry v. Del Rio, No. 01-0728, 44 Tex. 
Sup. Ct. J. 1147, 2001 Tex. LEXIS 81 (Sept. 12, 2001). 
After that issue was resolved, trial began in an Austin state 
court on September 17, 2001, lasting two weeks. As the 
federal court Ccadline drew near, the three-judge court 
indicated its willingness to extend its deadline in order to 
allow the state trial court sufficient time to render its decision 
and impose a new redistricting plan. After rejecting all of the 
plans proposed by the parties, the state trial court in Austin 
proposed its own congressional plan, which it subsequently 
modified after receiving comments from the parties. Del Rio 
v. Perry, No. GN003665, Final-Judgment (D. Ct. Travis 
County, Tex., 353d Judicial Dist., Oct. 10, 2001). With the 
federal court deadline already past and the three-judge court 
preparing for trial on the plan imposed by the state court, the 
Texas Supreme Court, on expedited appeal, vacated the state 
trial court’s congressional plan on procedural grounds on 
October 19, 2001. See Perry v. Del Rio, No. 01-0988, 45 
Tex. Sup. Ct. J. 52, 20061 Tex. LEXIS 98 (Oct. 19, 2001). 
Thus, almost three weeks after the federal court’s initial

ARGUMENT 
Appellants have identified no plausible basis for 
overturning the congressional redistricting plan drawn and 
unanimously approved by the three-judge court. After a full 
trial in which the court received what Appellants 
acknowledge was “abundant” evidence concerning the 
possibility of creating an additional miajority-Latino 
congressional district in South Texas, the three-judge court 
found “that the Latino population is not sufficiently compact 
Or numerous to support another, effective majority-Latino 
citizenship district in . . . South Texas.” J.S. App. 14. It 
concluded that, “under the totality of the circumstances, the 
failure to create seven such districts will not prevent full and 
equal Latino participation in the political process” and thus 
was not a violation of section 2 of the Voting Rights Act. 
JS. App. 15. Appellants mount a number of factual and 
legal attacks on this straightforward conclusion, but none 
holds water. 
First, Appellants’ argument that the district court 
distorted the three-pronged test set forth in Thornburg v. 
Gingles simply mischaracterizes what the court below said. 
Appellants argue that the court erred in rejecting their claim 
that they had satisfied the first prong of Gingles, which 
requires a section 2 plaintiff to show that it is physically 
possible to create an additional reasonably compact minority 
district. They further argue that they satisfied this 
requirement by offering maps showing how to create an 
additional district with a bare majority of Spanish-surname 
registered voters. 
But the district court did not in fact address the question 
whether a court should look to voter registration data, voting- 


-9- 
age population (VAP) data, or citizen voting-age population 
(CVAP) data in analyzing Gingles’ first prong. It found that 
Appellants had failed to demonstrate the possibility of 
creating a seventh “effective” majority-Latino district, and it 
therefore rejected Appellants’ section 2 arguments at a later 
stage of the analysis — the final assessment of the “totality of 
circumstances.” See 42 U.S.C. § 1973(b). This finding was 
compelled first by the absolute lack of evidence in the record 
to support a claim that an additional effective district could 
be drawn. Appellants, apparently confident in their legal 
argument that likely electoral outcomes are irrelevant under 
section 2, introduced absolutely no evidence to suggest that 
the spread-out districts they proposed would actually give 
Latino voters a realistic opportunity to elect their preferred 
candidates in an additionai district. And the evidence 
introduced by other parties supported the conclusion that the 
combination of high polarization in voting patterns and low 
turnout among Latino registered voters meant that Latino- 
preferred candidates stood a very good chance of losing in a 
district with a bare majority of Latino registered voters. See 
infra Point I. 
Second, to the extent that the district court did address 
the first prong of Gingles, it rejected Appellants’ section 2 
claim on the separate and independent ground that Appellants 
had failed to satisfy that threshold requirement because their 
proffered districts were not “sufficiently compact.” J.S. App. 
14. Appellants argue that the court applied this requirement 
too strictly, but they fail to append their actual map 
corresponding to the data set forth in their appendix. An 
examination of that map reveals that they were asking the 
district court to create a series of long thin districts, including 
two that would have originated at the Mexican border and 
stretched to the outskirts of Austin and Houston, respectively. 


-10- 
While it may be true that these districts’ “compactness 
scores” — at least on one measure of compactness — were no 
worse than those of the least compact districts previously 
created by the Texas Legislature (which were the subject of 
this Court’s review in Bush v. Vera, 517 U.S. 952 (1996)), 
that is not the appropriate test for determining whether a 
federal court is compelled by the Voting Rights Act to adopt 
such districts when drawing a plan following a legislative 
deadlock. Rather, the district court was correct in 
determining that districts that stretch for hundreds of miles 
for the sole purpose of reaching a 50.1% quota of Spanish- 
surname registered voters are not compelled by the Voting 
Rights Act. See infra Point II. 
Third and finally, Appellants’ critique of the 
thoroughness of the district court’s factual findings provides 
no conceivable basis for reversal and is particularly 
unpersuasive given the exigent circumstances under which 
the federal court was acting, due to the impending election 
deadlines and the complete failure of the state legislative and 
judicial processes to produce a congressional district plan. 
Here, the trial court’s opinion makes_clear why it did what it 
did and why it rejected Appellants’ claim. Especially given 
Appellants’ total lack of proof that the districts they proposed 
would actually create realistic electoral opportunities for 
Latino voters, no more is required. See infra Point III. 
I. THE DISTRICT COURT DID NOT ERR IN 
REQUIRING A SHOWING THAT IT WAS 
POSSIBLE TO CREATE SEVEN “EFFECTIVE” 
MAJORITY-LATINO DISTRICTS. 
Appellants argue that the Voting Rights Act compels 
courts to ignore whether proposed remedial districts will 


-11- 
actually offer minority voters a realistic opportunity to elect 
their preferred candidates. Rather, Appellants claim that one 
only need look at demographics: If a proposed district is at 
50.1% or more in terms of Spanish-surname registration, 
then the district must be created, regardless of the actual 
impact on Latino voters. That is manifestly wrong. It would 
be a strange interpretation of the Voting Rights Act indeed to 
read it as requiring creation of an additional majority- 
minority district where a careful examination of the totality 
of the circumstances makes clear that doing so would 
actually reduce the political power of the protected class. 
A. Section 2 Requires an Inquiry into the Actual 
Opportunities of Minority Voters to Elect 
Candidates of Their Choice, Not a Bright-Line 
Test. | 
In Thornburg v. Gingles, 478 U.S. 30 (1986), this 
Court’s first examination of vote-dilution claims under 
amended section 2 of the Voting Rights Act, the Court 
- admonished trial judges to appraise the “totality of 
circumstances” and conduct a “searching practical 
evaluation” based on a “functional view of the political 
process.” 478 U.S. at 45, 77-79 (citations and internal 
quotation marks omitted). There is no bright-line test based 
on population statistics. Rather, a court must determine 
whether the challenged districts actually dilute the voting 
strength of minority voters and, concomitantly, whether a 
proposed remedy will actually enhance minority electoral 
opportunities. See id. 
Eight years later, in Johnson v. De Grandy, 512 U.S. 997 
(1994), the Court emphasized once again the need for a 
functional analysis of electoral opportunities. It rejected the 


Ad 
section 2 claims of Latino plaintiffs because Latinos 
“constitute[d] effective voting majorities in a number of. . . 
districts substantially proportional to their share in the 
population.” 512 U.S. at 1024 (emphasis added). The Court 
reasoned that such rough proportionality is a_ strong 
“indication that minority voters have an equal opportunity, in 
spite of racial polarization, ‘to participate in the political 
process and to elect representatives of their choice.’” Jd. at 
1020 (quoting Section 2). In so doing, the Court made clear 
that courts should look at “effective” or “functional” voting 
majorities, rather than relying entirely upon strict 
demographic tallies such as “50% Latino VAP” or “50% 
Spanish surname.” See, e.g., id. at 1000 (“effective voting 
majorities”); id. at 1004 (“a functional majority of Hispanic 
voters”); id. at 1014, 1021, 1023 n.19 (“an effective voting 
majority”); id. at 1017 (“districts in which minority voters 
form an effective majority’); id. at 1024 (“an effective 
majority”). And the Court defined a district with an 
“effective voting majority” as one “with a sufficiently large 
minority population to elect candidates of its choice.” /d. at 
1008. 
These cases thus teach that probable effectiveness — not 
simplistic arithmetic tallies — is the key to a proper 
application of the Voting Rights Act. In that Act, Congress 
sought to ensure minority voters actual opportunities at the 
polls, and it asked the courts to take on the sensitive and 
sometimes difficult task of determining whether a given 
districting scheme in fact is diluting minority voting strength 
either by unduly “packing” minority voters into too few 
districts or by unduly “fracturing” them into too many. See 
Gingles, 478 U.S. at 46 & n.11. 


| 
23. 
Other federal courts have also recognized a need for a 
“functional” evaluation, examining a variety of practical 
considerations, including differential rates of turnout, when 
considering the validity of a section 2 claim under the 
“totality of circumstances.” For example, in Ketchum vy. 
Byrne, 740 F.2d 1398 (7th Cir. 1984), the Seventh Circuit 
held that a district court “must carefully consider and 
evaluate the data concerning voter registration and turn-out 
in the black and Hispanic communities to determine the 
practical need for a super-majority of the respective minority 
groups in order to give the minorities a reasonable and fair 
opportunity to elect candidates of their choice.” /d. at 1414 
(emphasis added). “There is simply no point,” the court 
explained, “in providing minorities with a ‘remedy’ for the 
illegal deprivation of their representational rights in a form 
which will not in fact provide them with a realistic 
opportunity to elect a representative of their choice.” /d. at 
1413; see also Barnett v. City of Chicago, 141 F.3d 699, 702- 
03 (7th Cir. 1998) (discussing low black turnout and citing 
Ketchum, 740 F.2d at 1413-16), cert. denied, 524 U.S. 954 
(1998); African American Voting Rights Legal Defense Fund, 
Inc. v. Villa, 54 F.3d 1345, 1348 n.4 (8th Cir. 1995) (same), 
cert. denied, 516 U.S. 1113 (1996); Overton v. City of Austin, 
871 F.2d 529, 542 (Sth Cir. 1989) (Jones, J., concurring) (“It 
would be a Pyrrhic victory for a court to create a [majority- 
minority] district in which [the] minority population . . 
continued to be defeated at the polls.”); Romero v. City of 
Pomona, 883 F.2d 1418, 1425 & n.13 (9th Cir. 1989) (“Raw 
population totals are relevant only to the extent that they 
reveal whether the minority group constitutes an effective 
voting majority in a proposed single-member district given 
such factors as low voter registration and turnout patterns.”’) 
(internal citation omitted) (emphasis added). 


-14- 
This is not to say that majority-minority districts 
proposed by plaintiffs in section 2 cases must always have a 
population that is more than 50% minority. “[T]he question 
of the size of the majority necessary to ensure the likelihood 
of success at the polls cannot be determined by a set formula, 
but must be evaluated on a case-by-case basis according to 
the political and other characteristics of the relevant 
population.” Monroe v. City of Woodville, 819 F.2d 507, 510 
n.l1 (Sth Cir. 1987) (citing Ketchum). In some places, 
because of turnout rates and crossover voting patterns, a 
district can be “effective” even if African-Americans or 
Latinos constitute substantially less than 50% of the 
population. See Page v. Bartels, 144 F. Supp. 2d 346, 362- 
66 (D.N.J. 2001) (three-judge court) (finding that districts 
with less than an African-American majority may provide a 
realistic opportunity to elect candidates of choice for African- 
Americans). But see Valdespino v. Alamo Heights Indep. 
Sch. Dist., 168 F.3d 848, 852-53 (Sth Cir. 1999) (holding that 
there can no violation of section 2 unless the protected group 
constitutes at least a majority of a reasonably compact 
district), cert. denied, 528 U.S. 1114 (2000). But in other 
places with high polarization and differential turnout rates, a 
bare majority may not be enough. 
B. Appellants Made No Effort to Prove that Their 
Proposed Districts Would Give Latino Voters a 
Reasonable Opportunity to Elect Candidates of 
Their Choice. 
The record in the trial court is devoid of evidence to

Il. THE DISTRICT COURT CORRECTLY 
ASSESSED THE COMPACTNESS OF 
APPELLANTS’ PROPOSED DISTRICTS. 
Appellants also attack the district court’s compactness 
ruling, arguing that the court “failed to apply any legal 
standard” in determining whether the Latino population was 
sufficiently compact to justify the creation of seven — rather 
than six — majority-Latino districts. J.S. 18 (emphasis 
added). That is simply untrue. The court was fully able to 
examine the districts proposed by Appellants in their effort to 
satisfy the Gingles factors, and it reached the appropriate 
conclusion that Appellants had failed to demonstrate the 
possibility of creating an additional reasonably compact 
Latino majority district. 
This Court has made clear that section 2 does not require 
a State (or likewise a court) to create, on predominantly racial 
lines, a district that is not “reasonably compact.” Bush v. 
Vera, 517 U.S. at 979 (plurality op.) (citing Johnson v. 
DeGrandy, 512 U.S. at 1008 (1994)); see also Abrams vy. 
Johnson, 521 U.S. at 91 (“Here the District Court found, 
without clear error, that the black population was not 
sufficiently compact for a second majority-black district. . . . 
So the first of the Gingles factors is not satisfied.”). Under 
Gingles, section 2 is not violated if a reasonably compact 
minority district cannot be drawn. And under Shaw, 
noncompact districts that are drawn for the predominant 
purpose of attaining a particular racial composition 
presumptively violate the Fourteenth Amendment. See Bush 
v. Vera, 517 U.S. at 969-72 (plurality op.) (invalidating a 
noncompact district that had been intentionally drawn to 
meet a target of 50.1% African-American population). 


-18- 
The district court properly applied this Court’s dictates 
and avoided the specter of districts that violate the Fourteenth 
Amendment. Specifically addressing the Appellants’ 
proposal for an additional Latino district, the court correctly 
noted that it was not required to create a majority-minority 
district where sufficient compactness was lacking, J.S. App. 
9-14, and found that Appellants had not satisfied the 
compactness requirement in the first prong of Gingles. 
This finding is amply supported by the record. In 
evaluating whether a district is sufficiently compact for 
section 2 purposes, “[d]istrict shape is not irrelevant.” Bush 
v. Vera, 517 U.S. at 980 (plurality op.). An examination of 
the Appellants’ proposed districts indicates they were asking 
the court to create very long and noncompact districts, 
including two that would have originated at the Mexican 
border in far-southern Hidalgo County, stretching from there 
to the outskirts of Austin and Houston, respectively.° 
Moreover, the record further indicates that the districts were 
drawn in order to achieve a precise race-based target — 50.1% 
of Spanish surname registered voters; indeed, three of the 7 
districts have Spanish surname registrations of less than 52% 
and one is exactly 50.1%. J.S. App. 43-45. One. expert 
witness testified that, while disregarding _ traditional 
redistricting principles and subordinating compactness, the 
majority-minority districts proposed in Appellants’ Plan 
1123C divided counties and cities on the basis of race, 
shifting Latino precincts into Latino districts and Anglo

Appellants’ argument that its proposed Latino districts

redistricting principles where the goal is to create districts 
matching a particular racial or ethnic quota.

J.S. App. 4-9 (where lower court explained that it “did not

It also splits 17 more municipalities than the plan adopted by the court

make brief, definite, pertinent findings and conclusions upon 
the contested matters; there is no necessity for over-

Committee Notes to Fed. R. Civ. P. 52(a). Rule 52 is 
satisfied if the district court’s findings give the reviewing 
court a clear understanding of the factual basis for the 
decision. Burma Navigation Corp. v. Reliant Seahorse MV, 
99 F.3d 652, 657 (Sth Cir. 1996). Indeed, unlike Appellants’ 
seeming request, Rule 52(a) “exacts neither punctilious detail 
nor slavish tracing of the claims issue by issue and witness by 
witness.” Jd. (internal quotation marks omitted). The 
explicit mandates and purpose of Rule 52(a) do not suddenly 
change within the context of Voting Rights Act cases, despite 
the “intensely local appraisal” the lower court must make. 
See J.S. 20 (quoting White v. Regester, 412 U.S. 755, 769- 
770 (1973)). 
The district court operated under tremendous time 
pressures in performing its complex and enormous task. 
Moreover, unlike in an ordinary section 2 case, where there 
are specific districts being challenged, here the court was 
tasked with drawing an entire 32-district congressional plan 
for the State of Texas. The court heard expert and lay 
testimony on myriad plans, which it then had to distill in 
order to draw its own plan. 
The court did its job thoroughly and completely. In this 
case, the court clearly described the methodology it used, the 
expert testimony upon which it relied, the specific factors it 
used to draw the districts, the sequence of these factors, and 
the manner in which it checked whether the plan was 
detrimental to either political party. See J.S. App. 1-9. - 
Following that analysis, the court explained its understanding

of Appellants’ claim, and examined that claim under the

population is not sufficiently compact or numerous to

clearly supplied this Court with an appropriate roadmap for 
appellate review. 
Appellants’ reference to this Court’s summary remand in 
a 1993 Voting Rights Act case, Statewide Reapportionment 
Advisory Committee v. Theodore, 508 U.S. 968 (1993), is 
wholly inapposite. See J.S. 21. In that case, the issue was 
whether the trial court had applied the right statute; the 
United States sought a remand (which was granted) because 
the district court errantly thought the litigation arose under 
section 5 of the Act rather than section 2, and thus made “no 
such dispostive finding with respect to any of the Gingles 
threshold requirements.” See Brief for the United States as 
Amicus Curiae at 9, 12, Statewide Reapportionment Advisory 
Committee v. Theodore, 508 U.S. 968 (1993) (Nos. 92-155 
and 92-219). That is certainly not the case here. The court 
below clearly indicated its application of section 2 of the 
Voting Rights Act, as well as the Gingles three-part test in 
finding that the Appellants failed to meet both the first prong 
of the Gingles test, as well as the “totality of circumstances” 
test. See J.S. App. 9-15. Moreover, given that “failure to

violation,” see LULAC v. Roscoe Ind. Sch. Dist., 123 F.3d 
843, 847 (Sth Cir. 1997) (internal quotation marks omitted), 
the district court was under no obligation “to expressly 
mention all the evidence in its opinion.” Jd. at 846. 
Even if the district court’s findings lack some detail, as | 
Appellants’ allege, vacatur and remand is inappropriate

where remand proceedings are unlikely to “add anything 
essential to the determination of the merits.” Withrow v.
