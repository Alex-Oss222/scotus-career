ARGUMENT 
Like the State of Texas, appellee Brian Manley moves 
to affirm summarily the District Court decision rejecting 
plaintiffs’ claims under section 2 of the Voting Rights Act 
(VRA) on the basis of the single, undefined term “effec- 
tive” in the court’s opinion. The most natural reading of 
the term is as part of a simple reiteration of the first 
precondition to Section 2 liability, as established in Thorn- 
burg v. Gingles, 478 U.S. 30 (1986), yet Manley would 
instead conjure out of that isolated term a whole series of 
unstated fact findings and legal conclusions with respect 
to district “effectiveness.” 
A review of the District Court decision, its actions, 
and the evidence it considered proves Manley’s conjura- 
tion a true phantom. Plaintiffs satisfied every established 
test for the first Gingles precondition, and even produced 
evidence of practical “effectiveness” as to their proposed 
Latino voter-majority districts; furthermore, the court’s 
own adopted congressional map refutes any contention 
that the panel credited the State’s and Manley’s scant 
testimony of purported ineffectiveness. 
By presenting compact voter-registration Latino 
majority districts, plaintiffs passed every test that any 
federal court has ever endorsed to determine the first 
Gingles precondition. The District Court, therefore, quite 
simply erred in concluding otherwise. While the court’s 
failure to provide adequate findings and conclusions pre- 
cludes a complete understanding of what test the court 
did apply, what it did not apply - namely, any established 
test for the first Gingles precondition - is much clearer.}

While such partisan concern in a state where Con- 
gressional Democrats are losing ground might be under- 
standable, the Voting Rights Act (VRA) countenances no 
such parochial interest. Under section 2 of the VRA, the 
overriding consideration is to remedy minority vote dilu-. 
tion, not to maximize re-election. Yet, Manley’s “effective- 
ness” test would elevate partisan interests over these 
voting rights. The test would even convert matters long 
considered evidence of potential vote dilution - such as 
low minority registration rates or low turnout - into 
phenomena that block any chance of success in proving 
dilution - and concomitantly block any chance to address 
low levels of political participation through a section 2 
remedy. Manley would create such a topsy-turvy world, 
all in service of narrow partisan interests. 
This case requires plenary review in order to ensure 
that such partisan-inspired doctrines did not affect the 
District Court’s largely unexplained decision, and to fore- 
stall these attempts in the future.

by plaintiffs, while reducing the super-majorities that 
ensued from Latino population growth over the past

and the concomitant ability to elect the Latino candidate 
of choice in each of the districts. 
All of the South Texas congressional and senate dis- 
tricts that plaintiffs proposed contained a majority of 
Latinos eligible and qualified to go to the polls and cast a 
vote on election day. This Latino majority of registered 
voters exceeds both the Fifth Circuit’s population thresh- 
old for the first Gingles precondition (citizen voting age 
population (CVAP) majority) and, on a practical level, far 
exceeds the historical threshold for effective Latino vot- 
ing majorities in Texas.”
