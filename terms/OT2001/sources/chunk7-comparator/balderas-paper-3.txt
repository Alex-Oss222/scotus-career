QUESTIONS PRESENTED 
Whether Appellants can establish §2 liability regarding the 
Texas Senate and congressional districts solely by drawing a 
seventh majority Spanish-surname-registered-voter (SSRV) 
border district that (a) subordinates the traditional districting 
principle of compactness to race and (b) causes decreases in 
Latino voting ‘strength in other existing SSRV-majority 
districts, jeopardizing the districts’ effectiveness. 
Whether Appellants can satisfy the first Gingles precondition 
by drawing seven SSRV-majority Texas Senate districts that 
must be intentionally underpopulated, based on race, to add the 
seventh SSRV-majority district to the existing six. 
Whether the three-judge court’s per curiam opinions—which 
adequately state the factual and legal bases for the court’s 
judgments in the congressional and Texas Senate redistricting 
cases—violate Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 52(a).

I. THE QUESTIONS PRESENTED ARE NOT SUBSTANTIAL AND 
THE COURT SHOULD SUMMARILY AFFIRM. 
The three-judge court correctly rejected Appellants’ §2 claims 
in the congressional and Texas Senate redistricting litigation. App., 
at 11-12, 25-26. In each case, Appellants urged the creation of a 
seventh majority Spanish-surname-registered-voter (SSRV) district 
along the Texas border.’ To achieve this end, Appellants pulled 
SSRV from other currently effective Latino districts and contorted 
border population into a highly irregular, seventh SSRV district. 
The three-judge court properly recognized the impermissibility of 
Appellants’ approach, and this Court should summarily affirm 
because: (1) a seventh SSRV border district cannot be created 
without subordinating the traditional districting criteria of 
compactness to race; (2) Appellants’ inability to demonstrate the 
effectiveness of their seven proposed SSRV districts defeats their 
§2 claims under the “totality of the circumstances” test; and (3) 
Appellants’ §2 challenge to the Texas Senate districts, in particular,

A. Appellants Mischaracterize the Basis for the Three- 
Judge Court’s Rejection of Their §2 Claims. 
Appellants erroneously state that the three-judge court “held 
that Latinos were not naynerous enough to constitute a ‘majority’ in 
an additional congressional district or an additional senate district” 
even though SSRV population in the proposed districts was 50% or 
greater. J.S., at 9 (emphasis added). They further mistakenly 
conclude that the court imposed an additional, impermissible 
requirement under Thornburg v. Gingles, 478 U.S. 30 (1986), that 
SSRV population exceed a voter majority “by an immeasurable 
amount.” /d. But no such thing occurred. 
The three-judge court’s holding was not focused solely on 
numerosity, as Appellants suggest. Instead, the court concluded 
that “the Latino population is not sufficiently compact or numerous 
to support another, effective majority Latino citizenship district.” 
App., at 14 (congressional), 26 (Texas Senate). In other words, the 
court identified two additional concerns—compactness and 
effectiveness—that defeat Appellants’ §2 claims. And this Court’s 
precedent fully supports the lower court’s reasoning. 
1. Thethree-judge court correctly rejected Appellants’ 
request for a seventh SSRV border district that 
subordinates the traditional districting principle of 
compactness to race. 
The court’s first concern, compactness, renders Appellants’ 
proposal impermissible on two levels. First, it demonstrates that 
Appellants cannot satisfy the first precondition under Gingles, 
which requires a §2 plaintiff to demonstrate that the protected 
minority group is “sufficiently large and geographically compact 
to constitute a majority in a single-member district.” 478 U.S., at

509 U.S. 630 (1993). 
Along the Texas border, it is impossible to create a seventh 
Latino district that is reasonably compact. Although border areas 
have extensive Latino population, an additional Latino district 
cannot be achieved without dividing a naturally compact Latino 
population into separate components and then linking those 
components to non-Latino population from unrelated communities 
as much as two-hundred miles away. The racially manipulated, 
non-compact configuration that emerges confirms that there is no 
§2 obligation to construct a seventh Latino border district. “If, 
because of the dispersion of the minority population, a reasonably 
compact majority-minority district cannot be created, §2 does not 
require a majority-minority district.” Bush v. Vera, 517 U.S. 952, 
979 (1996); see also Shaw v. Hunt, 517 U.S. 899, 916 (1996) (“[A] 
plaintiff must show that the minority group is ‘geographically 
compact’ to establish §2 liability. No one looking at [North 
Carolina] District 12 could reasonably suggest that the district 
contains a ‘geographically compact’ population of any race. 
Therefore, where that district sits, ‘there neither has been a wrong 
nor can [there] be a remedy.”) (citations omitted). 
Appellants contend that other equally or more non-compact 
districts have existed in Texas’s past, J.S., at 19, but they fail to 
recognize that lack of compactness raises Fourteenth Amendment 
concerns when it results from race-based districting decisions. See 
Shaw, 509 U.S., at 647 (noting that equal-protection concerns 
would arise if a State “concentrated a dispersed minority population 
in a single district by disregarding traditional districting principles 
such as compactness, contiguity, and respect for political 
subdivisions); see also, e.g., Hunt v. Cromartie, 532 U.S. 234 
(2001); Bush, 517 U.S. 952; Miller v. Johnson, 515 U.S. 900

B. Appellants’ Seven Proposed SSRV Senate Districts Are 
the Impermissible Product of Intentional, Race-Based 
Underpopulation. 
Appellants’ Jurisdictional Statement does not distinguish their 
§2 claims with respect to congressional and Texas Senate 
- redistricting. | And, for the most part, this approach is 
understandable because, in each context, the seven SSRV districts 
Appellants requested were similar due to the comparable number of 
districts in the two plans (i.e., 32 congressional and 31 Texas 
Senate seats). 
There is, however, one significant difference: an ideally 
populated Texas Senate district contains more population than a 
congressional district, since there is one fewer district across which 
to spread Texas’s total population. And because of the increased 
size of Texas Senate districts, it is possible to create seven majority 
SSRV districts only if those districts are intentionally 
underpopulated to enhance SSRV.’ Because such intentional, race- 
based redistricting runs afoul of Shaw v. Reno and its progeny—and 
because there is insufficient population to create seven SSRV 
districts in the absence of race-based population adjustments—the 
three-judge court correctly concluded that the “Latino population

Il. THE THREE-JUDGE COURT’S FACTUAL FINDINGS SATISFY 
RULE 52 AND ARE SUFFICIENT FOR PROPER APPELLATE 
REVIEW. 
The Court should reject Appellants’ final argument that the 
three-judge court failed to comply with Federal Rule of Civil 
Procedure 52. The per curiam opinions in the Texas Senate and 
congressional redistricting cases satisfy Rule 52 and permit proper 
appellate review by “‘giv[ing] the reviewing court a clear 
understanding of the factual basis for the decision.” Folger Coffee 
Co. v. Olivebank, 201 F.3d 632, 635 (CAS 2000) (quoting Burma 
Navigation Corp. v. Reliant Seahorse MV, 99 F.3d 652, 656 (CA5 
1996)); Unt v. Aerospace Corp., 765 F.2d 1440, 1444 (CA9 1985) 
(holding that remand was not necessary, even though findings did 
not articulate specific factual bases for holding, because the basis 
for the court’s decision was clear); see also FED. R. Civ. P. 52(a). 
Moreover, even if the Court were to decide that the findings in 
the per curiam opinions were somehow deficient, remand would not 
be necessary because ““‘the failure of a District Court to comply 
with Rule 52(a) in respect of findings of fact does not demand 
reversal of the correct judgment . . . if a full understanding of the

1954)). Because a full understanding of the issues is easily 
obtained from the per curiam opinions, the Court should not reverse

court’s Texas Senate and congressional judgments.

The Court should summarily affirm the judgments of the three-
