ARGUMENT 
Despite its contrary aim, the State of Texas’ Motion to 
Affirm (“Texas Motion”) further demonstrates why this 
case requires plenary review. Faced with a brief and 
partially inscrutable four-sentence disposition of plain- 
tiffs’ claims, the State must both defend the District 
Court’s failure to make findings to support and explain 
its decision, and explain why the court's evident failure 
to follow established standards under the first section 2 
precondition does not warrant reversal. 
The State responds to this conundrum by proffering 
an unsupportable, revisionist reading of the District 
Court decision. Even though the court used terminology 
that, over the years, has become synonymous with the 
first precondition from Thornburg v. Gingles, 478 U.S. 30 
(1986), the State argues that the court’s conclusion actu- 
ally rested on the “totality of the circumstances” and on 
constitutional concerns, emanating from Shaw v. Reno, 509 
U.S. 630 (1993), purportedly raised by the configuration 
of plaintiffs’ proposed Latino-majority districts. While the 
State’s scanty defense of the court’s express Gingles prong 
one conclusion implicitly concedes trial court error, the 
State’s alternative, revisionist reading founders on two 
:mmovable barriers - established Voting Rights Act pre- 
cedent and the District Court’s own actions. 
The purported constitutional concerns simply have 
no application at the liability stage of a section 2 case. Yet, 
because the court’s decision cannot possibly be read to 
have reached remedy considerations, the State untenably 
asserts that these concerns are relevant to compactness 
analysis under Gingles prong one. Equally problematic, 
the wording of the District Court's decision, as well as 
the court’s own redistricting plan belie any consideration 
of the State’s argued constitutional concerns.

Similarly flawed, the State’s assertion that the 
appealed decision rested on an analysis of “totality” can- 
not be reconciled with the court’s failure to discuss any 
Gingles precondition besides the first, and its omission of 
discussion of any Senate factor. Moreover, the State’s 
assertion that the court’s reference to “effective” districts 
is anything other than a mis-application of Gingles one 
misreads the language of the District Court’s own ruling.! 
The State’s Motion raises more questions about the 
District Court decision than it answers; rather than sup- 
porting summary affirmance, it reiterates the plaintiffs’ 
call for this Court’s review.

may not. If the uncommonly high standard of compact-

the rest of the state — operated to block section 2 plaintiffs 
at the Gingles precondition stage of the proceedings, then 
Vera's permission to depart from traditional redistricting 
criteria at the remedy phase would be a nullity. Similarly,

Ill. PLAINTIFFS’ PROPOSED SENATE DISTRICTS 
PARALLEL TEXAS STATE PRACTICES 
The State does not claim that plaintiffs’ proposed 
redistricting plans are unconstitutionally malappor- 
tioned. Plaintiffs’ congressional redistricting plan con- 
tained a total deviation of zero percent. Their proposed 
Senate redistricting plan contained a total deviation of 
9.46 percent, presumptively constitutional and lower than 
the 9.71 percent total deviation in the court-affirmed LRB 
plan. 
Instead, the State again imputes to the District Court 
an analysis and conclusion that appear nowhere in its 
decision: that majority Latino districts containing less 
than the ideal district population fail to satisfy the first 
Gingles precondition and contravene this Court’s holding 
in Shaw v. Reno. 
The Court’s decision in Holder v. Hall, upon which the 
State relies, was not a redistricting case, but dealt with 
the question of expanding the size of a governing author- 
ity in response to a section 2 challenge. Holder v. Hall, 512 
U.S. 874 (1994). This Court ruled that section 2 requires a 
vote dilution plaintiff to propose a “reasonable alterna- 
tive practice as a benchmark against which to measure 
the existing voting practice.” Id. at 880. Here, plaintiffs do 
not seek to change the method by which representatives 
are elected and thus Holder’s discussion of “reasonable 
alternative practice” is inapposite. Nevertheless, if the 
ruling in Holder were to extend to this case, the redistrict- 
ing practices of the State would be the reasonable 
benchmark against which to measure plaintiffs’ proposed 
Latino majority districts. 
Because the State has chosen to systematically under- 
populate its Latino majority districts in South Texas, 


9 
plaintiffs’ proffered “reasonable alternative practice” of 
seven Latino majority districts in South Texas with smaller 
population deviations than those enacted by the State 
more than satisfies the Holder rule. The LRB redistricting 
plan, upheld by the District Court, purposefully under- 
populates all but one of its seven Latino majority dis- 
tricts: District 6 (-4.87), District 19 (-4.87), District 20 
(+.62), District 21 (-4.92), District 26 (-4.88), District 27 
(-4.72), District 29 (-4.73). By contrast, the Latino majority 
Senate districts plaintiffs presented in Plan 1206S are far 
less underpopulated: District 6 (+3.28), District 18 (-1.55), 
District 19 (-1.12), District 20 (-2.2), District 21 (-3.33), 
District 26 (-2.43), District 27 (-4.13), District 29 (-4.62). 
J.S. App. 46; App. 21. 
Finally, the State argues that underpopulated Latino- 
majority Senate districts alone offer conclusive proof of 
race-based redistricting in violation of Shaw v. Reno. The 
argument is legally unfounded and could not have 
formed the basis of the District Court’s decision. It is also 
inconceivable that lawyers who counseled the State as it 
systematically underpopulated Latino-majority Senate 
districts would now seriously argue that that very prac- 
tice violates the Constitution.

simply does not provide that basis for appellate review. 
-The ultimate evidence of the District Court’s failure
