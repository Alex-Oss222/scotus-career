QUESTION PRESENTED 


Whether proof that a stockbroker liquidated positions in 
a discretionary investment account and then converted the 
proceeds to his own use, without more, establishes fraud “in 
connection with” a sale of securities within the meaning of 
§ 10(b) of the Securities Exchange Act of 1934.

STATEMENT 


This case requires this Court to determine whether a 
stockbroker’s conversion of money, including proceeds from 
the sales of securities in a.client’s discretionary investment 
account, violates § 10(b) and Rule 10b-5. The parties agree 
that the outcome turns on the proper interpretation of 
§ 10(b)’s requirement that a deception be “in connection 
with” a securities transaction. This case arises in the 
following setting: 

1. In January 1991, the National Association of Security 
Dealers (NASD), “pursuant to its self-regulatory duties,” 
conducted “a routine examination” of Dominick & 
Dominick, a brokerage firm located in Bethesda, Maryland. 
Pet. App. 34a.! The NASD “inadvertently discovered” that 
money had been transferred on more than twenty-six 
separate occasions from an investment account of William 
Wood and his daughter Diane Okstulski (the Woods) to 
accounts controlled by Charles Zandford or held in 
Zandford’s name. Pet. App. 34a. The Woods set up the 
account with Zandford as their broker in November 1987.

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


The SEC urges this Court to conclude that § 10(b) and 
Rule 10b-5 are violated whenever a broker converts 
proceeds from the sales of securities in a client's account. 
However, § 10(b) prohibits only those types of fraud shown 
to be “in connection with the purchase or sale of any 
security.” The court of appeals conducted the case-by-case 
analysis required by the language of § 10(b) and correctly 
held that neither Zandford’s fraudulent scheme nor the 
conversion of the proceeds of securities sales established that 
the fraud was in connection with a sale of securities. 

The plain language of §10(b) limits the provision’s 
application to manipulative or deceptive devices used “in 
connection with” a securities transaction. However, 
“manipulation or deception in connection with a securities 
transaction” means something different from “a securities 
transaction in connection with manipulation or deception.” 
By excluding “a broader field involving any type of common 
law fraud in which a security was involved,” this language 
“serves a useful filtration-function.” 8 Louis Loss & Joel 
Seligman, Securities Regulation 3686 (3d ed. 1991). The SEC 
urges this Court largely to eliminate that function by 
construing the phrase to encompass any nexus between 
deceptive devices and securities transactions. 

Congress provided for a limited departure from its 
scheme of industry self-regulation by creating a catch-all for 
the use, by any person, of new manipulative and deceptive 
devices that corrupt the integrity of the market or influence 
investor understanding. Section 10(b) should be interpreted 
flexibly only to achieve those purposes. 

Consistent with the limited purposes of § 10(b), the court 
of appeals properly held that evidence of conversion of 
funds from a securities investment account does not by itself 
establish a violation of § 10(b). The court concluded that the 
fraud was not shown to be in connection with a securities 
transaction because the fraud did not undermine market 
integrity or affect investor understanding. This conclusion is 


consistent with the tests used by the Second and Seventh 
Circuits. 

The SEC contends that the result reached by the court of 
appeals cannot be squared with Superintendent of Insurance v. 
Bankers Life & Casualty Co., 404 U.S. 6 (1971). Bankers Life, 
however, does not support the SEC’s contention that a 
fiduciary’s misappropriation of the proceeds of securities 
sales necessarily violates § 10(b). As the court of appeals 
recognized, in Bankers Life, the defendant made 
misrepresentations about a particular security that induced 
another party either to “purchase or sell [a] security,” Pet. 
App. 13a, and the seller was “duped into believing that it . . . 
would receive the proceeds” of the sale. Bankers Life, 404 
U.S. at 9. In contrast, the Woods made no investment 
decision relating to any specific security. Moreover, the 
SEC's any nexus test is not supported by this Court's use of 
the word “touching” to describe the “in connection with” 
requirement. As Judge Friendly’s careful analysis of that 
language in Chemical Bank v. Arthur Andersen & Co., 726 F.2d 
930 (2d Cir.), cert. denied, 469 U.S. 884 (1984), makes clear, the 
“touching” language restates, but does not articulate a test 
for interpreting, the “in connection with” requirement of 
§ 10(b). 

Alternatively, the SEC attempts to connect Zandford’s 
deception directly to the securities sales. The SEC relies on 
this Court's decision in O’Hagan for the proposition that a 
sale of a security by a fiduciary who is feigning loyalty at the 
time of the sale violates § 10(b). However, O’Hagan, which 
addressed the use of misappropriated information in an 
insider trading case, does not support the SEC’s use of 
fiduciary principles here. Although this Court announced 
no general “in connection with” test in O'Hagan, several 
factors influenced its decision that O’Hagan’s deception was 
in connection with a securities transaction: (1) O’Hagan’s 
fraud was consummated by a securities transaction; (2) 
misappropriated information can ordinarily be capitalized 
on only through a securities transaction; and (3) O’Hagan 


__ 


simultaneously violated his fiduciary duty and harmed 
members of the investing public. This Court was also 
concerned about the consequences of not finding O’Hagan 
liable under § 10(b) because insider trading implicates the 
core congressional purposes behind the Act. None of these 
factors or concerns is present here. 


First, the SEC has not proved that Zandford’s sales were 
wrongful. Zandford was authorized, by virtue of the 
discretionary nature of the account, to engage in securities 
transactions without specific authorization for each 
transaction. That Zandford was feigning loyalty at the time 
of the sales does not, without more, establish a § 10(b) 
violation. Second, while O'Hagan could profit from the 
misappropriated information only through a securities 
transaction, the court of appeals correctly recognized that, 
because Zandford had control of the Woods’ money before 
any securities transactions took place, the securities sales 
were not an essential part of his scheme. Third, Zandford’s 
fraud did not harm the investing public in the same way as 
O’Hagan’s. Zandford’s conduct, though fraudulent, is not 
the type of deception that Congress perceived as a threat to 
market integrity, nor does it affect investor confidence in 
anything other than a particular broker or brokerage house. 
Further, holding that there is no § 10(b) violation in this case 
will not create a regulatory gap. Zandford’s fraud is covered 
by other laws and regulations. In contrast, O’Hagan’s 
trading on nonpublic information is the type of activity that 
led to federal regulation of the investment markets. 

Finally, the SEC contends that its interpretation that 
§ 10(b) and Rule 10b-5 apply to broker conversions merits 
deference. Pet. Br. 34-35. Although the SEC has been 
expressly delegated rulemaking authority under § 10(b), its 
interpretation of the “in connection with” requirement is not 
entitled to deference. Indeed, although O’Hagan raised 
questions about the proper application of the “in connection 
with” requirement of § 10(b), the government did not 


request, and this Court did not grant, Chevron deference to 
the SEC’s interpretation of that language. 

Moreover, even if this Court is willing to defer to the 
SEC on this point, there is no SEC adjudication applying 
§ 10(b) that is worthy of deference. The SEC primarily relies 
on a series of its public announcements and this Court's 
holding in United States v. Mead Corp., 533 U.S. 218, 121 S. Ct. 
2164 (2001), that agency adjudications can be worthy of 
Chevron deference. However, the SEC materials are not 
analogous to the agency adjudications referred to in Mead, 
121 S. Ct. at 2173 n.12, which are much like published court 
opinions. The Mead adjudications contain extensive legal 
analysis, including examination of the underlying statutory 
language and the elements of the statutory provision. The 
materials offered by the SEC here, which consist merely of 
factual summaries and conclusory claims that § 10(b) 
applies, simply do not deserve deference. Finally, even if 
Chevron analysis were appropriate here, the SEC's proposed 
interpretation, that § 10(b) is violated any time a broker 
misappropriates a client's funds or securities, should be 
rejected because it is an unreasonable interpretation of 


§ 10(b).

In construing §10(b) and Rule 10b-5, implementing

In addition to the fact that O’Hagan’s fraud was

IV. THE SEC’S INTERPRETATION IS NOT ENTITLED 
TO CHEVRON DEFERENCE 


The SEC contends that its “interpretation of Section 10(b) 
and Rule 10b-5 . . . must be given effect by the courts” under 
the doctrine of Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Resources 
Defense Council, Inc., 467 U.S. 837, 843-44 (1984), Pet. Br. 38, 
and that it “has interpreted Section 10(b) and Rule 10b-5 to 
prohibit the fraudulent misappropriation by broker-dealers 
... Of customer securities and the proceeds of securities 
sales.” Pet. Br. 34-35. Both of these contentions are 
incorrect. First, this Court has never held that Congress 
gave the SEC the power to interpret the “in connection with” 
requirement of § 10(b) authoritatively, and the SEC has 
never previously requested that this Court defer to its 
interpretations of § 10(b). Second, the agency adjudications 
to which the SEC asks this Court to defer lack the rigorous 
analysis and interpretation of § 10(b) or Rule 10b-5 necessary 
to warrant Chevron deference. For these reasons, the SEC is 
not entitled to the deference it seeks. 


A. Both this Court and the SEC Have Traditionally 
Understood that the SEC’s Interpretation Is Not 
Entitled to Chevron Deference 


The SEC’s interpretation of § 10(b) is entitled to Chevron 
deference only if Congress intended to delegate to the 
agency the authority to speak with the force of law. See 
Mead, 121 S.Ct. at 2172 & n.11; Chevron, 467 U.S. at 865. 
Although the SEC enjoys some expressly delegated 
rulemaking authority under § 10(b), including the power to 
promulgate Rule 10b-5, the Act does not allow the agency to 
define the “in connection with” limitation. See Adams Fruit 
Co. v. Barrett, 494 U.S. 638, 650 (1990) (holding that express 
grant of authority to promulgate standards under statute did 
not empower agency to regulate the scope of judicial power 


43 


thereunder). The long-standing practice of this Court, as 
well as the SEC, strongly implies that the SEC lacks this

(b) Proposed rule changes; notice; proceedings 


(1) Each self-regulatory organization shall file with the 
Commission, in accordance with such rules as the 
Commission may prescribe, copies of any proposed rule or 
any proposed change in, addition to, or deletion from the 
rules of such self-regulatory organization (hereinafter in this 
subsection collectively referred to as a “proposed rule 
change”) accompanied by a concise general statement of the 
basis and purpose of such proposed rule change. The 
Commission shall, upon the filing of any proposed rule 
change, publish notice thereof together with the terms of 
substance of the proposed rule change or a description of the 
subjects and issues involved. The Commission shall give 
interested persons an opportunity to submit written data, 
views, and arguments concerning such proposed rule

change. No proposed rule change shall take effect unless 
approved by the Commission or otherwise permitted in 
accordance with the provisions of this subsection. 

(2) Within thirty-five days of the date of publication of 
notice of the filing of a proposed rule change in accordance 
with paragraph (1) of this subsection, or within such longer 
period as the Commission may designate up to ninety days 
of such date if it finds such longer period to be appropriate 
and publishes its reasons for so finding or as to which the 
self-regulatory organization consents, the Commission 
shall — 

(A) by order approve such proposed rule change, or 

(B) institute proceedings to determine whether the 
proposed rule change should be disapproved. Such 
proceedings shall include notice of the grounds for 
disapproval under consideration and opportunity for 
hearing and be concluded within one hundred eighty days 
of the date of publication of notice of the filing of the 
proposed rule change. At the conclusion of such proceedings 
the Commission, by order, shall approve or disapprove such 
proposed rule change. The Commission may extend the time 
for conclusion of such proceedings for up to sixty days if it 
finds good cause for such extension and publishes its 
reasons for so finding or for such longer period as to which 
the self-regulatory organization consents. 

The Commission shall approve a proposed rule change 
of a self-regulatory organization if it finds that such 
proposed rule change is consistent with the requirements of 
this chapter and the rules and regulations thereunder 
applicable to such organization. The Commission shall 
disapprove a proposed rule change of a self-regulatory 
organization if it does not make such finding. The 
Commission shall not approve any proposed rule change 
prior to the thirtieth day after the date of publication of 
notice of the filing thereof, unless the Commission finds 
good cause for so doing and publishes its reasons for so 
finding.

(c) Amendment by Commission of rules of self- 

regulatory organizations 

The Commission, by rule, may abrogate, add to, and 
delete from (hereinafter in this subsection collectively 
referred to as “amend”) the rules of a self-regulatory 
organization (other than a registered clearing agency) as the 
Commission deems necessary or appropriate to insure the 
fair administration of the self-regulatory organization, to 
conform its rules to requirements of this chapter and the 
rules and regulations thereunder applicable to such 
organization, or otherwise in furtherance of the purposes of 
this chapter, in the following manner: 

(1) The Commission shall notify the self-regulatory 
organization and publish notice of the proposed rulemaking 
in the Federal Register. The notice shall include the text of 
the proposed amendmem to the rules of the self-regulatory 
organization and a statement of the Commission's reasons, 
including any pertinent facts, for commencing such 
proposed rulemaking. 

(2) The Commission shall give interested persons an 
opportunity for the oral presentation of data, views, and 
arguments, in addition to an opportunity to make written 
submissions. A transcript shall be kept of any oral 
presentation. 

(3) A rule adopted pursuant to this subsection shall 


incorporate the text of the amendment to the rules of the 


self-regulatory organization and a statement of the 
Commission's basis for and purpose in so amending such 
rules. This statement shall include an identification of any 


14a 


facts on which the Commission considers its determination 
so to amend the rules of the self-regulatory agency to be 
based, including the reasons for the Commission's 
conclusions as to any of uns facts which were disputed in 
the rulemaking. 

(4)(A) Except as provided i in paragraphs (1) through (3) 
of this subsection, rulemaking under this subsection shall be 
in accordance with the procedures specified in section 553 of 
Title 5 for rulemaking not on the record. 

(B) Nothing in this subsection shall be construed to 
impair or limit the Commission’s power to make, or to 
modify or alter the procedures the Commission may follow 
in making, rules and regulations pursuant to any other 
authority under this chapter. 

(C) Any amendment to the rules of a self-regulatory 
organization made by the Commission pursuant to this 
subsection shall be considered for all purposes of this 
chapter to be part of the rules of such self-regulatory 
organization and shall not be considered to be a rule of the 
Commission. 

(5) With respect to rules described in subsection (b)(5) of 
this section, the Commission shall consult with and consider 
the views of the Secretary of the Treasury before abrogating, 
adding to, and deleting from such rules, except where the 
Commission determines that an emergency exists requiring 
expeditious or summary action and publishes its reasons 
therefor.

money penalties in civil actions

proceedings under this chapter.

(e) Mandamus 

Upon application of the Commission the district courts 
of the United States and the United States courts of any 
territory or other place subject to the jurisdiction of the 
United States shall have jurisdiction to issue writs of 
mandamus, injunctions, and orders commanding (1) - 
person to comply with the provisions of this chapter, the 
rules, regulations, and orders thereunder, the rules of a 
national securities exchange or registered securities 
association of which such person is a member or person 
associated with a member, the rules of a registered clearing 
agency in which such person is a participant, the rules of the 
Municipal Securities Rulemaking Board, or any undertaking 
contained in a registration statement as provided in 
subsection (d) of section 780 of this title, (2) any national 
securities exchange or registered securities association to 
enforce compliance by its members and persons associated 
with its members with the provisions of this chapter, the 


16a 


rules, regulations, and orders thereunder, and the rules of 
such exchange or association, or (3) any registered clearing 
agency to enforce compliance by its participants with the 
provisions of the rules of such clearing agency. 


(f) Rules of self-regulatory organizations 

Notwithstanding any other provision of this chapter, the 
Commission shall not bring any action pursuant to 
subsection (d) or (e) of this section against any person for 
violation of, or to command compliance with, the rules of a 
self-regulatory organization unless it appears to the 
Commission that (1) such self-regulatory organization is 
unable or unwilling to take appropriate action against such 
person in the public interest and for the protection of 
investors, or (2) such action is otherwise necessary or 
appropriate in the public interest or for the protection of 
investors.
