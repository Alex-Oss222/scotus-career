ARGUMENT 


Respondent essentially offers the Court two "solutions" in 
this case: (1) require Kansas to immunize all inmates who 


participate in the Sexual Abuse Treatment Program (SATP) or 
(2) classify all inmates as maximum security inmates and give 
them the least amount of prison privileges possible, making 
them earn anything better. Resp. Br. 7. 

The first "solution"—which the Tenth Circuit endorsed in 
this case—puts the cart before the horse, by solving a problem 
that does not exist. If there is no unconstitutional 
“compulsion” to begin with, then there is no constitutional 
reason why Kansas should have to immunize inmates’ 
statements. 

The second "solution" is an ironic suggestion that, 
although perhaps theoretically possible to implement, would 
actually work to the detriment of thousands of inmates in this 
country who would not otherwise be placed in maximum 
security conditions. Thus, this “solution” is probably both 
counterproductive and fanciful, at best. 

With all due respect, the best “solution” is to permit the 
States the ability to require violent sex offenders such as 
respondent to make some difficult choices that, in the long run, 
will benefit the offenders themselves and society. Without

in a meaningful way, many inmates will refuse to do so, as 
respondent has amply demonstrated. Permitting inmates to 
participate only on their own terms—which will undermine a 
key component of the treatment—is no "solution." This Court 
should conclude that the Kansas SATP is constitutional.

Il. Kansas Neither Concedes Nor Acknowledges That 
Respondent Would Be "Punished" For Invoking His 
Fifth Amendment Rights When He Refuses To 
Participate In The Sexual Abuse Treatment Program 


Contrary to suggestions made by respondent, Kansas has 
neither conceded nor acknowledged that respondent would be 
"punished" for invocation of his Fifth Amendment nghts. 
Rather, he would suffer a loss of privileges—to which he has 
no entitlement and in which he has no liberty interest—for 
refusing to comply with the requirements of the Sexual Abuse 
Treatment Program (SATP). Refusing to participate in a 
meaningful way in the Kansas SATP is a choice that 
respondent indeed can make, but he has no constitutional nght 
to insist that such a choice have absolutely no consequences 
for him. 

This proposition is poignantly illustrated by an exchange 
between the warden of Lansing prison, David R. McKune, and 
one of respondent's attorneys, during McKune's deposition:

consequences if they refuse not to choose to enter the

Moreover, neither court below made a factual finding that 
respondent would be "punished" for refusing to participate in 
the SATP. Rather, the question whether particular 
circumstances amount to "punishment" for constitutional 
purposes is a question of law subject to de novo review, as in 
Kansas v. Hendricks, 521 U.S. 346 (1997). 


Selective quoting of snippets of the record taken out of 
context does not establish that respondent would be 
“punished” for invoking his Fifth Amendment rights when he 
refuses to participate in the Kansas SATP. Rather, Kansas 
respectfully urges the Court to consider the entirety of the 
evidence in the Joint Appendix, as well as the relevant 
“compulsion” cases discussed in Kansas's opening brief. 
Doing so will demonstrate that respondent is not being 
"compelled" to incriminate himself, and thus he has no valid 
Fifth Amendment claim. 


II. Only A Liberty Interest Analysis Can Provide Prison 
Administrators Wi.s Clear Guidance 


Respondent concedes, as he must, that only a liberty 
interest analysis—as in Sandin v. Conner, 515 U.S. 472

(1995)—-will give prison administrators clear guidance. Also, 
respondent concedes, again as he must, that prison inmates 
such as himself have no constitutional entitlement to any 
particular security or housing classification. 

Nonetheless, respondent urges on this Court an 
indeterminate balancing of interests that will provide no one 
guidance and that will ensure plenty of litigation by prison 
hallengi , of the 
programs that exist in the state and federal prison systems. 

Moreover, respondent also recognizes that the application 
of the Turner v. Safley, 482 U.S. 78 (1987) balancing test to 
claims other than those involving First Amendment interests 
has never been made clear by this Court. Respondent, 
however, urges formulaic application of the four 7urner 
factors—like the Tenth Circuit's analysis in this 
respect—focusing heavily on the question whether respondent 
has any alternative means of exercising his Fifth Amendment 
rights. 

But this demonstrates the potential impropriety of the 
Turner factors outside the First Amendment context. In a 
Fifth Amendment case, it is impossible to see how the affected 
inmate would ever have an alternative way of exercising his 
rights. He either speaks or he invokes the privilege; there is 
nothing else he can do. 


The Tenth Circuit and respondent both argue that all the 
State needs to do in such situations is immunize the inmate. 
But there are reasons the State might not wish to do so, and 
there is no constitutional reason why the State must. Indeed, 
using the 7Jurner factors as both the Tenth Circuit and 
respondent do in this case, a Fifth Amendment claim by an 
inmate will always compel the State to immunize the inmate's 
statements, because there is no alternative means for the 
inmate to exercise his rights.

This result is contrary to the fundamental purpose of the 
Turner analysis—requiring federal courts to give deference to 
the reasonable judgments of federal and state prison 
administrators in managing extremely difficult inmate 
populations. It also puts the cart before the horse in the Fifth 
Amendment context, because a court may—as the Tenth 
Circuit did here—essentially conclude that the State must 
immunize inmates’ statements without ever really grappling 
with the preliminary Fifth Amendment issue, which is whether 
an inmate is being subjected to unconstitutional "compulsion."

The parties do not disagree in this case on the privileges 
that would be withheld from respondent for his refusal to 
participate in the SATP. Rather, we disagree as to the legal 


significance of those withheld privileges. With all due respect, 
Kansas relies principally on the discussion in its opening brief 


to refute respondent's attempted characterization of cases such 
as Baxter v. Palmigiano, 425 U.S. 308 (1976), Minnesota v. 
Murphy, 465 U.S. 420 (1984), and Ohio Adult Parole 
Authority v. Woodard, 523 U.S. 272 (1998), as somehow 
involving /ess significant consequences than in this case. 
Indeed, in all three cases, the person invoking his Fifth 
Amendment privilege faced significantly greater consequences 
than the loss of a personal television. 


Moreover, respondent does not even begin to address the 
complex and potentially suggestive history of the privilege 
against self-incrimination. Instead, respondent basically 
asserts that the Fifth Amendment prevents more than just 
confessions obtained by torture—a point that Kansas will 
obviously concede—but adds virtually no further explication. 
Again, Kansas relies principally on the historical discussion in

its opening brief to suggest that loss of a personal television 
simply does not fall within the protections of the privilege. 


Lastly, respondent suggests that "[t]o Mr. Lile, the 
sanctions threatened by the State make the assertion of his 
privilege costly." Resp. Br. 31. But respondent's personal 
opinion is not the constitutional measure of the Fifth 
Amendment's scope. Indeed, this Court previously and clearly 
has rejected any notion that what amounts to "punishment" for 
constitutional purposes is to be measured by the subjective 
beliefs of the person affected. "[W]hether a sanction 
constitutes punishment is not determined from the defendant's 
perspective.” Department of Revenue v. Kurth Ranch, 511 
U.S. 767, 777 n.14 (1994). 


IV. Respondent's Proposed Analysis Would Give Prison 
Administrators The Ironic Incentive To Place All Sex 
Offenders—If Indeed Not All Inmates—In Maximum 
Security When They Enter Prison 


Kansas finds it more than a trifle ironic that respondent 
suggests that Kansas avoid potential Fifth Amendment 
problems by (1) classifying a// inmates as maximum security 
when they first enter prison, (2) giving them the minimum 
conditions and privileges that are constitutionally acceptable, 
and (3) making them earn any additional or better privileges or 
conditions. Resp. Br. 7. Although perhaps theoretically 
possible, such a "solution" would be dramatically different 
from longstanding, established prison management practices 
and principles. Such a "solution" would also work to the 
detriment of tens of thousands of inmates who would not 
otherwise be placed in the maximum security category or in 
maximum security conditions. 

Thus, in the end, respondent's suggested "solutions" are 
not really solutions at all. Instead, they illustrate just how far 
respondent (and the Tenth Circuit below) have to strain to 
justify invalidating the laudable Kansas SATP. Respondent
