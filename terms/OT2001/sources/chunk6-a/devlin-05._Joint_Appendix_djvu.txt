FIRST AMENDED COMPLAINT 
FOR DECLARATORY AND INJUNCTIVE RELIEF 


Introduction 


1. This action is brought on behalf of the Transportation 
Communications International Union Staff Retirement Plan 
(the “Plan”) pursuant to sections 502(a)(1)(B) and 502(a)(3) 
of the Employee Retirement Income Security Act of 1974, 
as amended (“ERISA”), 29 U.S.C. §§ 1132(a)(1)(B) and 
1132(aX(3), and 28 U.S.C. §§ 2201 and 2202, to obtain 
appropriate equitable and declaratory relief to adjudicate the 
rights of the participants in the Plan and their beneficiaries to 
receive benefits under certain amendments made to the Plan. 


* * * 


25 
Class Action Allegations 


12. Plaintiffs bring to this action against defendants 
Santoro and DeBarr individually and as representatives of a 
class including all participants of the Plan who first earned 
Credited Service under the Plan before May 15, 1993 (the 
“Class”) and their beneficiaries. 


13. The members of the Class are so numerous that 
joinder of all class members is impracticable. The Class 
exceeds 700 members, including approximately 402 retired 
participants, 123 terminated vested participants, 206 active 
participants, and their beneficiaries. 


14. Common questions of law and fact exist as to all 
members of the Class and predominate over any questions 
sclely affecting individual members of the Class. Among the 
questions of law and fact common to the Class are: 


(a) whether an amendment to the Plan adopted on 
October 13, 1990, and effective on January 1, 1991 
(the “COLA amendment”), providing for an auto- 
matic cost of living adjustment (“COLA”) to a Plan 
participant’s retirement benefit on every third anni- 
versary of his retirement date, should be declared 
null and void as of the date of its adoption, on 
account of its having been adopted as the result of a 
breach of fiduciary duty committed by defendants 
Bobo, Kilroy, Lynch, and Mazur; and, 


(b) whether an October 6, 1997 amendmen: to the Plan, 
which eliminated the increases in monthly benefit 
amounts put into place under the COLA amendment 
and eliminated all future COLA increases for all 
participants who separated from service prior to 
January 1, 1991, is a lawful amendment. 


15. The Class is composed of all participants in the Plan 
who first earned Credited Service, as that term has been 


26 


defined in the Plan from time to time, prior to May 15, 1993, 
and their beneficiaries, as that term is defined in ERISA 
section 3(8), 29 U.S.C. § 1002(8). 


16. The Class is composed of two Subclasses. 


(a) The first Subclass consists of members of the Class 
who retired or separated from service with TCU 
before November 1, 1995 and their beneficiaries (the 
“Retiree Subclass”). The Retiree Subclass contains 
approximately 402 retired participants and 72 term- 
inated vested participants. 


(b) The second Subclass consists of members of the 
Class who earned any Credited Service under the 
Plan on or after November 1, 1995, and their 
beneficiaries (the “Active Sublcass”). The Active 
Subclass contains approximately 245 participants. 


17. Defendant Santoro is a member of the Class and of the 
Retiree Subclass, and his claims and defenses are typical of 
the claims and defenses of the Retiree Subclass. Plaintiffs 
allege, on information and belief, that Santoro is familiar with 
the issues raised by the instant complaint, and will vigorously 
represent the interests of the Retiree Subclass. 


18. Defendant DeBarr is a member of the Class and of the 
Active Subclass, and his claims and defenses are typical of 
the claims and defenses of the Active Subclass. Defendant 
DeBarr is the Executive Director/Operations of TCU. As an 
active employee, he is aware of the pending litigation styled 
Scardelletti v. Bobo, Civil Action No. 95-52 JFM (the “Bobo 
litigation”) and familiar with the issues presented therein. 
Plaintiffs allege, on information and belief, that DeBarr will 
vigorously represent the interest of the Active Subclass. 


19. Certification of the Class and the Subclasses is 
appropriate under Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(1) because the 
prosecution of separate actions by or against individual 
members of th= Class would create a risk of inconsistent or 


27 


varying adjudications of the Plan’s obligations with regard to 
the COLA amendment and the October 6, 1997 amendment. 
Class certification is also appropriate under Fed. R. Civ. P. 
23(b\(2), because the Plan amendments at issue act on 
grounds generally applicable to the Class and the Subclasses, 
making declaratory and injunctive relief appropriate on a 
class-wide basis. 
* * * 


Claim 
(Declaratory Judgment and Injunctive Relief) 


36. Plaintiffs hereby incorporate by reference paragraphs 
1 through 35 as if set forth in full herein. 


37. As a direct consequence of the fiduciary breaches 
committed by defendants Bobo, Kilroy, Lynch, and Mazur, 
the Plan has been damaged, and will be damaged in the 
future, by the payment of benefits provided under the COLA 
amendment. Plaintiffs are therefore entitled under ERISA 
section 502(a)(3), 29 U.S.C. § 1132(a)(3), to final declaratory 
and injunctive relief declaring the COLA amendment void 
from its inception, and of no force and effect, as to all 
members of the Class, and as to defendants Bobo, Kilroy, 
Lynch, and Mazur. 


38. In the alternative, with respect to members of the 
Retiree Subclass, the benefits provided by the COLA amend- 
ment are not “accrued benefits” or a “retirement-type sub- 
sidy” within the meaning of ERISA section 204(g), and may 
be lawfully eliminated by an amendment to the Plan. 
Plaintiffs are entitled to final declaratory and injunctive relief 
declaring that the October 6, 1997 amendment is a valid and 
enforceable amendment eliminating benefits provided by the 
COLA amendment for the members of the Retiree Subclass. 


39. In the alternative, with respect to members of the 
Active Subclass, the COLA benefits are not subject to 


28 


reduction by a Plan amendment on account of being “accrued 
benefits” within the meaning of ERISA section 204(g). 
However, because the COLA amendment was adopted as the 
direct result of a fiduciary breach causing damages to the Plan 
by the payment of benefits provided under the COLA 
amendment, Plaintiffs are entitled under ERISA section 
502(a)(3), 29 U.S.C. § 1132(a)(3), to final declaratory and 
injunctive relief declaring the COLA amendment void from 
its inception, and of no force and effect, as to all members of 
the Active Subclass, and as to defendants Bobo, Kilroy, 
Lynch, and Mazur. 


Relief 


WHEREFORE, Plaintiffs pray that the court enter an 
order: 


1. Declaring the COLA amendment null and void as of 
the date of its adoption and eliminating any obligations 
thereunder as to all members of the Class and defendants 
Bobo, Kilroy, Lynch, and Mazur; 


2. In the alternative, (a) declaring that the October 6, 
1997 amendment is a valid and enforceable Plan 
amendment and that under the terms of that amendment, 
the Plan has no obligation, on and after November |, 
1997, to provide COLA benefits to members of the 
Retiree Subclass; and, (b) declaring the COLA 
amendment null and void as of the date of its adoption 
and eliminating any obligations thereunder to members 
of the Active Subclass and defendants Bobo, Kilroy, 
Lynch, and Mazur. 


3. Ordering such further relief as the court deems 
appropriate.

ARGUMENT 


I. Mr. Devlin Is Not Entitled To Intervene 


To intervene as of right, Mr. Devlin must make a timely 
application to intervene, claim “an interest relating to the 
transaction which is the subject of the action” and 
demonstrate that “disposition of the action may as a practical 
matter impair or impede the applicant’s ability to protect that 
interest, unless the applicant’s interest is adequately rep- 
resented by existing parties.” Fed. Rule Civ. P. 24(a)(2). 
“<The district court is entitled to the full range of reasonable 
discretion in determining whether these requirements [of Rule 
24(a)(2)] have been met.”” Virginia v. Westinghouse Elec. 
Corp., 542 F.2d 214, 216 (4th Cir. 1976) (quoting Rios v. 
Enterprise Ass'n Steamfitters Local Union No. 638, 520 F.2d 


352, 355 (2d Cir. 1975)). 


Mr. Devlin is not entitled to intervene under Rule 24(a)(2) 
because his request to intervene is untimely and because 
Retiree Class representative, Mr. Santoro, and class counsel, 
Ms. Kraft, adequately represent Mr. Devlin’s interests. 


83 
A. Mr. Devlin’s Motion To Intervene Is Not Timely. 


“The purpose of the [timeliness] requirement is to prevent 
a tardy intervenor from derailing a lawsuit within sight of the 
terminal.” United States v. South Bend Community School 
Corp., 710 F.2d 394, 396 (7th Cir. 1983), cert. denied, 466 
U.S. 926 (1984). To determine the timeliness of an 
intervention motion, a “court should look at how far the suit 
has progressed, the prejudice which delay might cause other 
parties, and the reason for the tardiness in moving to 
intervene.” Gould v. Alleco, Inc., 883 F.2d 281, 286 (4th Cir. 
1989), cert. denied, 493 U.S. 1058 (1990). Thus, a key factor 
courts examine is the proximity of a motion to intervene to a 
tentative settlement of the action, because of the prejudice the 
intervention would cause the existing parties. See Aleut 
Corp. v. Tyonek Native Corp., 725 F.2d 527, 530 (9th Cir. 
1984) (motion to intervene as of right was not timely when 
sought shortly before the parties agreed to a settlement); 
County of Orange v. Air California, 799 F.2d 535, 537-538 
(9th Cir. 1986), cert. denied, 480 U.S. 946 (1987) (motion to 
intervene as of right filed a few days after the parties had 
advised the district court of their settlement was not timely). 


In assessing a belated applicant’s reason for delay in 
moving to intervene, courts recognize that “[a] party seeking 
to intervene must act as soon as he ‘knows or has reason to 
know that his interests might be adversely affected by the . 
outcome of the litigation.”” United States v. Oregon, 913 
F.2d 576, 589 (9th Cir. 1990), cert. denied sub nom. Makah 
Indian Tribe v. United States 501 U.S. 1250 (1991) (delayed 
motion to intervene not timely where the potential intervenors 
inquired about the negotiations concerning the administrative 
plan settling the action and attempted to participate in the 
negotiations); see also County of Orange v. Air California, 
799 F.2d at 538 (motion filed after learning of proposed 
settlement not timely where potential intervenor should have 
known that existing parties might settle the action).

B. Mr. Devlin’s Interests Are Adequately Represented. 


Mr. Devlin’s request to intervene as of right also should be 
denied because his interests are adequately represented by

Mr. Santoro, the Retiree Class representative and Ms. Kraft,

interests are adequately represented, against which the

Neugebauer v. A.S. Abell Co., 77 F.R.D. 712, 714 (D. Md. 
1978) (same). Moreover, “(t}here is a presumption of 
adequate representation when the persons attempting to 
intervene are members of a class already involved in the 
litigation or are intervening only to protect the interests of 
class members.” Jenkins by Jenkins v. Missouri, 78 F.3d 
1270, 1275 (8th Cir. 1996) (rejecting claim of inadequate 
representation based upon disagreements over the details of 
the remedy sought).

Santoro represents and both Mr. Devlin and Mr. Santoro 
share the same ultimate objective of restoring, to the extent

a complaint that Mr. Santoro and Ms. Kraft followed a 
different litigation strategy than Mr. Devlin would have 
chosen. “A mere disagreement over-titigation strategy or 
individual aspects of a remediation plan does not, in and of 
itself, establish inadequacy of representation.” Bradley v. 
Milliken, 828 F.2d 1186, 1192 (6th Cir. 1987) (motion to 
intervene was properly denied because “mere disagreement 
over litigation strategy or individual aspects of a remediation 
plan does not,); see also Chiglo v. City of Preston, 104 F.3d 
185, 188 (8th Cir. 1997) (affirming denial of intervention 
where city’s decision not to appeal adverse decision did 
not constitute inadequate representation); B.H. v. McDonald, 
49 F.3d 294, 297 (7th Cir. 1995) (existing party’s refusal to 


90 


demand open court hearings did not reflect inadequate 
representation warranting intervention); United States v. 
Yonkers Bd. of Educ., 902 F.2d 213, 218 (2d Cir. 1990) (state 
adequately represented its interest and its citizens in choosing 
not to oppose district court’s order; citizens could not 
intervene to appeal order). Notwithstanding Mr. Devlin’s 
disagreement, the decision to pursue settlement negotiations 
is a legitimate litigation strategy employed to secure the 
rights of the Retiree Subclass, and does not constitute 
inadequate representation or warrant intervention. 


II. Mr. Devlin Does Not Meet the Requirements For 
Permissive Intervention. 


An applicant may, in the court’s discretion, be permitted to 
intervene upon timely application and after considering 
“whether the intervention will unduly delay or prejudice the 
adjudication of the rights of the original parties.” Fed. R. 
Civ. P. 24(b). As discussed above, Mr. Devlin’s request to 
intervene plainly is not timely. In addition, allowing Mr. 
Devlin to intervene would unduly delay and prejudice the 
adjudication of the rights of the Trustees and defendant class. 
See generally Norair Eng’g Corp. v. Washington Metro. Area 
Transit Auth., 33 F. Supp. 2d 422, 424 (D. Md. 1998) 
(denying motion to intervene as unduly prejudicial to the 
existing parties where potential intervenors raised issues not 
common to the existing parties that would complicate the 
case); Benford v. American Broadcasting Co. Inc., 98 F.R.D. 
42, 47 (D. Md. 1983) (denying intervention where potential 
intervenors would only reargue defenses already raised and 
delay the outcome of the case). ‘i 


After months of negotiating, the parties to this action 
reached a settlement in principle, obtained preliminary 
approval of the settlement and have notified class members of 
the proposed settlement. Mr. Devlin’s attempt to intervene 
now so that he may re-litigate issues concerning the 1991 
COLA decided more than two years ago will delay the 


91 


resolution of this case. Delaying resolution of this case could 
derail the settlement entirely, and would certainly delay the 
benefit improvements that class members would be entitled to 
receive under the proposed settlement. For this reason, Mr. 
Devlin’s request for permissive intervention should be 
denied. 


Permissive intervention also should be denied when the 
potential intervenor will not “significantly contribute to full 
development of the underlying factual issues in the suit and to 
the just and equitable adjudication of the legal questions 
presented.” H.L. Hayden Co. of New York v. Siemens 
Medical Systems, Inc., 797 F.2d 85, 89 (2d Cir. 1986) 
(citation omitted); see also Arney v. Finney, 967 F.2d 418, 
421 (10th Cir. 1992) (permissive intervention properly denied 
where potential intervenor was a member of the class and his 
intervention would not aid the class in adjudicating its 
claims); League of United Latin Amer. Citizens v. Clements, 
884 F.2d 185, 189 (Sth Cir. 1989). 


Mr. Devlin is certainly not in a position to assist in the 
development of the underlying factual issues. For all 
practical purposes, the factual issues in this case were fully 
developed, litigated, and decided in the related case of 
Scardelletti v. Bobo, No. JFM-95-52, where the Court 
concluded that the record developed in extensive discovery 
did not present any material issues of fact. The reality is that 
the only issues presented by the present action are pure 
questions of law. 


Similarly, Mr. Devlin is not in a position to significantly 
contribute to the resolution of the legal issues presented. At 
the June 5, 1998 hearing, Mr. Devlin’s counsel readily 
conceded that he was unfamiliar with and lacked knowledge 
concerning the principal legal issue in this case—whether the 
COLA amendment could be deemed void ab initio. Exhibit 
B to Devlin Aff. at 19-20. Given the representations by Mr. 
Devlin’s counsel at the June 5, 1998 hearing, Mr. Devlin 


92 


cannot credibly claim that his intervention would assist in 
developing the factual issues of this case or in adjudicating 
the legal questions. 


Most importantly, as a member of the Retiree Class, Mr. 
Devlin will have a full opportunity to raise all of his concerns 
about the settlement agreement, the negotiations, and the 
adequacy of the class representatives at the fairness hearing 
on November 12, 1999, prior to any approval of the proposed 
settlement. Intervention is not required to achieve that end. 
Because intervention is wholly unnecessary to allow Mr. 
Devlin to raise any factual or legal issues concerning the 
settlement and because of the substantial delay and likely 
prejudice that would otherwise result, the Trustees 
respectfully urge the Court to deny Mr. Devlin’s motion to 
intervene. 


CONCLUSION 


For the foregoing reasons, Mr. Devlin’s request to 
intervene and other relief sought in the Cross Motion should 


be denied.

14. Another method of terminating a defined benefit 
pension plan is to seek a distress termination from the PBGC. 
In general, in a distress termination, the employer sponsoring

or show that continued funding of the plan will render the 
plan sponsor insolvent. If the PBGC determines that a

would be greater than $15 million. The principal reason that 
this roughly estimated claim is not larger is that PBGC would 
not treat future COLA increases called for under the 1991

18. The third measurement of the liabilities of a pension 
plan is referred to in Exhibit R-2 as “RPA °94 Current 


135 


Liability” or “RPA94 CL.” This is a reference to the 
Retirement Protection Act of 1994, which modified ERISA’s 
existing minimum funding requirements by establishing a 
standard minimum funding requirement that all plans must 
satisfy. In general, RPA °94 requires the plan’s enrolled 
actuary to calculate the plan’s liabilities using certain 
mandated interest and mortality assumptions prescribed by 
the Internal Revenue Service for all plans. Once the plan’s 
liabilities are calculated using these assumptions, the actuary 
determines the plan’s “funded ratio,” that is, the ratio of the 
plan’s liabilities to its assets, determined as of the beginning 
of the plan year. If the plan’s funded ratio falls below certain 
Statutory benchmarks, then additional funding requirements 
are triggered, and the plan’s sponsor is required to make 
larger contributions in order to fund the plan’s liabilities at a 
faster rate than would be required under the general minimum 
funding requirements. If a plan’s RPA ’94 funded ratio falls 
below 90% in the current plan year and for two of the three 
years prior to the current plan year, the additional funding 
requirements are triggered, and the level of required 
contributions will be substantially higher than under normal 
minimum funding rules. Consequently, for purposes of the 
settlement discussions in this litigation, the most important 
measure of the Plan’s liabilities was the RPA °94 Current 
Liability, because that liability determines the rate at which 
the Plan would have to be funded if the settlement, or the 
litigation, results in higher levels of benefits becoming 
payable under the Plan.

MEMORANDUM 


The Fourth Circuit has remanded this action for the sole 
purpose of having me set forth my reasons for issuing an 
injunction under the All Writs Act in accordance with Fed. R. 
Civ. P. 65. 


The reason for my issuance of the injunction is that Robert 
Devlin has, by his pattern of conduct, demonstrated that 
unless he is enjoined, he will institute litigation in other 
districts to undermine the class action settlement this court 
has approved (a ruling that has now been affirmed by the 
Fourth Circuit).'° This pattern is evidenced by other cases 
that Devlin filed both before and after the settlement 
agreement was approved while this action was pending. 
After I ordered recission of the 1991 COLA, Devlin filed two 
actions in the Southern District of New York, seeking a 
preliminary injunction to restore the COLA. The New York 
court denied the motion, finding that this court was the proper 
forum in which such a motion should be filed. The Second 
Circuit affirmed. Thereafter, Devlin sought to amend his

members of the retiree subclass. | will refer to this group collectively as 
“Devlin.”

complaint in the New York actions to reassert the same 
COLA claims previously raised and rejected. In the proposed 
amended complaint Devlin claimed that “the entire 
proceeding in Maryland” had been “tainted.” The New York 
court denied the motion for leave to amend. 


After this court had granted preliminary approval to the 
settlement agreement, Devlin sought to intervene. I denied 
the motion to intervene as untimely but Devlin and his 
counsel did attend the fairness hearing. Devlin appealed my 
denial of his motion to intervene and my approval of the 
settlement agreement. My rulings were affirmed by the 
Fourth Circuit on appeal. 


Notwithstanding my approval of the settlement agreement, 
while the approval was pending, Devlin instituted another 
action in the Southern District of New York alleging that the 
prosecution of the present suit by the Trustees was a breach 
of their fiduciary duties and that the settlement of this 
litigation was the product of collusion. Self-evidently, the 
institution of that action constituted an attack upon my 
approval of the settlement agreement and was an attempt to 
circumvent the jurisdiction not only of this court but also of 
the Fourth Circuit in which the appeal of my rulings was 
to be heard. 


I, was against the background of these lawsuits that on

“The All Writs Act empowers a federal court to enjoin 
parties before it from attempting to relitigate decided issues 
and to prevent collateral attack of its judgments.” Jn re

States v. Cohen, 152 F.3d 321, 325 (4th Cir. 1998) (“where an 
injunction is proper in order to protect or effectuate the 
judgments of a federal court, it is within that court’s power to 
issue the injunction under the All Writs Act.”). Here, Dev-

decided and to collaterally attack my rulings, including my 
approval of the settlement agreement. Nothing short of an 
injunction (and, as it turned out, the threat of the imposition 
of contempt sanctions) deterred him from his conduct. 
Moreover, he has suffered no cognizable harm as the result of 
the issuance of the injunction since, as both the United States 
District Court for the Southern District of New York and the 
Second Circuit have recognized, this court is the only 
appropriate forum for him to challenge, directly or indirectly, 
the class action settlement agreement.
