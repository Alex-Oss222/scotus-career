REPLY FOR THE PETITIONER 


Respondents freely admit here, just as they did below, that 
this case squarely presents and perfectly illustrates the 
entrenched circuit conflict over whether non-named class 
members may appeal a class action settlement. BIO 8 & 
nn.4-5. Respondents furthermore do not dispute that the 
decision below cannot be reconciled with this Court’s 
precedents holding (i) that the Fourteenth Amendment’s Due 
Process Clause protects a class member’s right to appeal (see 
Pet. 19-22), and (ii) that so-called “quasi parties” in 
petitioner’s position have standing to appeal (id. 16-19). 
Indeed, respondents do not dispute for a moment that the 
majority rule in the circuits that the Fourth Circuit adopted in 
this case simply makes no sense on its own terms: petitioner 
is a member of the certified class in this case and thus plainly 
is a “party” to this litigation, as evidenced by the fact that the 


: 2 
district court entered an injunction against him on the basis of 
the settlement; petitioner thus plainly has the right to appeal. 
See id. 18-19. 

Having thereby conceded the essential elements of this 
Court’s certiorari inquiry, respondents are reduced to arguing 
that any ruling by this Court would be “advisory” (BIO 11- 
13) and that the question presented is not sufficiently 
important to warrant this Court’s attention (id. 13-18). 
Neither argument is persuasive. 


I. Petitioner Is Not Seeking An “Advisory” Opinion. 


This petition presents a live case or controversy meriting 
review in this Court because the Fourth Circuit held that 
petitioner lacked standing to appeal “[a]s a threshold matter” 
(Pet. App. Al4) and avowedly left the merits of the case 
unresolved. Respondents’ argument that footnote seventeen 
of the panel majority’s opinion effectively decided the merits 
is facially absurd. That footnote could not be clearer: “we do 
not decide this issue.” Pet. App. A24 n.17 (emphasis added). 


Respondents nonetheless contend that they are confident 
they will prevail on remand. Their bravado, however, falls 
far short of rendering this Court’s ruling on the question 
presented by this petition “advisory.” If this Court grants 
petitioner the relief he seeks — a holding that he has standing 
to appeal — the case will be remanded for disposition on the 
merits. The fact that the court of appeals might later rule for 
respondents is immaterial. Cf. Order of Nov. 8, 2001, No. 
01-408, Holmes Group, Inc. v. Vornado Air Circulation Sys., 
cert. granted Nov. 8, 2001 (granting certiorari to consider 
whether jurisdiction properly rested with Federal Circuit or 
instead regional circuit, notwithstanding that Federal Circuit 
panel’s reasoning on the merits of the case would presumably 
be adopted by the regional circuit on remand if the petitioner 
prevailed). 


3 


Respondents’ confidence that they will win on remand is, 
moreover, entirely misplaced, for their statement that footnote 
seventeen “address[ed] in some detail each of Devlin’s 
objections to the settlement” (BIO 12) is, once again, a flat 
misstatement of what the court did. As that footnote explains 
in its very first sentence, the panel majority avowedly 
discussed only “one of the bases for Devlin’s challenge to the 
class settlement” - viz., “that COLA benefits are accrued 
benefits that could not be eliminated by amendment without 
prior notice to and approval from the Secretary of Labor.” 
Pet. App. A24 n.17 (emphasis added). The majority did not 
so much as mention, for example, petitioner’s argument that 
the settlement was unfair because it did not require active 
employees to make pension contributions that retirees had 
made for many years. See Pet. 5. And although respondents 
make much of the fact that the concurring judge in this case 
concluded that the district court “was well within its 
discretion” in approving the settlement as fair (BIO 11 (citing 
Pet. App. A35)), the much more salient point is that the 
majority did not address the settlement’s fairness. 

Even as to the one argument addressed by the panel 
majority — regarding which, as noted, it took care to make 
clear “we do not decide” (Pet. App. A24 n.17) — the court 
acknowledged that the correct rule of law “is at best unclear.” 
Id. Respondents themselves concede that the law is totally 
unsettled: “What benefits fall within this tautological 
definition and are therefore protected by section 204(g)’s 
anticutback rule has been the subject of much litigation that 
has produced no clear answer.” BIO 3 n.1. It is also 
noteworthy that the Scardelletti respondents (i.e., the plaintiff- 
trustees) originally took petitioner's position in the district 
court, arguing that “the benefits granted by the COLA 
amendment were ‘accrued’ benefits that could not be reduced 
by a voluntary plan amendment.” Jd. 2. 

The Fourth Circuit’s passing dictum casting doubt on this 
one aspect of petitioner’s appeal, moreover, will benefit from 


4 


further consideration on remand. The majority below 
suggested that, even if ERISA prohibited a pension plan from 
making the plan amendment in this case, a district court might 
still be able to approve the same amendment: “In other 
words, it was not a Plan amendment that revoked the COLA 
increases, but rather the district court’s final order declaring 
the 1991 Amendment void.” Pet. App. A24 n.17. But that 
reasoning would make the relevant ERISA provision a 
nullity, for it would always permit plan administrators (as in 
this case) to secure declaratory judgments from federal 
district courts permitting them to enact otherwise unlawful 
plan amendments. Congress could not have intended that 
result. 


The decisions cited by respondents (BIO 11) for the 
proposition that this petition seeks an advisory ruling are 
accordingly entirely dissimilar from this case, which is 
presumably why the brief in opposition lifts quotes from them 
out of context without any further explanation. In truth, 
respondents seem to have taken these citations largely from 
page 172 of the Stern & Gressman treatise, where they are 
properly cited for propositions that have nothing to do with 
this case. See ROBERT L. STERN ET AL., SUPREME COURT 
PRACTICE § 4.4, at 172 (7th ed. 1993) (citing Rice v. Sioux 
City Memorial Park Cemetery, 349 U.S. 70, 75 (1955), for 
the proposition that, “[i}f the statute upon which the 
controversy rests has expired or been amended in a manner 
which will prevent the problem from arising in the future, 
certiorari may be denied despite a square conflict’); id. (citing 
The Monrosa v. Carbon Black Export, 359 U.S. 180, 184 
(1959), for the proposition that “[i]f it appears that upon a 
grant of certiorari the Supreme Court might be able to decide 
the case on another ground and thus not reach the point upon 


5 


which there is conflict, the conflict itself may not be sufficient 
reason for granting review”). 


' . Thus, the Court DIG’ed Rice v. Sioux City Memorial Park 
Cemetery because an intervening state statute “bar[red] the ultimate 
question presented in this case from arising again in that State,” such that 
the Court concluded that the case did not satisfy its rule that certiorari was 
appropriate “where a state court has decided a federal question of 
substance not theretofore determined by this court.” 349 U.S. 70, 73 
(1955). “A federal question raised by a petitioner may be ‘of substance’ 
in the sense that, abstractly considered, it may present an intellectually 
interesting and solid problem. But this Court does not sit to satisfy a 
scholarly interest in such issues. Nor does it sit for the benefit of the 
particular litigants.” /d. at 74. Rice thus stands for the proposition, 
inapposite here, that certiorari is inappropriate where the Court's decision 
“would be of no importance save to the litigants themselves.” Rudolph v. 
United States, 370 U.S. 269, 269 (1962) (per curiam). 


The Court DIG’ed The Monrosa v. Carbon Black Export because 
it concluded after briefing and argument that the particular contract before 
it did not implicate the circuit conflict asserted by the petition for 
certiorari: “While this Court decides questions of public importance, it 
decides them in the context of meaningful litigation. Its function in 
resolving conflicts among the Courts of Appeals is judicial, not simply 
administrative or managerial. Resolution here of the extent to which these 
bill of lading provisions may be given effect by our courts can await a day 
when the issue is posed less abstractly.” 359 U.S. 180, 184 (1959). The 
Monrosa is accordingly regularly cited for the proposition, inapposite 
here, that “[njow that plenary consideration has shed more light on this 
case than in the nature of things was afforded at the time the petition for 
certiorari was considered, we have concluded that the writ should be 
dismissed as improvidently granted.” Belcher v. Stengel, 429 U.S. 118, 
119-20 (1976) (per curiam). 

Finally, the Court DIG’ed Ticor Title Insurance Co. v. Brown, in 
part because “[{o}n the day we granted certiorari we were informed that the 
parties had reached a settlement designed to moot the petition, which now 
awaits the approval of the District Court,” which led the Court to conclude 
that “as matters have developed it is not clear that our resolution of the 
constitutional question will make any difference even to these litigants.” 
511 U.S. 117, 122 (1994) (per curiam). This case does not implicate 
Ticor’s holding. 


6 

Respondents’ remaining contention — that this Court’s 
consideration of this case would “delay [the retirees’] receipt 
of the benefits of the settlement” (BIO 6 n.13) — is, to be 
blunt, outrageous. The settlement restores a portion of the 
retirees’ COLA benefits that the trustees revoked in violation 
of ERISA. Most relevant here, it was respondents — i.e., the 
plaintiff-trustees and the defendant class representatives 
whom they hand-picked — who designed the terms of the 
settlement, including a provision mandating that if even a 
single retiree appealed, no class member would receive any 
benefits until that appeal was totally concluded. (As the 
petition explained (at 20 n.7), the plaintiffs have conveniently 
been paying all the attorney’s fees of the class representatives 
throughout the case.) The only function of this provision is to 
strong-arm class members into abandoning their objections. 
Indeed, petitioner purposefully filed his petition for certiorari 
well before the statutory deadline and requested in wriling 
that respondents revise the settlement to disburse some 
benefits to retirees while his appeal is pending; respondents 
categorically refused. It is hard to imagine a_ better 
illustration of why it is essential that this Court address the 
question presented and hold that settlements do not extinguish 
objectors’ right to appeal. 


Il. The Question Presented Is Vitally Important. 


As an alternative ground for denying certiorari, 
respondents suggest that the question presented is not 
important enough to warrant this Court’s attention. 
Objectively, that is not correct. As the petition explained, this 
Court recently granted certiorari to decide the very similar 
question whether objectors to a derivative-action settlement 
have standing to appeal. Felzen v. Andreas, 134 F.3d 873 
(CA7), cert. granted, 524 U.S. 980 (1998), aff'd by equally 
divided Court, 525 U.S. 315 (1999). For two reasons, the 
grounds for granting certiorari are even more substantial here: 
(i) the circuit conflict in Felzen was substantially narrower 


7 


(only two-to-one); and (ii) the conflict presented here arises in 
all class actions, not merely derivative suits. See Pet. 12. 


Of note, respondents do not dispute that the question 
whether an objecting class member has the ability to appeal is 
vitally important. The appellant’s right to due process is at 
stake. So, too, is the proper disposition of innumerable class 
actions. As the petition explained, class action settlements are 
frequently collusive and approved by district judges upon 
only brief consideration. See Pet. 24-25. The prospect of an 
appeal puts greater pressure on the litigants and the district 
judge to enter into a lawful and equitable settlemen:. It was 
an objector’s appeal that led to this Court’s seminal decision 
in Amchem Products, Inc. v. Windsor, 521 U.S. 591 (1997). 
See also, e.g., Jn re Synthroid Mktg. Litig., 264 F.3d 712, 715, 
722-23 (CA7 2001) (reversing denial of post-settlement 
motions to intervene and partially reversing order approving 
settlement); Crawford v. Equifax Payment Servs., Inc., 201 
F.3d 877, 882 (CA7 2000) (reversing denial of post- 
certification motion to intervene and then reversing approval 
of settlement); /n re General Motors Corp. Pick-Up Truck 
Fuel Tank Prods. Liability Litig., 55 F.3d 768, 778-79 (CA3) 
(appeal by objecting class members leads to rejection of 
settlement as unfair and inadequate), cert. denied, 516 U.S. 
824 (1995). 


Although respondents’ argument is not entirely clear, it 
seems to be that the circuit conflict over objectors’ right to 
appeal makes no practical difference because objectors 
always can intervene in the case and appeal on that basis. But 
respondents just ignore the myriad reasons — all set forth in 
the petition and summarized once again here — why that is not 
true: 


e Class members do not have an automatic right to 
intervene. Intervention will be denied if they do not 
satisfy the requirements of Fed. R. Civ. P. 24(a) — in 
particular, the requirement that the class representatives 
are not adequately representing their interests. 


8 


° Intervention is costly and burdensome for the class 
member. The objector not only must file a complaint 
or answer in intervention, but also is then subject to all 
the ongoing requirements of a litigating party, 
including discovery. 

e Requiring intervention is directly contrary to the point 
of class action litigation. On the view of the 
respondents and the Fourth Circuit, every class member 
who suspects that he might be disadvantaged by a 
settlement that might occur in the case must promptly 
intervene. By contrast, the goal of class action 
litigation — notably, a goal that the panel majority 
erroneously thought it was furthering — is to avoid 
having numerous class members in the case who can 
“usurp the role of the class representative” (Pet. App. 
A21). 


e Requiring intervention undermines sound judicial 
administration. The District Courts will be burdened 
by requests to intervene. When they understandably 
deny those requests, the putative intervenor will 
immediately appeal as of right. 

Respondents’ specific reliance on the Seventh Circuit’s 
appealability rule (BIO 15-16) is simply befuddling. As the 
petition explained (at 13) and respondents do not seem to 
contest, the rule in the Seventh Circuit is that a class action 
objector has an effectively per se right to intervene, even after 
the settlement is consummated, in order to preserve his right 
to appeal as a formal “party.” Jn re Synthroid Mktg. Litig., 
264 F.3d at 715; Crawford, 201 F.3d at 880-81. In 
petitioner’s view, that approach places a totally unnecessary 
burden on the district judge. But the relevant point here is 
that the Fourth Circuit rejected the Seventh Circuit’s 
approach — it held that petitioner could not appeal 
notwithstanding that he had sought to intervene well before 
the district court approved the settlement. Of particular note, 
the Seventh Circuit adopted its incredibly permissive 


9 


approach to intervention precisely because objectors’ right to 
appeal would otherwise be denied — i.e., the Seventh Circuit’s 
decisions flatly refute respondents’ claim that this circuit 
conflict makes no difference. Crawford, 201 F.3d at 881 
(“Because only parties may appeal, it is vital that district 
courts freely allov’ the intervention of unnamed class 
members who object to proposed settlemenis and want an 
option to appeal an adverse decision.”). 


Finally, respondents at points attempt to cast aspersions 
on the manner in which petitioner litigated this case in the 
district court. The point of these asides is unclear — they do 
not suggest, and respondents do not contend, that this is an 
inappropriate vehicle to resolve the question presented. 
Moreover, respondents’ claims are simply false. The class 
counsel consistently withheld information from petitioner. 
See, e.g., C.A. App. 526 (Apr. 7, 1999 letter from petitioner 
to class counsel: “Dear Ms. Kraft: On at least four (4) 
separate occasions I have requested that you furnish the terms 
of the settlement of the action brought against the former Plan 
trustees, to no avail.”), 528 (Apr. 7, 1999 letter from 
petitioner to class counsel: “You have also failed to furnish 
the terms of the settlement of the action brought against the 
former trustees and I request you end the secrecy that has 
been placed on all these matters and furnish the information 
requested.”). Respondents’ statement that they provided 
“periodic reports on the status of negotiations and the terms 
under discussion” (BIO 5) is just a euphemism for the fact 
that they said they were negotiating the abrogation of the 
COLA but, as the just-quoted letters explain, would not 
provide petitioner with the details. Petitioner also did not 
object “to any idea of settlement” (id.); there is no support for 
any such proposition in the cited pages of the appellate record 
(C.A. App. 525-35). His position was, and is, that ERISA 
prohibited abrogating the COLA for retirees, and that any 
settlement would accordingly have to treat the retirees 


10 
equitably.’ And while it certainly is the case that petitioner’ 
- did not formally move to intervene until the settlement was 
finalized and he received a copy, there is no reason in law or 
logic to hold that he had to act earlier in order to preserve his 
right to appeal the settlement. Not only would it have been 
me sere at * ne an ear rg it would have 
been impossible. 
CONCLUSION 


For the foregoing reasons, as well as those stated in the 
Petition for a Writ of Certiorari, certiorari should be granted.
