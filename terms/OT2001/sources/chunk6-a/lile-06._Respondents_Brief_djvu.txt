STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
1. Nature of the Case. 


Respondent Robert Lile is an inmate in medium secu- 
rity at the Lansing Correctional Facility in Lansing, Kan- 
sas. He refuses to incriminate himself by participating in 
Kansas’ sexual abuse treatment program (“SATP”). The 
State violates Mr. Lile’s Fifth Amendment right against 
self-incrimination because it demands incriminating 
information. The State, despite contending that SATP is 
voluntary, compels such admissions by threatening sanc- 
tions.' Mr. Lile can choose to admit crimes and face 
prosecution or remain quiet and be punished for his 
silence.

SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT 


The Fifth Amendment provides that “[n]o per- 
son .. . Shall be compelled in any criminal case to be a 
witness against himself.” U.S. Const. amend V. It guaran- 
tees a person's right “to remain silent unless he chooses 
to speak in the unfettered exercise of his own will, and to 
suffer no penalty . . . for such silence.” Estelle v. Smith, 451 
U.S. 454, 468 (1981) (quoting Malloy v. Hogan, 378 U.S. 1, 8 
(1964) (emphasis added)). 


The State suggests that SATP does not violate the 
Fifth Amendment because it is a voluntary program. It 
makes this assertion while at the same time acknowledg- 
ing that should Mr. Lile invoke his Fifth Amendment 
rights, the State will transfer him to maximum security 
and punish him with penalties imposed for only the 
“most severe violations” in prison. When applied to 
inmates outside SATP, these sanctions are viewed as pun- 
ishments; when someone in SATP invokes his constitu- 
tional rights, the State suggests that these same 
punishments are merely the State’s way of encouraging 
participation. 


Indeed, Mr. Lile would like to participate in SATP 
today. He has participated in prison programs before and 
has benefitted from them. But for Mr. Lile to participate 
to the State’s satisfaction in SATP, he must admit that he 
committed the crimes for which he was convicted, 
despite his insistence that he is not guilty. Furthermore, 
he must list, in exacting detail, all other sexual offenses 
fot.which he has never been charged. The State would 
then gauge the veracity of any such statements through 
polygraph testing. 


If Mr. Lile states that he committed the crime for 
which he was convicted, the State can prosecute him for 
perjury. He would also lose any chance he might have for 
a new trial based upon newly-discovered evidence. If he 
admits to any previously uncharged sexual offense 
involving a child, prison officials must report him. 


The State concedes that it seeks incriminating infor- 
mation from Mr. Lile. It attempts to minimize this by 
making two points, both flawed. The first is that, to the 
best of its knowledge, the State has never prosecuted 
anyone for a previously unreported crime (although the 
State has conceded that it will report any prosecutable 
information). Pet. App. 7a.> The second is that SATP is

There are workable solutions that would enable the 
State to meet its goals of rehabilitation. As both the 
District Court and Tenth Circuit ruled, the State can 
immunize Mr. Lile for any incriminating statements he 
makes. This protection would ensure the candid exchange 
that the State claims it needs for SATP. Then, if Mr. Lile

Alternatively, the State could place all sex offenders

Both solutions would uphold the Constitution and

ARGUMENT 


I. The State’s Operation of SATP Amounts to Uncon- 
stitutional Compulsion. 


The Fifth Amendment right against self-incrimina- 
tion is “the mainstay of our adversary system of criminal 
justice.” Johnson v. New Jersey, 384 U.S. 719, 729 (1966). It 
is to be “broadly applied and generously implemented in 
accordance with the teaching of the history of the privi- 
lege and its great office in mankind’s battle for freedom.” 
In re Gault, 387 U.S. 1, 50 (1967); see also Maness v. Meyers, 
419 U.S. 449, 461 (1975) (“This Cow _aas always broadly 
construed its protection to assure that an individual is not 
compelled to produce evidence which later may be used 
against him as an accused in a criminal action.”). 


Mr. Lile agrees with the State that this case hinges on 
whether the State’s system of sanctions amounts to 
unconstitutional compulsion under the Fifth Amendment. 
Hoffman v. United States, 341 U.S. 479, 486 (1951) (quoting 
Feldman v. United States, 322 U.S. 487, 489 (1944)) (cita- 
tions omitted) (“This guarantee against testimonial com- 
pulsion, like other provisions of the Bill of Rights, must 
be accorded liberal construction in favor of the right it 
was intended to secure.”). 


In this case, Mr. Lile faces compulsion. The State 
demands participation in SATP and, at the same time, 
demands that Mr. Lile surrender his Fifth Amendment 
rights. It forces compliance through sanctions and the 
real prospect of personal violence. This Court should not 
permit the State to punish inmates like Mr. Lile, espe- 
cially when simple alternatives exist that would not only 


preserve the Fifth Amendment but improve the efficiency 
of SATP. 


A. A Fifth Amendment claim does not require a 
liberty interest. 


The State argues that this Court should employ its 
liberty interest test under the Due Process Clause of the 
Fourteenth Amendment from Sandin v. Conner, 515 U.S. 
472 (1995), when analyzing “compulsion” within the self- 
incrimination privilege of the Fifth Amendment. In 
Sandin, this Court held that prison segregation does not 
rise to the level of a protected liberty interest under the 
Due Process Clause unless it imposes “atypical” and “sig- 
nificant” hardships on the inmate in relation to the ordi- 
nary incidents of prison life. Id. at 486. 


This Court in Sandin reasserted its central holding 
from Meachum v. Fano, 427 U.S. 215 (1976), that the Due 
Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not 
require a hearing before a prisoner is transferred from 
one state prison to another having harsher conditions, 
unless a state law or practice creates a liberty interest in 
continued confinement at the first prison. Sandin, 515 US. 
at 483. Leaping from Sandin’s due process analysis to self- 
incrimination, the State, in turn, urges that “the touch- 
stone for unconstitutional compulsion in the prison set- 
ting is the imposition of atypical hardships.” Pet. Brief 15. 
Courts considering analogous claims have reached the 
opposite conclusion. 


For example, in analyzing Meachum, the First Circuit 
Court of Appeals declined to rely on the dicta cited by 
the State in its brief that “prison officials have discretion 


10 


to transfer [prisoners] for whatever reason or no reason at 
all.” Furtado v. Bishop, 604 F.2d 80, 87 n.6 (ist Cir. 1979). 
The court explained that “we understand this statement 
to describe the applicable Massachusetts law in that case 
and not to constitute a pronouncement that a transfer can 
never violate the Constitution.” Id. The court in Furtado 
held that nothing in this Court’s due process decisions 
“expressly ruled out a challenge to a transfer to segrega- 
tion on the ground that it violated constitutional rights 
other than the right to procedural due process of law.” Id. 
at 87. 


In fact, in the sister case to Meachum that was before 
this Court at the same time, Montanye v. Haymes, 427 U.S. 
236 (1976), this Court noted that “[a]s long as the condi- 
tions or degree of confinement to which the prisoner is 
subjected is within the sentence imposed upon him and is 
not otherwise violative of the Constitution, the Due Process 
Clause does not in itself subject an inmate's treatment by 
prison authorities to judicial oversight.” Id. at 242 
(emphasis added); see also Olim v. Wakinekona, 461 U.S. 
238, 248 n.9 (1983) (citing Montanye, this Court reiterated 
that a state may place an-inmate in any penal institution 
unless “the reason for confining the inmate in a particular 
institution is constitutionally impermissible”). 


Other courts have interpreted Meachum similarly. As 
the Tenth Circuit Court of Appeals noted in Frazier v. 
DuBois, “Meachum addressed only the Due Process 
Clause, and it did not preclude the possibility that a 
transfer might violate other constitutional provisions.” 
922 F.2d 560, 561 (10th Cir. 1990).

“While a prisoner enjoys no constitutional right 
to remain in a particular institution and gener- 
ally is not entitled to due process protections 
prior to such a transfer, prison officials do not 
have the discretion to punish an inmate for exer- 
cising his first amendment rights by transferring 
him to a different institution.” 


Id. at 561-62 (citing Murphy v. Missouri Dep't of Corr., 769 
F.2d 502, 503 (8th Cir. 1985)).4

Indiana argued that it could transfer an inmate “for what- 
ever reason or for no reason at all.” The Seventh Circuit 
disagreed, noting that “while plaintiff may not have a 
due process interest or expectation in remaining at the

Ill. The Tenth Circuit’s Decision is Consistent with the 
History and Origins of the Fifth Amendment. 


The State’s argument that the Tenth Circuit’s decision 
does not comport with the history and origins of the Fifth 
Amendment is baseless. Under its view, only those pun- 
ishments akin to “incarceration, banishment, or mutila- 
tion” would constitute impermissible compulsion. Pet. 
Brief 30-31. Contrary to the State’s position, “(t]he roots 
of the privilege are deeper than the rack and the screw 
used to extort confessions. They go to the nature of a free 
man and to his relationship to the state.” United States v. 
Wade, 388 U.S. 218, 261 (1967) (Fortas, J., concurring in 
part and dissenting in part). Indeed, this Court noted in 
Ullmann v. United States, 350 U.S. 422 (1956), that the 
privilege against self-incrimination “was aimed at a more 
far-reaching evil - a recurrence of the Inquisition and the 
Star Chamber, even if not in their stark brutality.” Id- at 


427-28. 


Courts routinely apply the Fifth Amendment to pun- 
ishments of all varieties. As this Court noted in Lefkowitz 
v. Cunningham, 431 U.S. 801, 806 (1977), “the touchstone 
of the Fifth Amendment is compulsion, and direct eco- 
nomic sanctions and imprisonment are not the only pen- 
alties capable of forciag the self-incrimination which the 
Amendment forbids.” 


The privilege serves many important purposes: 


It reflects many of our fundamental values and 
most noble aspirations: our unwillingness to 
subject those suspected of crime to the cruel 
trilemma of self-accusation, perjury or con- 
tempt; our preference for an accusatorial rather
