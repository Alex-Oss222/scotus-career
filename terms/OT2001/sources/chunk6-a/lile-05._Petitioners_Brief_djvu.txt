JURISDICTION 
This Court’s jurisdiction to review the final judgment of the 
Tenth Circuit is invoked pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1254(1). The 
Tenth Circuit issued its decision in this case on September 5, 
2000, and denied the State’s timely petition for rehearing and 
suggestion for rehearing en banc on October 24, 2000. The 


State filed a timely petition for a writ of certiorari on January 
22, 2001, and this Court granted review on May 14, 2001. 


CONSTITUTIONAL PROVISION INVOLVED 


The Fifth Amendment to the Constitution provides in 
pertinent part that 


“No person . . . shall be compelled in any criminal case 
to be a witness against himself... .” 


STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


1. Nature of the Suit. The Kansas Department of 
Corrections employs a comprehensive system of earnable 
inmate privileges in order to manage the offender population 
and reinforce constructive behavioral changes. As part of this 
system, inmates work their way through privilege incentive 
levels in accordance with their behavior, at all times subject to 
reduction for disciplinary, work or recommended program 
infractions. 

Inmate Lile, a convicted, violent sex offender, filed this suit 
after his prison privileges were to be reduced to Level I (from 
Level III) for his refusal to participate in recommended 


2 


treatment, the Sexual Abuse Treatment Program (SATP). 
Inmate Lile objected to the SATP’s requirement that he accept 
responsibility for his sex crimes. He claims that the change in 
his prison privileges for refusing to accept responsibility in the 
SATP would violate his Fifth Amendment privilege against 
self-incrimination. 


2. Background. 


A. Facts of Conviction. Robert G. Lile was convicted 
in 1983 of the rape, aggravated sodomy, and aggravated 
kidnapping of a female high school student. See State v. Lile, 
237 Kan. 210, 699 P.2d 456, 457 (Kan. 1985); Lile v. McKune, 
45 F. Supp. 2d 1157, 1159-60 (D. Kan. 1999). 


At Lile’s trial, the victim testified that she had never met 
Lile prior to the day of the crimes. She testified that the day of 
the events she was on her way home from school when she 
Saw a Car stopped beside the road with the hood up. Lile, who 
was standing by the car, flagged her down and asked for help. 
He asked her to pump the brakes of the car while he was 
looking under the hood. He then came to the driver’s door and 
told the victim to slide over. He pulled a gun, which turned 
out to be a starter’s pistol, from his belt, pointed it at the 
victim, and ordered her to lock the passenger door. Lile then 
forced the victim to perform oral sex. After a few minutes, he 
drove her to a field where he ordered her to remove her 
clothes. Lile then raped her. See Lile, 699 P.2d at 457. 


After getting away from Lile, the victim immediately 
returned to her school, visibly upset, and reported the incident. 
When Lile was arrested, the police found him in possession of 
the starter pistol. Although Lile argued that he had met the girl 
a few days before these events, and that their sexual 
encounters were consensual, both the Kansas Supreme Court 
and a federal district court easily concluded that the evidence 
supported Lile’s convictions beyond a reasonable doubt. See 
Lile, 699 P.2d at 458 (“We have no hesitancy in holding that,

favorable to the prosecution, there was sufficient evidence for 
the jury to find the defendant guilty beyond a reasonable doubt 
of rape and aggravated sodomy’); Lile, 45 F. Supp. 2d at 1161 
(“The court finds the evidence, when viewed in the light most 
favorable to the prosecution, clearly is sufficient for a 
reasonably jury to find beyond a reasonable doubt that 
petitioner was guilty of the charged offenses”). 


In 1983, Lil. also was convicted of attempted aggravated 
escape from custody, after he tried to escape from Lansing. 
See Lile v. Simmons, 929 P.2d 171, 172 (Kan. Ct. App. 1996). 


B. The Kansas Internal Management Policy And 
Procedure System. In 1996, the Kansas prisons adopted an 
Internal Management Policy and Procedure system (“IMPP”’). 
Joint Appendix (“J.A.”) 47. The Kansas IMPP is designed to 
“implement a comprehensive system of earnable offender 
privileges which will provide an effective means of managing 


’ the offender population and reinforcing constructive 


behavioral changes in offenders.” J.A. 13. 


The IMPP identifies “earnable privileges’—such as 
television ownership, use of funds, and visitation—and the 
“Incentive Levels” to which those privileges are attached. /d. 
at 14-19. The Incentive Levels begin with “Intake Level,” 
when an inmate first enters prison, and progress through 
Levels I, I, and III in prison, and then to Levels IV and V for 
post-incarceration supervision. /d.; see also id. at 51-56. The 
IMPP also provides for “Reductions in Level” due to various 
inmate infractions or misbehavior. Among the bases for a 
reduction in Incentive Level are “[rjefusal to participate in 
recommended programs ....” /d. at 19; see also id. at 57-58. 
A fold out chart of the Incentive Levels and the corresponding 
earnable privileges is located at page 27 of the Joint Appendix.

is recommended for sex offenders by prison officials.

E. Inmate Lile. Based upon the offenses of which he 
was convicted, prison officials eventually recommended that 
inmate Lile participate in the SATP. He refused, because of

suit, obtaining injunctive relief. Otherwise, the consequences 
of inmate Lile’s refusal to participate would have been (under 
the IMPP) a reduction from Level III status to Level I, with a 
corresponding reduction in privileges. He also would have

3. The District Court Proceedings. The District Court 
initially granted a temporary injunction to inmate Lile, J.A. 
172-79, precluding the KDOC from reducing his privilege 
incentive level and keeping him on the SATP waiting list. The 
District Court ultimately granted summary judgment to inmate 
Lile on his Fifth Amendment claim. Petition Appendix (“Pet. 
App.”) 33a-54a. The District Court further ordered the 
injunction continued until appellate review is completed. Jd. 
at 55a. 


4. The Tenth Circuit's Decision. The Tenth Circuit 
affirmed the District Court. Pet. App. 3a-32a. The Tenth 
Circuit began its Fifth Amendment analysis by briefly 
addressing the incrimination component of the privilege. Jd. 
at 6a-7a. The Tenth Circuit found the incrimination element 
of the Fifth Amendment privilege satisfied here for two 
reasons. First, the written sexual history form might require 
persons such as inmate Lile to reveal uncharged sexual 
offenses. Jd. at 6a-7a. Second, completion of the Acceptance 
of Responsibility form could subject inmates such as Lile to 
perjury charges for having maintained their innocence at trial. 
Id. at 7a. 


The Tenth Circuit then addressed the “compulsion” element 
of the privilege. The court began with an examination of the 
adverse consequences to an inmate for refusing to accept 
responsibility or to provide a written sexual history. 
Observing that inmate Lile faced transfer to maximum security 
(from medium security) if he failed to comply with the SATP 
requirements, the court noted that such a “transfer would 
restrict [inmate Lile’s] privileges.” Pet. App. 10a. 


Specifically, he [inmate Lile] would lose his personal TV; 
he would have limited access to prison organizations and 
activities and would be unable to participate in the IMPP; 


8 


he would have limited access to the gym and yard area; 
there would be a substantial reduction in the amount of 
money he could spend in the canteen per pay period, from 
$140 per payroll . . . to $20 per payroll . . .; he would 
receive a reduction in pay and intake property; and he 
would have substantially restricted visitation privileges. 


Id. 


The Tenth Circuit, however, correctly recognized that Lile 
would not be denied parole nor lose any good time credits (or 
the opportunity to earn such credits) because he refused to 
participate in the SATP. Pet. App. 10a-13a. In other words, 
his sentence would be unchanged. The Tenth Circuit thus 
concluded that the only adverse consequences inmate Lile 
would suffer for refusing to participate in the SATP would be 
“transfer to maximum security prison and the resulting loss of 
privileges.” Jd. at 13a. According to the court, “[t]he critical 
question, then, is whether the State’s imposition of these 
consequences in response to [inmate Lile’s] invocation of his 
Fifth Amendment right against self-incrimination constitutes 
impermissible coercion.” /d. 


In resolving the compulsion issue, the Tenth Circuit opined 
that “(t]here is no single test or factor that is dispositive, so we 
begin by examining Defendants’ arguments.” Pet. App. 13a. 
The court first addressed the argument that there is no 
compulsion here because inmate Lile’s loss of privileges 
implicates no protected liberty interest under Sandin v. 
Conner, 515 U.S. 472 (1995). Although recognizing that 
changes in an inmate’s prison classification ordinarily do not 
deprive him of a liberty interest, the Tenth Circuit opined that 
“nowhere in the relevant jurisprudence does the Supreme 
Court even hint that an individual attempting to show a 
violation of his Fifth Amendment privilege must have a 
protected liberty interest for compulsion to occur.” Pet. App. 
14a. Rather, the court held that “[cJompulsion can be 
established by hardships that may not be atypical and that do 


9 


not constitute enforceable liberty interests.” /d. at 15a; see 
also id. at 17a (“It is clear that the compulsion element of a 
Fifth Amendment claim can be established by penalties which 
do not constitute a deprivation of protected liberty interests - 
under the Due Process Clause”). 


The Tenth Circuit then asserted that the “next critical 
question is whether the consequences imposed constitute 
penalties that are sufficiently potent and substantial to 
implicate the Fifth Amendment.” Pet. App. 17a-18a. 
Reviewing again the changes in privileges that would 
accompany a move from medium to maximum security 
(including loss of a personal TV), the Tenth Circuit concluded 
that “these deprivations and changes in environment are 
penalties sufficient to implicate the Fifth Amendment because 
they have substantial or potent effects on the inmate.” /d. at 
18a-19a. In reaching that conclusion, the court emphasized 
that a critical factor is that refusal to participate in the SATP, 
“standing alone,” results in the loss of privileges. /d. at 20a. 


The Tenth Circuit next considered the argument that there 
is no compulsion because participation in the SATP is 
voluntary. The court opined that the fact that the change in 
privileges in this case automatically followed refusal to 
participate in the SATP undermined the argument that 
participation “is truly voluntary.” Pet. App. 20a. In 
supporting that conclusion, the Tenth Circuit distinguished 
Ohio Adult Parole Authority v. Woodard, 523 U.S. 272 (1998) 
(inmate invoked privilege in clemency proceeding), and 
Minnesota v. Murphy, 465 U.S. 420 (1984) (person on 
probation claimed privilege)—both of which found no Fifth 
Amendment violation or compulsion—as cases in which the 
person was not “punished or penalized solely for choosing to 
remain silent.” Pet. App. 23a. 


The Tenth Circuit then tured to “[t]wo additional 
considerations” that it asserted might be helpful in determining 
what constitutes compulsion. Pet. App. 23a. “The first is a 


10 


distinction between a privilege and a penalty.” /d. Opining 
that such a distinction may have constitutional significance in 
this context, the Tenth Circuit without analysis concluded that 
inmate Lile’s loss of privileges in this case is “properly 
characterized as imposing penalties.” /d. at 25a. 


The Tenth Circuit also considered “the automaticity of the 
penalty.” Pet. App. 25a. Again, the court opined that 
Supreme Court decisions finding no compulsion in the context 
of probation, parole or clemency, all involved situations in 


- which adverse consequences for remaining silent were not 


“automatic.” Jd. at 25a-26a. Thus, the court concluded that 
“the State has violaved [inmate Lile’s] Fifth Amendment right 
against self-incrimination.” Jd. at 27a. 


Finally, after finding a constitutional violation, the Tenth 
Circuit applied the balancing test of Turner v. Safley, 482 U.S. 
78 (1987), which requires an assessment of “whether the 
prison policy at issue ‘is reasonably related to legitimate 
penological interests.” Pet. App. 27a (quoting Turner). The 
Tenth Circuit recognized that the state has a legitimate—-even 
compelling— interest in rehabilitating sex offenders, both for 
the offenders’ own benefit and for the public safety. /d. at 28a.

SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT 


There is no dispute in this case that the Kansas SATP is a 
legitimate, therapeutic treatment program. No evidence 
suggests that the Kansas SATP is a subterfuge for gathering 
incriminating evidence to support prosecution of inmates for 
previously uncharged sex offenses. Indeed, there is no 
evidence that any inmate has ever been charged with or 
prosecuted for offenses discussed or disclosed in the Kansas 


program. 


The Kansas SATP is, however, an important component of 
Kansas’s efforts to rehabilitate convicted sex offenders, both 
for their own benefit and for the protection of the public. 
Mental health professionals agree that acceptance of 
responsibility is a critical component of an effective treatment 
program. Thus, effective treatment is the primary justification 
for the Kansas SATP requirement, and that purpose is 
unrelated to any criminal investigative or prosecutorial 
purposes. 


It is common—and in fact essential—that prison 
administrators utilize incentives to rehabilitate and manage 
inmate populations. Changing or withholding prison 
privileges in which an inmate has no constitutionally protected 
liberty interest, or subjecting an inmate to prison conditions 
that are not atypical, generally cannot violate the Constitution. 
Sandin v. Conner, 515 U.S. 472 (1995). There is nothing 
arbitrary or irrational about Kansas prison admuinistrators 


12 


giving convicted sex offenders the choice of meaningful 
participation in a treatment program or the withholding of 
certain privileges. Thus, the choice presented to inmate Lile 
in this case does not amount to “compulsion” under the Fifth 
Amendment. 


This Court’s recent decisions suggest a bright-line rule for 
determining what amounts to unconstitutional “compulsion” 
in the prison context. Where, as here, (1) the inmate’s 
sentence has not been increased, (2) he has not been subjected 
to atypical prison conditions, and (3) prison officials have not 
acted arbitrarily or irrationally, the State cannot have crossed 
that constitutional line. Indeed, this Court has held that neither 
(1) the threat of a reduced opportunity for clemency, Ohio 
Adult Parole Auth. v. Woodard, 523 U.S. 272 (1998), (2) the 
threat of revocation of parole, Minnesota v. Murphy, 465 U.S. 
420 (1984), nor (3) the threat of adverse inferences being 
drawn at a prison disciplinary hearing, Baxter v. Palmigiano, 


425 US. 308 (1976), amount to _ unconstitutional 


“compulsion.” 

If there is no unconstitutional “compulsion,” then there is 
no reason the State must immunize all statements that inmates 
make while participating in the SATP. Nor, absent any Fifth 
Amendment violation, is there any reason to engage in a 
balancing of interests under Turner v. Safley, 482 U.S. 78 
(1987). Turner does, however, stand for the fundamental 
proposition that federal courts must pay considerable 
deference to state prison regulations, particularly those which 
serve rehabilitative and safety purposes. 


Moreover, the Turner balancing analysis here favors 
upholding the Kansas IMPP approach to an inmate’s refusal to 
accept responsibility in the SATP. The State has a legitimate 
interest in making sure that inmates engage in meaningful 
treatment programs. The State also has a legitimate interest in 
the potential prosecution and punishment of those who have 
committed unreported sexual offenses, especially those 


13 


involving child victims. Although Kansas has yet to charge or 
prosecute even a single inmate as a result of participation in 
the SATP, the Constitution should not force the State to 
choose between completely immunizing the participants in the 
SATP, or perhaps abandoning the SATP altogether.

I. There Is No Unconstitutional Compulsion In This 
Prison Case 


The Fifth Amendment provides that no person “shall be 
compelled in any criminal case to be a witness against 
himself.” U.S. Const. amend. V. “The [A]mendment speaks 
of compulsion,” United States v. Monia, 317 U.S. 424, 427 
(1943), and thus “t]he Court has held repeatedly that the Fifth 
Amendment is limited to prohibiting the use of ‘physical or 
moral compulsion’ exerted on the person asserting the 
privilege.” Fisher v. United States, 425 U.S. 391, 397 (1976). 
Indeed, “[t]he Constitution does not prohibit every element 
which influences a [person] to make incriminating admissions. 
* ** The constitutional guarantee is only that the witness be 
not compelled to give self-incriminating testimony.” United 
States v. Washington, 431 U.S. 181, 187-88 (1977) (emphasis 
added). 


As a result, the “criminal process, like the rest of the legal 
system, is replete with situations requiring ‘the making of 
difficult judgments’ as to which course to follow. Although a 
defendant may have a nght, even of constitutional dimensions, 
to follow whichever course he chooses, the Constitution does 
not by that token always forbid requiring him to choose.” 
McGautha v. California, 402 U.S. 183, 213 (1971) (citation 
omitted).°

There is no suggestion in this case that government officials

A. The Touchstone For Unconstitutional Compulsion 
In The Prison Setting Is The Imposition Of Atypical 
Hardships 


1. Atypical Hardships And “Compulsion.” \n Sandin v. 
Conner, 515 U.S. 472, 484 (1995), this Court held that prison 
officials could place a prisoner in disciplinary segregation for 
thirty days without providing procedural due process because 
such segregation did not impose an “atypical and significant 
hardship on the inmate in relation to the ordinary incidents of 
prison life.” See also Meachum v. Fano, 427 U.S. 215, 228 
(1976) (noting that absent some state-created nght, “prison 
officials have discretion to transfer [prisoners] for whatever 
reason or for no reason at all’). In other words, when an 
inmate has no constitutionally protected liberty interest in a 
particular housing level, the Constitution generally cannot be 
violated when that housing level is changed. 


Surprisingly, the Tenth Circuit in this case found 
compulsion in a reduction of privileges to which that court 
acknowledged inmate Lile had no constitutional or statutory 
right, and in which he had no protected liberty interest of any 
kind. See Pet. App. 13a-14a (“{A]n inmate’s prison 
classification ordinarily does not deprive him of liberty(] 
because he is not entitled to a particular degree of liberty in 
prison”) (quoting Templeman v. Gunter, 16 F.3d 367, 369 
(10th Cir. 1994)). In doing so, the Tenth Circuit opined that 
“nowhere in the relevant jurisprudence does the Supreme 
Court even hint that an individual attempting to show a 
violation of his Fifth Amendment privilege must have a 
protected liberty interest for compulsion to occur.” Pet. App. 
14a. Notably, however, the Tenth Circuit could not cite a 
single case from any court (other than the district court below)

finding compulsion in the prison setting absent the imposition 
of atypical hardships.‘ 


The Tenth Circuit pointed to this Court’s recent decision in 
Ohio Adult Parole Authority v. Woodard, 523 U.S. 272 
(1998), as an example of a Fifth Amendment “compulsion” 
case in the prison setting in which this Court did not apply an 
“atypical hardship” analysis. However, Woodard did not 
involve a challenge to prison regulations in the prison setting 
but, rather, a challenge to a clemency procedure that operated 
outside prison and which had nothing to do with the 
management of inmates. Moreover, in Woodard this Court 
stated that, “[i]n our opinion, the procedures of the Authority 
do not under any view violate the Fifth Amendment privilege.” 
Id. at 286 (emphasis added). Thus, the fact that this Court did 
not engage in an atypical hardships/liberty interest analysis in 
Woodard—when the claim did not involve prison regulations 
or a prison setting and this Court found no Fifth Amendment 
violation under any view of that provision—gives no support 
to the Tenth Circuit’s assertion that compulsion may be found 
in the prison setting absent the imposition of atypical 
hardships. 


The Tenth Circuit also pointed to other cases that it opined 
stand for the proposition that “Supreme Court precedent

2. The “Penalty” Analysis. Ultimately, the Tenth Circuit 
characterized the application of the IMPP regulation here as a 
penalty. Pet. App. 24a. But this was wrong. A long line of 
cases hold that the Fifth Amendment is not violated when a 
person’s assertion of the privilege prevents him from 
establishing his entitlement to a government benefit. See, e.g., 
Jenkins v. Anderson, 447 U.S. 231, 236 (1980) (“(T]he

But the question is whether he can at the same time take the

him and that even so the President must appoint him to a post 
of honor and trust. We have no hesitation in answering that 
question ‘No.’”). The withholding of prison privileges—to 
which an inmate has neither a statutory nor constitutional 
entitlement—does not amount to a “penalty” for constitutional 


purposes. 


The Tenth Circuit also tried to buttress its “Sandin analysis”

characterized as deprivations of protected liberty interests.”

without their critics: “Nothing in the historical development of 
the privilege suggests that threatened loss of employment was 
the kind of compulsion against which the amendment aimed 
to protect; the ‘compulsions’ of concern to the framers were 
torture, bullying and imprisonment for contempt.” Henry J. 
Friendly, The Fifth Amendment Tomorrow: The Case for 
Constitutional Change, 37 U. Cin. L. Rev. 671, 707 (1968); 
see also Leonard W. Levy, History and Judicial History: The

The Court should not extend the penalty cases—with their 
questionable historical basis, see also Part I.C. below—to 
justify a finding of compulsion in this prison setting. Rather, 
here constitutional “compulsion” should not be found absent 
the imposition of atypical hardships on an inmate. To the best 
of Kansas’s knowledge, the Court has never applied these 
“penalty” cases to find a Fifth Amendment violation in the 
prison setting, and it should not do so in this case. 


3. The Constitutional Rule. The fact that the Tenth Circuit 
could find compulsion in this case highlights the need for a 
bright line rule. The Court’s previous decisions suggest such 
a rule: when (1) an inmate’s sentence has not been increased, 
(2) he has not been subjected to atypical prison conditions, and 
(3) prison officials have not acted arbitrarily or irrationally 
toward the inmate, there cannot be unconstitutional 
“compulsion” for Fifth Amendment purposes. 


Prison administrators need such clear guidance in order to 
implement effective, incentive-based systems of rehabilitation. 
A rule allowing prison administrators to utilize reasonably the 
naturally occurring incidents of prison life to encourage 
socially constructive behavior and rehabilitate inmates is 
consistent with this Court’s precedents—and it is plain 
common sense. 


An inmate comes to prison with no right to any particular 
housing level or security classification. Meachum v. Fano, 
427 US. at 224 (“The initial decision to assign the convict to 
a particular institution is not subject to audit under the Due 
Process Clause, although the degree of confinement in one 
prison may be quite different from that in another.”’); Pet. App. 
13a-14a (““[AJn inmate’s prison classification ordinarily does 
not deprive him of liberty[ ] because he is not entitled to a 


- 


21 


79 


particular degree of liberty in prison’”’) (quoting Templeman v. 
Gunter, 16 F.3d 367, 369 (10th Cir. 1994)); Lile v. Simmons, 
929 P.2d 171, 173 (Kan. Ct. App. 1996) (“Kansas law does 
not create a constitutionally cognizable liberty interest in a 
prison inmate’s movement from a higher to a lesser security 
status”) (cited approvingly in Bankes v. Simmons, 963 P.2d 
412, 420 (Kan. 1998)). 


Prison administrators thus have constitutional leeway to 
grant more favorable housing and other privileges as a means 
of encouraging rehabilitation and preparing inmates for 
release. Indeed, it might be a dereliction of their duty not to do 
so. See Pell v. Procunier, 417 U.S. 817, 823 (1974) (“another 
paramount objective of the corrections system is the 
rehabilitation of those committed to its custody”). 


Likewise, prison administrators should be free to withhold 
privileges previously bestowed in order to implement a 
rehabilitative incentive plan, unless doing so deprives an 
inmate of some type of liberty interest. Hewitt v. Helms, 459 
U.S. 460, 468 (1983) (“It is plain that the transfer of an inmate 
to less amenable and more restrictive quarters for nonpunitive 
reasons is well within the terms of confinement ordinarily 
contemplated by a prison sentence”) (limited in Sandin v. 
Connor, 515 U.S. 472, 482 (1995), because its approach did 
not provide enough deference to prison officials). 


The changes in privileges that inmate Lile faces in this case 
serve nonpunitive purposes. The IMPP necessarily recognizes 
the fundamental prison management and rehabilitation 
proposition that an inmate who refuses to participate in 
recommended treatment should not continue to receive the 
benefits reserved for those who are actively working to rejoin 
society as productive citizens. Those who refuse to atone for 
their offenses by participating in required treatment in order to 
minimize the risk of reoffending are not being punished for 
invoking their Fifth Amendment privilege; they are only

experiencing the consequences of their deliberate choice to not

C. The Tenth Circuit’s Decision Is Contrary To The 
History And Origins Of The Fifth Amendment 


The Fifth Amendment provides that no person “shall be 
compelled in any criminal case to be a witness against 
himself.” U.S. Const. amend. V. In this case, the Tenth 
Circuit correctly noted that the amendment’s language is 
“somewhat narrow.” Pet. App. Sa. That court also opined that

doing, the Tenth Circuit ignored the limitation imposed by the 
very passage it quoted from Gau/t—that the privilege is to be 
“broadly applied and generously implemented in accordance 
with the teaching of the history of the privilege.” 387 U.S. at 
50 (emphasis added). Indeed, the Tenth Circuit engaged in no 
historical analysis in this case. 


History is particularly important in this context. “[T]he 
privilege against self-incrimination is a specific provision of 
which it is peculiarly true that ‘a page of history is worth a 
volume of logic.” Ullmann v. United States, 350 U.S. 422, 
438 (1956) (quoting N.Y. Trust Co. v. Eisner, 256 U.S. 345, 
349 (1921)); see also Leonard W. Levy, Origins of the Fifth 
Amendment: The Right Against Self-Incrimination xv (2d ed., 
Macmillan 1986) (1968) (“[T]he courts have assured us, again 
and again, that our understanding of the nght against 
compulsory self-incrimination is dependent upon history”). 


For purposes of this case, it is important to recognize that 
“the development of [the Fifth Amendment] protection was in 
part a response to certain historical practices, such as 
ecclesiastical inquisitions and the proceedings of the Star 
Chamber.” Andresen v. Maryland, 427 U.S. 463, 470 (1976).

When brought before the Star Chamber, accused individuals 
could either take an oath to answer all questions truthfully or 
be whipped. Mark Berger, Taking the Fifth: The Supreme

government “may not be coercion prove a charge against an 
accused out of his own mouth.” Malloy v. Hogan, 378 U.S. 1, 
7-8 (1964). And it is certainly true that the Court “has thought 
the privilege necessary to prevent any ‘recurrence of the 
Inquisition and the Star Chamber, even if not in their stark 
brutality.” Couch v. United States, 409 U.S. 322, 327 (1973) 
(quoting Ul/imann, 350 U.S. at 428). 


But, historically, the privilege protected individuals from 
government efforts to force them to assist in the investigation 
and prosecution of criminal charges against them; it did not 
forbid the denial of government benefits or privileges to those 
who chose to remain silent. In this prison case, the words of 
Justice Stewart remind of the narrower scope of consequences 
historically deemed “compuls‘on”: 


When a suspect was brought before the Court of High 
Commission or the Star Chamber, he was commanded to 
answer whatever was asked of him, and subjected to a

Griffin v. California, 380 U.S. 609, 620 (1965) (Stewart, J.,

B. The Tenth Circuit Incorrectly Relied On The 
Turner v. Safley, 482 U.S. 78 (1987), Balancing Test 


This Court has repeatedly emphasized that prison 
regulations are accorded a particular deference. See, e.g., 
Turner v. Safley, 482 U.S. 78 (1987); O'Lone v. Estate of 
Shabazz, 482 U.S. 342 (1987); Jones v. North Carolina 
Prisoners’ Labor Union, 433 U.S. 119 (1977). Thus, in 
Turner, the Court held that “when a prison regulation impinges 
on inmates’ constitutional nghts, the regulation is valid if it is 
reasonably related to legitimate penological interests. In our 
view, such a standard is necessary if ‘prison administrators . . . 
and not the courts, [are] to make the difficult judgments 
concerning institutional operations.” 482 U.S. at 89 (quoting 
Jones, 433 U.S. at 126). 


The Court in Turner set out four “factors that are relevant 
in determining the reasonableness of the regulation at issue”: 
(1) whether there is a valid, rational connection between the 
prison regulation or practice and a legitimate governmental 
interest; (2) whether the regulation or practice allows inmates 
an alternative means of exercising the subject constitutional 
right; (3) the impact of accommodation of the asserted nght on 
guards, other inmates, and the allocation of resources 
generally; and (4) the absence of ready alternatives to the 
regulation or practice. /d. at 89-91. 


The Tenth Circuit conceded that some deference was 
appropriate here, and it purported to accord deference via the 
Turner balancing test. Pet. App. 25a-32a. Unlike this Court 
in Sandin v. Conner, however, in which the deference to 
prison regulations resulted in finding no constitutional 
violation at all, the Tenth Circuit considered deference in this 
case only after concluding that a constitutional violation had 
occurred. Thus, the Tenth Circuit opined that its view of the 
Turner “balancing differs from Defendants’ argument that 
government interest is significant in determining whether the 
Fifth Amendment has been violated because we apply the 


36 


balancing analysis only after a constitutional violation has 
been found.” Pet. App. 26a n.12 (emphasis added). The Tenth 
Circuit then concluded that application of the Turner balancing 
test “easily” weighed in favor of inmate Lile. /d. at 32a. 


The Tenth Circuit’s application of the Turner balancing test 
in this case raises several questions: (1) Does Turner apply at 
all in Fifth Amendment cases? (2) If Turner applies, does the 
Court give deference to the Kansas prison regulations in the 
first instance in determining whether there is a constitutional 
violation at all, or only after finding a constitutional violation 
to determine whether the regulation is nonetheless 
permissible? (3) If Turner applies here, are all four factors 
appropriate to consider? The state addresses these questions 
in turn. 

1. Does Turner Apply To Fifth Amendment Claims? 
It may be inappropriate to apply the Turner factors in the Fifth 
Amendment context. Turner involved claims under the First 
Amendment and the constitutional right to marry. Lower 
courts, including the Tenth Circuit, have recognized that 
application of the Turner factors to other constitutional claims 
may be inappropriate or problematic. See, e.g., Michenfelder 
v. Sumner, 860 F.2d 328, 331 n.1 (9th Cir. 1988) (noting that 
the second Turner factor is “much more meaningful in the first 
amendment context than the fourth, where the right is to be 
free from a particular wrong”); Dunn v. White, 880 F.2d 1188, 
1194 n3 (10th Cir. 1989) (“The Turner test appears 
analytically to have only limited applicability to a prisoner’s 
fourth amendment challenge’’). 


Moreover, it does not appear that the Court has ever applied 
Turner to a case involving the Fifth Amendment. Rather, what 
Turner represents is the fundamental proposition that prison 
regulations that arguably impinge on constitutional rights that 
free citizens would enjoy are not necessarily unconstitutional 
in the prison context. Indeed, Turner essentially is an 
application of the general principle of deferential review in the 


37 


particular context of prison regulations affecting First 
Amendment interests. See, e.g., Foote v. Spiegel, 995 F. Supp. 
1347, 1349 n.2 (D. Utah 1998) (“[T]}his court determines that 
the Supreme Court’s decision in Washington |v. Harper, 494 
U.S. 210 (1990)] only commands use of the Turner ‘standard 
of review’ (i.e., reasonableness instead of strict scrutiny) when 
assessing prisoners’ or detainees’ constitutional claims; it does 
not command the use of the Turner factors themselves’’). 


Thus, all that Turner calls for in this context is that the 
regulation be reasonably related to the governmental interest 
at stake. Holding inmates accountable for their 
rehabilitation—which in the case of sex offenders demands 
full acceptance of responsibility—is reasonably related to the 
government’s interests in rehabilitation and safety. 


Or, to put it another way, the central and decisive inquiry in 
this case is whether—taking into account the prison setting 
and the purposes of the Kansas SATP—inmate Lile is being 


_ compelled to incriminate himself? Effectively, the deference 


to prison regulations that is inherent in Turner is subsumed in 
the “compulsion” inquiry, which must by necessity take 
careful account of the setting and context in which the Fifth 
Amendment claim arises. 


2. Does The Court Give Deference Before Or After 
Finding A Constitutional Violation? In neither Turner itself 
nor O'Lone v. Estate of Shabazz, did this Court separate the 
finding of a constitutional violation from the required 
deference in First Amendment cases. Subsequently, in Sandin 
v. Connor, 515 U.S. at 482-83, a procedural due process case, 
this Court once again highlighted the historical deference 
given to state prison officials, but did so completely 
independent of any reference to the Turner balancing test. 


Thus, had the Tenth Circuit properly accorded the Kansas

whether there is a constitutional violation at all, that court

participate in sex offender treatment], the district court and

deference to the decisions of prison administrators.” Jones, 
433 US. at 125. 


It seems illogical to say—as the Tenth Circuit essentially

constitutional rights but nonetheless may be valid. What 
Turner really stands for, and what this Court’s cases

necessarily have the same constitutional freedoms as free 
citizens. Prison regulations that are reasonably related to 
legitimate penological objectives generally do not violate the 
Constitution. Ultimately, as many lower courts have 
recognized, Turner adopts a deferential standard of review for 
inmates’ claims of constitutional violations.

Another frequent reality in the prison context is limited 
resources. Kansas does not have unlimited beds for 
incarcerated sex offenders to participate in the SATP. Instead, 
to some extent, Kansas prison administrators must prioritize 
and ration those opportunities. An inmate such as Lile should 
not be permitted to nonetheless drain those limited resources, 
by occupying a space in the program, to the detriment of other 
inmates who have demonstrated a willingness to engage in 
meaningful treatment on the program’s terms, rather than only 
on their own terms. 


The Court long has recognized the important role that 
acceptance of responsibility plays in rehabilitation efforts. 
Such acceptance demonstrates that an offender “is ready and 
willing to admit his crime and to enter the correctional system 
in a frame of mind that affords hope for success in 
rehabilitation over a shorter period of time than might 
otherwise be necessary.” Brady v. United States, 397 U.S. 
742, 753 (1970). 


There is nothing irrational or arbitrary about denying that 
opportunity to inmate Lile once he has made clear that he 
refuses to cooperate in obtaining meaningful treatment. Thus, 
he would not be punished for asserting his Fifth Amendment 
privilege; rather, he would be denied the continuation of
