Corrections (KDOC) employs a comprehensive system of 
earnable inmate privileges in order to manage the offender

part of this system, inmates work their way through privilege 
incentive levels in accordance with their behavior, at all times 
subject to reduction for disciplinary, work or recommended 
program infractions Inmate Lile, a convicted sex offender’, 
filed this suit after he was reduced to Level I (from Level III) 
for refusing to participate in his recommended treatment 
program, the Sexual Abuse Treatment Program (SATP), 
because it required him to accept responsibility for his sex 
crimes. Inmate Lile claims his privilege reduction to Level I for 
refusing to accept responsibility in sex offender treatment 
violates his Fifth Amendment privilege against  self- 
incrimination.

Also, the District Court ordered the injunction continued until

consequences inmate Lile would suffer for refusing to

According to the court, “[t}he critical question, then, Is 
whether the State’s imposition of these consequences in 
response to [inmate Lile’s] invocation of his Fifth Amendment 
right against self-incrimination constitutes impermissible

In resolving the compulsion issue, the Tenth Circuit 
opined that “[t]here is no single test or factor that is 
dispositive, so we begin by examining Defendants’ arguments. 
Pet. App. 13a. The court first addressed the argument that 
there is no compulsion here because inmate Lile’s loss of 
privileges implicates no protected liberty interest under Sandin 
v. Conner, 515 U.S. 472 (1995). Although recognizing that 
changes in an inmate’s prison classification ordinarily do not 
deprive him of a liberty interest, the Tenth Circuit opined that 
“nowhere in the relevant jurisprudence does the Supreme 
Court even hint that an individual attempting to show a 
violation of his Fifth Amendment privilege must have a 
protected liberty interest for compulsion to occur.” Pet. App. 
14a. Rather, the court held that “[c]ompulsion can be 
established by hardships that may not be atypical and that do 
not constitute enforceable liberty interests.” /d. at 15a, see 
also id. at 17a (“It is clear that the compulsion element of a 
Fifth Amendment claim can be established by penalties which 
do not constitute a deprivation of protected liberty interests 
under the due process clause’). 


The Tenth Circuit then asserted that the “next critical 
question is whether the consequences imposed constitute 
penalties that are sufficiently potent and substantial to implicate 
the Fifth Amendment.” Pet App. 17a. Reviewing again the 
changes in privileges that would accompany a move from 
maximum to medium security (including loss of a personal TV, 
see Pet. App. 17a), the Tenth Circuit concluded that “these 


5 


deprivations and changes in environment are penalties sufficient 
to implicate the Fifth Amendment because they have substantial 
or potent effects on the inmate.” /d. at 18a. In reaching that 
conclusion, the court emphasized that a critical factor is that 
refusal to participate in the SATP, “standing alone,” results in 
the loss of privileges. /d. at 19a. 


The Tenth Circuit next considered the argument that there 
is no compulsion because participation in the SATP is 
voluntary and changing inmate Lile’s privileges when he 
refused to participate is the withholding of a privilege rather 
than the imposition of punishment. The court opined that the 
fact that the change in privileges in this case automatically 
followed refusal to participate in the SATP undermined the 
argument that participation “is truly voluntary.” Pet. App. 20a 
In supporting that conclusion, the Tenth Circuit distinguished 
Ohio Adult Parole Authority v. Woodard, 523 U.S. 272 (1998) 
(inmate invoked privilege in clemency proceeding), and 
Minnesota v. Murphy, 465 U.S. 420 (1984) (person on 
probation claimed privilege)}—both of which found no Fifth 
Amendment violation or compulsion—as cases in which the 


person was not “punished or penalized so/e/y for choosing to 
remain silent.” Pet. App. 22a. 


The Tenth Circuit then turned to “[{t]wo additional 
considerations” that it opined might be helpful in determining 
what constitutes compulsion. Pet. App. 23a. “The first is a 
distinction between a privilege and a penalty.”  /d 
Acknowledging that such a distinction exists and that it may 
have constitutional significance in this context, the Tenth 
Circuit nonetheless concluded that inmate Lile’s loss of 
privileges in this case is “properly characterized as imposing 
penalties.” /d. at 24a. 


The Tenth Circuit also considered “the automaticity of the 
penalty.” Pet. App. 25a. Again, the court opined that 
Supreme Court decisions finding no compulsion in the context 
of probation, parole or clemency, all involved situations in 


6 


which adverse consequences for remaining silent were not 
“automatic.” /d. at 25a. Thus, the court concluded that “the 
State has violated [inmate Lile’s] Fifth Amendment right 
against self-incrimination.” /d. at 26a. 


Lastly, having found a constitutional violation, the Tenth 
Circuit applied the balancing test of 7urner v. Safley, 482 US. 
78 (1987), which requires an assessment of “whether the prison 
policy at issue ‘is reasonably related to legitimate penological 
interests.” Pet. App. 27a (quoting 7urner) The Tenth Circuit 
recuynized that the state has legitimate interests in the public 
safety and rehabilitating sex offenders. /d. at 28a. The court 
also recognized that 


[t]here is a rational connection between the policy of 
having prisoners admit responsibility for their offenses and 
disclose their sexual history in order to participate in the 
SATP and the legitimate governmental interest in 
rehabilitation of sex offenders. In fact, most mental health 
experts agree that a “sex offender must admit his guilt for 
treatment and rehabilitation to be successful.” 


Id. at 28a (internal citations omitted). 


Nonetheless, the Tenth Circuit concluded that the 
balancing of interests favors inmate Lile, because (1) “there is 
no evidence that [inmate Lile] has any alternative means of 
exercising his Fifth Amendment right,” Pet. App. 29a, (2) 
“there is no evidence that accommodation of the right would 
have any negative effect on guards, other prisoners, or prison 
resources,” id., and (3) the alternative to the policy would be 
to grant immunity or privilege any statements disclosed by the 
inmate. /d at 30a. Asserting that the state could still 
prosecute inmate Lile for other sex crimes—tt just could not 
use any statements he made in the SATP—the Tenth Circuit 
concluded that “the 7urner balancing easily weighs in favor of 
{inmate Lile].” /d. at 32a.

REASONS FOR GRANTING THE WRIT 


The issue whether a State may withdraw privileges from 
a convicted sex offender for refusing to participate in a 
treatment program and accept responsibility for his crimes is 
one this Court decided to review previously, but the procedural 
posture of that earlier case ultimately prevented the Court from 
reaching the merits. See Montana v. Imlay, 506 U.S. 5 (1992) 
(dismissing the case because the offender would serve the same 
term of imprisonment regardless of the Court’s resolution of 


the merits). Justice White, in his dissent from the dismissal, 
noted that: 


In the decision below, the Montana Supreme Court held 
that, “absent any grant of immunity” from prosecution for 
incriminating statements made during therapy, the Fifth 
Amendment “prohibit[s] augmenting a defendant’s 
sentence because he refuses to confess to a crime or 
invokes his privilege against self-incrimination”” The 
constitutional question is an important one and the 


decision below places the Montana Supreme Court in 
conflict with other courts. 


Imlay, 506 U.S. at 6. 


The Court should take this opportunity to address the 
important constitutional question left unanswered in /mlay. 
The issue is cleanly presented in this case, there is no danger of 
mootness, the Tenth Circuit addresses the substantive issue at 
length in its opinion, and the Tenth Circuit’s resolution of the 
issue is inconsistent with both this Court’s and other courts’ 
decisions. Indeed, this Court’s decisions since it dismissed 
Moniana v. Imlay almost ten years ago have strengthened the 


State’s argument that the policy at issue here does not violate 
the Fifth Amendment.

Whether Unconstitutional Compulsion May Result 
From-Prison Conditions That Are Not Atypical Is An 
Important Issue That Merits This Court’s Attention. 


A. The Tenth Circuit’s Decision That 
Unconstitutional Compulsion May Result From 
Prison Conditions That Are Not Atypical Is 
Contrary To This Court’s Recent Decisions. 


In Sandin v. Conner, 515 US. 472 (1995), this Court 
recognized that “prisoners do not shed all constitutional rights 
at the prison gate, but ‘[l]awful incarceration brings about the 
necessary withdrawal or limitation of many privileges and 
rights, a retraction justified by the considerations underlying 
our penal system.” /d. at 485 (internal citation omitted) 
(quoting Jones v. N. C. Prisoners’ Labor Union, Inc. , 433 US. 
119, 125 (1977)). Consequently, this Court held that prison 
officials could place a prisoner in disciplinary segregation for 
thirty days without providing procedural due process because 
such segregation did not impose an “atypical and significant 
hardship on the inmate in relation to the ordinary incidents of 
prison life.” /d. at 484. In other words, when an inmate has no 
constitutionally protected right to, or liberty interest in, a 
particular housing level, his constitutional nghts cannot be 
violated when that housing level is changed. 


Reducing prison privileges—in which an inmate has no 
constitutionally protected liberty interest—for failing to accept 
responsibility in treatment is not compulsion under the Fifth 
Amendment. This Court has held that neither (1) the threat of 
a reduced opportunity for clemency, Ohio Adult Parole 
Authority v. Woodard, 523 U.S. 272 (1998), (2) the threat of 
revocation of parole, Minnesota v. Murphy, 465 U.S. 420 
(1984), nor (3) the threat of adverse inferences being drawn at 
a prison disciplinary hearing, Baxter v. Palmigiano, 425 U.S. 
308 (1976), constitute compulsion under the Fifth Amendment. 
Nonetheless, the Tenth Circuit held in this case that the threat 


9 


of a reduction in institutional privileges constitutes compulsion 
under the Fifth Amendment. 


In Ohio Adult Parole Authority v. Woodard, 523 U.S. 272 
(1998), this Court held that a death row inmate could be forced 
to choose between incriminating himself or having adverse 
inferences drawn from his silence at his clemency interview. 
Despite the fact that “[i]n Woodard, plaintiff did not face a 
possible loss of privileges and benefits, but rather a possible 
loss of life,” Searcy v. Simmons, 97 F. Supp. 2d 1055, 1059 
(D. Kan. 2000), the Tenth Circuit distinguished Woodard 
opining that “Mr. Woodard was not being punished for 
invoking the Fifth Amendment privilege.” Pet. App. 20a-21a. 


In Minnesota v. Murphy, 465 U.S. 420 (1984), this Court 
held that the Fifth Amendment was not violated when a parolee 
was questioned by his parole officer about prior criminal 
activity after having been told that failure to be truthful could 
result in revocation of parole. In Lile, the Tenth Circuit relied 
on a footnote in Murphy in which this Court opined that “a 
state may validly insist on answers to even incriminating 
questions . . . as long as it recognizes that the required answers 
may not be used in a criminal proceeding.” /d. at 435 n.7. 
However, Justice White, author of Murphy, later declared that 
any “assumption that this Court’s decision in Minnesota v. 
Murphy mandates . . . immunity from prosecution based upon 
incriminating statements made to a therapist... reflect a 
possible misunderstanding of [a State’s] obligations under the 
law.” Montana v. Imlay, 506 U.S. 5, 8 (1992) (White, J, 
dissenting from dismissal of writ of certiorari as improvidently 
granted). Cf McMorrow v. Little, 109 F.3d 432, 436 (8th Cir. 
1997) (“[Minnesota v. Murphy] does not, however, overrule 
the Court’s earlier cases that state that the government can 
penalize someone for refusing to cooperate on government 
matters, even when the person does so by invoking his 
privilege against self-incrimination”) (citing Lefkowitz v. 
Cunningham, 431 U.S. 801, 806 (1977); Lefkowitz v. Turley, 


10 


414 US. 70, 84 (1973), Uniformed Sanitation Men Ass'n Vv. 
Comm'r of Sanitation, 392 U.S. 280, 284 (1968), Gardner v. 
Broderick, 392 US. 273, 278-79 (1968), Baxter v. 
Palmigiano, 425 U.S. 308, 320 (1976)). 


Finally, in Baxter v. Palmigiano, 425 U.S. 308 (1976), the 
Court held that negative inferences could be drawn from an 
inmate’s silence in a prison disciplinary hearing without 
violating the Fifth Amendment, in part because “[d]isciplinary 
proceedings in state prisons... involve the correctional 
process and important state interests other than conviction for 
crime.” /d at 319. The Tenth Circuit, however, distinguished 
Baxter, opining that “[hJere, unlike in Baxter, Plaintiff's refusal 
to submit to the interrogation .. . standing alone, would 
certainly result in the [adverse consequences].” Pet. App. 19a. 
In Baxter the Court declared that 


[i]t is thus undisputed that an inmate’s silence in and of 
itself is insufficient to support an adverse decision by the 
Disciplinary Board. In this respect, this case is very 
different from the circumstances before the Court in the 
Garrity-Lefkowitz decisions, where refusal to submit to 
interrogation and to waive Fifth Amendment privilege, 
standing alone and without regard to the other evidence, 
resulted in loss of employment or opportunity to contract 
with the State 


Baxter, at 317-18 (citing Garrity v. New Jersey, 385 U.S. 493 
(1967) (holding that police officers questioned concerning 
alleged fixing of traffic tickets in their boroughs could not be 
threatened with loss of employment for refusing to answer), 
Lefkowitz v. Turley, 414 U.S. 70 (1973) (holding that statute 
precluding award of government contract to any person 
refusing to answer questions concerning state contract violated 
the Fifth Amendment)). 


The Tenth Circuit’s analysis ignores the fact that it is not 
inmate Lile’s silence alone but rather his conviction of a sexual 


offense, plus the recommendation for treatment, plus his refusal 
to participate in that treatment which results in adverse 
consequences Seen in this light, this case is much more similar 
to Baxter than to either Garrity or Lefkowitz See Asherman 
v. Meachum, 957 F 2d 978, 982 (2nd Cir 1992) (“The fact 
that a public employee might face the unpleasant choice of 
surrendering his silence or losing his job is no bar to an adverse 
consequence so long as the consequence is imposed for failure 
to answer a relevant inquiry and not for refusal to give up a 
constitutional right”) (citing Gardner v. Broderick, 392 U § 

273, 278-79 (1968), Uniformed Sanitation Men Ass'n v. 

Comm 'r of Samiation, 392 U.S 280, 284 (1968)), A4cMorrow 
v. Little, 109 F 3d 432, 436 (8th Cir 1997) (“{P}rison officials 
may constitutionally deny benefits to a prisoner who, by 
invoking his privilege against self-incrimination, refuses to 
make statements necessary for his rehabilitation, as long as 
their denial is based on the prisoner's refusal to participate in 
his rehabilitation and not his invocation of his privilege”)

C. The Tenth Circuit’s Decision Provides This Court 
With An Opportunity To Consider Whether The 
Turner v. Safley, 482 U.S. 78 (1987), Balancing 
Test Applies In The Fifth Amendment Context. 


In Turner v. Safley, 482 U.S. 78 (1987), the Court held 
that “when a prison regulation impinges on inmates’ 
constitutional rights, the regulation is valid if it is reasonably 
related to legitimate penological interests.” /d at 89. The 
Court then set out the following “factors that are relevant in 
determining the reasonableness of the regulation at issue”. (1) 
whether there is a valid, rational connection between the prison 
regulation or practice and a legitimate governmental interest, 
(2) whether the regulation or practice allows inmates an 
alternative means of exercising the subject constitutional right, 
(3) the impact of accommodation of the asserted right on 
guards, other inmates, and the allocation of resources 
generally, and (4) the absence «* ready alternatives to the 
regulation or practice /d at 89-9] 


After finding a Fifth Amendment violation in this case, the 
Tenth Circuit applied the 7urner balancing test and found that 
it “easily” weighed in favor of inmate Lile Pet App. 32a

relation to the ordinary incidents of prison life.” Sandin v. 
Conner, 515 U.S. 472, 484 (1995). 


In Sandin, the Supreme Court addressed liberty interests 
in prison in the context of a procedural due process claim The

applicable prison regulation, a constitutionally protected liberty

which ... imposes atypical and significant hardship on the 
inmate in relation to the ordinary incidents of prison life.” /d. 
at 484 (citations omitted). Applying this test; the Court held 
that being placed in disciplinary segregation for thirty days did 
not impose an atypical and significant hardship, and therefore 
the inmate had no protected liberty interest which required due 
process. See id. at 486. Relying on earlier Supreme Court 
precedent, Meachum v. Fano, 427 U.S. 215, 225 (1976), this 
court recognized that, under the Due Process Clause, a change

him of liberty[ ] because he is not entitled to a particular degree

d. Other Analytical Factors 


Two additional considerations may generally be helpful in 
determining whether state action constitutes impermissible 
compulsion. The first is a distinction drawn between a 
privilege and a penalty. Defendants argue that a transfer from 
medium to maximum security housing and the attendant loss of 
privileges for refusing to participate in a prisoner treatment 
program do not constitute a penalty or punishment. Rather, 
these consequences are merely part of the Department's system 
of privileges and incentives utilized to encourage inmates to 
participate in programs. In other words, the State argues that 
punishment only occurs in response to a wrongful act taken by 
a prisoner. 


This circuit has held that the characterization of state 
action as either withdrawing a privilege or imposing a penalty 
is indicative of whether the action constitutes compulsion. In 
United States v. Rogers, 921 F.2d 975, 982 (10th Cir 1995), 
the court held that “the denial of a downward adjustment under 
§ 3E1.1 does not constitute a penalty or an enhancement of 
sentence” for purpose of the Fifth Amendment. In reaching 
this conclusion, we explained: 


There is a difference between increasing the severity of a 
sentence for failure to demonstrate remorse and refusing 
to grant a reduction from the prescribed offense level. “It 
is one thing to extend leniency to a defendant who is 
willing to cooperate with the government, it is quite 
another thing to administer additional punishment to a 
defendant who by his silence has committed no additional 
offense.’” 


Id. at 982-83 (citations omitted). 


We do not believe that the distinction between a penalty 
and a privilege should be used as an absolute test for 
compulsion, but we believe that the distinction may be helpful 
in determining whether the government has sought to compel

Fifth Amendment 


Fifth Amendment, applicable to the states through the

US Const. Art V. The Amendment “must be accorded liberal

answers Minnesota v. Murphy, 465 U.S. 420, 426 (1984)

The privilege should be affirmatively and timely asserted 
Murphy, 465 US. at 429. A state does not abridge the nght

431 US. 181, 186 (1977).

The Fourth Amendment protects individuals against 
unreasonable searches and seizures. US. Constitution, Art 
IV. Where personal and deeply rooted privacy concerns are 
implicated, the Fourth Amendment “guarantees the privacy, 
dignity and security of persons against certain arbitrary and 
invasive acts by officers of the Government or those acting at 
their direction.” Skinner v. Railway Labor Executives’ Ass'n, 
489 US 602, 613-14 (1989) (citations omitted) It protects 
against intrusions not justified by the circumstances, or 
conducted in an improper manner Schmerber v. Califorma, 
384 U.S. 757, 767 (1966). 


Although convicted prisoners “do not forfeit all 
constitutional protections by reason of their conviction and 
confinement in prison,” Bell v. Wolfish, 441 US 520, 545 
(1979), the Supreme Court has recognized that incarceration 
necessarily limits the privileges and nights of convicted 
prisoners, Pell v. Procunier, 417 US 817 (1974) It is 
established, for instance, that prisoners have no legitimate 
expectation of privacy in their cells, and that absent substantial 
evidence in the record that officials have exaggerated their 
response, a prisoner's expectation of such privacy must yield 
to institutional security concerns. Wolfish, 441 U.S at 547-48, 
Hudson v. Palmer, 468 U.S. 517 (1984) 


A search of a prisoner's body, however, is a qualitatively 
different matter Dunn v. White, 880 F.2d 1188, 1190 (10th 
Cir. 1989), cert. denied, 493 US. 1059 (1990) A prisoner 
retains a privacy interest in the integrity of his own person.

F 3d 1063, 1068 (10th Cir. 1993) (inmate has a constitutional

prison guards). 


A State's acquisition and examination of evidence may be 
a search under the Fourth Amendment if such governmental 
action “infringes an expectation of privacy that society 1s 
prepared to recognize as reasonable.” Skinner, 489 US at 
616 Accordingly, the collection of revealing private 
physiological data has been found to be an invasion of privacy 
interests. /d. at 617 


Whether the government is pursuing civil or criminal 
objectives, such intrusion into constitutionally protected 
privacy rights requires analysis of the reasonableness of the 
search under the Fourth Amendment. “[T]he permissibility of 
a particular practice is judged by balancing its intrusion on the 
individual's Fourth Amendment interests against its promotion 
of legitimate governmental interests.” Skinner, 489 US. at 
619 (quotation and citations omitted). See e.g., Dunn, 880 
F 2d at 1193-94 (prison officials to balance intrusiveness of 
testing prisoner’s blood for AIDS against the need for such 
testing) The reasonableness of the intrusion requires the 
examination of the nature of the search and of all the 
surrounding circumstances. Skinner, 489 US at619 Where 
privacy expectations are reduced and the intrusion of privacy 
interests is minimal, the Fourth Amendment demands less 
governmental justification than where a heightened privacy 
interest is involved. Winston v. Lee, 470 U.S. 753, 766 (1985), 
Harrington v. Almy, 977 F.2d 37, 44 (ist Cir. 1992)
