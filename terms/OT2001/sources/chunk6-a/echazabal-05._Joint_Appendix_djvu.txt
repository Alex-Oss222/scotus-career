but that said offer was contingent upon Plaintiff completing a 
pre-employment physical examination. 


14. On or about January 2, 1996, Plaintiff underwent the pre- 
employment physical examination required by Chevron. By 
letter dated February 6, 1996, Chevron advised Plaintiff that 
they had determined that Plaintiff's disability 
prevented/precluded Plaintiff from performing the tasks of the 
position of Plant Helper, and accordingly, Chevron was 
withdrawing its offer of employment. That same letter also 
advised Plaintiff that Chevron would keep the results of that 
pre-employment physical examination confidential. Thereafter, 
by letter dated February 21, 1996, Chevron advised Irwin that 
they had determined that Plaintiff's disability 
prevented/precluded Plaintiff from working anywhere within 
the Plant, and accordingly, Chevron requested/instructed Irwin 
to remove Plaintiff from performing any job within the Plant. 


15. On or about February 28, 1996, Plaintiff was advised by 
Irwin that both Irwin and Chevron had determined that Plaintiff 
would no longer be permitted to perform any job at the Plant 
because of his disability even though Plaintiff was fully capable 
of performing the position of Plant Helper as well as other 
positions/jobs within the Plant without injury to himself or 
others. 


16. On or about February 28, 1996, Plaintiff met with 
Irwin’s director of human resources, Manfred F. Kiesser, who 
affirmed that Plaintiff would no longer be permitted to perform 
any work at the Plant.

informed Irwin, who in turn informed Plaintiff, that Plaintiff 
would no longer be entitled to perform any work at the Plant or

DECLARATION OF RON DIPADUA 
I, Ron Di Padua, declare: 


1. Iam employed at the Chevron El Segundo Refinery as 
a Shift Supervisor in the Coker/Sulfur Acid Division, also 
referred to as the coker unit. During my shift, I am responsible 
for the day-to-day activities in my unit including supervising 
the personnel who work there. 


2. The plant helper hired for the coker unit in January 1996 
would be working under my supervision if assigned to my 
crew, or under the supervision of another shift supervisor if the 
was assigned to another crew. 


3. One function of the coker unit is to thermally crack 
residium and extract distillates from it. The coker unit has three 


47 


furnaces and six coke drums. They are involved in a batch 
process with three drums typically being filled while the other 
three are in the recovery/cooling process for preparation of 
removal. The coke drums are large, permanently installed 
pressure vessels (approximately 27 feet in diameter and 97 feet 
tall). Residium is heated to between 550 degrees F to 915 
degrees F and pumped into the coke drums. Under pressure and 
heat the residium is fractionized. Distillates are separated out 
and taken away. Corrosion inhibitors are added in the 
distillation section. This includes Nalco 5163 Corrosion 
Inhibitor and Nalco 5186 Corrosion Inhibitor. Nalco 5743 
process Antifoam is injected into the coke drums while they are 
feeding towards the end of the feeding cycle. Hydrocarbons 
distilled from the coke include thermocracked naptha heavy, 
naptha light, and naptha sour. Sour water and other recyclable 

oils are also byproducts of the process. ' 


4. One of our goals is to minimize leakage to the 
environment. But some weepage and escape of these chemicals 
and/or hydrocarbons occurs, in particular at flanges, block 
valves and flow valves. Air monitors regularly check for leaks. 
When a small leak is suspected the operators use Snoop Liquid 
Leak Detector to locate leaks. Plant helpers are not directly 
involved in these operations. But they are working in and 
around the area where these chemicals are stored and used and 
are occasionally exposed to leakage. There is also a risk of a 
major event, e.g., major leak, fire or explosion. These occur 
only very infrequently but can result in a plant helper in the 
coker unit having major exposure to the chemicals and products 
used and produced in these operations. 


5. Once the thermocracking and distillation process in a 
particular coke drum has run its course, the next steps are to 
cool it, open the drum and empty the coke so the process can 
resume. Cooling involves flushing the still sealed coke drum 
with water. The water is drained and, as a result of having 
mixed with the coke and petroleum product in the drum, 


48 


contains contaminants such as manganese. While the coke 
drum is draining, plant helpers are directly involved in 
removing the heads, top and bottom, from the coke drums. This 
includes using an impact gun to remove bolts fastening the 
heads. They commonly may need to use products called Rust 
Buster or Master Mechanic (like a heavier duty version of 
WD40) to spray onto the bolts to loosen stuck bolts on the 
heads. As the heads are being removed and continuing 
thereafter, the plant helpers are exposed to steam coming off 
the petroleum coke containing 10-12% VCM (volatile 
combustible matter). The steam contains small amount of 
contaminants which it picked up from the coke. One of the 
plant helpers (called the driller) then uses a high pressure water 
spray (approximately 2,500 Ibs/sq inch) to actually cut the coke 
that is in the now opened coke drum. The driller drills or cuts 
through it so that it falls out of the drum and into an open coke 
pit. Sometimes the plant helper hits a “hot spot” and steam and 
coke fines blow back up to the top deck where he is working. 
Mixed in with the coke is also small amounts of lead, iron, 
manganese, selenium and other substances. Plant helpers also 
clean the heads, inlet lines, deck and associated equipment. The 
plant helper will also grease valves and start resealing the drum 
heads. This activity commonly requires th. plant helper to use 
“plumbers dope,” and various greases and oils to make it easier 
to thread flanges and bolts. A plant helper will commonly mix 
plumber’s dope with SS Jet Lube or GS68 oil and apply this to 
flanges and bolts. 


6. Plant helpers are also involved in using a crane to lift 
coke out of the coke pit and put it on a conveyor belt which 
carries the coke to another area for loading onto trucks to be 
taken away from the refinery. A plant helper is typically 
assigned to monitor the conveyor belt and clean up when coke 
falls off the belt. Once the coke is cut out of the drums, it is 
open to the environment and plant helpers are exposed to coke 
dust. 


49 


7. When open-topped trucks are used to transport the coke, 
a solution including Corexit is automatically sprayed onto the 
load to suppress dust while the coke is being trucked. 


8. From time to time we also halt processing operations and 
have a shutdown to allow maintenance or modifications to 
occur. During shutdowns there is typically a lot of welding 
going on in the unit. A plant helper working in the coke unit 
would necessarily be exposed to welding fumes when he is 
working nearby or downwind. 


9. Plant helpers also use GS220 oil on gear boxes and 
winch motors, GS68 oil on joy motors, Quindlubic 822 oil for 
operating chutes, actuators and rams, and Duralith NLG1 #2 
Grease for primary crusher and rollers on the belts as well as 
for greasing the crane bucket. 


10. There is no way to perform the functions of a plant

who had normal liver function and was performing the duties 
of a plant helper.

I. INTRODUCTION AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND 


Defendants Chevron and Irwin’s separate motions for 
summary judgment came on regularly for hearing on December 
29, 1997. Having carefully considered the papers submitted and 
oral argument of counsel, the Court hereby GRANTS 
Chevron’s motion for summary judgment, DENIES Irwin’s 
motion for summary judgment, and GRANTS Irwin’s motion 
for summary adjudication as to punitive damage claims. 


This case arises from plaintiff Mario Echazabal’s 
(“Echazabal”) layoff from his position with Irwin Industries, 
Inc. (“Irwin”), a maintenance contractor for Chevron U.S.A.., 
Inc. (“Chevron”), following Chevron’s withdrawal of a job 
offer to Echazabal, allegedly on the basis of Echazabal’s 
condition of chronic active hepatitis. 

Echazabal filed a First Amended Complaint (“1 AC”) against 
defendants Chevron and Irwin in Los Angeles Superior Court 
on April 8, 1997. The Notice of Removal was filed May 8, 
1997. Plaintiff alleges the following causes of action against 
defendant Chevron only, except as noted: (1) violation of 
FEHA for disability discrimination, against Chevron and Irwin;

iolation of ADA (42 U.S.C. §§ 12101), against Chevron 
a (3) borer of 29 U.S.C. §794; (4) violation of 
plaintiff ’s right to privacy under Cal. Const. Art. I; (5) violation 
of the Confidentiality of Medical Information Act, Cal. Civ. 
Code § 56; and (6) intentional interference with contractual 
relations. The 1AC seeks punitive damages for all causes of 
action. (IAC 22:1-11.)

(Echazabal Decl. 92, 4, 17; DiPadua Depo. 131-174; Saner

On December 28, 1995, Chevron offered Echazabal a plant

On January 2, 1996, Echazabal’s pre-employment physical

Echazabal suffers from chronic hepatitis C, a viral infection 
characterized by ongoing liver inflammation. (Fedoruk Decl. 
45.) Dr. McGill reviewed Echazabal’s medical records, 
including laboratory results from blood samples taken eight 
times between October, 1992 and January, 1996. Each of the 
blood tests showed elevated levels of liver function enzymes. 
(Chevron’s 6.) It is disputed whether plaintiff's liver was in 
fact functioning properly, despite these elevated enzyme levels. 
(Fedoruk Decl. 48, Gitn:ck Decl. 47-8.) It is disputed whether 
such elevated enzyme levels reflect ongoing liver cell damage 
(McGill Decl. $1 1-12; Tang Depo. 54:21-55:8), or are simply 
evidence of liver inflammation and leaking of enzymes into the 
bloodstream, compatible with normal liver function (Fedoruk 
Decl. 48, Gitnick Decl. 47,9). Echazabal’s enzyme levels had 
not improved despite years of treatment with interferon, the 
standard treatment for hepatitis C. (McGill Decl. 7, 9, 13.) 
Plaintiff has also presented evidence that although he has 
chronic active hepatitis C, he has no symptoms of liver failure, 
and there are no clinical findings on physical examination 
which demonstrate impairment of liver function. Plaintiff has 
presented evidence that blood tests which assess the ability of 
his liver to maintain normal body functions, including tests 
which measure albumin and prothrombin production by the 
liver, have been normal overall. (Fedoruk Decl. 45-8; Gitnick

Ill. SUMMARY JUDGMENT STANDARD 


Summary judgment shall be granted if the evidence 
supporting the motion for summary judgment shows that “there 
is no genuine issue as to any material fact and that the moving 
party is entitled to a judgment as a matter of law.” Fed. R. Civ. 
P. 56(c). A party moving for summary judgment may carry its 
initial burden by pointing out to the district court that there is 
an absence of a genuine issue of material fact. Celotex Corp. v. 
Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 323 (1986). “The plain language of Rule 
56(c) mandates the entry of summary judgment, after adequate 
time for discovery and upon motion, against a party who fails 


181 


to make a showing sufficient to establish the existence of an 
element essential to that party’s case, and on which that party 
will bear the burden of proof at trial.” Celotex, 477 U.S. at 322. 


To avoid summary judgment, the non-movant must set forth 
specific facts showing that there remains a genuine issue of 
material fact for trial. Celotex, 477 U.S. at 324. The non- 
movant “may not rest upon the mere allegations or denials of 
the adverse party’s pleading.” A factual dispute is “genuine” 
if a reasonable jury could return a verdict for the nonmoving 
party. Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. at 242, 248 
(1986). The evidence of the non-movant is to be believed, and 
all justifiable inferences are to be drawn in the non-movant’s 
favor. /d. at 255. If the nonmoving party’s evidence is merely 
colorable or is not significantly probative, then summary 
judgment may be granted. /d. at 249-50.

1. Applicable Legal Standards

§12940. Plaintiff's Second and Third Causes of Action allege

The substantive standards for determining liability under the 
ADA and the Rehabilitation Act are the same. McDonald v. PA 
Dept. of Public Welfare, 62 F.3d 92 (3d Cir. 1995).° 


The Americans with Disabilities Act (“ADA”), 42 U.S.C. 
§12112, provides, in relevant part, that “(a) No covered entity 
shall discriminate against a qualified individual with a 
disability because of the disability of such individual in regard 
to. . . hiring.” Similarly, the Rehabilitation Act, 29 U.S.C. 
§794, provides, in relevant part, that “No otherwise qualified 
individual with a disability in the United States . . . shall, solely 
by reason of his or her disability, be excluded from the 
participation in, or denied the benefits of, or be subjected to 
discrimination under any program or activity receiving Federal 
financial assistance.”

F.3d 1261 (4th Cir. 1995), the Fourth Circuit set out the 
elements of an ADA claim, which are also applicable to the 
Rehabilitation Act:

The caselaw holds that an employer who refuses to employ 
an individual on the basis of medical information which turns 
out not to be completely accurate may nevertheless avoid 
liability. “A[n] employer is not required to be unfailingly 
correct in assessing a person’s qualifications for a job. . .

reasonable.” Cook v. State of RI. Dept. of MHRH, 10 F.3d 17 
(Ist Cir. 1993). In Bento, supra, the employer participated in 
the plaintiff's referral to two different doctors at two different 
times, neither of whom had provided an unqualified 
endorsement of the plaintiff's physical ability to do the work 
without danger to himself. Bento was a longshoreman, whose 
medical clearance specified that his pulse rate not rise above 
120 beats per minute. The Court held, “Whether or not wrong 
as a matter of medical fact, the defendant’s decision was a 
reasonable one, based on what, in the exercise of due diligence, 
it had learned.” Bento at 745. In Walker v. Attorney General, 
572 F.Supp. 100 (D.D.C. 1983), an FBI employee with angina 
was discharged after the FBI concluded that this medical 
condition would prevent him from performing his job. In 
Walker, contemporaneous medical information, available but 
not communicated to the FBI at the time the decision was 
made, indicated that Walker’s condition was not as severe as 


Walker’s own physician had represented. Nevertheless, the 
Court held,

owed plaintiff a duty to inquire further into his medical 
condition and to uncover the fact that the diagnosis of both 
plaintiff's cardiologist and the National Naval Medical 
Center was in error. . . . [T]he FBI was reasonable in 
concluding the plaintiff was not qualified for his position. 
Whatever duty the agency may have had to inquire into the 


188 


accuracy of plaintiff's doctor’s diagnosis (and the Court

more than adequately complied with by referring the

decision to fire plaintiff was therefore justified, and cannot 
be challenged under the Rehabilitation Act. Of course, had

the employer cannot be accused of discrimination against an 
‘otherwise qualified’ employee when it chooses to fire him. 


Walker, supra at 102-3. On this basis, the Court granted

Kiesser’s communications with Dr. Tang. (Kiesser Depo. 
115:14-20 and passim.) As required by Rizzo, supra, Irwin 
engaged in an “individualized assessment” of Echazabal, 
“based on a reasonable medical judgment that relies on. . . the 
best available objective evidence.” Rizzo, supra at 763. The 
evaluation provided by Tang indicated that the danger to 
Echazabal was immediate, as required by Jimeno v. Mobil Oil 

- Corp., 66 F.3d 1514, 1534 (9th Cir. 1995) (FEHA). Irwin 
gathered “all relevant information regarding the applicant’s .. . 
medical history and independently assess[ed] both the 
probability and severity of potential harm,” as required by 
Mendez v. Gearan, 956 F.Supp. 1520, 1527 (N.D. Cal. 1997) 
(Rehab. Act). Irwin, like Chevron, can be analogized to the 
employers in Bento and Irwin, who, while they may have 
reached medically incorrect judgments, behaved in an 
objectively reasonable, and therefore lawful, manner. Irwin did 
not simply rely upon Chevron’s letter; it sought further 
objective evidence from Dr. Tang, which confirmed the opinion 


193 


of Chevron’s doctor. As in Walker, the employer was under no 
duty to inquire still further. 


Irwin also argues that Echazabal’s causes of action fail 
because Echazabal is not disabled. However, the ADA and 
FEHA protect not only actually disabled persons, but also those 
“regarded as” disabled by their employers. 42 U.S.C. § 
12102(2) (ADA); Cal. Gov. Code § 12926(k) (FEHA)); see also 
29 C.F.R. § 1630.2(1) (EEOC ADA Regs.); 2 Cal. Code of 
Regs. § 7293.6(e)(2)(A)-(D) (FEHA Regs.). Where a plaintiff’ s 
condition is treated by his employer as posing a direct threat, 
such a plaintiff has almost by definition been “regarded as” 
disabled by that employer. Roe v. District of Columbia, 842 


F.Supp. 563, 568 (D.D.C. 1993). Such is the situation in this 
case. 


However, in Opposition to Irwin’s Motion for Summary 
Judgment, plaintiff has also argued the issue of “reasonable 
accommodation” by Irwin. An employer violates the ADA in 
excluding an otherwise qualified disabled person from 
employment, even if he poses a direct threat to himself, if the 
threat can “be eliminated by reasonable accommodation,” Doe, 
supra, at 1264-65. FEHA similarly requires that the direct threat 
not be able to be eliminated by “reasonable accommodations.” 
Cal. Gov. Code § 12940(a)(1); see also Cal. Gov. Code 
§ 12940(d). Under both the ADA and FEHA, an employer has 
an affirmative obligation to make reasonable accommodation 
to an individual’s disability, so long as such accommodation 
does not constitute an undue burden. School Board of Nassau 
County v. Arline, 480 U.S. 273, 289, fn. 19 (1987); Prilliman 
v. United Airlines, 53 Cal. App. 4th 935 (1997). Reasonable 
accommodations include job restructuring and reassignment. 2 
C.C.R. § 7293.9(0); 29 C.F.R. § 1630.29(0). 


In this case, plaintiff has presented admissible evidence 
raising genuine issues of material fact as to whether Irwin could 
have made reasonable accommodation to plaintiff's disability 


194 


(whether real or perceived), for example, by placing him in 
positions for which he was qualified and which would not have 
involved exposure to hepatotoxic hydrocarbons. Plaintiff has 
presented admissible evidence that plaintiff was qualified for 
such positions, that such positions existed and were filled by 
Irwin during the relevant times, that plaintiff made himself 
available for such positions, and that Irwin nevertheless did not 
assign him to such positions. Because genuine issues of 
material fact remain as to whether reasonable accommodations 
by Irwin might have rendered plaintiff “otherwise qualified” for 
employment by Irwin, summary judgment on the First and 
Second Causes of Action as to defendant Irwin should be 
DENIED. 


E. Irwin’s Motion for Summary Judgment as to Claims 
for Punitive Damages 


The availability of punitive damages is a question of state 
law. Central Office Tel. v. AT&T Co., 108 F.3d 981, 993 (9th 
Cir. 1997). In order to recover punitive damages under 
California law, a party must prove the defendant was guilty of 


malice or oppression (or fraud, not at issue in this case), defined - 


by Cal. Civ. Code § 3294(a) in pertinent part as follows: 


(1) ‘Malice’ means conduct which is intended by the 
defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or despicable 
conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful 
and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others. 


(2) ‘Oppression’ means despicable conduct that subjects a 
person to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of 
that person’s rights. 


In Opposition to Irwin’s motion for summary judgment as to 
claims for punitive damages, plaintiff argues in effect that 
malice may be inferred from any facts supporting violation of 
the ADA (and, by implication, FEHA as well). (Pl.’s Opp. 
33:18-34:16.) This position is not supported by law. 


195 


‘Something more than the mere commission of a tort is 
always required for punitive damages. There must be 
circumstances of aggravation or outrage.’ ... [A] conscious 
disregard [rather than reckless disregard or misconduct] . . . 
may constitute malice within the meaning of section 3294 of 
the Civil Code. In order to justify an award of punitive 
damages on this basis, the plaintiff must establish that the 
defendant . . . wilfully and deliberately failed to avoid 
(harmful consequences to the plaintiff). 


Taylor v. Superior Court, 24 Cal. 3d 890, 894-5 (1979). 


In this case, although plaintiff has presented admissible 
evidence raising genuine issues of material fact sufficient to 
oppose Irwin’s motion for summary judg:nent on Echazabal’s 
claims that his rights under the ADA and FEHA were violated, 
plaintiff has filed to present that “something more,” admissible 
evidence raising a genuine issue of material fact as to Irwin’s 
conscious disregard of Echazabal’s rights. Irwin’s motion for 
summary judgment as to punitive damages should therefore be 
GRANTED.

IV. 


For the foregoing reasons, we conclude that the ADA’s 
direct threat defense means what it says: it permits employers 
to impose a requirement that their employees not pose a 
significant risk to the health or safety of other individuals in the 
workplace. It does not permit employers to shut disabled 
individuals out of jobs on the ground that, by working in the 
jobs at issue, they may put their own health or safety at risk. 
Conscious of the history of paternalistic rules that have often 
excluded disabled individuals from the workplace, Congress 
concluded that disabled persons should be afforded the 
opportunity to decide for themselves what risks to undertake. 
The district court’s grant of summary judgment to Chevron on 
Echazabal’s ADA claim is reversed.”

that Echazabal “presented no admissible evidence in support of 
his contention that Chevron by its February 21, 1996 letter 
intended that the relation between Irwin and Echazabal be 
terminated.” 

In Seaman’s v. Standard Oil, 686 P.2d 1158 (Cal. 1984) (in 
bank), the California Supreme Court held that intent to interfere 
with a contract “may be established by inference as well as by 
direct proof. Thus [the jury may] infer culpable intent from 
conduct ‘substantially certain’ to interfere with the contract.” 
Id. at 767; see also Ramona Manor Convalescent Hosp. v. Care 
Enters., 225 Cal. Rptr. 120, 124-26 (Ct. App. 1986) (finding 
that the jury’s determination of intent supported by the 
evidence, and focusing on the existence of knowledge of the 
third-party contract). While the California Supreme Court later 
overruled Seaman's to the extent that Seaman’s suggested that 
its holding applied to actions for tortious interference with 
prospective economic relations as well as to actions for tortious 
interference with existing contractual relations, it did not upset 
Seaman ’s holding with respect to existing contractual relations. 
See Penna v. Toyota Motor Sales, U.S.A., Inc., 902 P.2d 740 
(Cal. 1995). In fact, the Penna court reinforced Seaman’s 
holding with respect to existing contracts by “draw[ing] and 
enforc[{ing] a sharpened distinction between claims for the 
tortious disruption of an existing contract and claims [for 
tortious interference with a] prospective contractual or 
economic relationship.” Jd. at 750. For the latter claims, the 
court held, the plaintiff cannot succeed in demonstrating 
intentional interference with prospective economic relations by 
introducing only evidence “that the defendant . . . knowingly 
interfered with the plaintiff's expectancy.” Jd. at 751. By 
adopting a stricter test for cases involving prospective 
economic relationships, the Penna court reinforced the Seaman 
conclusion that, with respect to an existing contract, a jury may 
infer an intent to interfere from the defendant’s knowledge of 


220 


the third-party contract accompanied by conduct “substantially 
certain” to interfere. 


Summary judgment was inappropriate here because there is 
no dispute that Chevron had knowledge of Echazabal’s 
employment contract with Irwin, and a genuine issue of 
material fact exists as to whether Chevron engaged in conduct 
“substantially certain” to interfere with that contract. First, 
Chevron obviously knew of Echazabal’s employment contract 
with Irwin—had it not, it would have had no occasion to send 
a letter to Irwin requiring that it remove Echazabal from the 
refinery or move him to a position in which he would not be 
exposed to solvents and chemicals. 


Second, a fact-finder could conclude, by drawing 
permissible inferences, that Chevron’s actions were 
“substantially certain to interfere” with Echazabal’s 
employment contract with Irwin. “Interference” includes 
inducing a disruption of the contractual relationship. See 
Savage v. Pacific Gas & Elec. Co., 26 Cal. Rptr. 2d 305, 314 
(Ct. App. 1993). It also includes making the plaintiff s 
performance of the contract “more expensive or burdensome.” 
Ramona Manor, 225 Cal. Rptr. 2d at 124. In this case, it was 
substantially certain that Chevron’s actions would disrupt 
Echazabal’s relationship with Irwin and make his performance 
of his employment contract more burdensome. In response to 
Echazabal’s EEOC filing, Irwin stated that it had no positions 
at the Chevron refinery (except for one clerk position) in which 
exposure to chemicals and solvents would not be likely. 
Accordingly, a fact-finder could conclude that Chevron’s letter 
to Irwin made it certain that Echazabal would be removed from 
his employment at the refinery. In addition, a fact-finder could 
also conclude that Chevron’s actions made it substantially 
certain that Echazabal would not obtain any work with Irwin. 
After it removed Echazabal from the Chevron refinery, Irwin, 

a general contractor providing maintenance and construction 
services to the petrochemical and energy industries, quickly 
determined that it would not assign Echazabal to a position at 


221 


any refinery because of his condition. Moreover. as Irwi 
acknowledged, “there is very little non-refinery work Mr. 
Echazabal is qualified to perform and very few such 
opportunities available.”* These facts are sufficient to preclude 
summary judgment. 


Chevron argues in the alternative that summary j i 
appropriate because Chevron’s communication rg 
“justified.” Justification remains an affirmative defense to a 
charge of intentional interference with a contract. See 
Seaman's, 686 P.2d at 1165. However, “{ijf the means 
[employed to interfere with the contract] are unlawful, the 
interference cannot be justified and is beyond the reach of this 
affirmative defense.” Savage, 26 Cal. Rptr. 2d at 315. 
Interfering with the performance of a person’s employment 
contract on a ground prohibited by federal law would fall 
within this rule. Because we hold in the accompanying opinion 
that Chevron is not entitled to summary judgment with respect 
to whether its actions in refusing Echazabal employment at the 
refinery were in violation of federal law, there is also a genuine 
issue of material fact as to whether Chevron’s actions in 
seeking to have Irwin prohibit him from working at the refinery 
were unlawful. Accordingly, summary judgment is not 
appropriate.
