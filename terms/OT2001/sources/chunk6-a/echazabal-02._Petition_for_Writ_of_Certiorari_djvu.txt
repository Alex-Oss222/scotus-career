QUESTION PRESENTED 


Respondent, who has chronic hepatitis C, a debilitating liver 
condition, applied for a job at Chevron’s refinery that would 
have exposed him to a range of liver-toxic chemicals. After 
Chevron’s doctors and respondent’s own physician agreed that 
daily exposure to these chemicals would accelerate the 
deterioration of respondent’s liver and that a_ large-scale 
exposure from a burst pipe, fire, or other emergency could 
quickly kill him, Chevron declined to hire him. In conflict with 
decisions of other courts of appeals as well as with EEOC 
regulations and guidelines squarely on point, the Ninth Circuit, 
in an opinion by Judge Reinhardt, held that under the 
Americans with Disabilities Act, an employer may not refuse 
to hire an employee whose medical condition would make the 
particular position he seeks dangerous to his health or life. 
Judge Trott dissented. 


The question presented is: 

Whether a person who is unable to carry out the essential 
functions of a job without incurring significant risks to the 
person’s own health or life is a “qualified individual” who 
satisfies “qualification standards” for that job within the 
meaning of the Americans with Disabilities Act.

STATEMENT 


Chevron withdrew an offer to employ respondent in a 
refinery job in which he inevitably would have been exposed to 
liver-toxic substances after Chevron’s physicians determined 
that respondent had a “history of a long term liver problem, [a] 
diagnosis of chronic active Hepatitis C, and significantly 
elevated liver enzymes.” C.A. App. 81. Chevron’s physicians 
concluded that the “exposure to hepatotoxic chemicals”

involved in the job “would further damage [respondent’s] 
already reduced liver capacity,” “seriously endanger his health,” 
and “potentially cause [his] death.” /d. at 81-82. Respondent’s 
own doctor advised Chevron that respondent should not be 
exposed to hepatotoxins. App., infra, 37a, 47a. 


The Ninth Circuit majority (Reinhardt and Bright, JJ.) 
nevertheless held that respondent was “qualified” for the 
refinery job within the meaning of the Americans with 
Disabilities Act (“ADA”) because he posed no “direct threat” 
to other people. Chevron could not refuse to hire respondent on 
the ground that doing the job “most probably will endanger his 
life.” App., infra, 21a. 


That “bizarre” result, as Judge Trott described it (App., infra, 
21a), creates numerous conflicts with other courts of appeals. 
The Ninth Circuit’s ruling conflicts with the holding of the 
Seventh Circuit that an employee is not a “qualified individual” 
under the ADA if there is “no way to do the job * * * without 
subjecting himself to the very things his doctors recommended 
he stay away from.” Koshinski v. Decatur Foundry, Inc., 177 
F.3d 599, 601, 603 (7th Cir. 1999). It conflicts with decisions 
that recognize a “direct threat” defense to ADA liability where 
an employee’s “assigned tasks presented grave risks to [the] 
employee” as a result of a medical condition. Moses v. 
American Nonwovens, Inc., 97 F.3d 446, 447 (11th Cir. 1996); 
see also Borgialli v. Thunder Basin Coal Co., 235 F.3d 1284, 
1288-1294 (10th Cir. 2000). It conflicts with decisions that 
safety requirements may be included in the “qualification 
standards” for a position. EEOC v. Exxon Corp., 203 F.3d 871, 
873-874 (Sth Cir. 2000); EEOC v. Amego, Inc., 110 F.3d 135, 
143 (1st Cir. 1997). And it is at odds with cases interpreting 
virtually identical language in the Rehabilitation Act, which 
hold that persons who “pose a significant risk of harm to 
themselves” are not “qualified.” Doe v. New York Univ., 666 
F.2d 761, 777 (2d Cir. 1981); see also Daugherty v. City of El

Paso, 56 F.3d 695, 697-698 (Sth Cir. 1995); Knapp v. 
Northwestern Univ., 101 F.3d 473, 482-484 (7th Cir. 1996). 


Beyond creating sharp conflicts among the circuits, the court 
of appeals’ decision expressly overrides regulations of the 
Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, the expert agency 
Congress charged with implementing the ADA’s employment 
provisions. App., infra, 11a-12a. Those regulations provide that 
an employer may establish as a “qualification standard” . 
“medical” and “safety” requirements that an applicant must 
meet, and that such standards may exclude an applicant who 
would pose a “significant risk of substantial harm to the health 
or safety of the individual or others.” 29 C.F.R. §§ 1630.2(q), 
(r), 1630.15(b)(2). The court of appeals’ ruling also is 
inconsistent with the command of the Occupational Safety and 
Health Act (as well as state worker protection laws) that 


employers provide a safe workplace for all employees. 29 
U.S.C. § 654. 


; The “absurd” result in this case—one that will cost workers’ 
lives and force unwilling employers to be complicit in their 
injuries—is not what Congress had in mind when it enacted the 
ADA, as the plain language and legislative history demonstrate. 
App., infra, 23a. Absent this Court’s intervention, businesses 
will face intolerable uncertainty in determining how to comply 
with the requirements of the ADA in the face of conflicting 
judicial and regulatory commands, while also complying with 
OSHA and state worker protection laws. These factors have led 
commentators to describe the Ninth Circuit’s ruling as “one of 
the most important decisions under the ADA during the 10 
years since it has been in effect,” and to observe that it “cries 
out for Supreme Court review.” Hudson, Paternalism is Out, 
ABA Journal, Feb. 2001, at 30.

A. The Statutory And Regulatory Scheme 


The Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990 prohibits an 
employer from discriminating in employment decisions 
“against a qualified individual with a disability because of the 
disability of such individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 12112(a). A 
“qualified individual with a disability” is someone with a 
disability “who, with or without reasonable accommodation, 
can perform the essential functions of the employment position 
that such individual holds or desires.” Jd. § 12111(8). The ADA 
provides that “consideration shall be given to the employer’s 
judgment as to what functions of a job are essential,” and that 
an employer’s “written description” of the job is evidence of its 
essential functions. /bid. 

It is “a defense to a charge of discrimination” under the ADA 
that an individual was denied employment as the result of the 
employer’s “application of qualification standards” that are 
“job-related and consistent with business necessity.” 42 U.S.C. 
§ 12113(a). The statute provides one example of a qualification 
standard: “The term ‘qualification standards’ may include a 
requirement that an individual shall not pose a direct threat to 
the health or safety of other individuals in the workpiace.” Jd. 

§ 12113(b). “Direct threat” is defined as “a significant risk to 
the health or safety of others that cannot be eliminated by 
reasonable accommodation.” Jd. § 12111(3). 


The responsibility of an employer who denies employment 
to an applicant because the job would endanger his health or life 
may be analyzed in terms of whether the applicant is a 
“qualified individual” pursuant to ADA sections 12111(8) and 
12112(a), or in terms of whether the absence of such danger is 
a proper “qualification standard” and therefore a defense to

B. Chevron’s Determination That Respondent Was Not 
Qualified For The Plant Helper Job 


Respondent Mario Echazabal worked at Chevron’s oil 
refinery in El Segundo, California from 1972 until 1996, 
employed by independent maintenance contractors, most 
recently by Irwin Industries, Inc. (“Irwin”). In 1992, Echazabal 
applied to work directly for Chevron in the refinery’s coker 
unit. Chevron offered him the job, contingent on a satisfactory 
medical examination. Chevron’s regional physician, Dr. Baily, 
conducted that examination and concluded from the results of 
tests showing elevated levels of liver enzymes in Echazabal’s 
blood that he had “an uncorrectable liver abnormality, and 
should avoid exposure to solvents and other liver toxic 
chemicals in order not to exacerbate his liver problems.” Dr. 
Baily’s successor, Dr. McGill, agreed with this conclusion. On 
that basis, Chevron rescinded its offer of employment to 
Echazabal, who continued to work at the refinery for Irwin. 
Thereafter, Echazabal’s own doctors diagnosed him with 
chronic active hepatitis C and treated him with the drug 
Interferon. App., infra, 34a, 36a; C.A. App. 86. 

In late 1995 Echazabal again applied to Chevron for a 
position as plant helper in the refinery’s coker unit. Chevron’s 
official “Job summary” (C.A. App. 156-159) included a 
description of the “physical/environmental demands” of the 
plant helper job and of worker abilities necessary to withstand 
those demands. Jd at 159. That description identified as 
“airborne contaminants and chemicals in work environment” 

hydrocarbon liquids and vapors, acid, caustic, refinery waste

IV. 


For the foregoing reasons, we conclude that the ADA’s 
direct threat defense means what it says: it permits employers 
to impose a requirement that their employees not pose a 
significant risk to the health or safety of other individuals in the 
workplace. It does not permit employers to shut disabled 
individuals out of jobs on the ground that, by working in the 
jobs at issue, they may put their own health or safety at risk. 
Conscious of the history of paternalistic rules that have often 
excluded disabled individuals from the workplace, Congress 
concluded that disabled persons should be afforded the 
opportunity to decide for themselves what risks to undertake. 
The district court’s grant of summary judgment to Chevron on 
Echazabal’s ADA claim is reversed."”

MEMORANDUM’ 


In an opinion filed concurrently with this memorandum 
disposition, we reverse the district court’s grant of summary 
judgment on Echazabal’s claim under the Americans with 
Disabilities Act and remand for further proceedings. This 
memorandum disposition addresses Echazabal’s appeal of the 
district court’s grant of summary judgment on one of his state 
law claims—that Chevron is liable for intentionally interfering 
with his employment contract with Irwin. The district court 
granted Chevron’s summary judgment motion on the ground 
that Echazabal “presented no admissible evidence in support of 
his contention that Chevron by its February 21, 1996 letter 
intended that the relation between Irwin and Echazabal be 
terminated.” 


In Seaman's v. Standard Oil, 686 P.2d 1158 (Cal. 1984) (in 
bank), the California Supreme Court held that intent to interfere 
with a contract “may be established by inference as well as by 
direct proof. Thus [the jury may] infer culpable intent from 
conduct ‘substantially certain’ to interfere with the contract.” 
Id. at 767; see also Ramona Manor Convalescent Hosp. v. Care 
Enters., 225 Cal. Rptr. 120, 124-26 (Ct. App. 1986) (finding 
that the jury’s determination of intent supported by the 
evidence, and focusing on the existence of knowledge of the 
third-party contract). While the California Supreme Court later 
overruled Seaman’s to the extent that Seaman’s suggested that 
its holding applied to actions for tortious interference with 
prospective economic relations as well as to actions for tortious 
interference with existing contractual relations, it did not upset 
Seaman's holding with respect to existing contractual relations. 
See Penna v. Toyota Motor Sales, U.S.A., Inc., 902 P.2d 740 


2 This disposition is not appropriate for publication and may not be 
cited to or by the courts of this circuit except as may be provided by 
9th Cir. R. 36-3. 


27a 


(Cal. 1995). In fact, the Penna court reinforced Seaman's 
holding with respect to existing contracts by “draw[ing] and 
enforc[ing] a sharpened distinction between claims for the 
tortious disruption of an existing contract and claims [for 
tortious interference with a] prospective contractual or 
economic relationship.” /d. at 750. For the latter claims, the 
court held, the plaintiff cannot succeed in demonstrating 
intentional interference with prospective economic relations by 
introducing only evidence “that the defendant . . . knowingly 
interfered with the plaintiff's expectancy.” Jd. at 751. By 
adopting a stricter test for cases involving prospective economic 
relationships, the Penna court reinforced the Seaman conclusion 
that, with respect to an existing contract, a jury may infer an 
intent to interfere from the defendant’s knowledge of the third- 
party contract accompanied by conduct “substantially certain” 
to interfere. 


Summary judgment was inappropriate here because there is 
no dispute that Chevron had knowledge of Echazabal’s 
employment contract with Irwin, and a genuine issue of 
material fact exists as to whether Chevron engaged in conduct 
“substantially certain” to interfere with that contract. First, 
Chevron obviously knew of Echazabal’s employment contract 
with Irwin—had it not, it would have had no occasion to send 
a letter to Irwin requiring that it remove Echazabal from the 
refinery or move him to a position in which he would not be 
exposed to solvents and chemicals. 


Second, a fact-finder could conclude, by drawing permissible 
inferences, that Chevron’s actions were “substantially certain to 
interfere” with Echazabal’s employment contract with Irwin. 
“Interference” includes inducing a disruption of the contractual 
relationship. See Savage v. Pacific Gas & Elec. Co., 26 
Cal. Rptr. 2d 305, 314 (Ct. App. 1993). It also includes making 
the plaintiff's performance of the contract “more expensive or 
burdensome.” Ramona Manor, 225 Cal. Rptr. 2d at 124. In this 


28a 


case, it was substantially certain that Chevron’s actions would 
disrupt Echazabal’s relationship with Irwin and make his 
performance of his employment contract more burdensome. In 
response to Echazabal’s EEOC filing, Irwin stated that it had no 
positions at the Chevron refinery (except for one clerk position) 
in which exposure to chemicals and solvents would not be 
likely. Accordingly, a fact-finder could conclude that Chevron’s 
letter to Irwin made it certain that Echazabal would be remowed 
from his employment at the refinery. In addition, a fact-fimder 
could also conclude that Chevron’s actions made it substantially 
certain that Echazabal would not obtain any work with Irwin. 
After it removed Echazabal from the Chevron refinery, Irwin, 
a general contractor providing maintenance and constructtion 
services to the petrochemical and energy industries, quickly 
determined that it would not assign Echazabal to a positioin at 
any refinery because of his condition. Moreover, as Irwin 
acknowledged, “there is very little non-refinery work Mr. 
Echazabal is qualified to perform and very few swch 
opportunities available.”’ These facts are sufficient to precliude 
summary judgment. 


Chevron argues in the alternative that summary judgment is 
appropriate because Chevron’s communication to lrwin was 
“justified.” Justification remains an affirmative defense to a 
charge of international interference with a contract. See 
Seaman's, 686 P.2d at 1165. However, “{i]f the means 
{employed to interfere with the contract] are unlawful, the 
interference cannot be justified and is, ‘beyond the reach of this 


> It appears that the few opportunities with Irwin that might 
conceivably have become available to Echazabal at some point were 
work opportunities that, paid a substantially lower wage. Thus, even 
had Echazabal been, offered such work and accepted it, Chevron’s 
actions would haive interfered with his employment contract by 
making that cosntract less valuable. 


29a 


affirmative defense.” Savage, 26 Cal. Rptr. 2d at 315. 
Interfering with the peformance of a person’s employment 
contract on a ground prohibited by federal law would fall within 
this rule. Because we hold in the accompanying opinion that 
Chevron is not entitled to summary judgment with respect to 
whether its actions in refusing Echazabal employment at the 
refinery were in violation of federal law, there is also a genuine 
issue of material fact as to whether Chevron’s actions in 
seeking to have Irwin prohibit him from working at the refinery 
were unlawful. Accordingly, summary judgment is not 
appropniate. 


REVERSED and REMANDED for further proceedings 
consistent with this disposition.

Ill. SUMMARY JUDGMENT STANDARD 


Summary judgment shall be granted if the evidence 
supporting the motion for summary judgment shows that “there 
is no genuine issue as to any material fact and that the moving 
party is entitled to a judgment as a matter of law.” Fed. R. Civ. 
P. 56(c). A party moving for summary judgment may carry its 
initial burden by pointing out to the district court that there is an 
absence of a genuine issue of material fact. Celotex Corp. v. 
Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 323 (1986). “The plain language of Rule 
56(c) mandates the entry of summary judgment, after adequate 
time for discovery and upon motion, against a party who fails 
to make a showing sufficient to establish the existence of an 
element essential to that party’s case, and on which that party 
will bear the burden of proof at trial.” Celotex, 477 U.S. at 322. 


To avoid summary judgment, the non-movant must set forth 
specific facts showing that there remains a genuine issue of 
material fact for trial. Celotex, 477 U.S. at 324. The non- 
movant “may not rest upon the mere allegations or denials of 
the adverse party’s pleading.” A factual dispute is “genuine” if 
a reasonable jury could return a verdict for the nonmoving 
party. Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. at 242, 248 


43a 


(1986). The evidence of the non-movant is to be believed, and 
all justifiable inferences are to be drawn in the non-movant’s 
favor. Jd. at 255. If the nonmoving party’s evidence is merely 
colorable or is not significantly probative, then summary 
judgment may be granted. /d. at 249-50.

The “direct threat” analysis engaged in by Chevron, set out

from the refinery was based on the letter from Chevron and 
Kiesser’s communications with Dr. Tang. (Kiesser Depo. 
115:14-20 and passim.) As required by Rizzo, supra, Irwin 
engaged in an “individualized assessment” of Echazabal, 
“based on a reasonable medical judgment that relies on. . .the 
best available objective evidence.” Rizzo, supra at 763. The 
evaluation provided by Tang indicated that the danger to 
Echazabal was immediate, as required by Jimeno v. Mobil Oil 
Corp., 66 F.3d 1514, 1534 (9th Cir. 1995) (FEHA). Irwin 
gathered “all relevant information regarding the applicant’s. . . 
medical history and independently assess[ed] both the 
probability and severity of potential harm,” as required by 
Mendez v. Gearan, 956 F.Supp. 1520, 1527 (N.D. Cal. 1997) 
(Rehab. Act). Irwin, like Chevron, can be analogized to the 
employers in Bento and Irwin, who, while they may have 
reached medically incorrect judgments, behaved in an 
objectively reasonable, and therefore lawful, manner. Irwin did 
not simply rely upon Chevron’s letter; it sought further 
objective evidence from Dr. Tang, which confirmed the opinion 


55a 


of Chevron’s doctor. As in Walker, the employer was under no 
duty to inquire still further. 


Irwin also argues that Echazabal’s causes of action fail 
because Echazabal is not disabled. However, the ADA and 
FEHA protect not only actually disabled persons, but also those 
“regarded as” disabled by their employers. 42 U.S.C. § 
12102(2) (ADA); Cal. Gov. Code § 12926(k) (FEHA); see also 
29 C.F.R. § 1630.2(1) (EEOC ADA Regs.); 2 Cal. Code of 
Regs. § 7293.6(e)(2)(A)-(D) (FEHA Regs.). Where a plaintiff s 
condition is treated by his employer as posing a direct threat, 
such a plaintiff has almost by definition been “regarded as” 
disabled by that employer. Roe v. District of Columbia, 842 
F.Supp. 563, 568 (D.D.C. 1993). Such is the situation in this 
case. 


However, in Opposition to Irwin’s Motion for Summary 
Judgment, plaintiff has also argued the issue of “reasonable 
accommodation” by Irwin. An employer violates the ADA in 
excluding an otherwise qualified disabled person from 
employment, even if he poses a direct threat to himself, if the 
threat can “be eliminated by reasonable accommodation,” Doe, 
supra, at 1264-65. FEHA similarly requires that the direct threat 
not be able to be eliminated by “reasonable accommodations.” 
Cal. Gov. Code § 12940(a)(1); see also Cal. Gov. Code 
§ 12940(d). Under both the ADA and FEHA, an employer has 
an affirmative obligation to make reasonable accommodation 
to an individual’s disability, so long as such accommodation 
does not constitute an undue burden. School Board of Nassau 
County v. Arline, 480 U.S. 273, 289, fn. 19 (1987); Prilliman v. 
United Airlines, 53 Cal. App. 4th 935 (1997). Reasonable 
accommodations include job restructuring and reassignment. 2 
C.C.R. § 7293.9(0); 29 C.F.R. § 1630.29(0). 


In this case, plaintiff has presented admissible evidence 
raising genuine issues of material fact as to whether Irwin could 


56a 


have made reasonable accommodation to plaintiff's disability 
(whether real or perceived), for example, by placing him in 
positions for which he was qualified and which would not have 
involved exposure to hepatotoxic hydrocarbons. Plainitff has 
presented admissible evidence that plaintiff was qualified for 
such positions, that such positions existed and were filled by 
Irwin during the relevant times, that plaintiff made himself 
available for such positions, and that I. win nevertheless did not 
assign him to such positions. Because genuine issues of 
material fact remain as to whether reasonable accommodations 
by Irwin might have rendered plaintiff “otherwise qualified” for 
employment by Irwin, summary judgment on the First and 
Second Causes of Action as to defendant Irwin should be 
DENIED.

The availability of punitive damages is a question of state 
law. Central Office Tel. v. AT&T Co., 108 F.3d 981, 993 (9th 
Cir. 1997). In order to recover punitive damages under 
California law, a party must prove the defendant was guilty of 
malice or oppression (or fraud, not at issue in this case), defined 
by Cal. Civ. Code § 3294(a) in pertinent part as follows: 


(1) ‘Malice’ means conduct which is intended by the 
defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or despicable 
conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful 
and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others. 


(2) ‘Oppression’ means despicable conduct that subjects a 
person to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of 
that person’s rights. 


In Opposition to Irwin’s motion for summary judgment as to 
claims for punitive damages, plaintiff argues in effect that 
malice may be inferred from any facts supporting violation of 


57a 


the ADA (and, by implication, FEHA as well). (Pl.’s Opp. 
33:18-34:16.) This position is not supported by law. 


‘Something more than the mere commission of a tort is 
always required for punitive damages. There must be 
circumstances of aggravation or outrage.’ . . . [A] conscious 
disregard [rather than reckless disregard or misconduct] . . . 
may constitute malice within the meaning of section 3294 of 
the Civil Code. In order to justify an award of punitive 
damages on this basis, the plaintiff must establish that the 
defendant. . .wilfully and deliberately failed to avoid 
(harmful consequences to the plaintiff). 


Taylor v. Superior Court, 24 Cal. 3d 890, 894-5 (1979). 


In this case, although plaintiff has presented admissible 
evidence raising genuine issues of material fact sufficient to 
oppose Irwin’s motion for summary judgment on Echazabal’s 
claims that his rights under the ADA and FEHA were violated, 
plaintiff has filed to present that “something more,” admissible 
evidence raising a genuine issue of material fact as to Irwin’s 
conscious disregard of Echazabal’s rights. Irwin’s motion for 
summary judgment as to punitive damages should therefore be 
GRANTED. 


Vv. CONCLUSION 


Defendant Chevron’s Motion for Summary Judgment is 
GRANTED. 


Defendant Irwin’s Motion for Summary Judgment is 
DENIED as to the First and Second Causes of Action, but 
GRANTED as to the issue of punitive damages.
