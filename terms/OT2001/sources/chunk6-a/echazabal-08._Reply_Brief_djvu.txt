In furtherance of a campaign against “paternalism” that 
would force employers to hire disabled persons for jobs that 
will seriously harm or kill them—an agenda Judge Trott 
described as “bizarre” and at odds with numerous “statutes and 
rules” designed to assure worker safety—trespondent asks this 
Court to engage in an unprecedented mode of statutory interpre- 
tation. Echazabal urges the Court to override the ADA’s 
unambiguous statutory language, which shows that the business 
necessity defense encompasses an employer’s rejection of an 
applicant who fails job-related medical tests because he has a 
condition that puts him at significant risk of serious injury. 
Instead, Echazabal would have this Court adopt rigid legal 
requirements that both Congress and the EEOC considered and 
explicitly rejected. That plainly is not an acceptable way to 
interpret the Act. 


I. Echazabal faced a significant risk of injury or death. 
Echazabal and his amici seek to disguise the danger that Echaz- 
abal faced, misstating the nature of chronic active Hepatitis C 
and the deadly consequences of exposing him to hepatotoxic 
chemicals present in small quantities in the plant and to the risk 
of a larger leak or spill. In doing so, Echazabal contradicts 
federal safety standards and authoritative medical literature, 
which show that he faced imminent and serious danger because 
of his medical condition. Employers have an unwaivable 
obligation under OSHA and state laws—on pain of civil and 
criminal sanctions and punitive damages—to protect each 
employee from known risks to life and health. 

Echazabal portrays himself as having a stable medical 
condition that will not be exacerbated by exposure to hepato- 
toxins present in the refinery and that will not affect his ability 
to work for decades, if ever. That, unfortunately, is not the case. 
Although Hepatitis C is typically asymptomatic for a period of 
time, up to half of those infected progress to debilitating 
“cirrhosis and end-stage liver disease,” sometimes “within a 
few years”; Hepatitis C is the leading cause of liver transplants; 
and 10,000 people die each year from the disease. 1 CECIL

Serious disease with “substantial” “morbidity and mortality

extreme. At the time of his 1995 application to Chevron he was 
50 years old with a “history of heavy alcohol intake.” J.A. 122. 
He thus had all three of the factors—over 40, male, and heavy 
alcohol use—that are most “associated with the risk of develop- 
ment of cirrhosis” and “liver cancer.” CECIL TEXTBOOK 794;

Echazabal claims that, despite all these warnings that he was at 
serious risk, Chevron should have hired him for a job in which 
he would face on a daily basis another documented and leading 
risk factor for liver failure, cirrhosis, and transplantation— 
further “hepatotoxic exposures.” CECIL TEXTBOOK 794, 817.

put a person with “pre-existing * * * abnormalities of the * * *

There was nothing inconsistent in Chevron not demanding

If expert testimony presented years after the employment 
decision were enough to cast doubt on Chevron’s decision here, 
an employer could not make a decision even with the advice of 
multiple experienced physicians—including the employee’s 
own physician. And respondent offers no suggestion for what 
an employer should do in the event of disagreement among 
doctors over a life-threatening condition. Other circuits have 
adopted a more sensible approach that does not require a 
business to abandon reasonable efforts to ensure employee 
safety just because some disease assessments are “probabilistic” 
(Resp. Br. 21): “in the midst of conflicting expert testimony 
regarding the degree of serious risk of harm or death, the 
court’s place is to ensure that the exclusion * * * of an individ- 
ual was individualized, reasonably made, and based upon 
competent medical evidence.” Knapp, 101 F.3d at 485. Amici 
American College of Occupational and Environmental Medi- 
cine ef al. explain the impracticalities and injurious conse- 
quences of denying employers the ability to rely on reasonable 
opinions of experienced physicians. 


II. Echazabal’s claim is barred by the ADA’s unambigu- 
ous language. Like the Ninth Circuit majority, Echazabal 
asserts (at 10, 15) that Congress rejected threat to self as a 
defense when it crafted a “direct threat” provision that imposes 
“specific limitations” on an employer’s available defenses. But 
Congress provided in Section 121 13(b) only that qualification 
standards “may include” a requirement that a person not pose 
a direct threat to others. Echazabal has no response to the auth- 
orities we cited (at 30-33) showing that the phrase “may 
include” is non-restrictive, introducing an example and not an 
exhaustive list. Echazabal’s reliance (at 23-24) on Section 
12111(9) misstates the language of that provision, which is that 
a reasonable accommodation “may include”-{among other 
things) “reassignment to a vacant position * * * and other 
similar accommodations.” Reassignment to a filled position 
would be the opposite of reassignment to a vacant position, not 
a “similar” accommodation. There is no limiting language of 


6 


that sort in Section 12113(b). Nothing in that provision pre- 
cludes a threat-to-self inquiry authorized elsewhere in the ADA. 


Under the Rehabilitation Act, contrary to Echazabal’s 
tangled discussion (at 13-16), threat to self was uniformly 
recognized by the courts as disqualifying, just like threat to 
others, regardless of whether the case involved federal or other 
employment or participation in federally funded programs. See, 
e.g., Chiari, 920 F.2d at 316-317 (city employment); Mantolete 
v. Bolger, 767 F.2d 1416, 1422-1424 (9th Cir. 1985) (federal 
employment); Doe, 666 F.2d at 777 (medical school program); 
Mikucki v. United States Postal Service, 1986 WL 10516, at *7 
(D. Mass. Sept. 22, 1986) (federal employment); see also 
Chandler, 2 F.3d at 1393-1395 (city employment); Daugherty 
v. El Paso, 56 F.3d 695, 698 (Sth Cir. 1995) (same); Knapp, 
101 F.3d at 482-485 (private university program). Congress 
modeled the ADA on these judicial interpretations of the Act 
and regulations. See Bragdon, 524 U.S. at 645; 56 Fed. Reg. 
35730; Feldblum, Medical Examinations and Inquiries Under 
the ADA, 64 TEMP. L. REV. at 542-543. There is no indication 
anywhere in the ADA that Congress intendeJ to eliminate threat 
to self as a permissible reason for excluding an applicant from 
employment, as it had been under the Rehabilitation Act—and 
certainly none in Section 12113(b). 


Chevron met the requirements of the “direct threat to self” 
defense set forth in the EEOC’s regulations, to which deference 
is owed. 29 C.F.R. § 1630.2(r). More pertinently, however, it 
satisfied the job-relatedness and business necessity defense that 
is set forth directly in the statute, and repeated three times for 
emphasis, in Sections 12112(b)(6), 12112(d), and 12113(a). 
Echazabal has failed to explain how anything in Section 
12113(b) confines the more general scope of those provisions, 
which plainly permit an employer to refuse to hire an applicant 
who poses a threat to self. 


7 


Section 12113(a) provides a general defense for all pre- 
employment “tests” and “qualification standards” that are “job- 
related and consistent with business necessity,” which the 
EEOC recognizes may include “medical [and] safety” require- 
ments. 29 C.F.R. § 1630.2(q); see 42 U.S.C. § 12112(b)(6). 
Here, Chevron required blood and other medical “tests,” which 
revealed the grave risk Echazabal would face in the plant helper 
position. See OXFORD ENGLISH DICTIONARY (2d ed. 1989) 
(defining “test” as “an instance of testing the * * * physiologi- 
cal, or other qualities and conditions of a human subject”). 
Section 12113(b), on which Echazabal so heavily relies, does 
not mention “tests” at all, but relates only to “[t]he term ‘qualifi- 
cation standards,” so it cannot conceivably narrow the general- 
ity of the Section 12113(a) defense as it applies to tests. 
Chevron also satisfied the “qualifications standards” defense. 
Chevron’s written job description—to which the ADA gives 
special weight (42 U.S.C. § 12111(8);29C.F.R. § 1630.2(n)}— 
required that plant helper applicants have the “physical ability” 
to withstand the “environmental demand” of working with 
hepatotoxic substances. J.A. 65-66. Chevron’s doctors reason- 
ably concluded that Echazabal lacked that ability. 


Section 121 12(d) further provides that it is not “discrimina- 
tion” of any kind for an employer to require applicants to 
undergo pre-employment “medical examinations and inquiries” 
and to “condition an offer of employment on the results.” 
§ 12112(d)(3). The employer need show only that the medical 
examination is “job-related and consistent with business 
necessity.” § 12112(d)(4)(A); see also 29 C.F.R. § 1630.14. 
Chevron easily satisfied the job-relatedness and business 
necessity requirements (as we show in Section III below) when 
its doctors complied with NIOSH guidelines and examined 
applicants to determine whether they were at “increased risk” 
from hepatotoxic exposures, and when it denied employment to 
Echazabal because its doctors concluded that he fell into the 
highest risk category. Congress explained that the purpose of 
the “medical examination” provisions was precisely to allow 


8 


employers to determine whether an applicant was unfit for a 
“safety * * * sensitive positio[n],” and that Congress did not 
intend “to override any legitimate medical standards or require- 
ments.” H.R. Conf. Rep., 1 Leg. Hist. 64-65. Clearly, Congress 
did not believe that medical examinations to ensure safety 
constituted impermissible “paternalism.” 


Our analysis does not make Section 12113(b) mere “sur- 
plus.” Resp. Br. 25. Congress in the ADA treated threat to 
others separately in Sections 12113(b) and 12111(3)—provi- 
sions that concern only “qualification standards” that screen out 
persons who pose a threat to others. Congress left medical 
examinations, tests, and other qualification standards to be 
analyzed under the ordinary business necessity yardstick of 
Sections 12112(d) and 12113(a). Section 12113(b) reflects a 
desire to assure members of the public that the ADA would not 
jeopardize their safety. It also responds to concerns that 
employee fears may pressure an employer to deny employment 
to persons stereotypically perceived by co-workers as posing 
a threat to them. The heightened standard of the “direct threat” 
provision protects disabled employees from qualification 
standards based on prejudice or irrational fears of contagion and 
other dangers, while leaving professional medical judgments 
that a person is a safety risk to be analyzed under the business 
necessity test. The statute cannot be rewritten, however, to 
make the “direct threat” provision override the general business 
necessity defense expressly applicable to every other kind of 
job-related “test,” “qualification standard,” “selection 
criteri[on],” and “medical examination.” 


III. Chevron’s decision was supported by business 
necessity. Chevron’s denial of employment to Echazabal fits 
easily within the job-relatedness and business necessity defense 
that applies to pre-employment medical examinations, tests, and 
qualification standards. Disability advocates preferred a more 
demanding standard that appeared in an earlier bill—“necessary 
and substantially related to the ability of the individual to 


-_-~ «= 


9 


perform the essential functions of the particular employment 
position.” But the business community and President Bush 
opposed that formulation. Congress accordingly rejected it and 
adopted instead the requirement that medical tests and qualifi- 
cation standards be “job-related and consistent with business 
necessity,” a formulation borrowed from Title VII. Feldblum, 
supra, 64 Temp. L. REV. at 534-539, 546-547; 42 U.S.C. 
§°2000e-2(k). Disability advocates then lobbied the EEOC to 
exclude threat to personal safety from the scope of the business 
necessity defense by regulation, contending that consideration 
of harm to self would be “paternalistic” and predicated on 
“negative stereotypes.” 56 Fed. Reg. 35730 (1991). But the 
EEOC rejected that argument too as inconsistent with Con- 
gress’s intent. /bid. Courts should not adopt legal standards that 
Congress and the EEOC have disapproved. 


Employment decisions are job related and supported by 
business necessity when they comply with NIOSH guidelines, 
achieve efficient staffing, maintain employee morale, avoid 
damaging publicity, prevent OSHA and state law worker safety 
citations and possible criminal prosecution, and minimize tort 
liabilities. Congress understood that it is “acceptable to deny 
employment to an applicant” who “poses a significant risk to 
* * * property.” S. Rep., | Leg. Hist. 125; see also H.R. Rep. Pt. 
2, 1 Leg. Hist. 329. Clearly, Congress recognized that business 
necessity is a flexible concept. It can hardly be supposed that 
preventing the loss of a worker’s life, with all that entails for the 
enterprise, is a lesser business concern than preventing damage 
to machinery or other property. “Safety is our business” is not 
merely a slogan. It reflects the experience of American compa- 
nies that workplace safety is important to productivity and 
profitability, meeting ethical responsibilities, and preserving 
employee relations. Congress would scarcely have included the 
elaborate provisions of Section 12112(b) authorizing “medical 
examinations” and employment decisions based on their 
“results,” if it had shared Echazabal’s view that looking after 
worker safety is mere paternalism.

The business necessity defense was well established under 
Title VII at the time of the ADA’s adoption. It was expounded 
in a long line of this Court’s decisions, and was well understood 
to be “lenient” in comparison to BFOQ standards. Johnson 
Controls, 499 U.S. at 198. This Court repeatedly held that an 
employer’s refusal to hire satisfied the business necessity 
standard if it “significantly served”—even though it was not 


“require[d]” by—“/egitimate employment goals of safety ahd - 


efficiency” and bore a “‘manifest relationship to the employ- 
ment in question.’” Beazer, 440 U.S. at 587 & n.31 (emphasis 
added). This Court held in Furnco Constr. Corp. v. Wasers that 
avoiding “loss of production,” loss of “reputation,” and 
maintaining “safe and efficient operation of [the] business” 
were factors showing the business necessity of a hiring practice. 
A practice need only “be reasonably related to the achievement 
of some legitimate purpose” to satisfy the business necessity 
test. 438 U.S. 567, 571-576 (1978). And because courts “are 
generally less competent than employers” to assess the need for 
“business practices” and “hiring procedures,” they should not 
“substitut[e their] own judgment” as to appropriate employment 
criteria. Jd. at 574, 578. See also Dothard, 433 U.S. at 331-332 
& n.14 (an employer may “test * * * directly” for any character- 
istic that is “necessary to safe and efficient job performance”’); 
Griggs v. Duke Power Co., 401 U.S. 424, 432-436 (1971) (to 
ensure an applicant is “fit for the job,” an employer may use 
“testing or measuring procedures” to “measure the person for 
the job” because that “fulfill[s] a genuine business need”); 
Watson v. Fort Worth Bank, 487 U.S. 977, 997-998 (1988) 
(“the ‘business necessity’ or ‘job relatedness’ defense” is made 
out by showing “legitimate business reasons” for a practice; it 
is “obvious” that “safety and efficiency’” goals are sufficient). 
Echazabal has supplied no reason to believe that Congress, 
when it imported the business necessity defense into the ADA, 
had in mind a more stringent standard. 


We have shown (at 24-28), and the United States agrees (at 
17-19 & n.7), that hiring a person who would be at significant 


11 


risk on the job would expose an employer to OSHA and 
equivalent state charges, tort suits, and even criminal prosecu- 
tion. Echazabal’s supposition (at 37) that an employer who 
“discloses the relevant risks” to a hypersusceptible applicant 
“will face no prospect of liability” is mere wishful thinking. The 
OSH Act’s purpose is “‘to assure so far as possible every 
working man and woman in the Nation safe and healthful 
working conditions.’” Chao v. Mallard Bay Drilling, 122 S. Ct. 
738, 744 n.9 (2002). There is no question that OSHA may 
charge an employer with violating the Act’s general duty clause 
if it fails to protect a worker from “recognized hazards” likely 
to cause “death or serious physical harm.” 29 U.S.C. § 654(a); 
McKie Ford, Inc. v. Secretary of Labor, 191 F.3d 853 (8th Cir. 
1999). And it could be alleged in light of Echazabal’s history, 
Chevron’s physicians’ warnings, and NIOSH guidelines that 
exposure to hepatotoxins is a “recognized hazard.” The em- 
ployer’s duty under the OSH Act is not waivable, and employee 
consent to the risk would not foreclose an OSHA enforcement 
action. National Realty Co. v. OSH Review Comm'n, 489 F.2d 
1257, 1266 n.36 (D.C. Cir. 1973); see EEOC v. Waffle House, 
122 S. Ct. 754, 762-766 (2002); Brooklyn Sav. Bank v. O'Neil, 
324 U.S. 697, 704-706 (1945). A tort plaintiff would also 
contend that any waiver was against public policy and unlawful. 
Echazabal concedes (at 39 n.15), moreover, that state courts 
routinely hold that federal laws like the OSH Act and ADA do 
not preempt state criminal prosecutions. Echazabal’s prediction 
(at 39) that an employer would not be prosecuted or sued if it 
took “all reasonable steps to safeguard” employee health begs 
the question whether tort plaintiffs, enforcement agencies, and 
juries would think it reasonable for an employer to send a 
person it knows to have multiple risk factors for rapid progres- 
sion to liver failure into a job involving daily hepatotoxic 
exposures. Under the business necessity standard, that is not a 
chance employers are required to take. 


IV. The ADA authorizes individual tests. Recognizing 
that the business necessity defense allows employers to reject 


12 


applicants to ensure plant safety and efficiency, Echazabal 
contends (at 26-32) that under the ADA, as under Title VII, the 
defense is limited to cases of “disparate impact” in which 
facially neutral qualification standards incidentally disadvan- 
tage a protected group. According to Echazabal, the defense 
does not apply to “disparate treatment.” An employer can 
justify disparate treatment under Title VII only if it satisfies the 
more stringent “bona fide occupational qualification” defense. 
There is no BFOQ defense under the ADA, so it must be the 
case, Echazabal asserts, that disparate treatment is never proper 
under the ADA. Echazabal points to no statutory text, legisla- 
tive history, or caselaw support for this strained interpretation. 


Echazabal’s effort to narrow the scope of the ADA’s 
business necessity defense ignores a crucial difference between 
race, gender, and age, on the one hand, and physical infirmity 
on the other. While employment action based on race is almost 
never justifiable, and action based on gender or age is almost 
always invidious and hence justifiable only if an employer 
satisfies the rigorous requirements of the BFOQ defense, 
disability conditions are far more likely to correlate directly to 
safety and efficiency considerations in the workplace. That 
practical distinction led Congress to strike a different balance 
business needs in the ADA than in the civil rights laws, as 
shown by the lack of a BFOQ requirement in the ADA, by 
Congress's repeated provision instead of a flexible and broadly 
applicable “business necessity” defense (§§ 12112(b)(6), 
12112(d)(4)(A), 12113(a)), and by Congress’s specific authori- 
zation of pre-employment medical examinations and tests to 
detect medical conditions that create safety risks and undermine 
efficient business operations. §§ 12112(d), 12113(a). See, e.g., 
Statement of President Bush, 1990 U.S.C.C.A.N. at 602 (ADA 
strikes a “careful balance” between “the rights of individuals 
with disabilities and the legitimate interests of business”); H.R. 
Conf. Rep., 1 Leg. Hist. 64-65 (Congress did “not intend for 
this Act to override any legitimate medical standards or 


——— 


13 


requirements established by * * * empioyers for applicants for 
safety * * * sensitive positions”). 

Echazabal’s attempt to superimpose Title VII's distinction 
between disparate impact and disparate treatment upon the 
ADA is not compatible with the ADA’s language and structure. 
The ADA expressly permits an employer to require that an 
applicant pass a “medical examination” and to deny employ- 
ment based “on the results” of that examination if doing so is 
“job-related” and a “business necessity.” § 12112(d)(3), (4). 
Echazabal (at 26-27) regards employment decisions based on 
individualized determinations that the candidate has a disquali- 
fying medical condition as “disparate treatment,” so Section 
12112(d) completely refutes his contention that disparate 
treatment cannot be justified under the ADA as “job-related and 
consistent with business necessity.” So does the Section 
12113(b) direct threat defense, which Echazabal concedes (at 
32) permits “disparate treatment.” 


Echazabal’s sole prop for his argument is his demonstrably 
incorrect claim (at 28) that “the Section 121 13(a) ‘qualification 
standards’ defense” reaches “only” criteria that “‘screen out or 
tend to screen out’” disabled persons, which is supposedly 
“language of discriminatory effect rather than disparate treat- 
ment.” But Section 12113(a) is on its face broader than Echaz- 
abal pretends. It extends not only to “qualification standards,” 
but also to individualized “tests,” such as the medical tests that 
Chevron relied on here and that are specifically authorized by 
Section 12112(d). In addition, it expressly authorizes not only 
standards and tests that “screen out or tend to screen out” 
disabled persons, but also those that “otherwise deny a job or 
benefit to an individual with a disability.” That all encompass- 
ing language plainly covers denials that result from disparate 
treatment as well as from the application of neutral standards. 


Contrary to Echazabal (at 29), EEOC regulations do not 
“limit” the business necessity defense to cases of discriminatory 


14 


impact—nor could they reasonably do so, given the plain 
meaning of the statute. 29 C.F.R. § 1630.15(b)(1) mirrors the 
language of ADA § 12113(a), making the business necessity 
test applicable to any test or standard that denies employment 
as a result of disability. Though 29 C.F.R. § 1630.15(c) does 
refer to disparate impacts, it lists only “some * * * potential 
defenses” and is “not intended to be exhaustive.” 29 C.F.R. Part 
1630, App., § 1630.15. Section 1630.14(c) separately permits 
individualized medical examinations that are “job-related and 
consistent with business necessity.” 

Beyond the obvious legal infirmities in the scheme Echaz- 
abal would superimpose on the ADA, he is also incorrect to 
claim (at 26-27) that Chevron engaged in “classic * * * dispa- 
rate treatment” by declining to hire him “because of” disability. 
Chevron refused to hire Echazabal because medical examina- 
tions and tests showed that he would be at grave risk of serious 
injury or death from exposure to hepatotoxins in the coker 
unit—a decision that would have been the same regardless of 
whether Echazabal’s condition was a “disability” within the 
meaning of the ADA. Chevron’s criterion was “safety,” not 
“disability.” In fact, Chevron could not reliably determine at the 
time of its decision whether Echazabal was “disabled.” 


Chevron focused the medical tests on a neutral, safety-based 
qualification standard that precluded from employment in the 
coker unit not anyone with a “disability,” but anyone who could 
not satisfy the “physical ability” and “environmental demand” 
of working in an environment containing specified “contami- 
nants and chemicals,” including liver-toxic “hydrocarbon 
liquids & vapors” and “solvents.” J.A. 65-66. Thus, even on 
Echazabal’s incorrect theory that the business necessity defense 
is limited to cases in which a neutral qualification standard has 
a disparate impact on persons with disabilities, it applies here. 

Dothard and Johnson Controls provide further support for 
Chevron’s employment decision. In Dothard, this Court 


15 


recognized that the “touchstone” for “business necessity” is that 
an employment practice assures “safe and efficient job perfor- 
mance.” It stated that an employer may use “a test for appli- 
cants that measures * * * directly” whether they can safely and 
efficiently perform the job. 433 U.S. at 331-332 & n.14. 
Chevron’s medical examination conformed to that model. The 
Court went on to approve an explicitly gender-based regulation 
denying women certain safety-sensitive prison positions, on the 
ground that the employer had established that male gender was 
a BFOQ—“an extremely narrow exception” to the non-discrim- 
ination rule that is considerably more demanding than business 
necessity. It was in the context of its discussion whether gender 
is a BFOQ, not in the context of the business necessity defense, 
that this Court stated that women “[iJn the usual case” can 
choose whether “a particular job is too dangerous.” /d. at 334- 
335. Read as a whole, Dothard confirms that an individualized 
medical examination to determine whether an applicant can 
safely do the job (not whether he is “disabled”), like that used 
by Chevron, passes muster as a business necessity. 


In Johnson Controls this Court agreed with the EEOC that 
a sweeping, facially discriminatory prohibition on employing 
any woman of childbearing age from jobs working with lead, 
designed to protect her fertility and fetuses, did not satisfy the 
BFOQ analysis. The “restrictive” BFOQ standard was not met 
because lead also harmed male fertility, the Pregnancy Disabil- 
ity Act explicitly barred discrimination based on the potential 
for pregnancy, harm to a woman’s fertility or fetus would not 
interfere with her ability to do the job, and OSHA had specified 
procedures for mitigating the risk short of exclusion from the 
job. 499 U.S. at 198-204, 208. Even under the BFOQ standard, 
the Court recognized, “safety” problems that “actually 
interfer{e] with the employee's ability to perform the job” 
would suffice. Jd. at 204; see also id at 213 (even under the 
BFOQ defense, avoiding “costs” and “injury * * * to employ- 
ees” were valid “business concerns”) (White, J., concurring); 
id. at 224 (Scalia, J., concurring). Unlike reduced fertility, 


16 


serious liver disease exacerbated by hepatotoxic exposures in 
the workplace would obviously interfere with Echazabal’s 
ability to do the plant helper job. 


V. No accommodation was reasonably available. Some 
amici— tellingly, not Echazabal himself—suggest that Chevron 
might have made some “reasonable accommodation” for Echaz- 
abal’s liver condition. But Echazabal did not ask Chevron for an 
accommodation at the time of Chevron’s decision, thereby 
failing to engage in the “interactive process” the statute 
requires. E. g., Loulseged v. Akzo Nobel Inc. , 178 F.3d 731, 735- 
736 (Sth Cir. 1999); 29 C.F.R. § 1630.9. Furthermore, Chevron 
tendered evidence in support of its motion for summary judg- 
ment that no accommodation was possible that would permit 
Echazabal safely to perform the job. J.A. 34, 45, 49. As the 
district court held (J.A. 184 n.6), Echazabal never asserted in 
opposition to Chevron’s motion that the company “could or 
should” have made an accommodation, even though “[t}he 
employee retains at all times the burden of persua[sion] * * * 
that reasonable accommodations were available.” LaChance v. 
Duffy’s Draft House, 146 F.3d 832, 836 (11th Cir. 1998). That 
was not an accidental omission. Echazabal did raise the 
accommodation issue as to his employer, Irwin. J.A. 184 n.6. It 
is too late now for Echazabal to argue that a reasonable accom- 
modation was available for his condition and, indeed, he does 
not attempt to do so. See Reno v. Koray, 515 U.S. 50, 55 n.2 
(1995). 


The suggestion of Echazabal’s physician witnesses that 
Chevron could monitor his medical condition is no accom- 
modation at all. Chevron monitored his condition for three 
years, during which time Interferon treatment failed and his 
enzyme levels confirmed the existence of severe and incurable 
disease. Monitoring could not eliminate the danger that every 
moment spent exposed to hepatotoxins put Echazabal at 
incremental risk. See Gonzales v. New Braunfels, 176 F.3d 834, 
838 (Sth Cir. 1999) (retesting that does not “eliminate the 


17 


inherent safety risk” posed by the employee’s condition is not 
a reasonable accommodation). 


VI. Echazabal bore the burden of proof. Chevron’s only 
burden under the ADA was to show that its medical exam- 
inations, tests, and qualification standards were job-related and 
consistent with business necessity—a burden it easily met. 42 
U.S.C. §§ 12112(d)(4)(A), 12113(a). At that point, the burden 
shifted to Echazabal to prove that it was improper for Chevron 
to have relied on the medical opinions in determining that he 
could not safely work in the plant helper position. See Beazer, 
440 U.S. at 587 n.31 (once the employer shows that it acted to 
further “legitimate employment goals of safety and efficiency,” 
the “ultimate burden” shifted to the plaintiff to prove a viola- 
tion); Furnco, 438 U.S. at 578 (the employer’s showing that its 
action “is reasonably related to the achievement of some 
legitimate goal” shifts the burden to the plaintiff to prove that 
action was nevertheless discriminatory); Watson, 487 U.S. at 
997-998 (an employer must show “that its employment prac- 
tices are based on legitimate business reasons”; once it does so, 
“the ultimate burden of proving” discrimination “remains with 
the plaintiff at all times”). Because medical examinations to 
determine safety are not “discrimination” at all according to 
Section 12112, a plaintiff complaining about such an examina- 
tion clearly bears the burden to prove that the examination was 
improper in order to establish that he was “discriminated” 
against because of his disability. Chevron made out, and 
Echazabal did not rebut, the business necessity defense. 


Echazabal’s claim a/so fails because he did not prove as an 
initial matter that he was a “qualified individual” within the 
ADA’s protection. Neither respondent (at 45) nor the United 
States (at 24) disputes that Echazabal must prove that he is 
“qualified” under Section 12112(a). Both contend, however, 
that this does not require him to show that he can perform job 
tasks safely. That is not a tenable argument. 


18 


The Act defines a “qualified individual” as someone who 
“can perform the essential functions of the employment posi- 
tion.” 42 U.S.C. § 12111(8). That language demands a forward- 
looking inquiry into the individual’s capacity to carry on essen- 
tial tasks. A person cannot reasonably be deemed capable of 
performance if he is unusually susceptible to serious injury or 
death from the job. Even Echazabal acknowledges (at 48) that 
an employee “‘cannot’ perform essential job tasks” where 
“imminent death or injury will prevent him from completing 
those tasks.” Reading the term “can perform” narrowly to mean 
“perform right now,” however, Echazabal suggests that this 
limitation applies only when “an employee seeks a job in which 
he will immediately die or suffer serious injury.” 


This case illustrates the absurdity of Echazabal’s approach. 
Though medical science did not allow Chevron to predict that 
Echazabal would be grievously injured or killed on his very 
first day on the job, it did show that Echazabal had degenerative 
liver disease and was in a high risk category, susceptible to 
catastrophic liver failure at any time. J.A. 83-84. In these 
circumstances, Echazabal failed to show that he could perform 
the plant helper job on an ongoing basis. See Chiari, 920 F.2d 
at 317 (employee with Parkinson’s disease was not qualified for 
job where he posed “a substantial risk” to himself and others 
because “disease was degenerative,” meaning “physical 
exertion would become progressively more dangerous to him”). 


The United States concedes (at 26) that in some cases “there 
will be substantial overlap between the issue whether an 
individual is qualified and whether the individual poses a threat 
to health and safety,” citing “firefighters” and “pilots” as 
“examples” of workers raising “paramount” safety concerns. 
Chevron’s plant helper position is also such a job. Chevron’s 
written job description is entitled to consideration in determin- 
ing the position’s “essential functions.” 42 U.S.C. § 12111(8); 
29 C.F.R. § 1630.2(n)(3)(ii). That description made clear that 
a plant helper must be able to withstand exposure to hepatotoxic 


19 


chemicals. Daily exposure is “integral to [the] job,” and 
someone with Echazabal’s exceptional vulnerability to expo- 
sure cannot satisfy the job’s “essential functions.” See Amego, 
110 F.3d at 143-144 (treating threat to heath and safety as part 
of question whether employee was “qualified” where the safety 
risk arose “in the context of a core function of the job”); 
Borgialli, 235 F.3d at 1295. 


Echazabal seeks to avoid this conclusion by citing (at 49) 
his historical ability to work under similar conditions. But 
Hepatitis C is ordinarily asymptomatic for several years. And 
past is not prologue in anticipating its development. Echazabal 
himself concedes (at 47) that the relevant inquiry is not whether 
he has performed the job; what matters is his current medical 
condition. See also U.S. Br. 24. Echazabal was not diagnosed 
with Hepatitis C until 1993. By the time Chevron refused to 
hire him in early 1996 lengthy treatment with Interferon had 
failed, a history of eight liver tests showed his disease was 
“severe,” and it could be concluded, given his plethora of risk 
factors, that working with hepatotoxins would put him at high 
risk for liver failure, cirrhosis, liver cancer, transplant, and 
death. Supra, Section I. That, not his past work, was the reality 
Chevron had to take into account. 


There is no merit to the claim that employees should not be 
required to show that they can perform a job safely because the 
employer has “superior information and knowledge about work- 
place requirements and operations.” Amicus Br. of National 
Council on Disability 22. Chevron set forth its job requirements 
and conditions in the job description and, during the course of 
discovery, Echazabal’s experts examined the plant and reached 
their own conclusions about its environment. Echazabal knew 
his own work record and medical diagnosis. 


Requiring Echazabal to prove he is “qualified” to perform 
the job’s essential tasks without immediate danger to himself 
conforms to judicial and regulatory interpretations of the Re- 


20 


habilitation Act, upon which Congress based the ADA. See Pet. 
Br. 46-47. The United States admits (at 27-28) that “concerns 
about threats to self * * * are considered in the determination 
whether a plaintiff is a ‘qualified individual’” under the 
Rehabilitation Act. Prior to passage of the ADA, the EEOC and 
the courts consistently read the term “qualified” in the Rehabili- 
tation Act to subsume questions of health and safety. 43 Fed. 
Reg. 12295; 29 C.F.R. § 1614.203(a)(6); Arline, 480 U.S. at 
287 n.16; supra, p. 6 (citing cases). 


Echazabal argues (at 45) that if an ADA plaintiff must 
prove he can perform essential functions safely to be “quali- 
fied,” Section 12113’s “defenses” are superfluous. The ADA, 
however, is full of repetitions and redundancies. As we ex- 
plained in our opening brief (at 44), the Act makes multiple 
uses of the concept of “reasonable accommodation,” for 
example, including it both us an element of the plaintiff's prima 
facie case and as part of the employer’s “undue hardship” 
defense. See also Amicus Br. for Chamber of Commerce 26-28 
& n.11. Echazabal’s proposed reading will do nothing to 
eliminate redundancy in the ADA, and linguistic overlap 
between Sections 12112 and 12113 provides no ground for 
rejecting a reading otherwise consistent with the Act’s language 
and adoption of Rehabilitation Act standards. Though Congress 
divided the elements of an ADA claim and defenses among 
various sections of the statute, this structure does not alter the 
fact that, as under Title VII, a plaintiff “clearly [has the] 
ultimate burden of proving a violation,” which requires him to 
prove he was a qualified person who was discriminated against 
because of disability. Beazer, 440 U.S. at 587 n.31. Echazabal 
cannot satisfy that ultimate burden without proving that he 
could safely work in the coker unit. 


CONCLUSION 
The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
