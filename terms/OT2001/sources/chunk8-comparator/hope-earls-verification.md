# Hope and Earls: historical source-control verification

Historical evidence only; legal cutoff June 27, 2002. No simulated commitment, vote, coalition, or assignment is proposed here.

## Scope and method

Read only the relevant Hope/Earls sections of the scoped June 27 comparator, the complete Cornell syllabi and every separate writing and footnote for dockets 01-309 and 01-332, and the saved official reporter texts through whole-text comparison and review of unmatched passages and critical reporter pages. No current model draft, commitment, Stone case-position input, Canonical Decision Record, or full brief was read. Underlying affidavits, exhibits, regulations, and reports are described as the opinions report them; they were not independently inspected here.

The official reporter controls minor slip-text corrections. In Hope, majority note 1 correctly describes review of a grant of summary judgment; Cornell says denial at one point. Thomas's note 2 correctly dates the June incident June 7 in the reporter, where Cornell says June 11. The quoted pleading in Thomas's note 7 says May 11, 1997, but the opinions' narrative and chronology consistently identify the relevant incident as May 11, 1995. Do not transform that quoted date discrepancy into a third incident or different year.

## Hope v. Pelzer, 536 U.S. 730

### Historical action and topology

No. 01-309; argued April 17, 2002; decided June 27, 2002; review of the Eleventh Circuit, 240 F.3d 975.

| Writing | Historical author and joins | Reporter pages |
|---|---|---|
| Court, all Parts I-V | Stevens; O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | 733-748 |
| Dissent, all Parts I-II and notes | Thomas; Rehnquist, Scalia | 748-764 |

The result was reversal, 6-3. Both the official reporter's syllabus and the Court's operative sentence say reversed; there is no express remand direction in the opinion. The comparator's phrase reversed and remanded should be tightened to the published disposition, while recognizing that reversal leaves further litigation rather than entering a damages judgment.

Primary sources: [official reporter](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-536/pdf/USREPORTS-536-730.pdf), [Court opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/01-309.ZO.html), [Thomas dissent](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/01-309.ZD.html).

### Two incidents and individual responsibility

The Court reviewed summary judgment for the guards, viewing the record favorably to Hope. These are assumed allegations for that review, not findings after trial:

- **May 11, 1995:** After an argument with another inmate on a highway work detail, both were returned to prison and attached to the hitching post. Hope remained two hours, until a captain determined the other inmate had caused the incident. Water and bathroom breaks were offered every 15 minutes and logged. His raised arms grew tired and movement caused the cuffs to cut his wrists. The Court did not import June's seven-hour duration or denied-break allegations into May. See 734-735.
- **June 7, 1995:** A dispute on arrival at the worksite led to a struggle with a guard. Hope was subdued, handcuffed, placed in leg irons, and returned to prison. He alleged seven hours on the post, exposure without his shirt, one or two water breaks, no bathroom breaks, and a guard's deliberate taunting about water. The majority used those allegations to describe aggravated danger and humiliation. See 735, 738.
- **Defendant-specific limits:** Thomas identified the remaining defendants as Officer Gene McClaran, Sergeant Mark Pelzer, and Lieutenant Jim Gates. McClaran allegedly ordered attachment in May; Pelzer allegedly attached Hope in May; Gates allegedly attached him in May and June. No June participation was alleged against McClaran or Pelzer. Thomas stressed that Hope did not allege Gates supervised June confinement or personally prolonged it, removed clothing, denied water, or taunted him. The majority expressly did not decide the sufficiency of attribution to any named officer. See 746-747, 748-752.
- **Identity and evidentiary disputes:** Thomas's note 6 describes conflicting accounts of who attached Hope in May, including an incident report naming Keith Gates and Mark Dempsey, and an unresolved dispute whether Keith Gates was Lieutenant Jim Gates. Thomas assumed identity for his analysis; that was not a finding. Thomas also disputed the no-bathroom-break allegation and the all-day shirtlessness account, pointing to the District Court's contrary statement and photographs showing a shirt. These remain disputes described in the opinions, not independently resolved facts. See 749-751 nn.6-8.

The majority's express assumption in Part V covered both occasions: extended attachment apparently inflicting gratuitous pain or discomfort after any immediate threat or continuing work refusal had ended. On that assumption the attachment itself was unconstitutional; clothing, water, and bathroom deprivations aggravated the violation but were not indispensable to its reasoning. This is not a rule that every initial attachment to any restraint, regardless of duration, emergency, justification, or personal involvement, violates clearly established law. See 746-748.

### Rule versus application

**Constitutional rule:** Gratuitous infliction of pain without penological justification violates the Eighth Amendment. For prison conditions, deliberate indifference to health or safety is the subjective requirement; obvious risk may support an inference of that state of mind. Whitley and Rhodes supplied the prohibition of unnecessary/wanton pain, Hudson the conditions inquiry, and Farmer the knowledge/inference rule. On the assumed facts, the security emergency had ended and the punishment lacked justification. See 736-738.

**Notice rule:** Anderson requires the unlawfulness to be apparent in light of pre-existing law; it does not require a prior decision on the identical act. Lanier equated civil qualified-immunity notice with the fair-warning standard under criminal section 242. A general constitutional rule can apply with obvious clarity to new facts. Factually similar cases strongly support notice but are not categorically necessary. Conversely, the majority preserved Lanier's qualification that an earlier decision expressly leaving an application open can make a high degree of factual specificity necessary. The question was notice under the law in 1995. Lanier's 1997 explanation clarifies the governing notice standard; it is not itself a pre-1995 warning to these guards. See 739-741.

**Actual immunity decision:** The Court did more than reject a rigid factual-match requirement. It concluded that the alleged treatment had sufficient fair warning to defeat summary judgment on the qualified-immunity ground the Eleventh Circuit used. But Part V expressly reserved whether and to what extent each defendant could be responsible for the acts if proved; it preserved defenses other than qualified immunity on the appellate court's stated ground. It did not enter liability, damages, or a conclusive judgment that every named guard personally performed every alleged act. See 741-748.

### Notice sources and qualifications

- **Gates v. Collier:** Binding pre-1981 Fifth Circuit authority in the Eleventh Circuit prohibited prolonged handcuffing to fences/cells and painful forced positions. The majority found no material notice distinction between a fence and a purpose-built post. Thomas found the brief account insufficiently specific about duration, conditions, purpose, or the later Farmer state-of-mind requirement. See 742-743, 762-763.
- **Ort v. White:** Its holding allowed temporary denial of water to obtain work compliance; the majority relied on its reasoning distinguishing immediate coercion from later gratuitous punishment and warning against threatening health. The majority expressly did not treat Ort's actual holding as invalidating the same practice. Thomas thought its permission to enforce work rules, and its conditional observations about different facts, failed to give clear notice here. See 743-744, 761-762.
- **ADOC regulation:** It authorized the post for work refusal or disruption but required logs, quarter-hour water/bathroom offers, and release to the squad when the prisoner said he was ready to work. May's log existed; June's did not. The majority treated disregard of release safeguards as supporting knowledge of wrongful punishment. Thomas stressed the authorization, compliance in May, lack of allegations assigning June supervision to Gates, and no alleged request to return to work. Violation of an agency rule alone is not substituted for a constitutional violation. See 744, 760-761.
- **DOJ report:** The 1994 report and exchanges with ADOC condemned systematic, unjustified corporal punishment and buttressed the majority's objective notice analysis. The majority acknowledged no record evidence that its contents reached these defendants. Thomas viewed the report as neither actual notice nor sufficient legal clarity given contrary district decisions. Do not state that the guards personally received the report. See 744-746, 759-760.
- **Lower district decisions and Austin:** The majority distinguished prior district cases by compliance options, breaks, or short duration and treated binding circuit authority as controlling. Thomas considered the numerous prior district decisions permitting restraint or granting immunity powerful evidence of reasonable uncertainty. Austin v. Hopper, decided in 1998, supplied factual findings incorporated into this summary-judgment record, not law giving notice in 1995. The DOJ report was judicially noticed on appeal, and the regulation was added at the appellate court's request. See 734 n.1, 737 n.7, 744 n.11, 747 n.12, 755-758.

### What the dissent does and does not dispute

Thomas, joined by Rehnquist and Scalia, expressly accepted that conduct can be obviously unlawful without a materially similar prior case. He denied that the Eleventh Circuit imposed an absolute factual-twin prerequisite, cited its own obvious-violation decisions, and treated the actual uncertainty and each officer's limited alleged role as decisive. His disagreement is therefore not support for an absolute factual-match rule. See 752-755.

He would affirm immunity because reasonable officers could regard initial restraint after disruption as authorized discipline, prior district decisions supported that view, Gates and Ort did not sufficiently clarify the circumstances, and the majority attributed other officers' aggravating acts to these defendants. He also questioned deliberate-indifference proof. His note 12 reserved his original-understanding objection to applying the Eighth Amendment to nonsentenced prison conditions but expressly applied the existing contrary doctrine here; that reservation was not a holding replacing the governing rule. See 756-764.

Stevens's authored opinion and the full joins of O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer support the majority rule, its application, and Part V's reservation together. There are no separate majority writings supplying narrower grounds. Rehnquist and Scalia joined Thomas's whole dissent; agreement across the Court about the possibility of obvious notice must not be converted into agreement on reversal or denial of immunity in this case.

## Board of Education v. Earls, 536 U.S. 822

### Historical action and topology

No. 01-332; argued March 19, 2002; decided June 27, 2002; Tenth Circuit judgment, 242 F.3d 1264, reversed, 5-4. The District Court had granted summary judgment to the School District in an action seeking declaratory and injunctive relief. The Supreme Court's operative text says reversal, with no express remand direction.

| Writing | Historical author and joins | Reporter pages |
|---|---|---|
| Court, all Parts I-III | Thomas; Rehnquist, Scalia, Kennedy, Breyer | 825-838 |
| Concurrence; full joinder in Court opinion | Breyer alone | 838-842 |
| Dissent | O'Connor; Souter | 842 |
| Dissent, all Parts I-II | Ginsburg; Stevens, O'Connor, Souter | 842-855 |

No partial joins or judgment-only votes. Primary sources: [official reporter](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-536/pdf/USREPORTS-536-822.pdf), [Court](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/01-332.ZO.html), [Breyer](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/01-332.ZC.html), [O'Connor](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/01-332.ZD.html), [Ginsburg](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/01-332.ZD1.html).

### Program and procedural limits

The written 1998 policy covered middle/high-school extracurricular participation generally; actual implementation covered competitive activities sanctioned by the Oklahoma Secondary Schools Activities Association, including academic team, agricultural/home-economics groups, band, choir, pom pon, cheerleading, and athletics. Testing occurred before participation, randomly during participation, and upon reasonable suspicion. The suit did not challenge athletics testing or reasonable-suspicion testing. Earls had standing; James's standing, questioned because of grades, was unnecessary to decide. See 825-827 nn.1-2.

Testing sought illegal drugs, not medical conditions or authorized prescription use. Students produced urine behind a closed stall while a faculty monitor listened outside; samples were split and sealed. Policy required confidential separate files and need-to-know disclosure. Results were not supplied to law enforcement, did not affect academics, and did not produce ordinary school discipline; consequences concerned extracurricular participation and counseling. See 826, 832-834.

The escalation terms matter: after a first positive, parents were contacted and continued participation required proof of counseling within five days of the meeting and another test two weeks later. A second positive brought a 14-day extracurricular suspension, four hours of counseling, and monthly tests. A third produced exclusion for the rest of the school year or 88 school days, whichever was longer. Do not turn the first positive into automatic year-long exclusion or the phrase no school discipline into no consequences. See 833-834.

### Controlling reasoning and disputed predicates

The Court applied a fact-specific balance under historical Vernonia, not a categorical authorization of all school drug testing. School custody and responsibility supported special needs beyond criminal law enforcement. The balance considered students' diminished privacy, the particular collection/use safeguards, and the importance and effectiveness of preventing drug use. Extracurricular regulation diminished privacy further; athletic communal undress was relevant but not essential to Vernonia. See 828-834.

The majority accepted the District Court's finding of a drug problem, citing apparent intoxication, student statements, marijuana near a parking lot, drugs/paraphernalia in a club member's car, and community reports. It rejected a requirement of an epidemic or a demonstrated problem among a sufficient number of the precise group tested. Childhood drug risks permitted preventive action before widespread local use; ordinary student health and safety mattered, not only unusual activity-specific hazards. Chandler, Von Raab, and Skinner supplied the preventive-testing discussion. See 834-837.

Testing was a reasonably effective response; the Constitution did not require testing only the most likely users, proving athletes' leadership in a drug culture, or selecting the least intrusive alternative. The majority also identified targeting/stigma and administrative difficulties with suspicion-based testing. It expressly withheld judgment on the policy's wisdom. See 837-838. This is not a finding that every possible broader program, different disclosure practice, or law-enforcement use would be reasonable.

**Implementation dispute:** Respondents alleged prescription lists were accessible to others and results shared with sponsors without need. The majority regarded the choir teacher as having an independent need to know on trips, noted that no student was alleged actually to have seen the information, and did not treat the cited carelessness as materially increasing intrusion. Ginsburg stressed that the written policy called for sealed medication lists not viewed by employees, and that disputed confidentiality compliance should not be assumed in the School District's favor at summary judgment. Preserve the distinction between the policy's requirements, alleged practice, and each opinion's assessment. See 833, 848-849.

**Drug-problem dispute:** Ginsburg emphasized the superintendent's contemporaneous reports describing non-alcohol/tobacco drugs as not a major problem, plus absence of proof of greater drug use or unusual safety risk among the selected nonathletes. Those were competing evaluations of the record and the proper balance; the majority did not find that nonathletic clubs led an epidemic. See 834-837, 849-852.

### Each separate writing's premises

**Breyer:** He joined the entire Court opinion. His concurrence emphasized a serious national problem, limited success of supply-focused enforcement, schools' broader caretaking duties, and a demand-reduction program using counseling rather than criminal or academic punishment. Testing could furnish a socially acceptable reason to resist peer pressure. He acknowledged genuine embarrassment for some students, valued public participation in developing this particular policy, and stressed that testing did not encompass the whole school and that conscientious refusal cost extracurricular participation rather than school attendance. He also feared discriminatory or stigmatizing suspicion-based enforcement. He did not claim to know the program would work; he found the effort constitutionally reasonable. Community participation was a consideration, not a finding of unanimous individual consent. See 838-842.

**O'Connor, joined by Souter:** Both preserved their disagreement with Vernonia and concluded this program failed even under that existing precedent's balancing. They joined Ginsburg's dissent for the merits analysis. Do not present their position as solely a refusal to recognize Vernonia as precedent. See 842.

**Ginsburg, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Souter:** Vernonia's athletes had reduced privacy, heightened physical risks, and leadership in a severe local drug culture; Tecumseh's broad nonathletic group lacked those features. Optional extracurricular participation is educationally important, much like honors courses, rather than a sufficient waiver of privacy. Occasional shared lodging/restrooms is not routine athletic communal undress. She challenged confidentiality assumptions at summary judgment, distinguished general drug risks from safety-sensitive customs/railway duties, and found no reasonable fit between testing this comparatively low-risk population and the stated need. The policy could discourage protective extracurricular involvement among students needing it most. Random testing was added to, not substituted for, suspicion-based testing, weakening the argument that it eliminated selective targeting. Chandler supplied the distinction between a concrete special need and a symbolic anti-drug message; schools' teaching responsibilities favored modeling constitutional safeguards. She would affirm the Tenth Circuit. See 842-855 and nn.1-4.

Thomas authored the majority's balance; Rehnquist, Scalia, and Kennedy supplied full joins without separate Earls writings. Breyer's added emphases are his own, while his full join supplies the fifth vote for the Court's complete rationale. Stevens joined Ginsburg but not O'Connor's separate statement; Souter joined both dissents. These historical positions are evidence for later comparison, not predetermined simulated outcomes.
