## Stewart v. Smith, 536 U.S. 856

**Historical public result:** No. 01-339, decided June 28, 2002; Ninth Circuit judgment, 241 F.3d 1191, reversed and remanded for further proceedings consistent with the opinion. The reporter and Cornell texts disclose no argued date. Sources: [official reporter](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-536/pdf/USREPORTS-536-856.pdf), [Cornell opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/01-339.ZPC.html).

### Procedural predicates and separate state-law source

Smith was convicted in Arizona in 1982 of first-degree murder, kidnapping, and sexual assault, receiving death on the murder count and consecutive 21-year terms on the others. His federal petition alleged ineffective assistance of trial counsel at sentencing. He had presented that claim in his third Arizona Rule 32 petition in 1995; the Pima County Superior Court found it waived under Rule 32.2(a)(3) because he omitted it from two earlier petitions. It rejected his asserted excuse that appellate and prior Rule 32 counsel were unwilling to challenge a colleague from the same Public Defender's office. The federal District Court treated that procedural ruling as a bar and rejected the same conflict argument. These are the prior courts' dispositions as reported, not fresh factual findings about trial counsel's performance. See 857-858.

The Ninth Circuit regarded the state rule as regularly followed and therefore adequate, but not independent of federal law. Its independence conclusion rested on its understanding that determining whether a claim had sufficient constitutional magnitude required assessing that particular claim's merits. The June 28 opinion addresses independence; adequacy was the Ninth Circuit's already accepted predicate, not a newly developed Supreme Court holding about every application of Arizona's rule. See 858.

The historical Supreme Court had granted review and certified a state-law question in Stewart v. Smith, 534 U.S. 157 (2001). That is historical procedural history only. It does not establish that a corresponding Supreme Court certification or other order occurred in the simulated timeline.

**Separate source fact:** The Arizona Supreme Court's en banc response, Stewart v. Smith, 202 Ariz. 446, 46 P.3d 1067 (2002), interpreted what Rule 32.2(a)(3) required in 1995. As reproduced in the federal opinion, the need for a knowing, voluntary, intelligent waiver depended on the category of right implicated at the claim's core, not whether the particular alleged violation actually occurred. This is a state-court interpretation of state procedure, analytically separate from the historical Supreme Court order requesting it. The supplied federal opinion establishes that the historical response existed and what it said; this memo does not establish its admission into the present record, a new in-world certification, or the full contents of the state decision beyond the published recitation. See 859-860.

### Federal holding and exact qualifications

1. **State classification is not federal merits adjudication.** Rule 32.2(a)(3), as authoritatively construed, required identification of the type of right, not adjudication of a federal constitutional violation. The Court found no indication that this categorization itself depended on interpreting what federal law required. Consequently this waiver determination was independent of federal law. Delaware v. Prouse supplied the distinction between identifying a right and resting on a federal ruling. See 859-860.
2. **The direct-review/habeas relationship was assumed.** Ake v. Oklahoma supplied the rule that a state procedural determination depending on a federal constitutional ruling is not independent for direct review. Citing Coleman v. Thompson, Stewart assumed, without deciding, that the same independence standard governed the federal habeas question, because Arizona's ruling satisfied it anyway. Do not convert this assumption into a new holding that every direct-review jurisdiction rule and federal habeas default rule are identical. See 860.
3. **The actual state order still mattered.** A facially independent state rule does not settle the issue if the particular state decision rests primarily on a federal merits ruling. The Court examined the order and found no such ruling. Its reference to a colorable ineffective-assistance claim explained why the asserted intra-office conflict did not excuse omission; it was not an adjudication that trial counsel had performed adequately or that the sentencing claim lacked merit. See 860-861.
4. **Cause remained open.** The Court expressly voiced no opinion whether Smith had supplied valid cause to overcome his state procedural default. Reversal and remand corrected the independence ruling; the decision did not conclusively dispose of the cause question, adjudicate the underlying Sixth Amendment claim, or enter a new death sentence. See 861.

The state rule's heightened waiver requirement remains a qualification: claims implicating rights of sufficient constitutional magnitude require knowing, voluntary, intelligent waiver rather than mere omission. The opinion does not announce that every constitutional claim, or every ineffective-assistance claim, is waived merely because an earlier petition omitted it. Nor does it create a new exhaustion rule, make Arizona's classifications binding as federal merits law, or equate procedural default with lack of federal subject-matter jurisdiction.

## United States v. Bass, 536 U.S. 862

**Historical public result:** No. 01-1471, decided June 28, 2002; certiorari granted and Sixth Circuit judgment, 266 F.3d 532, summarily reversed. The operative text says reversal and contains no express remand direction. No argued date is disclosed. Sources: [official reporter](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-536/pdf/USREPORTS-536-862.pdf), [Cornell opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/01-1471.ZPC.html).

### Procedural predicates

A federal grand jury in the Eastern District of Michigan returned a second superseding indictment charging Bass, among other things, with intentionally killing two people with a firearm. The Government filed a notice seeking death. Bass, who was black, alleged racial selection and sought dismissal of the notice or, alternatively, discovery about federal capital charging. The District Court ordered discovery; after the Government stated it would not comply, the court dismissed the death notice. A divided Sixth Circuit panel affirmed the discovery order. The Supreme Court therefore reviewed the discovery threshold and the judgment sustaining that order, not a completed trial on selective prosecution. See 862-863.

The Government's announced noncompliance is an express historical procedural fact. It cannot independently justify discovery or establish the discrimination allegation; the Court reversed because the threshold for the order had not been met. The opinion does not adjudicate Bass's guilt, sentencing eligibility, or the final sentence.

### Evidence, comparators, and denominators

Historical Armstrong required some evidence of both discriminatory effect and discriminatory intent to obtain selective-prosecution discovery. On effect, the applicant needed a credible showing that similarly situated people of another race were not prosecuted. Bass resolved only that element. See 863-864.

- **Charging data:** The Sixth Circuit relied on national statistics reporting that black defendants were charged with death-eligible offenses more than twice as often as white defendants. The Court held that aggregate charge counts did not identify how similarly situated defendants were treated. The missing comparison is between defendants alike in legally relevant circumstances, not merely the racial composition of those already charged. The opinion does not supply an eligible-population denominator, offense-adjusted comparison, or list of matched defendants, and does not prescribe one exclusive statistical technique or a complete matching checklist. Do not transform the lower court's aggregate-count description into a proved race-specific prosecution rate among equivalent candidates. See 863-864.
- **Plea data:** Higher aggregate plea-bargain frequency for white defendants did not show that Bass had been denied comparable access to a bargain: he received an offer and rejected it, as the Court recited from the Government's petition. Completed bargains, offers, and acceptance decisions are distinct events. The opinion does not establish comparative offer terms or decide a claim supported by different evidence of unequal terms. See 864.
- **Local versus national evidence:** The Court assumed for argument that a nationwide showing could satisfy Armstrong instead of evidence concerning the actual decisionmakers in Bass's case. It held the presented numbers inadequate even under that assumption. A categorical requirement of local data, or categorical exclusion of national statistics, is not the holding. See 863-864.
- **Different decisions and decisionmakers:** The single footnote describes the January 1995 death-penalty protocol: the 93 United States Attorneys continued to decide whether to charge a capital-eligible offense; all such charged cases had to be submitted to the Attorney General, who decided whether to seek death. Those stages must not be collapsed. Aggregate charging data do not automatically establish race-based treatment at the Attorney General's later authorization stage. The source does not provide Bass-specific decisionmaker comparisons. See 863 n.*.

### Relief and scope

Bass was not entitled to the ordered discovery because he failed to provide relevant evidence of unequal treatment of similarly situated persons. The Court stopped there and did not independently decide discriminatory purpose. Its reliance on protecting a core executive function, drawn from Armstrong, reinforced enforcement of that threshold; it did not immunize charging decisions from constitutional review. See 863-864.

The summary reversal rejects the appellate judgment sustaining the unsupported discovery order, whose noncompliance had produced dismissal of the death notice. Do not describe the disposition as a finding of nondiscrimination, a final rejection of all possible selective-prosecution evidence, or an order imposing death. Do not add an express remand when recounting the historical decretal language.
