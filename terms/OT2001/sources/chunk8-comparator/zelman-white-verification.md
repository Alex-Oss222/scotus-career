## Zelman v. Simmons-Harris

### Result and exact opinion structure

Nos. 00-1751, 00-1777 (Hanna Perkins School), and 00-1779 (Taylor); argued February 20, 2002; decided June 27, 2002. Review of the Sixth Circuit, 234 F.3d 945. The Court reversed, 5-4, holding Ohio's Pilot Project Scholarship Program consistent with the Establishment Clause. The opinion's operative direction is reversal, not a separately stated remand or new injunction.

### Court's rule, application, and limits

The secular purpose was undisputed. The Court therefore decided effect: when neutral educational aid goes to a broad class of individuals, defined without religion, and reaches religious institutions wholly through recipients' genuine independent choice, advancement of religious teaching or perceived endorsement is attributable to the recipients. Mueller, Witters, and Zobrest supplied the private-choice line; the opinion distinguished direct aid rather than announcing unrestricted approval of direct institutional grants. See 648-653.

The Court found Ohio's scheme neutral: eligibility followed residence and financial need; religious and nonreligious private schools could participate; adjacent public districts could participate; low-income families received priority and greater aid. The relevant choice inquiry included reasonable educational alternatives throughout the state-supported Cleveland system: ordinary public education, public education with tutoring, private secular or religious schools, community schools, and magnet schools. It did not limit the denominator to voucher-using children. See 653-659.

The Court rejected constitutional conclusions drawn simply from the proportion of participating religious schools or the proportion of vouchers used there. Those proportions varied with local school supply and annual parental decisions; two secular voucher schools had converted to community schools, with students continuing at those institutions. The inquiry still required genuine independent choice; demographics did not substitute for that inquiry. Financial advantages favoring public/community options and a private-school copayment dispelled claims of government steering toward religion, but the Court expressly said those disincentives were not necessary to constitutionality. See 654, 657-660.

Nyquist was distinguished and limited, not expressly overruled. Its aid package exclusively favored private schools and their parents, included direct grants, and had the function and incentives of sustaining nonpublic sectarian institutions. Its reservation concerning generally available scholarships mattered. Zelman held Nyquist inapplicable to neutral assistance offered directly to a broad class of individuals without regard to religion. See 661-663. The majority rejected speculative political divisiveness as a basis for invalidating this program; it did not adjudicate future regulatory conflicts at recipient schools. See 662-663 n.7.

### Material facts and epistemic limits

- **Published statutory account:** Cleveland alone met the state-control criterion. New tuition assistance initially covered kindergarten through third grade, expanding through eighth grade. For families below 200% of the poverty line, assistance covered 90% of tuition, capped at $2,250; private-school copayment could not exceed $250. Other families could receive 75%, capped at $1,875, without a copayment cap, only after low-income demand was satisfied. Parents endorsed checks to their selected private schools. Tutorial grants were also available; the statute required the number of tutorial grants offered to equal the number of tuition scholarships. This is an entitlement/allocation structure, not proof that equal numbers of children actually used each benefit. See 645-647.
- **Published historical figures:** In 1999-2000, 56 private schools participated, 46 religious; no adjacent public district elected to participate; over 3,700 voucher students enrolled, approximately 96% in religious schools; 60% were below poverty. Separate figures addressed different years, populations, and public funding measures. Do not combine the Court's magnet-school funding figures with O'Connor's figures as though they were one stipulated accounting. See 647-648, 663-665.
- **Record character:** Taxpayers won summary judgment below. The Court relied on published record materials and its assessment that no evidence showed students lacked genuine secular opportunities. O'Connor's observation that no voucher-eligible student was shown to have been turned away is an absence-of-record-evidence proposition, not an affirmative finding that every secular option had unlimited capacity. See 670-675.
- **Disputed inference:** Souter inferred constrained secular choice from limited secular enrollment/capacity, tuition differences, the cap, religious schools' subsidies, and parental survey results; he criticized counting separate public-school programs. The majority and O'Connor disputed those inferences and their legal significance. Do not label either side's contested inference an undisputed trial finding. See 656-660 nn.4-6; 670-676; 697-707 nn.7-17.
- **Further qualifications:** The precise additional state funding for adjacent public districts was disputed (645-646 n.1). Souter noted that schools must permit in-kind contributions or services toward the low-income share (711 n.21). O'Connor's maximum $8.2 million estimate assumed every voucher was at the maximum low-income amount (664-665). Neither this estimate nor the record's absence of rejected applicants proves a premise beyond its stated bounds.

## Republican Party of Minnesota v. White

### Result and exact opinion structure

No. 01-521; argued March 26, 2002; decided June 27, 2002. The Court reversed and remanded the Eighth Circuit's decision, 247 F.3d 854, which had affirmed summary judgment for the defendant officials. The announce clause violated the First Amendment. The case was a pre-enforcement challenge seeking declaratory and injunctive relief, not review of a sanction imposed on Wersal.

| Writing | Author and joins | Reporter pages |
|---|---|---|
| Court, Parts I-IV and conclusion | Scalia; Rehnquist, O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas | 768-788 |
| Concurrence, full joinder in Court opinion | O'Connor alone | 788-792 |
| Concurrence, full joinder in Court opinion | Kennedy alone | 792-797 |
| Dissent | Stevens; Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | 797-803 |
| Dissent, Parts I-III and conclusion | Ginsburg; Stevens, Souter, Breyer | 803-821 |

All five Justices joined the entire Court opinion; all four dissenters joined both dissents. No partial joins, judgment-only concurrence, or separate Souter/Breyer writing.

### Court's rule, application, and reservations

The announce clause prohibited a judicial candidate, including an incumbent, from announcing views on disputed legal or political issues. State-adopted constructions allowed discussion of past decisions, general case law and judicial philosophy, and restricted the prohibition to issues likely to arise before the candidate's court. The majority nevertheless read it to bar statements of current position without any promise, including criticism of a decision coupled with opposition to following it under stare decisis. The latter conclusion relied on respondents' oral-argument concession. See 770-774.

Content-based restriction of core candidate speech required the State to establish narrow tailoring to a compelling interest. The Court separated three possible meanings of impartiality, addressing both actual impartiality and its appearance:

1. **No bias for or against a party:** The clause was not narrowly tailored because it broadly regulated issue views, rather than party bias. The Court expressly acknowledged that some prohibited statements could show bias against a class of litigants; narrow tailoring, not complete irrelevance, was the failure. See 775-777 and n.7.
2. **No preconceptions about legal issues:** This was not a compelling interest; knowledgeable judges inevitably have prior legal views, and complete absence of them is not desirable. Laird supplied the distinction between prior views and disqualifying bias. See 777-778.
3. **Willingness to reconsider prior views:** The Court did not decide whether this was a compelling interest. Campaign-only restriction was severely underinclusive when the same statements were allowed before candidacy and in permitted judicial teaching, writing, and speaking afterward. Respondents did not establish that nonpromissory campaign statements uniquely damaged openmindedness. See 778-784.

The Court found no universal longstanding historical tradition sufficient to sustain the restriction. It rejected conducting judicial elections while suppressing the relevant issue discussion, but expressly disclaimed holding that judicial and legislative campaigns must be regulated identically. See 783-788. The judgment reversed the grant of summary judgment to respondents and remanded for proceedings consistent with the opinion; it did not itself announce a comprehensive replacement judicial code.

**Questions reserved:** The distinct pledges-or-promises clause was unchallenged and expressly left undecided (770). Equivalence between Minnesota's construed announce clause and the ABA's 1990 commitment clause was not decided (773-774 n.5). Whether openmindedness and its appearance qualify as compelling was unnecessary (778). Candidates were not required to announce views (783-784 n.11). No general rule invalidating recusal standards, campaign-finance rules, partisan restrictions, or restrictions on sitting judges followed from this disposition. Kennedy expressly reserved a general sitting-judge speech restriction under a possible Pickering/Connick theory (796-797); that reservation belongs to his concurrence.

### Material facts, construction disputes, and assumptions

Wersal criticized Minnesota decisions in 1996; the disciplinary complaint was dismissed, with constitutional concerns expressed by the Board. He withdrew because of feared further complaints, ran again in 1998, sought guidance, and alleged self-censorship. The majority's account and the dissents agree that he had never been sanctioned under this clause; they disagree about what the Board's responses and limiting constructions show. See 768-774; 799-800 n.2; 810-812 and n.2. Treat his chilled-speech description as the pleaded account reported by the Court, not proof of a prior enforcement action.

The crucial division about clause scope is legal: Ginsburg treated the state-adopted Eighth Circuit construction as limiting it to statements that effectively commit a candidate to decide an issue, not general views, and treated discussion of past cases as unqualifiedly permitted. She rejected enlarging that authoritative construction through counsel's spontaneous oral-argument answers. The majority read the same materials more broadly. No source-control memo can turn this disagreement into a shared stipulated meaning. See 772 n.4; 809-812.

The dissents' inference that campaign announcements create special incentives to prejudge was not a trial finding that every candidate would honor a view regardless of argument. Conversely, the majority's conclusion that respondents failed their strict-scrutiny burden was not a finding that campaigns can never produce bias. Stevens's concern about classes of litigants was expressly acknowledged as a possible concern by the majority. See 776-777 n.7, 780-784, 800-802, 815-820.

|---|---|
| Scalia | Authored the three-meaning analysis, treating legal views as distinct from party bias and campaign-only regulation as unsupported underinclusiveness. Meaningful candidate discussion received core protection even if some special judicial regulation could be justified. |
| Rehnquist | Full joinder in that analysis. The opinion relied on his Laird memorandum for the inevitability and value of prior legal views; no separate White writing states an additional premise. |
| O'Connor | Full joinder. | Kennedy | Full joinder, plus an independently broader preference for invalidating content-based candidate-speech restrictions outside recognized First Amendment exceptions without balancing. Judicial integrity remained an interest of the highest order; ethics standards and recusal stricter than due process could protect it. He declined broad condemnation of elected judges and distinguished candidate regulation from general restrictions on sitting judges. See 792-797. |
| Thomas | Full joinder in the Court's rule and all three impartiality analyses. No separate White writing supplies additional reasons. |
| Stevens | Joined Ginsburg without reservation and separately stressed the judicial office's difference from representative politics. Institutional impartiality cannot always be repaired by individual recusal; Mistretta supplied that concern.
