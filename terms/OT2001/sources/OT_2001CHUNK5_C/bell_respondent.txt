1. 
STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


Gary Bradford Cone was indicted for two counts of 
murder, three counts of assault with intent to commit 
murder, and one count of armed robbery after a crime 
spree culminating in the brutal beating death of an eld- 
erly couple. At trial, Mr. Cone was represented by attor- 
ney John Dice, who pleaded Mr. Cone not guilty by rea- 
son of insanity. The defense focused on Mr. Cone’s 
Vietnam Veterans Syndrome and amphetamine psy- 
chosis as the cause for his legal insanity. At the close of 
the guilt phase of the trial, Mr. Cone was convicted on 
all counts. 

A separate sentencing hearing followed, after which 
Mr. Cone was sentenced to death. The dispute before 
this Court concerns Mr. Cone’s legal representation dur- 
ing that sentencing proceeding.

gation evidence since he thought mitigation testimony 
about Gary Cone’s high school background, or how 
Vietnam affected his mind would “open the door” to tes- 
timony about unrelated later criminal acts.

SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT 


1. The Sixth Amendment to the United States 
Constitution guarantees an accused the right to the 
assistance of counsel for his defense in a criminal trial. 
In safeguarding this right, this Court has established 
different rules, applicable to different factual situations, 
to determine whether an accused’s Sixth Amendment 
rights have been violated to the degree that mandates a 
new trial. 


13. 


If an accused facing risk of imprisonment is not afford- 
ed counsel altogether, the Sixth Amendment is violated, 
and a defendant is awarded a new trial with the benefit 
of counsel. The rationale for automatic new trials in 
these cases stems from the advocate’s role of ensuring 
an accused is afforded all the other constitutional safe- 
guards surrounding a criminal trial. Without counsel, 
the prosecution’s case is not subjected to meaningful 
adversarial testing, defense proof is not marshaled to 
counter the force of the state’s case, and the finder of 
fact does not benefit from a balanced view of the evi- 
dence. In these cases, a trial with no counsel produces a 
result with no indicia of reliability, and the validity of 
the judgment is uncertain. In such cases it is not worth 
the cost of a case by case analysis to discern whether, 
notwithstanding the absence of counsel, the defendant 
nonetheless received a fair trial. Rather, it is presumed 
he did not. Simply put, for an adversarial proceeding to 
be fair, there must be two adversaries at work present- 

Contrast the complete denial of counsel with cases 
where an attorney is present acting as an advocate for 
the accused, but the attorney makes specific errors dur- 
ing the trial, such as failing to make a specific objection. 
In these cases, this Court has required the defendant 
raising the Sixth Amendment claim to demonstrate that 
the error complained of somehow affected the outcome of 
the trial. Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668 (1984). 
An automatic new trial is not required because of a spe- 
cific attorney mistake, since the accused has an advo- 
cate trained in the law that, though perhaps not perfect, 
nonetheless is generally subjecting the prosecution’s 
case to adversarial testing, and presenting the accused's 
case to the finder of fact in its best light. In these cases, 
the court assumes a reliable verdict, unless the accused 


14. 


can show that somehow the error complained of likely 
affected the outcome of the trial. Thus, if an attorney 
failed to object to an unconstitutionally vague aggravat- 
ing circumstance in a capital sentencing proceeding, and 
the jury did not find such aggravating circumstance to 
be supported by the evidence, the attorney error could 
not be shown to have affected the outcome of the trial. 
In these cases, it is far easier for a reviewing court to 
conduct a case by case analysis to determine if specific 
errors “prejudiced” the defendant. 

There are circumstances, however, when though an 
attorney is present at the criminal trial, such attorney 
totally fails to act as an advocate. In United States v. 
Cronic, 466 U.S. 648 (1984), this Court explained that an 
attorney’s total failure to subject the prosecution’s case to 
meaningful adversarial testing during a critical phase of 
a criminal trial should result in a presumption of preju- 
dice. The same concerns as to the reliability of a verdict 
untested by an adversary, which result in the presump- 
tion of prejudice with the complete denial of counsel, are 
also present with a “constructive” denial of counsel. The 
lower courts have sparingly applied Cronic in situations 
such as a lawyer who sleeps through substantial portions 
of a capital trial; or when a lawyer berates his client in 
front of the jury, calling him derogatory names, making 
it as if his attorney is a second prosecutor. 

In this case, Gary Cone’s attorney failed to present a 
case for life in the sentencing phase of his capital trial. 
After the prosecution put forth evidence of aggravating 
circumstances, Cone’s attorney failed to respond with 
any mitigation proof of his own. After the prosecution 
told the jury that Tennessee law obliged the jury to vote 
for death, the attorney replied by waiving closing argu- 
ment. The effect before the jury was devastating. The 
jury was left with the impression that Gary Cone’s 


15. 


lawyer agreed that death was the required sentence, 
since he failed to counter the state’s penalty phase case, 
and failed to disagree with the prosecutor’s call for 
death. His silence implied his consent. The attorney's 
failure to make a case for life amounted to a total failure 
to subject the prosecution’s case for death to meaningful 
adversarial testing. It would have been better had Gary 
Cone’s lawyer not shown up for the penalty phase. At 
least then the jury would not have had the impression 
that Cone’s own lawyer had given up, viewing death as 
the inevitable sentence. The Sixth Circuit properly 
applied Cronic in this case, and presumed prejudice due 
to the attorney's total abdication of advocacy in the 
penalty phase of Gary Cone’s trial. 

2. Federal law provides that relief may not be granted 
to a federal habeas petitioner challenging a state criminal

U.S. 364 (1993), to be contrary to clearly established fed-

B. This Court has established different rules

depending upon whether counsel is com-

This Court has employed different tests to determine 
whether the right to counsel is violated in a criminal 
case; some of which require a defendant to demonstrate 
prejudice arising from the violation in order to obtain 
relief, others not. The court has “uniformly found con-

the other hand, in cases where an actively participating

the defendant to demonstrate that the errors actually 
had an adverse effect on the defense.” Roe v. Flores-

(1984), establishes a rule requiring a defendant to prove 
both (1) deficient performance and (2) prejudice from the 
deficient performance to establish a Sixth Amendment 
claim. 


There is another category of cases, where, although a

essential requires us to conclude that a trial is 
unfair if the accused is denied counsel at a criti-

The essence of defense counsel’s role in a capital sen-
