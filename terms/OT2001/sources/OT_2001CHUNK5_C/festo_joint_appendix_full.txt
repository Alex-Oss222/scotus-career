b. The Magnetizable Sleeve 


Festo asserts that the SMC driven member’s non- 
magnetizable sleeve is an equivalent of the magnetizable 
sleeve in claim 1 of the Stoll patent. (Festo’s Post Trial 
Brief, pp. 3-7.) In support of this contention Festo urges 
that (1) the magnetizable sleeve limitation did not appear 
in the German Stoll patent and should not have been 
added to the U.S. patent (Festo’s Post Trial Brief, p. 5; Tr. 
457); and (2) the sleeve material does not affect the 
operation of the invention. (Festo’s Post Trial Brief, Ppp. 


I-41 


5-6; Tr. 642-645.) Neither of these arguments is 
convincing. 


First, the specification and prosecution history do not 
support Festo’s assertion that the magnetizable limitation 
should not have been in claim 1. In the specification the 
sleeve is described as “made of a magnetizable material 
to reduce magnetic leakage fields.” Col. 3, lines 61-62. 
The prosecution history shows that the patent was filed 
with a claim 1 that did not have the magnetizable 
limitation; claim 8, which was dependent from claim 1, 
had the limitation. In response to a rejection based on 
Section 112 ¥ 1, claim 1 was rewritten as claim 13 and 
claim 8 was canceled. (Px 14.) Since consideration was 
given to whether or not the term should appear in the 
claim after the first Office Action, Festo’s assertion that it 
was not meant to be there is not persuasive. 


Second, the specification contradicts Festo’s 
argument that the sleeve material does not affect the 
operation of the invention. Festo has tried to characterize 
the invention as dealing only with sealing, wiping and 
guiding. (Festo’s Post Trial Brief p. 3-4; Tr. 412-413.) 
Nevertheless, the specification not only describes the 
sleeve as being made from a magnetizable material but 
also explains the reason for choosing that material: 


If the driven assembly is provided with a sleeve 
made of a magnetizable material, which 
encircles the hollow cylindrical assembly of the 
magnet arrangement, magnetic leakage fields in 
the vicinity of the driven assembly can be kept 
to a minimum. In this way, undesirable braking 
forces, which would otherwise be generated 
while piston and driven assembly pass 


I-42 


magnetizable machine components, are avoided. 
(Px 15 col. 2, lines 23-30.) 


Festo performed an experiment which was supposed 
to demonstrate that regardless, of whether the sleeve was 
made of a magnetizable or a non-magnetizable material, 
the operation of the rodless cylinder was the same (Tr. 
697-700; Festo’s Post-Trial Brief, p. 6.) SMC criticized the 
test because it was not performed under load. (Tr. 
806-808; SMC’s Post-Trial Brief, p. 15.) More importantly, 
the test shed no light on whether or not both sleeves 
achieved the advantages that Stoll attributed to having a 
sleeve made of a magnetizable material: i.e. that both 
magnetizable and non-magnetizable sleeves served to 
minimize magnetic leakage fields in substantially the 
same way. 


According to the specification of the Stoll patent, a 
sleeve made of a magnetizable material achieves the 
benefit of minimizing magnetic leakage fields. Stoll 
claims a device with a sleeve made of a magnetizable 
material. The specification is silent concerning the use of 
a sleeve made of a non-magnetizable material. Moreover, 
at the time the application was filed, Stoll thought there 
was an advantage to using a sieeve made of a 
magnetizable material. (Tr. 461.) 


Although Stoll initially filed a claim broad enough to 
cover the use of a sleeve made of a magnetizable 
material, he opted not to pursue such a claim after the 
first Office Action. As the Federal Circuit said in Unique 
Concepts, Inc. v. Brown, 939 F.2d 1558, 1562, 19 USPQ2d 
1500, 1504 (Fed.Cir. 1991): 


1-43 


The statute requires that an inventor 
particularly point out and distinctly claim the 
subject matter of his invention. 35 U.S.C. § 112 
(1988). It would run counter’ to this statutory 
provision for an applicant for patent to 
expressly state throughout his specification and 
in his claims [a claim limitation] and then be 
allowed to avoid that claim limitation in a later 
infringement suit by pointing to one paragraph 
in his specification stating an alternative that 
lacks the limitation, and thus interpret the claim 
contrary to its plain meaning. Such a result 
would encourage an applicant to escape 
examination of a more broadly-claimed 
invention by filing narrow claims and then, after 
grant, asserting a broader scope of the claims 
based on a statement in the specification of an 
alternative never presented in the claims for 
examination.?2 


The subject matter disclosed but not claimed in a 
patent application is dedicated to the public. Miller v. 
Brass Co., 104 U.S. 350 (1881). As the Federal Circuit 
pointed out in Unique Concepts, if the applicant intended 
to claim an alternative embodiment, he had to persuade 
the examiner to issue the claim. As is apparent from the 
Stoll patent prosecution history, Stoll submitted one claim 
without the magnetizable material limitation and another 
with the limitation. Both claims were rejected and Stoll 


decided to only pursue a claim directed to a sleeve made 


of a magnetizable material. (Px 14.) 


22 See also International Visual Corporation v. Crown Metal 
Manufacturing Co, Inc., No. 92-1262 (Fed.Cir. 1993) (Lourie, J., 
concurring at p. 5). 


1-44 


The doctrine of equivalents is the exce; tion and not 
the rule. In this case, the meaning and scope of claim 1 of 
the Stoll patent was clear. Stoll consciously chose to not 
claim an embodiment of his invention that used a 
magnetizable sleeve. Festo cannot now try to use the 
doctrine of equivalents to recapture a claim that Stoll 
abandoned during prosecution “because other players in 
the marketplace are entitled to rely on the record made in 
the Patent Office in determining the meaning and scope 
of the patent.” Lemelson v. General Mills, Inc., 968 F.2d 
1202, 1208, 23 USPQ2d 1284, 1289 (Fed.Cir. 1992). 


Festo’s commercial embodiment, like SMC’s, uses a 
non-magnetizable sleeve. (Px 118, 210.) However, the 
patent claim, and not any commercial embodiment, 
serves as notice to others of the patentees rights. SMC 
had a right to rely on the claim language, in light of the 
specification and the prosecution history, and to design 
around it. London v. Carson Piri Scott & Co., 946 F.2d 1534, 
20 USPQ2d 1456 (Fed.Cir. 1991), supra, accord, State 
Industries v. A.O. Smith Corp., 751 F.2d 1226, 224 USPQ 418 
(Fed.Cir. 1985). Therefore, claims 1-3 and 5-7 of the Stoll 
patent are not infringed by the SMC rodless cylinders.

netted anything had it made SMC’s sales. There is no 
basis for awarding damages under a lost profits theory.

a hypothetical willing patent owner and a willing 
potential

VI. SUMMARY JUDGMENT AND DIRECTED 
VERDICT MOTIONS 


SMC moved for summary judgment of invalidity of 
the Stoll patent based on failure to disclose the best 
mode. (Dkt. 104.) SMC moved for summary judgment of 
noninfringement of the Stoll patent based on its use of a 
non-magnetizable sleeve. (Dkt. 117.) SMC also moved for 
summary judgment of noninfringement of the 
reexamined Carroll patent and the Stoll patent based on 
its use of one seal, instead of two. (Dkt. 122.) At the 
conclusion of plaintiff's evidence, SMC moved for a 
directed verdict. (Tr. 866.) SMC renewed this motion at 
the conclusion of the hearing on liability (Tr. 1302.) 


It is proper to grant summary judgment under Rule 
56(c), F.R.Civ.P. if “ the pleadings, depositions, answers to 
interrogatories, and admissions on file, together with the 
affidavits, if any, show that there is no genuine issue as to 
any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to 
a judgment as a matter of law.” The Supreme Court has 
said that under this standard, “the mere existence of some

2. Infringement Under Doctrine of Equivalents of

Based on the analysis contained in the report con-

the Stoll patent, the master recommended that SMC’s

respect to SMC’s infringement of claims 1-3 and 5-7 of

recommendation with respect to non-infringement of the 
Stoll patent. Festo has moved for summary judgment 
and/or a directed verdict of infringement of the Stoll

“In order to determine a question of patent infringe- 
ment, a district court must (1) determine as a matter of 
law the scope and meaning of the claims at issue and (2) 
determine as a factual matter whether the properly con- 
strued claims encompass or ‘read on’ the accused 
device.” Vaupel Textilmaschinen v. Meccanica Euro Italia, 
944 F.2d 870, 879 (Fed. Cir. 1991) (“Vaupel”). See also ZMI 
Corp. v. Cardiac Resuscitator Corp., 844 F.2d 1576, 1578 
(Fed. Cir. 1988). “To ascertain the meaning of claims, [the 
court considers] three sources: the claims, the specifica- 
tion, and the prosecution history.” Unique Concepts, Inc. v. 
Brown, 939 F.2d 1558, 1561 (Fed. Cir. 1991) (“Unique”). To 


I-71 


constitute an infringement, the accused device must con- 
tain each and every limitation of the claim or its substan- 
tial equivalent. Vaupel, 944 F.2d at 879. “An equivalent 
must be found for every limitation of the claim some- 
where in an accused device, but not necessarily in a 
corresponding component, although that is generally the 
case.” Corning Glass Works v. Sumitomo Elec. U.S.A., Inc., 
868 F.2d 1251, 1259 (Fed. Cir. 1989) (adding negative 
dopant to the cladding is equivalent to adding positive 


dopant to the core in the context of the claimed inven- 
tion). 


The court agrees with the conclusion of the Master 
that “when properly construed the Stoll claims cannot be 
read to cover the non-magnetizable sleeves used in the 
saec rodless cylinders.” Report, pp. 47-50, 68. Thus, there 
is no literal infringement as a matter of law. Festo does 
not dispute this ruling. 


However, the court disagrees that summary judg- 
ment can be granted with respect to the claim of infringe- 
ment by equivalents. A finding of infringement by 
equivalents requires a determination whether the accused 
device performs substantially the same function in sub- 
stantially the same way to achieve substantially the same 
result. Unique, 939 F.2d at 1564. See generally Graver Tank 
& Mfg. Co.. Inc. v. Linde Air Prods. Co., 339 U.S. 605, 608 
(1950). “This equitable doctrine evolved from a balancing 
of competing policies, each of which supports the Consti- 
tutional purpose of promoting the ‘useful arts.’ ” London 
v. Carson Pirie Scott & Co., 946 F.2d 1534, 1538 (Fed. Cir. 
1991) (“London”) (citing U.S. Const. art. |, § 8, cl. 8). 


I-72

‘distinct,’ as required by 35 U.S.C. § 112, so that 
the public has fair notice of what the patentee 
and the Patent Trademark office have agreed 
constitute the metes and bounds of the claimed 
invention. . . . On the other hand, the patentee 
should not be deprived of the benefits of his 
patent by competitors who appropriate the 
essence of an invention while barely avoiding

where an infringer, instead of inventing around

appropriating or even ‘stealing’ the patented 
invention, infringement may lie under the doc- 
trine of equivalents. Application of the doctrine 
of equivalents is the exception, however, not the 
rule. ... 


Id. (citations omitted). 


“The essence of prosecution history estoppel is that a 
patentee should not be able to obtain, through the doc- 
trine of equivalents, coverage of subject matter that was 
relinquished during prosecution to procure issuance of the 
patent.” Hoganas AB v. Dresser Indus., Inc., 9 F.3d 948, 
951-52 (Fed. Cir. 1993) (“Hoganas”) (emphasis added). 
“The legal standard for determining what subject matter 
was relinquished is an objective one, measured from the 
vantage point of what a competitor was reasonably enti- 
tled to conclude, from the prosecution history, that the 
applicant gave up to procure issuance of the patent.” Id. 
at 952. 

“The doctrine of prosecution history estoppel bars ‘a 
patentee from enforcing its claims against otherwise 
legally equivalent structures if those structures were 


1-73 


excluded by claim limitations added in order to avoid 
prior art.’ ” Vaupel, 944 F.2d at 882 (quoting Mannesmann 
Demag Corp. v. Engineered Metal Prods. Co., Inc., 793 F.2d 
1279, 1284 (Fed. Cir. 1986)). “In determining whether 
prosecution history estoppel applies because of a change 
in claim language during prosecution, the court must 
consider not only what was changed, but the reason for 
such change.” Id. (emphasis in original) (because the 
record does not indicate that the limitation was added to 
avoid prior art, there was no estoppel to finding infringe- 
ment by equivalents). “The scope of estoppel must be 
determined in light of the prior art that occasioned the 
change, as well as representations made to the patent 
examiner as to the reason for change.” Sun Studs, Inc, v. 
ATA Equipment Leasing, Inc., 872 F.2d 978, 987 (Fed. Cir. 
1989) (no estoppel where the discussion of patentability 
before the PTO was unrelated to the description of the 
geometric shape) (“Sun Studs”), overruled on other grounds 
by A.C. Aukerman Co., v. R.L. Chaides Const. Co., 960 Fd 
1020, 1038-39 (Fed. Cir. 1992). “[E]stoppel is not auto- 
matic whenever a claim is cancelled... . ” Id. at 987. 
ee history estoppel is ordinarily a question of 
aw. 


SMC vigorously argues that this Court should adopt 
the master’s recommendation that Festo is estopped 
under the doctrine of prosecution history estoppel from 
claiming that it is entitled to a range of equivalents which 
would erase “meaningful structural and functional! lim- 
itations of the claim on which the public is entitled to rely 
in avoiding infringement.” Hoganas, 9 F.3d at 954 (quoting 
Perkin-Elmer Corp. v. Westinghouse Elec. Corp., 822 F.2d 
1528, 1532 (Fed. Cir. 1987)). In Hoganas, the court found 


1-74 


prosecution history estoppel where: the examiner during 
prosecution issued an office action rejecting claims for 
obviousness; the claimant distinguished the claims so as 
to relinquish coverage of capillary-sized fibers; and the 
examiner subsequently allowed the claims based on this 
distinction. The court held: “In our view, a reasonable 
competitor could have concluded, from a reading of the 
prosecution history, that the examiner relied on this dis- 
tinction in allowing the claims, and thus, that Hoganas 
had given up coverage of the subject matter incorporat- 
ing this distinction.” Hoganas, 9 F.3d at 953. 


Here, in the First Office Action, the examiner rejected 
all claims under 35 U.S.C. § 112 par. 1, which provides: 
“The specification shall contain a written description of 
the invention, and of the manner and process of making 
and using it, in such full, clear, concise, and exact terms 
as to enable any person skilled in the art to which it 
pertains, or with which it is most nearly connected, to 
make and use the same, and shall set forth the best mode 
contemplated by the inventor of carrying out his inven- 
tion.” The examiner commented: “Exact method of opera- 
tion unclear. Is device a true motor or magnetic clutch.” 
Claims 4-12 were also rejected under 35 U.S.C. § 112 for 


being improperly multiply dependent. Report 37. They 
were not rejected for obviousness under 35 U.S.C. § 103. 


The initial claim 1 did not limit the sleeve of the 
driven member to being constructed of a magnetizable 
material. Claim 8, which was dependent from claim 1, 
had such a limitation. Report 31. In response to this first 
office action, Stoll cancelled independent claim 1 and 
substituted a completely new independent claim which 


I-75 


required that the driven member have a sleeve con- 
structed of a magnetizable material. Report 32. 


Although the examiner identified certain “cited refer- 
ences believed pertinent,” there is nothing in the record 
or the report to indicate that the limitation of a magnetiz- 
able sleeve was necessary to distinguish the claimed 
invention over prior art. Further, there is nothing in the 
record or the report to suggest that the limitation of a 
sleeve of magnetizable material was necessary to meet 
the requirements of specification under 35 U.S.C. § 112. 
Indeed, the reason why the newly substituted indepen- 
dent claim has the limitation that the sleeve be made of 
magnetizable material is a mystery, as nothing seems to 
turn on its composition for purpose of procuring the 
patent. At the very least, it is a question of fact. Sun Stud, 
872 F.2d at 987. This Court is not persuaded by SMC’s 
reliance on Haynes Int’l., Inc. v. Jessop Steel Co., 8 F.3d 
1573, 1577-78 (Fed. Cir. 1993) (“Haynes”), in which two 
claims reciting a relatively broad range of chromium con- 
tent were abandoned, and a third claim with a more 
specific chromium content was retained. In Haynes, 
unlike the present case, it was clear from the prosecution 
history “that chromium content was the principal distinc- 
tion between the claimed subject matter and the prior art, 
and this was the primary area of dialogue between Cabot 
and the PTO.” Id. at 1578. Accordingly, in light of the 
circumstances of the present record, this court declines to 
adopt the recommendation of the master that the claim of 
infringement by equivalents is barred under the doctrine 
of prosecution history estoppel. 


In addition, with respect to equivalency Festo has 
presented expert testimony of Dr. Robert Wolf and of Dr. 


1-76 


Stoll to support its claim of infringement under the doc- 
trine of equivalents. Festo argues that SMC has produced 
no evidence to dispute the testimony of Wolf and Stoll 
that magnetizable and non-magnetizable sleeves operate 
in substantially the same manner. However, the patent 
itself states that if the driven assembly is provided with a 
sleeve made of magnetizable material, “magnetic leakage 
fields in the vicinity of the driven assembly can be kept to 
a minimum. In this way, undesirable breaking forces, 
which would otherwise be generated while piston and 
driven assembly pass magnetizable machine components, 
are avoided.” Record 48. This language in the specifica- 
tion, together with the prosecution history, creates a dis- 
puted issue of fact appropriate for resolution by a jury 
under the function/way/result test. Unique, 939 F.2d at 
1564 (even where there was no literal infringement as a 
result of plain language of claim and prosecution history, 
the court examined expert testimony in evaluating 
whether there was infringement by equivalents). Accord- 
ingly, both motions are denied. | 


3. Literal Infringement of the Reexamined Carroll 
Patent 


Festo has moved for summary judgment on its claim 
of infringement with respect to the reexamined Carroll 
patent. Claim 9 of the reexamined Carroll patent requires 
“a pair of cushion members formed of resilient material, 
the cushion members being situated near opposite axial 
ends of the central mounting member to help prevent 
damage to the piston when the piston contacts an axial 
end of the cylinder... . ” 


I-77 


A. 6, 10 and 15 mm slider pistons 


The 6, 10 and 15 mm slider pistons have no cushions 
and Festo does not contend that they infringe. 


B. 25, 32, and 40 mm slider pistons 


SMC contends that the cushions in the 25, 32 and 40 
mm slider pistons cannot prevent damage by making 
contact with the end plates, because a bolt protrudes from 
the piston such that the end plate will make contact with 
the bolt rather than the cushion. (Testimony of Dr. Blanco, 
Tr. 1800). Festo has neither put forth any evidence to 
dispute the contention nor pressed the issue in its briefs. 
Accordingly, SMC’s motion for summary judgment on the 
claim of literal infringement will be granted. 


C. Basic Pistons 


As to the basic pistons, SMC contends that “in nor- 
mal operation,” their cushions do not “prevent damage to 
the piston” since they do not make contact with the end 
plate. This is because “in normal operation” the piston is 
stopped by stopping the load rather than stopping the 
load by stopping the piston. The court agrees with the 
conclusion of the master that the language of claim 9 of 
the reexamined Carroll patent does not distinguish 
between situations where contact with the axial end of 
the cylinder is the normal result of the completion of a 
stroke and where it comes into play only in the case of 
decoupling. Report 20-22. Accordingly, the court grant’s 
Festo’s motion for summary judgment with respect to the 
claim of literal infringement of claims 5, 6 and 9 of the 


1-78 


reexamined Carroll patent on the basic pistons with two 
seals and cushions. 


4. Infringement Under Doctrine of Equivalents of 
Reexamined Carroll and Stoll Patents Concerning 


Seals 


SMC has moved for summary judgment on non- 
infringement of the reexamined Carroll patent and the 
Stoll patent based on its use of one seal rodless cylinders, 
instead of two. Festo has also moved for summary judg- 
ment on their claim of infringement by equivalents. Since 
the Carroll and Stoll patents specifically recite two seals, 
there is no dispute that the one seal basic and the one seal 
slider SMC rodless cylinders do not literally infringe. 


Claim 1 of the Stoll patent states inter alia: 


Said piston further including plural guide ring 
means encircling said piston body and slidingly 
engaging said internal wall and first sealing 
rings located axially outside said guide rings for 
wiping said internal wall as said piston moves 
along said tube to thereby cause any impurities 
that may be present in said tube to be pushed 
along said tube so that said first annular mag- 
nets will be free of interference from said impu- 


rities. 


Claim 9 of the reexamined Carroll patent specifically 
defines “a pair of resilient sealing rings situated near 
opposite axial ends of the central mounting member and 
engaging the cylinder to provide a fluid-tight seal there- 


with.” 


1-79 


SMC argues that use of only one seal allows dirt to 
enter the piston chamber and is therefore not equivalent 
to two seals. The master rejected the argument as 
“flawed” as applied to the reexamined Carroll patent 
because it is based on the meaning and function of the 
sealing rings as described in the Stoll patent, not in the 
Carroll patent. Report 24. Rather, the Master found that 
under the Carroll patent: “The seals create a fluid-tight 
seal against the pressure medium, enabling the piston to 
be pushed by its force. This results in the piston being 
efficiently moved by the pressure medium without 
“blow-by” or air getting around the seals.” Report 24. 
Festo has presented undisputed evidence that for this 
purpose the one seal in the SMC device performs sub- 
stantially the same function and achieves substantially 
the same result in substantially the same way as two 
seals. 


The only evidence which SMC has produced to dis- 
pute the claim of infringement by equivalents is state- 
ments made by Festo in the context of prosecution of the 
Stoll patent. In particular, there is a statement by Dr. Stoll 
that two seals are necessary under the Stoll patent 
because without two sealing rings, dirt attracted by the 
magnet will enter the side without the sealing ring. This 
evidence concerning dirt and impurities does not bear on 
the meaning and function of the sealing rings as 
described in the reexamined Carroll patent. 


Accordingly, the Court allows Festo’s motion for 
summary judgment that the one seal SMC basic cylinders 
infringe claims 5, 6 and 9 of the reexamined Carroll 
patent under the doctrine of equivalents. However, the 
Court denies Festo’s motion for summary judgment that 
the one seal SMC basic cylinders infringe claim 1 of the 


1-80 


Stoll patent under the doctrine of equivalents. Consider- 
ing the above statement by Dr. Stoll on the importance of 
two seals, as well as conflicting testimony from Dr. Wolf 
and Dr. Stoll at the master’s hearing, there is a genuine 
issue of material fact as to whether one seal functions 
substantially the same way as two seals in eliminating 
interference from impurities.
