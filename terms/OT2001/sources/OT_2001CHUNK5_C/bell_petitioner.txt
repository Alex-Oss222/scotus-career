CAPITAL CASE 
QUESTIONS PRESENTED 


I. 


Did the court of appeals exceed its authority under 
28 U.S.C. §2254(d)(1), as interpreted by this Court in 
Williams v. Taylor, 529 U.S. 362 (2000), by granting habeas 
relief to respondent on his claim of ineffective assistance 
of counsel at the sentencing phase of his capital trial, 
where that claim had been previously adjudicated and 
rejected on the merits in Tennessee state court proceed- 
ings? 


Il. 


May a court of appeals bypass the prejudice prong of 
Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668 (1984), and presume 
prejudice under United States v. Cronic, 466 U.S. 648 
(1984), to find a violation of the Sixth Amendment right 
to counsel, where defense counsel conducts a thorough 
pre-trial investigation, presents lay and expert witness 
testimony in support of a mental health defense at trial, 
conducts direct and cross-examination, and addresses the 
jury at both the guilt and sentencing phases on behalf of 
his client, and where state and district courts have previ- 
ously determined that the defendant failed to demon- 
strate actual prejudice from any deficiencies identified in 
the attorney’s performance?

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


| In 1982, Gary Bradford Cone was convicted of two 

counts of first degree murder by a Shelby County, Ten- 
nessee, jury. Following a separate sentencing hearing, the 
jury sentenced Cone to death for each of the two mur- 
ders. Cone’s convictions and sentences were affirmed on 
direct appeal (Pet. App. 84), and later upheld in state 
post-conviction proceedings. (Pet. App. 101) Cone then 
sought a writ of habeas corpus in federal district court. 
The district court summarily denied relief, concluding 
that Cone failed to demonstrate prejudice from counsel's 
alleged deficiencies and, further, that he failed to demon- 
strate that the state court’s adjudication of his ineffective 
assistance claim “involved an unreasonable application of 
clearly established Federal law” under §2254(d)(1). (Pet. 
App. 77-83) | 


On appeal, nineteen years after Cone’s criminal trial 
and sentencing, the court of appeals reversed his death 
sentences, finding that defense counsel’s decision to pre- 
sent no mitigation evidence at the sentencing phase and 
to waive closing argument at sentencing not only 
amounted to constitutionally deficient performance, but 
was so deficient as to raise a presumption of prejudice, 
resulting in a violation of Cone’s Sixth Amendment right 
to counsel. (Pet. App. 37) The court of appeals based its 
decision on the same testimony presented to the State 
courts and upon which those courts relied in rejecting the 
same claims in state post-conviction proceedings. (Pet. 
App. 34) 


1. The brutal murders of Shipley and Cleopatra 
Todd were the climax of a series of criminal episodes that 


began on August 9, 1980, when Cone robbed a jewelry 
store in Memphis of approximately $112,000 in merchan- 
dise. (Pet. App. 85) The jewelry store manager gave a 
description of the suspect to police, and an officer in an 
unmarked police car spotted Cone driving his vehicle a 
short time after the robbery and pulled in behind him. 
(Id.) Cone apparently became alarmed and accelerated in 
an attempt to escape. A high-speed chase ensued through 
mid-town Memphis and into a residential neighborhood, 
where Cone abandoned his automobile and proceeded on 
foot. (Id.) As he fled from police, Cone shot one of the 
police officers who attempted to arrest him, shot a citizen 
who challenged him, pulled a gun on a third person and 
demanded that the latter give Cone his automobile. When 
the owner of the automobile fled, Cone snapped his pistol 
several times, but the ammunition in the weapon had 
been exhausted. (Id.) 


Cone managed to elude police on August 9th but 
appeared at the door of a home in the same neighborhood 
early the next morning and asked to use the phone. When 
the elderly resident, Lucille Tuech, refused him admit- 
tance, Cone drew his pistol and pointed it at her. Ms. 
Tuech quickly slammed the door, ran to another part of 
the house, and called the police. (Id.; R. 4, Add. 1, Vol. 
XIL, pp. 1207-08) Later that same afternoon, Cone broke 
into the home of Shipley and Cleopatra Todd,who lived a 
short distance from Ms. Tuech. Mr. Todd was 93 years of 
age; his wife was 79. The couple’s bodies were found 
three days later after authorities were alerted by anxious 
relatives. Both victims had been beaten to death. (Pet. 
App. 86) Mr. Todd sustained 16 blows, mostly to the back 
of the head, and Mrs. Todd sustained 22 blows to the 


back and left side of the head; defensive wounds were 
present on the hands of both. The cause of death to both 
victims was multiple blunt trauma to the head inflicted 
by an instrument consistent with a gun. (R. 4, Add. 1, Vol. 
XII, pp. 1293, 1295-96, 1298-1300) Cone’s fingerprints 
and hair samples were found in the Todds’ home, which 
had been ransacked, and he was apparently able to steal 
enough money from their home to fly from Memphis to 
Florida, where he appeared at the home of an acquain- 
tance on August 12. (Pet. App. 86) 


At trial, Cone did not dispute killing the Todds but 
claimed that he was insane at the time of the crimes or 
lacked the requisite mental capacity to commit the 
offenses. Cone presented the testimony of two expert 
witnesses to support his defense. Matthew Jaremko, a 
clinical psychologist, opined that Cone suffered from two 
interrelated disorders: a substance-abuse disorder and 
post-traumatic stress disorder. The substance-abuse dis- 
order was caused primarily by post-traumatic stress dis- 
order, which arose from Cone’s exposure to combat 
trauma in Vietnam and his continuing trauma after 
returning home as a veteran of an unpopular war. (J.A. 
12) According to Jaremko, Cone’s post-traumatic stress 
disorder manifested itself over the years in the form of 
flashbacks, nightmares, depression, nervousness, and 
sleep disorders. (J.A. 12-13) Dr. Jaremko testified that, in 
his opinion, Cone was suffering from a mental illness in 
August 1980, which rendered him incapable of conform- 
ing his conduct to the requirements of the law. He further 
testified that Cone expressed remorse for what happened. 
(J.A. 14) 


Jonathan Lipman, a neuropharmacologist, testified 
that he examined Cone and detailed his history of drug 
abuse. Dr. Lipman testified that Cone had no history of 
drug abuse until the age of 18, when he was first exposed 
to hashish while serving in the Army in Germany. Cone 
was later introduced to opium and amphetamines while 
serving in Vietnam, where he was reportedly issued 
amphetamines for 53 consecutive nights to maintain 
alertness while on guard duty. (J.A. 18) According to Dr. 
Lipman, that pattern of amphetamine use continued 
throughout the rest of Cone’s history. Dr. Lipman opined 
that, in August 1980, Cone was suffering from chronic 
amphetamine psychosis, which prevented him from 
knowing the wrongfulness of his acts and rendered him 
substantially incapable of conforming his conduct to the 
requirements of the law. (J.A. 18-19) 


In addition to expert testimony, Cone’s mother, Val- 
eree Cone, testified that she had noticed a change in her 
son after his return from Vietnam. She testified that Cone 
acted like he was “in another world” and slept very 
badly. He spoke to her about handling bodies while he 
was in Vietnam and the condition the bodies were in. 
(J.A. 9-10) She further testified that Cone did not laugh or 
cry, but seemed to be “just holding it in.” (J.A. 10) In 
rebuttal, the State offered two mental health experts, 
neither of whom found any basis for an insanity defense. 
(Pet. App. 90-91) 


The jury rejected Cone’s insanity defense, convicting 
him at the conclusion of the guilt phase of two counts of 
first degree murder, three counts of assault with intent to 
commit first degree murder, and robbery by use of a 
deadly weapon. (Pet. App. 84) At sentencing, the State 


relied on four aggravating circumstances to support 
imposition of the death penalty: (1) that Cone was previ- 
ously convicted of one or more felonies, other than the 
present charge, involving the use or threat of violence to 
the person; (2) that Cone knowingly created a great risk 
of death to two or more persons, other than the victim 
murdered, during his act of murder; (3) that the murder 
was especially heinous, atrocious, or cruel in that it 
involved torture or depravity of mind; and (4) that the 
murder was committed for the purpose of avoiding, 
interfering with, or preventing a lawful arrest or prosecu- 
tion of the defendant or another. Tenn. Code Ann. 
§§39-2-203(i)(2), (3), (5), and (6) (1982). (J.A. 22-23, 50-52) 


Through the testimony of a court clerk and a finger- 
print examiner, the State established that Cone had three 
prior convictions for robbery with a firearm. (J.A. 28-29, 
34-35) In addition, the State recalled at sentencing two 
Memphis police officers who had been involved in the 
initial efforts to apprehend Cone after the jewelry store 
robbery. Both officers testified that Cone evaded arrest. 
(J.A. 36-40)

3. Cone sought federal habeas relief in July 1997, 
asserting numerous grounds, including ineffective assis- 
tance based on counsel’s failure to introduce mitigating 
evidence and waiver of closing argument at sentencing. 
(R. 71, pp. 21-22) The district court summarily dismissed 
Cone’s ineffective assistance of counsel claim, finding in 
pertinent part that Cone failed to demonstrate any preju- 
dice from the failure of his trial counsel to introduce 
mitigating evidence at sentencing. (Pet. App. 78-80) The 
district court further found that Cone failed to meet 
§2254(d)(1)’s threshold requirement that a habeas peti- 
tioner demonstrate the state court’s rejection of his Sixth 
Amendment claim “involved an unreasonable application 
of clearly established Federal law.” (Pet. App. 80) 


As to counsel’s waiver of closing argument at sen- 
tencing, the district court found that Cone failed to offer 
any basis to dispute the state court’s determination that 
the decision was a tactical choice to foreclose rebuttal 
argument by the prosecution and, further, that the state 
appellate court’s decision on the issue was “a reasonable 
application of Sixth Amendment standards to the facts 
presented in the state post-conviction proceedings.” (Pet.

SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT 


1. As amended by the Antiterrorism and Effective 
Death Penalty Act of 1996 (“AEDPA”), 28 U.S.C. §2254(d), 
in pertinent part, prohibits a federal habeas court from 
granting relief on a claim adjudicated on the merits in 
state court proceedings uniess that adjudication “resulted 
in a decision that was contrary to, or involved an unrea- 
sonable application of, clearly established Federal law, as 
determined by the Supreme Court of the United States 
....” 28 U.S.C. §2254(d)(1). Simply put, AEDPA removed 
the authority of a federal court to disturb a state court 
judgment based upon its independent determination that 
the state court erred in deciding a constitutional question. 
As this Court explained in Williams v. Taylor, 529 U.S. 362, 
410 (2000), the most important distinction between 
review under the present §2254(d) and pre-AEDPA 


16 


review is that “an unreasonable application of federal law 
[by a state court] is different from an incorrect application 
of federal law.” A state court decision that is merely 
incorrect, but not unreasonable, may no longer be dis- 
turbed on habeas review. 


Nevertheless, en route to vacating Cone’s death sen- 
tences imposed over 19 years ago by a Tennessee jury, the 
court of appeals began its analysis with the clearly erro- 
neous assertion that its review of Cone’s ineffective assis- 
tance of counsel claim should be de novo. (Pet. App. 30-31) 
Although the Tennessee courts had previously deter- 
mined that Cone’s trial counsel made reasoned tactical 
decisions regarding the presentation of mitigation evi- 
dence and waiver of closing argument at sentencing, and 
that neither alleged attorney error had prejudiced the 
defense, the court of appeals rejected those conclusions 
“out of hand,” and, without any analysis of how coun- 


sel’s alleged deficiencies could have affected the out- - 


come, presumed prejudice. While the court of appeals 
paid lip service to AEDPA by quoting the pertinent provi- 
sion in the final paragraph of its opinion, compliance 
with §2254(d) requires more than mere talismanic recita- 
tion of its language. The court of appeals’ actual analysis 
of Cone’s ineffective assistance claim, indistinguishable 
from that employed in pre-AEDPA cases, belies its 
asserted application of §2254(d). 


The Tennessee courts, in assessing Cone’s claims, 
fully comprehended and reasonably applied the Strick- 
‘land standard for judging the performance of counsel 
under the Sixth Amendment, heeding this Court's 
instruction that “[jjudicial scrutiny of counsel’s perfor- 
mance must be highly deferential” and recognizing the 


17 


Sixth Amendment's strong presumption that “counsel's 
conduct falls within the wide range of reasonable profes- 
sional assistance.” Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668, 
689 (1984). Thus, because the decision of the Tennessee 
courts was neither contrary to nor an unreasonable appli- 
cation of Strickland, federal habeas relief was barred by 
§2254(d). 


2. Compounding its error under §2254(d), the court 
of appeals applied a presumption of prejudice to find a 
Sixth Amendment violation under the auspices of United 
States v. Cronic, 466 U.S. 648 (1984), ignoring the finding 
of the Tennessee appellate court that Cone’s convictions 
and sentences were the result of “overwhelming evi- 
dence” against him, and not “shortcomings of counsel.” 
But this Court has recognized only three narrow excep- 
tions in which prejudice may be presumed to find a 
violation of the Sixth Amendment right to counsel: (1) 
where counsel is denied altogether; (2) where the state 
interfered with counsel’s representation; and (3) where 
counsel is burdened with an actual conflict of interest 
that affects his or her performance. Aside from those 
circumstances, however, where a claim of ineffective 
assistance of counsel rests upon some articulable error by 
counsel in the course of representation, and where the 
“surrounding circumstances” do not prevent counsel’s 
effective functioning, a defendant must affirmatively 
demonstrate both deficient performance and prejudice as 
a result of attorney error. Cone’s ineffective assistance 
claim in this case was premised upon specific allegations 
of attorney error - the failure to present mitigation evi- 
dence and waiver of closing argument at sentencing. 
Cone was not without counsel during the sentencing 


18 


phase, nor was his lawyer precluded from rendering 
effective assistance by some structural impediment. To 
the contrary, Cone’s lawyer conducted a thorough pre- 
trial investigation, presented lay and expert witnesses in 
support of a mental health defense at trial, cross-exam- 
ined the State’s witnesses, and addressed the jury on his 
client’s behalf at both the guilt and sentencing phases of 
trial. The quality and effect of the lawyering afforded 
Cone was fully capable of assessment under Strickland, 
and the Sixth Circuit erred in relieving Cone of his bur- 
den of proving both components of that standard.

In 1996, Congress curtailed the power of federal 
courts to grant habeas relief to state prisoners through 
passage of the Antiterrorism and Effective Death Penalty 
Act of 1996, Pub. L. No. 104-152, 110 Stat. 1214, 1219 
(1996) (“AEDPA”). Under pre-AEDPA habeas law, federal 
courts reviewed “mixed” constitutional questions (i.e., 
those involving application of constitutional law to facts) 
de novo with a presumption of correctness as to factual 
findings by the state courts. Cuyler v. Sullivan, 446 U.S. 
335 (1980); Townsend v. Sain, 372 U.S. 293 (1963) (using the 
terms “independent,” “de novo,” and “plenary” to

describe pre-AEDPA habeas review of state court find-

Congress abolished de novo review of mixed questions of 
law and fact that have been previously adjudicated on the 


- merits in state court proceedings. No longer may a fed- 


eral court issue the writ based upon its independent 
judgment that the state court decision was incorrect. 
Rather, as this Court explained in Williams v. Taylor, 529 
U.S. 362 (2000), a federal court’s inquiry on such claims is 
limited to the reasonableness of the state court’s applica- 
tion of clearly established federal law. Id., 529 U.S. at 412. 


In this case, filed after enactment of AEDPA and 
therefore subject to its provisions, Lindh v. Murphy, 521 
U.S. 320, 336 (1997), the court of appeals’ analysis, while 
purporting to comply with the mandates of 28 U.S.C.

The court then compounded its error by conducting an

the writ and directly contravened this Court's decision in 
Williams v. Taylor, supra.

A. Scope of Review under §2254(d)(1) 


The relevant provision of AEDPA, 28 U.S.C. 
§2254(d)(1), prohibits a federal court from granting 
habeas relief to a state prisoner with respect to a claim 
adjudicated on the merits in state court unless that adju- 
dication “resulted in a decision that was contrary to, or 
involved an unreasonable application of, clearly estab- 
lished Federal law, as determined by the Supreme Court 
of the United States.” In Williams, this Court resolved a 
conflict among the circuits regarding the proper inter- 
pretation of that provision. 


First, the Court instructed that the “contrary to” and 
“unreasonable application” clauses of §2254(d)(1) have 
independent meaning. Id., at 404. The Court explained 
that a state court decision will be “contrary to” its clearly 
established precedent if the state court either “applies a 
rule that contradicts the governing law set forth in our 
cases,” id., at 405, or if the state court “confronts a set of 
facts that are materially indistinguishable from a decision 
of this Court and nevertheless arrives at a result different 
from our precedent.” Id., at 406. 


Second, the Court explained that a state court deci- 
sion involves an “unreasonable application” of this 
Court’s precedent if the state court “correctly identifies 
the governing legal rule but applies it unreasonably to 
the facts of a particular prisoner’s case.” Id., at 407-08. In 
making that inquiry, a federal court must assess whether 
the state court’s application of clearly established federal 
law was “objectively unreasonable.” Id., at 409. Observ- 
ing that Congress specifically used the term “unreason- 
able,” as opposed to terms like “erroneous” or 


21 


“incorrect,” the Court further instructed that “a federal 
habeas court may not issue the writ simply because that 
court concludes in its independent judgment that the 
relevant state-court decision applied established federal 
law erroneously or incorrectly. Rather, that application 
must also be unreasonable.” Id., at 411. 


Finally, the Court interpreted the language “clearly 
established Federal law, as determined by the Supreme 
Court of the United States.” That statutory phrase refers 
to “holdings as opposed to dicta” of this Court’s deci- 
sions as of the time of the state court decision. Id., at 412. 


- Thus, under Williams, a federal court’s review of a 
state court’s decision under §2254(d)(1) involves a two- 
part inquiry: first, whether the state court correctly iden- 
tified the governing legal rule; and second, whether the 
state court applied that rule in an objectively reasonable 
way to the facts of the prisoner’s case. When that stan- 
dard is applied to the Tennessee courts’ rejection of 
Cone’s ineffective assistance claim, it cannot be said that 
the state court’s decision contravened controlling case 
law or involved an unreasonable application of that case 
law. 


B. The Tennessee state courts correctly identified 
the governing legal rule. 


The threshold question under §2254(d) is whether the 
state court correctly identified the governing legal rule. 
As this Court noted in Williams, the merits of a claim of

C. The Tennessee state courts’ application of 
Strickland to the facts of Cone’s case was objec- 
tively reasonable. 


Having correctly identified the governing law (in this 
case Strickland), a state court’s application of that stan- 
dard to the facts of a prisoner’s case may be disturbed on 
habeas review only if “unreasonable” under §2254(d)(1). 
Although this Court acknowledged in Williams that the 
standard is difficult to define, it did provide guidance. 
First, the Court specifically rejected the subjective stan- 
dard of review set out in Drinkard v. Johnson, 97 F.3d 751, 
769 (Sth Cir. 1996), in favor of a standard of objective 
reasonableness. Second, the Court emphasized that the 
“most important point” of the analysis is the critical 
distinction between an “unreasonable” application of fed- 
eral law and an “incorrect” or “erroneous” application of 
federal law. Williams, 529 U.S. at 410, 412. 


26 


Since Williams, the circuits have divided over 
whether a federal court must first determine that the state 
court’s decision was erroneous before considering 
whether the decision, if erroneous, involved an unreason- 
able application of federal law. In Van Tran v. Lindsey, 212 
F.3d 1143 (9th Cir.), cert. denied, 531 U.S. 944 (2000), the 
court looked to the doctrine of “clear error” to determine 
what constitutes an “unreasonable application” of federal 
law, holding that when an independent review of the 
legal question by a federal court leaves the court with the 
“firm conviction” that clear error has occurred, the state 
court’s decision must be reversed as an “unreasonable 
application” of clearly established federal law. Van Tran, 
212 F3d at 1153-54. “Requiring federal courts to first 
determine whether the state court’s decision was erro- 
neous, prior to considering whether it was contrary to or 
involved an unreasonable application of controlling law,” 
the court reasoned, “promotes clarity in our own consti- 
tutional jurisprudence and also provides guidance for the 
state courts, which can look to our decisions for their 
persuasive value.” Id., at 1155. 


But, as the Fourth Circuit observed in Bell v. Jarvis, 
236 F.3d 149 (4th Cir. 2000) (en banc), cert. denied, 70 
U.S.L.W. 3235 (2001), because a federal court may grant 
habeas relief only if the state court decision is contrary to, 
or an unreasonable application of, Supreme Court prece- 
dent, “any independent opinions we offer on the merits 
of the constitutional claims will have no determinative 
effect on the case before us, nor any precedential effect 
for state courts in future cases,” constituting “at best... a 
body of constitutional dicta.” Jarvis, 236 F.3d at 162. Nor 


are the state courts - as co-equal interpreters of the 


27 


federal Constitution and this Court’s case law - in need 
of “guidance” by federal circuit courts in interpesting and 
applying this Court’s precedent. Id. Van Tran's clearly 
erroneous” standard would render meaningless Con- 
gress’ use of the term “unreasonable” in §2254(d)(1), a 
result this Court sought to avoid in Williams by making a 
clear distinction between an “incorrect or erroneous 
application” of federal law and an “unreasonable applica- 
tion” of federal law. Williams, 529 U.S. at 410. The focus of 
a federal court’s inquiry under §2254(d)(1) is not whether 
the federal court would find a constitutional violation if 
presented with a factual scenario identical to that in the 
state court, but the reasonableness of the state court's 
decision in light of the evidence before it. If sensonabie 
minds could disagree as to the outcome, the state court s 
decision is not an unreasonable application of controlling 
federal law. Williams, 529 U.S. at 410 (“reasonable jurists” 
standard in the Teague context is an objective inquiry). See 
Brief of Amicus CJLF.

Il. THE COURT OF APPEALS IMPROPERLY PRE- 
SUMED PREJUDICE TO FIND A SIXTH AMEND- 
MENT VIOLATION, WHERE CONE’S CLAIM OF 
INEFFECTIVE ASSISTANCE RESTED UPON 
SPECIFIC ERRORS BY COUNSEL AT TRIAL. 


Strickland enunciated the standard for addressing © 


Sixth Amendment claims based on “actual ineffective 
assistance” of counsel at trial, 466 U.S. at 683-84, estab- 
lishing a two-part test for such claims. First, a defendant 
must show deficient performance by counsel and, second, 
that counsel’s deficient performance prejudiced the 
defense. Id., at 687. The Court acknowledged that the 
variety of circumstances faced by defense counsel and the 
range of legitimate decisions regarding how best to han- 
dle a given case foreclosed development of “detailed 
rules” or guidelines for representation. Id., at 688-89. 
Rather, courts “must indulge a strong presumption that 


35 


counsel’s conduct falls within the wide range of reason- 
able professional assistance.” Id., at 689. In addition, the 
Court emphasized that the purpose of the Sixth Amend- 
ment right to counsel is “to ensure that criminal defen- 
dants receive a fair trial.” Thus, a defendant asserting a 
claim of ineffective counsel must demonstrate “a reason- 
able probability that, but for counsel’s unprofessional 
errors, the result of the proceeding would have been 
different.” Id., at 694. See also Roe v. Flores-Ortega, 528 U.S. 


470, 482 (2000). 


On the same day the Strickland opinion was issued, 
this Court also decided another ineffective assistance 
case, which limited the circumstances under which a 
reviewing court may presume prejudice to fin? a Sixth 
Amendment violation. In United States v. Cronic, 466 U.S. 
648 (1984), the defendant was charged in a complicated 
check-kiting scheme that had been under government 
investigation for over four years. When defense counsel 
withdrew, the trial court appointed a young real estate 
lawyer only 25 days before trial. Id., 466 U.S. at 649. On 
appeal, the Tenth Circuit found it unnecessary to inquire 
into .ounsel’s actual performance at trial or its possible 
impact on the outcome. Instead, it inferred that Cronic 
had been denied effective assistance based on five gen- 
eral criteria related to the circumstances of the case: (1) 
the time afforded for investigation and preparation; (2) 
the experience of counsel; (3) the gravity of the charge; 
(4) the complexity of possible defenses; and (5) the acces- 
sibility of witnesses to counsel. Id., at 652. This Court 
reversed, finding that a presumption of prejudice was 
appropriate only under circumstances that are “so likely 
to prejudice the accused that the cost of litigating their 


36 


effect in a particular case is unjustified,” the most 
obvious being a complete denial of counsel. Id., at 658. 
The Court instructed that “only when surrounding circum- 
stances justify a presumption of ineffectiveness can a Sixth 
Amendment claim be sufficient without inquiry into 
counsel’s actual performance at trial.” Id., at 662 
(emphasis added). While acknowledging that the five 
factors identified by the court of appeals might be rele- 
vant to an evaluation of a lawyer’s actual performance in 
a particular case, the Court concluded that Cronic’s case 
was not one in which the surrounding circumstances 
made it unlikely that he could have received the effective 
assistance of counsel. Id., at 665-66. Moreover, the Court 
noted that, should he later pursue claims based on “speci- 
fied errors made by counsel,” those claims should be 


evaluated under the standard enunciated in Strickland. 
Id., at 666 n. 41. 


This Court has recognized only three narrow circum- 
stances in which prejudice may be presumed. First, preju- 
dice may be presumed where there has been a complete 
denial of counsel. Second, a presumption is permitted 
when the state interferes with counsel’s assistance. Cron- 
ic, 466 U.S. at 659 and n. 25. Both of those circumstances 
are easily identified and, “because the prosecution is 
directly responsible, easy for the government to prevent.” 
Moreover, prejudice to the accused in such circumstances 
“is so likely that case-by-case inquiry into prejudice is not 
worth the cost.” Strickland, 466 U.S. at 692 (citing Cronic, 
466 U.S. at 658). Third, a more limited presumption of 
prejudice attaches “when counsel is burdened by an 
actual conflict of interest,” provided that defendant 


37 


shows the conflict “adversely affected counsel’s perfor- 
mance.” Smith v. Robbins, 528 U.S. 259, 287 (2000) (quoting 
Strickland, 466 U.S. at 692); see Cuyler v. Sullivan, 466 U.S. 


335, 350 (1980). 


Aside from those circumstances, however, where a 
claim of ineffective counsel rests upon some articulable 
error by counsel in the course of representation, and 
‘where the “surrounding circumstances” do not prevent 
counsel’s effective functioning, the Strickland analysis is 
fully competent to vindicate the accused’s Sixth Amend- 
ment right to counsel. Bad lawyering, no matter how bad, 
does not justify invoking a per se presumption of preju- 
dice under Cronic. As the First Circuit observed in Scarpa 
v. Dubois, 38 F.3d 1, 14 (1st Cir. 1994), cert. denied, 513 U.S. 
1129 (1995), once it becomes necessary to examine the 
trial record to evaluate counsel's particular errors, the per 
se presumption is no longer justified by Cronic’s rationale 
of avoiding the cost of case-by-case litigation. To apply 
Cronic in such situations would “do little more than 
replace case-by-case litigation over prejudice with case- 
by-case litigation over prejudice per se.” Scarpa, 38 F.3d at 
14. 


In Roe v. Flores-Ortega, supra, this Court recently reit- 
erated that, because the right to effective counsel is recog- 
nized, not for its own sake, but for its effect on the ability 
of the accused to receive a fair trial, “absent some effect 
of challenged conduct on the reliability of the . . . process, 
the [effective counsel] guarantee is not implicated.” 
Flores-Ortega, 528 U.S. at 482 (citing Cronic, 466 U.S. at 
658). The Court thus concluded that, “in cases involving 
mere ‘attorney error,’ we require the defendant to demon- 
strate that the errors ‘actually had an adverse effect on 


38 


the defense.’ ” Id. See also Smith v. Robbins, 528 U.S. 259, 
287 (2000) (listing the three categories of cases where 
prejudice is presumed as: (1) denial of counsel; (2) state 
interference; and (3) where counsel is burdened with an 
actual conflict of interest). 


Since Cronic, this Court has consistently rejected 
attempts to presume prejudice when the defendant's inef- 
fective assistance claim is premised on specific acts or 
omissions of counsel. In Flores-Ortega, the Court refused 
to presume prejudice where counsel failed to file a notice 
of appeal without defendant’s consent. Id., 528 U.S. at 
484. In Robbins, the Court refused to presume prejudice 
where counsel neglected to file a merits brief on appeal. 
Id., 528 U.S. at 288-89. In both cases, the Court required 
that the defendant demonstrate both the performance and 
prejudice prongs of Strickland to sustain an ineffective 
assistance of counsel claim.5 Indeed, to require any less in 
such cases would substantially undermine the “strong 
presumption of reliability” normally afforded judicial 
proceedings. Flores-Ortega, 528 U.S. at 482 (citing Robbins, 
528 U.S. at 286). By contrast, the Court refused to apply 
the Strickland test or the harmless error standard under 
Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18 (1967), where the denial 
of counsel left the defendant completely without repre- 
sentation on appeal. Penson v. Ohio, 488 U.S. 75, 88-89
