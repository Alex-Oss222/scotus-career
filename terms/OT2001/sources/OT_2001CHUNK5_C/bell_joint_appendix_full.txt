[1657] MATTHEW JAREMKO, Ph.D., called as a 
witness by the Defendant, after first having been duly 
sworn was examined and testified as follows: 


* * * 


[1670] DIRECT EXAMINATION 
BY MR. DICE: 


Q. All right. Dr. Jaremko, I would ask you if you 
have performed a patient history on your patient, and my 
client, Gary Cone? 


A. Yes. 


12 


Q. Allright. And so that I might ask you this, have 
you made a diagnosis of Mr. Cone in your examination of 
him? 


A. Yes. 


Q. All right. And specifically, would you tell the 
jury whether or not he is suffering from any diseases 
which affect the mind? 


A. Yes. 


Q. And what are those diseases, and what are the 
basis for your findings? 


A. It is my opinion that Mr. Cone has a combination 
of two disorders: One is a substance abuse disorder, but 
that is superimposed upon a post-traumatic stress disor- 
der. The substance abuse disorder — I'll get to in a minute, 
but I believe that the post-traumatic stress disorder is the 
major cause for why he got involved in drugs. This post- 
traumatic stress disorder is a result of him being exposed 
to the trauma of combat and the continuing trauma that 
occurred after he came back as a Vietnam Veteran from an 
unpopular war, in which he became disenfranchised from 
the [1671] society at large, causing him a great deal of 
personal stress, social maladjustment. 


The four features of a post-traumatic stress disorder 
that Mr. Cone has manifested over the years has been, of 
course, the exposure to the trauma of combat; but, sec- 
ondly, the reliving of the combat trauma. Mr. Cone mani- 
fests for some time, nightmares that are related to 
Vietnam - his being in Vietnam. He man fests what are 
called “flashbacks”, that is, thinking about — perceiving 


13 


himself being back in combat situation. The third cate- 
gory of this disorder is an emotional distance and a social 
distance from society at large, in which has a large 
change in his value system - what he perceives as being 
important. After suffering from the exposure to the com- 
bat trauma he apparently has changed from perceiving 
things like work and success and discipline as being 
important dimensions in his life to other kinds of values. 
He considers now things like kindness, pleasure, justice, 
truth, to be more important than acievement [sic]-ori- 
ented values. In other words, he’s distant from - he’s at 
odds with the traditional values that most of us are 
socialized by, because of his exposure to the Vietnam 
combat situation. 


Then the fourth category of this disorder is stress 
related symptoms, whether they be depression, nervous- 
ness, sleep disorders - he has manifested all of these 
problems. 


[1672] Now, I believe that he then started to use 
drugs sometime during his tenure in Vietnam, and has 
continued to use drugs as a self-way to self-medicate 
agxinst the stress that he feels from having been a victim 
ot the combat trauma. So, in summary, it’s my opinion 
that he has a combination of a substance abuse disorder 
that is a result of self-medication from the stress related 
disorder he incurred when he was in Vietnam. 


Q. Why do you say that the substance abuse disor- 
der is an outgrowth of his service in Vietnam, Dr. Jar- 
emko? 


14 


A. There is no evidence from my interview, or 
other’s interviews of Mr. Cone, that he used drugs before 
he went to Vietnam.

Q. All right. And would that illness that he was

[1675] A. I believe it would. 


Q. All right. And I would ask you whether or not

A. I can’t say about that. 


Q. All right. I would ask you, Dr. Jaremko, whether 
or not Mr. Cone has expressed to you any remorse for his

SENTENCING PHASE - MURDER FIRST DEGREE 


Ladies and Gentlemen of the Jury, you have now 
found the defendant guilty beyond a reasonable doubt of 
Murder in the First Degree as charged in counts 1 & 2 of 


- indictment numbers B74702 & B74703. 


It is now your duty to determine within the limits 
prescribed by law, the penalty which shall be imposed as 
punishment for this offense. Section 39-2402, Sub-section 
(b), Tennessee Code Annotated, provides that a person 
convicted of Murder in the First Degree shall be punished 
by death or by imprisonment for life. 


In arriving at this determination, you are authorized 
to weigh and consider any mitigating circumstances and 
any of the statutory aggravating circumstances which 
may have been raised by the evidence throughout the 
entire course of this trial, including the guilt finding 
phase or the sentencing phase or both. The Jury are the 
sole judges of the facts, and of the law as it applies to the 
facts in the case. In making up your verdicts, you are to 
consider the law in connection with the facts; but the 
Court is the proper source from which you are to get the 
law. In other words, you are the judges of the law as well 
as the facts under the direction of the Court. 


The burden of proof is upon the State to prove any 
statutory aggravating circumstance or circumstances 
beyond a reasonable doubt to a moral certainty. 


54 


Reasonable doubt is that doubt engendered by an 
investigation of all the proof in the case and an inability, 
after such investigation, to let the mind rest easily upon 
the certainty of your verdict. Reasonable doubt does not 
mean a doubt that may arise from possibility. Absolute 
certainty is not demanded by the law but moral certainty 
is required and this certainty is required as to every 
proposition of proof requisite to constitute the verdict. 
The law makes you, the Jury, the sole and exclusive 
judges of the credibility of the witnesses and the weight 
to be given to the evidence. 


Section 39-2404, Subsection (i), Tennessee Code 
Annotated, provides that no death penalty shall be 
imposed by a Jury but upon a unanimous finding of the 
existence of one or more of the statutory aggravating 
circumstances, which shall be limited to the following:

than twelve years of age and the defendant was 
eighteen years of age or older. 


2. The defendant was previously convicted of one or 
more felonies, other than the present charge, which 
involve the use of threat or violence to the person.

- another to commit the murder for remuneration or 
the promise of remuneration.

“Cruel” means designed to inflict a high

FINDINGS OF FACT AND CONCLUSIONS OF LAW 


FILED 9-19-86 


This matter is before the Court on Petition for Post- 
Conviction Relief filed by Gary Bradford Cone, through 
his counsel, James D. Kopernak. The State is represented 
by Eddie Peterson, Assistant District Attorney. This peti- 
tion was heard on the 29th day of April, 1986. 


The questions presented are as follows: 


1. Whether or not there was prosecutorial miscon- 
duct on the part of Joseph Patterson and Don Strother, 
Assistant District Attorneys, which affected the outcome 
of the trial? 


2. Whether or not failure of counsel to object during 
the State’s argument amount to ineffective assistance of 


counsel? 


79 


3. Was there ineffective assistance of counsel at the 
sentencing phase of the trial? 


The Court will now consider the first question pre- 
sented: 


This question arises out of final argument of the 
prosecutors in the guilt phase, in the murder first degree 
trial of petitioner. It appears that the day before the 
murders were committed, petitioner had allegedly robbed 
a Big Star ste-e and obtained $1,893.72. This robbery 
indictment was pending at the time of the murder trial. 
On the day of the murders, petitioner was being pursued 
by police after robbery of a jewelry store in which no 
money was obtained. After abandoning the automobile 
during the chase, petitioner eluded police and forced his 
way into the home of an elderly couple, the subject of the 
murders. Meanwhile, police found in the abandoned car a 
large amount of legend drugs and cash in the amount of 
$2,459.94. 


Petitioner claims that the prosecutor’s argument 
referring to the $2,459.94 found in petitioner’s automobile 
as proceeds from the sale of drugs was improper; that 
prosecutors knew, or should have known, that more than 
likely this money came from the robbery of the Big Star 
the day before. 


Petitioner’s trial attorney, John Dice, pointed out in 
his testimony that the reason he did not object to the 
State’s argument concerning the money found in the 
automobile was that he felt the damage had been done. 
He did not want to emphasize it further. It is hard to 
undo what a jury has heard, even with instructions from 
the Court. 


80 


The test for alleged improper prosecutorial misconduct 
is “whether the improper conduct could have affected the 
verdict to prejudice the defendant”. Judge v. State, 532 
S.W. 2d 340 (Tenn. Crim. App. 1976). Therefore, if the 
prosecutor’s conduct and argument to the jury was 
improper, the question to be answered is, “Did that con- 
duct affect the jury’s verdict to the defendant’s preju- 
dice?”. I think not. 


In Judge v. State, supra, the Court pronounced five 
factors to be considered in determining whether the pros- 
ecutor’s conduct was improper to such a degree as to 
warrant reversible error. 


(1) The conduct complained of viewed in context 
and in light of the facts and circumstances of the 


case; 


(2) Curative measures undertaken by the Court and 
the prosecution; 


(3) The intent of the prosecutor in making the 
improper statement; 


(4) The cumulative effect of the improper conduct 
and any other errors in the record; 


(5) The relative strength or weakness of the case. 


In considering point one, above, this Court takes into 
consideration the aggravated and horrible nature of these 
crimes; the fact that there was a large amount of drugs 
found in the trunk of petitioner’s automobile and the 
abundant evidence in the case as to the murders. This 
Court is of the opinion and finds that the argument of the 
prosecutors concerning money coming from a drug sale 
more likely than from a pending robbery case did not 


81 


affect the outcome of the trial. Had no money been men- 
tioned, the Court is of the opinion the verdict would have 
been the same, based on the facts and circumstances. 


Regarding the second point raised in the Judge case, 
supra, this Court notes that although no curative instruc- 
tions were given because of the lack of any objection, and 
considering the reasons given by trial counsel that he did 
not want to emphasize it any further since the jury had 
already heard it, this Court recognizes this as a viable 
tactic that counsel may use and does not find that Mr. 
Dice’s use of this tactic rendered his assistance ineffective 
as a matter of law. 


Upon consideration of the third point, Mr. Strother, 
the prosecutor, testified that he knew there was a pending 
robbery charge, but he did not concern himself with it 
because they were trying the murder case first. This 
Court finds that prosecutors knew, or should have 
known, that a large amount of money was allegedly taken 
by this defendant the day before the murders. This 
should not have been argued. 


This Court cannot say from the evidence that prose- 
cutors consciously argued it with this in mind. Although 
it appears that the $2,459.94 found in the automobile was 
more likely part of the $1,893.74, which was allegedly 
taken in the robbery the day before, there is no evidence 
to show it was actually a part of the robbery money. This 
Court cannot say, therefore, that prosecutors’ argument 
was malicious or with wilful improper intent. 


As to points four and five, this Court does not find 
any cumulative effect of improper conduct throughout 


82 


the record which would require a new trial in view of the 
strong and compelling evidence in the murder case. 


This Court is of the opinion that prosecutor’s con- 
duct in this case “did not so infest the trial with unfair- 
ness as to make the resulting conviction a violation of the 
Fourteenth Amendment’s Due Process clause”. See 
Darden v. Wainwright, 106 S.Ct. 2464, June 23, 1986. 


Let us turn to petitioner’s allegation that he was 
denied effective assistance of counsel at the sentencing 
phase of the trial. Petitioner sets forth several claims: 


1. Failure to interview critical prosecution witness 
Eileen Blankman; 


2. Failure to adequately advise the defendant of 
the probable effect of his not testifying at the 
penalty stage; 


3. Failure to adequately investigate and adequately 


prepare pre-trial as to mitigating factors and 
circumstances; 


4. The defense counsel’s failure at the penalty 
stage to put on proof of mitigating circum- 
stances and also his waiver of argument at that 


stage. 


These claims of ineffective assistance of counsel are 
considered in light of the defense offered by trial counsel. 
This defense, called “Vietnam Veterans’ Stress Syndrome” 
was novel in its approach and supported by an expert 
witness. These claims should further be assessed in light 
of the testimony of several attorneys in this hearing that 
Mr. Dice is one of the most thorough and conscientious 
criminal defense attorneys in Shelby County. Although 
they did not agree with some of the strategies used, they 


83 


nevertheless had no doubt of his abilities and compe- 
tence. 


In consideration of the first allegation concerning 
failure to interview critical prosecution witness Eileen 
Blankman, the Court notes. 


Mr. Dice’s testimony that he did not interview Eileen 
Blankman; that he knew she was a prosecution witness; 
that he discussed it with his client prior to trial and his 
client strongly insisted that he wanted to “keep her out of 
it”; in view of the overall preparation of the defense in 
this case, the Court does not reach the conclusion that 
failure to interview this witness rendered ineffective 
assistance of counsel. 


Looking at the second allegation, this Court finds 
that Mr. Dice did discuss with his client whether he 
should take the stand. Mir. Dice pointed out that things 
had gotten heated between him and prosecutors; that his 
client was very upset and admitted to his attorney that he 
would explode if he took the stand. Dice testified that he 
left the final decision to his client. 


After considering the facts presented, this Court is of 
the opinion and finds that Mr. Dice did adequately dis- 
cuss and advise his client as to the probable effects of his 
testifying at the penalty stage. 


Turning now to allegations three and four, claiming 
failure to adequately investigate and prepare, pre-trial, as 
to mitigating factors, and also the failure of counsel to 
put on proof of mitigation in the penalty stage, including 
his waiver of argument at that stage. This Court finds 
from the facts and circumstances that Mr. Dice put a great 


84 


deal of thought and preparation in this case; further, he 
pointed out he interviewed numerous family members 
and relatives whose testimony was contradictory and 
generally not helpful. He said his strategy was to get as 
much mitigation in during the guilt/innocence phase as 
he could. It is the opinion of this Court that the defense 
attorney’s performance, including his decision not to 
introduce any evidence in mitigation, did not fall below 
the objective standard of reasonableness set by the Sixth 
Amendment’s guarantee of the effective assistance of 
counsel. Darden v. Wainwright, supra. See, also, Strickland v. 
Washington, 466 U.S. 668. 


In reference to the waiver of final argument in the 
penalty phase, it was clear from testimony from the 
record that this was strategy on the part of defense coun- 
sel, in view of the prosecutor’s (Mr. Strother) reputation 
for devastating closing arguments. Even the attorney tes- 
tifying here at the bar confirmed the extremely effective 
and strong argument of Mr. Strother. 


As the Supreme Court said in the Wainwright case, 
supra,“ ...a defendant must overcome the presumption 
that, under the circumstances, the challenged action of 
counsel might be considered sound trial strategy”. 


Upon consideration of all the proof presented, this 
Court finds as a matter of fact and concludes as a matter 
of law, that petitioner’s counsel, John Dice and April 
Ferguson, did render effective assistance of counsel in all 
respects as required by Baxter v. Ross, 523 S.W.2d 930 
(Tenn. 1975). Accordingly, the Petition for Post-Convic- 
tion Relief is hereby denied.

SOCIAL HISTORY 


A full social history will not be reported here, since it is 
readily available elsewhere. 


Briefly, Gary Cone was the youngest of four children born 
to Valerie and Zack Cone. Zack was a career NCO in the 
military, and by virtue of this, the family moved around 
extensively. Mr. Cone Senior died of a myocardial infarct 
in November 1972. Mrs. Cone is living, but has had a 
stroke, and suffers from hypertension. Of the four chil- 
dren, only two are still living, Gary and his older sister 
Rita. The older child, Zack Leonard, died at age 15 of a 


93 


heart attack, following exposure to icy-cold lake water. 
Zack had residual heart problems secondary to rheumatic 
fever. Gary was 8 or 9 at the time of his brother’s death, 
and remembers seeing Zack’s body being hauled from the 
lake. It was reported by family members that Gary was 
greatly disturbed by his brother’s death. An older sister, 
Sue, who was about five years older than Gary, died in 
February 1990 of breast cancer. Gary was extremely close 
to both of these siblings. Rita, is legally blind, a residual 
of measles at age four. None of the Cone children ever 
married. 


Gary’s early development was apparently unremarkable. 
His mother described him as a “perfect” child. There was 
absolutely no suggestion of any behavioral disturbance, 
even in adolescence. Gary was described as a quiet, stu- 
dious child. However, his relationship with his father was 
cool, presumably because of Gary’s reluctance to partici- 
pate in typically masculine pursuits, such as deer hunt- 
ing. 

Physical health was apparently within normal limits. 
Gary entered school at age five, turning six soon after. 
Grades were always good. Gary spent his senior year in 
high school living with his grandmother. It was not clear 
if this was necessitated by a family move, or the result of 
conflict between Gary and his father. Following gradua- 
tion, Gary enlisted and served in the US military 1966 to 
1969. He was posted to Germany, and from there volun- 
teered for Vietnam. A dramatic difference in Gary was 
noted by family members on his return. He was described 
as edgy, hyperactive, and emotionally estranged. Gary 
attended the University of Arkansas 1969 to 1972, and 
achieved a B.S. in business and finance. During this time 


94 


Gary’s associates were mainly Vietnam veterans. While a 
student he met, and became engaged to, a young woman 
Glenda, who graduated ahead of Gary and moved to 
Tulsa to work. On his graduation Gary took a trip alone 
to Europe. On his return he turned himself into authori- 
ties in Oklahoma and received a 25-year sentence for 
armed robbery. A few months later, October 1972, an 
attempted escape brought another three years, with one

Drug abuse that began in Vietnam, escalated in prison.

August 1980. 


Review of available records revealed that Mr. Cone func-

BEHAVIORAL OBSERVATIONS 


Gary Cone presented as a short, extremely neatly 
groomed 42-year-old caucasian male of average build. 
Dress was institutional, grooming was excellent, hair and 
beard were greying, and glasses were worn. Mr. Cone 
was reserved, softly spoken, cooperative and adequately 
interactive in terms of the assessment process. He was 
emotionally distant, and socially alienated. Affect was 
superficially bright, but appeared blunted at core. There 
was some suggestion of suspiciousness, but not to the 
extent of paranoia. Gary Cone can best be described as 
remote. Indeed, the description of Mr. Cone made in 1981 
as de-personalized, with an automatic and bland style of 
delivery remains relevant. The almost autistic logic 
described then was again similarly in evidence. 


Speech was soft, but articulate, coherent and relevant. 
Conversations were spontaneous and well organized in 
terms of abstract ideas. For example, he is a voracious 
reader, and was willing to discuss literature and ideas 
extensively. He was considerably less forthcoming in vol- 
unteering information about emotional issues. Generally, 
Gary was evasive with evidence of disorganization and 
fragmentation of thought in relation to social relation- 
ships. There was no evidence of florid psychosis; both 
auditory and visual hallucinations were denied. Suicidal 
and homicidal ideations were similarly denied. There was 
no evidence of a delusional system. Insight was abysmal,

of social situations. 


Gary’s description of his social history was essentially as

TEST RESULTS 


Mr. Cone was found to be functioning within the superior 
range of intellectual abilities (Verbal IQ 131; Performance 
IQ 116; Full Scale IQ 129). The observed Verbal > Perfor- 
mance difference of 15 points, was similarly noted in 
1981. Intratest scatter had also remained stable. The range 
of observed scaled scores in both 1981 and today was 7 to 
18. Current test results demonstrated verbal intelligence 
within the very superior range. However, within the per- 
formance domain, Gary’s abilities only fell within the 
high average range. Psychomotor retardation was clearly 
an important factor. Scaled scores on the WAIS-R have a 
mean of 10, with a standard deviation of 3. Thus, scaled 
scores of 8 to 12 fall within the average range. The indi- 
vidual subtest scaled scores obtained by Mr. Cone are 
shown in the attached data sheet. On six of the eleven 
subtests, performance was >91st percentile compared to 
same age peers. However, performance on the Digit Span 
and Digit Symbol subtests was at the 16th and “5th 
percentile respectively. Mr. Cone’s abstract reasoning 
skills and his docility with arithmetic problems attracted 
perfect scores. Lowered performance on Digit Span can 
be a function of reduced attention. However it has also 
been associated with left hemisphere vulnerability. 
Difficulties with the Digit Symbol task generally reflects 


99 


psychomotor slowing, but problems with focus may also 
impact performance. 


The difficulties noted on certain WAIS-R subtests, was 
similarly evident in Mr. Cone’s performance on the Trail- 
making tests. Time to complete each part of the test was 
slow compared to overall intelligence. His performance, 
although error-free, placed him at the 50th percentile. 


Higher cognitive functions appeared intact. Absolutely 
no difficulties were observed with either the Category 
Test, or the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test. 


Tests of memory functions revealed significant differ- 
ences between verbal and visual memories. Despite Mr. 
Cone’s superior verbal intellect, his memory for verbal 
material was below average. In particular, he had consid-

with no social meaning was excellent. Problems were 
evidence with immediate memory, and did not deterio- 
rate further with a 30 minute delay. The already observed 
difficulty recalling strings of numbers (WAIS-R), was also 
evident on the WMS-R. 


The unusual, but stable, pattern observed in Mr. Cone 
appears to reflect psychopathology, rather than organ- 
icity. This was the general conclusion in 1981, and current 
test data, both cognitive and personality, appeared to 
support this.

DISCUSSION 


The similarity between current test data and that 
obtained a decade ago was interesting. In 1981 it was 
concluded that data was reflective of the “intrusion of 
aggressive impulse derivatives”. There was no clear evi- 
dence to support organic involvement. There was sim- 
ilarly no evidence to support the presence of florid 
psychosis. However, social history and limited earlier 
psychological data suggested no premorbid impulse con- 
trol difficulties. For example, Mr. Cone was described as a 
perfect child. Thus, any aggressive impulses might be 
assumed to be the product of some intervening variable. 
Possible candidates appears to be: drug dependence; 
head trauma; or a psychiatric disturbance. 


At this time active drug dependence is a non-issue. Mr. 
Cone claims no current use. Little evidence exists to 
support organic involvement secondary to head injury. 
However, there is considerable evidence to support the 
presence of a psychiatric disturbance. The social history 
is replete with events likely to produce symptoms consis- 
tent with a diagnosis of post-traumatic stress disorder 
(PTSD). 


102 


PTSD is an anxiety disorder that occurs in response to 
events that are typically viewed as being outside the 
realm of normal human experience. Mr. Cone has experi- 
enced several such events. The major traumas in his life 
have been: 


1. At age 8 or 9, witnessing his brother’s body being 
removed from the lake. Gary’s problems following 
this event are well documented historically. 


2. His grandmother’s death, just after high school grad- 
uation. Gary lived with her, and clearly viewed her as 
a safe haven from his father. 


3. Duty in Vietnam, 1968-1969. although not a combat 
soldier, experiences were beyond the realm of normal 
experiences for a 20-year-old. 


4. Rape and murder of his fiancé in December 1972. 


The extensive literature on trauma, and its aftermath has 
shown that the effects can be both direct and indirect. 
basically, the effects on Mr. Cone appear to have been 
indirect. One such indirect effect is avoidance and 
numbing. In these individuals, there is a reduced capacity 
to experience emotion, notably in the realms of intimacy, 
tenderness, grief and the appropriate expression of sexu- 
ality. They become emotionally detached, and the ability 
to experience pleasure is diminished. Mr. Cone fits this 
cluster of behaviors. Current literature tends to focus on 
two types of trauma victims, and Mr. Cone has a foot in 
each camp. The bulk of the literature reflects the effects of 
trauma on Vietnam veterans. A second body of literature 
is concerned with the long term effects of childhood 
trauma. Mr. Cone experienced both, plus the experience 
of the rape and murder of his fiance.
