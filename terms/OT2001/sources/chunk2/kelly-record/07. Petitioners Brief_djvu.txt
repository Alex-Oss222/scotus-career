STATEMENT OF FACTS 


On January 1, 1996, a South Carolina statutory 
amendment took effect that abolished parole or other 
forms of early release for all prisoners convicted of mur- 
der. No. 83, § 10, 1995 S.C. Acts 557, codified as $.C. Code 
§ 16-3-20 (Supp. 2000), App. 1-2. In capital cases, the same 
legislation limited the sentencing jury’s options to death 
and life imprisonment without possibility of parole, so 
long as the jury determined the defendant to be death- 
eligible by finding the existence of one or more statutory 
aggravating circumstances. Id.} 


Just five days after the no-parole amendment took 
effect, 17-year-old William Arthur Kelly killed a former 
co-worker at a Kentucky Fried Chicken restaurant during 
the course of a robbery. He confessed to the crime, and 
was duly indicted in a South Carolina state court for 
murder, kidnaping, armed robbery, and possession of a 
knife during the commission of a violent crime. The facts 
of the offense involve the gruesome stabbing of a preg- 
nant victim, and were summarized by the South Carolina 
Supreme Court as follows: 


On the night of January 5, 1996, police offi- 
cer Stephen Clare drove by the Batesburg KFC 
and noticed in the parking lot a car running 
with the driver’s door open. Knowing that it 
was after the restaurant’s usual closing time and 
recognizing the car as belonging to Shirley 
Shealy, the manager of the KFC, Officer Clare

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


In Shafer v. South Carolina, 121 S. Ct. 1263 (2001), this 
Court invalidated one of the two bases of the South 
Carolina Supreme Court's affirmance of petitioner’s 
sentence — namely, that post-Simmons changes in South 
Carolina’s capital sentencing procedures had wholly 
eliminated the state’s obligation to inform sentencing 
juries of the nonexistence of parole. The constitutionality 
of petitioner’s sentence thus rests on the validity of the 
state court’s remaining justification for denying his 
request for a Simmons instruction, which was that the 
prosecution never placed his future dangerousness at 
issue. This rationale is belied by the record. 


The state prepared the way for what would prove to 
be an onslaught of dangerousness rhetoric by placing 


15 


before the jury evidence of petitioner’s pretrial escape 
attempts, his possession of weapons in jail, his plan to 
take a correctional officer hostage, his expressed lack of 
remorse, his obsessive homicidal! ideation, and his history 
of torturing small animals. In addition, the trial court 
permitted the prosecution to introduce, as evidence of 
petitioner's “character,” graphic depictions of the 
extreme brutality with which he murdered the victim. 
Then, in his sentencing phase jury argument, the prosecu- 
tor squarely put before the jury the issue of petitioner’s

(b) Mitigating circumstances: 


(1) The defendant has no significant history of prior 
criminal conviction involving the use of violence against 
another person. 


(2) The murder was committed while the defendant 
was under the influence of mental or emotional distur- 
bance. 


(3) The victim was a participant in the defendant's 
conduct or consented to the act. 


(4) The defendant was an accomplice in the murder 
committed by another person and his participation was 
relatively minor. 


(5) The defendant acted under duress or under the 
domination of another person. 


(6) The capacity of the defendant to appreciate the 
criminality of his conduct or to conform his conduct to 
the requirements of law was substantially impaired. 


(7) The age or mentality of the defendant at the time 
of the crime. 


(8) The defendant was provoked by the victim into

The statutory instructions as to statutory aggravating 
and mitigating circumstances must be given in charge 
and in writing to the jury for its deliberation. The jury, if 
its verdict is a recommendation of death, shall designate 
in writing, and signed by all members of the jury, the 
statutory aggravating circumstance or circumstances 
which it found beyond a reasonable doubt. The jury, if it 
does not recommend death, after finding a statutory 
aggravating circumstance or circumstances beyond a rea- 
sonable doubt, shall designate in writing, and signed by 
all members of the jury, the statutory aggravating circum- 
stance or circumstances it found beyond a reasonable 
doubt. In nonjury cases the judge shall make the designa- 
tion of the statutory aggravating circumstance or circum- 
stances. Unless at least one of the statutory aggravating 
circumstances enumerated in this section is found, the 
death penalty must not be imposed. 


Where a statutory aggravating circumstance is found 
and a recommendation of death is made, the trial judge 
shall sentence the defendant to death. The trial judge, 
before imposing the death penalty, shall find as an affir- 
mative fact that the death penalty was warranted under 
the evidence of the case and was not a result of prejudice, 
passion, or any other arbitrary factor. Where a statutory
