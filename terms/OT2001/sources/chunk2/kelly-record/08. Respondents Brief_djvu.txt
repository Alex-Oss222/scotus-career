RESPONDENT'S STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
A. Procedural History 


Petitioner, William Arthur Kelly, is presently confined to 
the Lieber Correctional Institution of the South Carolina 


2 


Department of Corrections pursuant to orders of commitment 
from the Clerk of Court for Lexington County. During the 
November 1996 term, the Lexington County Grand Jury indicted 
Petitioner for murder, kidnapping, armed robbery, and possession 
or display of a knife during the commission of a violent crime. 
JA 2-4. On October 14", 1997 and February 16", 1998, Eleventh 
Circuit Solicitor Donald V. Myers gave notice of intent to seek 
the death penalty, and on August 13, 1998, the state served its 
Notice of Evidence in Aggravation. 


The case was called for trial before the Honorable Gary E. 
Clary and a jury on September 14", 1998. On September 17”, 
1998, Petitioner’s jury convicted him of all four charges. 


The sentencing phase of his trial began on September ie, 
1998. After presentation of evidence, Judge Clary submitted the 
following aggravating factors to the jury: 


(1) Murder committed while in the commission of 
a kidnapping; 


(2) Murder committed while in the commission of 
a burglary; 


(3) Murder committed while in the commission of 
a robbery while armed with a deadly weapon, 


(4) Murder committed while in the commission of 
larceny with use of a a deadly weapon; 


(5) Murder committed while in the commission of 
physical torture. 


JA 291-92. The following mitigating factors were submitted to 
the jury: 


3 


(1) The defendant has no significant history of 
prior criminal conviction involving the use of 
violence against another person, 


(2) The age of the defendant at the time of the 
crime; 


(3) The defendant was below the age of eighteen 
at the time of the crime. 


Judge Clary also instructed the jurors that they could consider any 
non-statutory mitigating circumstance. JA 297. 


On September 19", 1999, Petitioner's jury found the 
existence of all five (5) aggravating factors and recommended a 
sentence of death. That same day, Judge Clary sentenced 
Petitioner to death for murder, to thirty (30) years for armed 
robbery, and to a consecutive five (5) years for possession of a 
knife during commission of a violent crime. Since Petitioner was 
sentenced to death. Judge Clary did not sentence him for 
kidnapping pursuant to the rule in section 16-3-910 of the Code 
of Laws for South Carolina. R. 2094-97. 


A timely Notice of Appeal was filed and served on 
September 21%, 1998. In the direct appeal to the South Carolina 
Supreme Court, Petitioner was represented by Assistant Appellate 
Defender Robert M. Dudek. Assistant Attorney General S. 
Creighton Waters represented Respondent. Both parties filed 
briefs in the appeal, and Petitioner presented the following issues, 
inter alia: 


I. Whether the judge denied Due Process by 
refusing to instruct the jury that “life 
imprisonment” meant imprisonment “until death 
of the offender”, since the state offered evidence 


4 


of future dangerousness, the solicitor argued 
future dangerousness, and appellant's request to 
charge #1 was a correct statement of South 
Carolina law, was mandated by Simmons v. South 
Carolina, and the judge had a duty to charge it? 


I]. Whether the judge erred by refusing to charge, 
in the alternative, that future dangerousness was 
not an issue, since the state stipulated that future 
dangerousness was not an issue, and appellant was 
entitled to have that stipulation, defense request to 
charge #2, heard by the jury? 


The South Carolina Supreme Court heard oral argument 
on September 20", 2000, and the court issued its opinion 
affirming Petitioner’s conviction and sentence on January 8”, 
2001. State v. Kelly, 540 S.E.2d 851 (S.C. 2001). On January 
23, 2001, Attorney Dudek filed on behalf of Petitioner a 
“Petition for Stay of Execution”. The Remittitur was sent down 
on January 24", 2001. On January 25", 2001, the state filed a 
letter Return in which it did not object to Petitioner’s requested 
stay of execution. The South Carolina Supreme Court on 
February 7", 2001 granted the motion for stay of execution. 


Petitioner sent his petition for writ of certiorari to the 
United States Supreme Court on April 3", 2000, and the petition 
was docketed on April 5", 2000. Certiorari was granted on June 
25", 2001. 


Presented at Trial 


Although the defense requested and the judge gave a 
charge on voluntary manslaughter at trial, it is undisputed that 
Petitioner brutally killed 25-year-old and pregnant Shirley Slade 


5 


Shealy by stabbing her over thirty times at a Kentucky Fried 
Chicken (KFC) restaurant in Batesburg. Shealy was Petitioner’s 
former boss and a close friend of his sister. 


Shealy was the manager of the KFC restaurant and was 
last seen closing up for the night. A police officer who observed 
Shealy’s left running with the door open in the KFC parking lot 
discovered a grisly scene inside the restaurant. When found, 
Shealy’s hands were bound behind her back with duct tape, and 
she was near an overturned chair covered with blood and tape. A 
piece of an opaque latex glove was found attached to the duct 
tape, and a bloody dive knife was found on a rack in the kitchen. 
Over $1000 in cash was scattered throughout the scene, including 
a number of bills found stuck to the victim with her own blood. 
When the floor safe was later opened, it was virtually empty 
except for a rippéd deposit bag and a deposit slip. R. 1292-93, 
1302, 1337-39, 1423-42, 1510-11, 1565. ; 


Large amounts of blood spatter throughout the kitchen and 
hallway of the restaurant indicated a struggle and repeated violent 
blows during Petitioner’s sustained assault on Shealy. JA 27-33; 
R. 1437-43. The pathologist, Dr. Sexton, testified that there were 
thirty-one (31) stab or incision wounds to Shealy, including 
around twenty (20) wounds to her back. Petitioner also slit 
Shealy’s throat from ear to ear, severing both her jugular vein and 
her airway. Shealy had no defensive wounds because he hands 
were bound behind her back. JA 34-45. 


Items in the back seat floorboard of the victim’s car had 
been pushed over to the passenger side, leading police to theorize 
that Petitioner had concealed himself in Shealy’s car and waited 
for her. R. 1295, 1331-33, 1451-54. 


Police promptly started interviewing KFC employees and 
others who were at the KFC on January 5". Eventually, they 


6 


attempted to contact Petitioner, but they found out he did not 
come home that night. Petitioner's mother filed a missing 
persons report on Petitioner the next day. R. 1325-37. 


The break in the case came on the afternoon of January 8", 
when Petitioner's mother told police that Petitioner was at his 
sister’s house in Lowell, Massachusetts. R. 1340-41, 1353-54. 
There, Lowell police found Petitioner, who orally admitted to the 
murder and later signed a written statement. In the statement, 
Petitioner claimed he had to stab Shealy because she kept trying 
to have sex with him. He stated he panicked, took some money, 
and drove from South Carolina to Lowell. He stopped to buy 
some clothes on the way. JA 16-27; R. 1358-64, 1365-74. 


When Lowell police searched Petitioner, they found cash 
stained with Shealy’s blood. R. 4387, 1470, 1555. Petitioner’s 
car also contained his pants, T-shirt, and shoes covered in the 
victim’s blood. R. 1462-64, 1556. Finally, sneakers found in 
Petitioner’s car matched bloody footprints found at the scene. R. 
1475-76. 


Further proof of Petitioner’s robbery plan came from 
employees of the KFC, who testified that it was common 
knowledge among employees that money taken in after late 
afternoon was kept overnight in the floor safe. R. 1258, 1490-91. 
The employees also testified that the knife, duct tape, and latex 
gloves used by Petitioner were not of the type normally used by 
the KFC. R. 1268-70, 1495-97, 1500-01. 


The defense rested in the guilt phase without presenting 
a case.

C. Presentation and resolution of the federal 
issue at trial 


During his opening statement in the penalty phase, the 
solicitor began with themes of retribution, stating that the death 
penalty was the “only one sentence for the brutal, bloody, 
butchering murder [that Petitioner] committed”, and that the 
death penalty was “the punishment that fits the crime”. JA 63-64. 
Afier discussing the aggravating factors, the solicitor recounted 
how the murder and robbery was planned, and pointed out that 
Petitioner killed the victim even though he knew she was 
pregnant. JA 64-67. The solicitor concluded by again stating that 
“there’s only one sentence for this crime”. JA 68. 


The state’s penalty phase evidence began with the 
pathologist and crime scene analysts, who testified about 
Petitioner’s exceptionally brutal and prolonged assault on 
pregnant Shirley Shealy. JA 156-75; R. 1802-19. The 
pathologist testified that Shealy’s baby was healthy and normal, 
and might have even survived had it been born in a hospital 
setting at the time of the mother’s death. JA 175-78. The state 
also presented victim impact testimony from family members 
about the devastating effect Shealy’s death had on her husband, 
young son, parents, in-laws, and siblings. R. 1848-80. 


Jail guards testified about Petitioner’s behavior in prison, 
including an incident where Petitioner refused to obey orders. JA 
72-74. Petitioner also was in a small cell with two others where 
correctional officers discovered that the mortar around a cinder 
block had been dug away. The inmates attempted to conceal the 
work by stuffing colored toilet tissue in the cracks. JA 78-84, 91- 
102, 106-17. 


A fellow inmate testified that Petitioner frequently talked 
of escape and even targeted a particular slightly-built female 


; 
guard as his hostage. One time, Petitioner hid a shank in his hand 
when he called the guard over to the cell door, but he ultimately 
backed out of his escape plan. JA 129-33. A correctional officer 


confirmed finding on Petitioner a shank made from a toothbrush 
and a metal object. JA 139-42. a 


Another former inmate testified that Petitioner bragged 
about his crime, asked other inmates to call him “KFC,” and 
talked about taking a nurse hostage. JA 146-49. Petitioner 
carried a styrofoam cup on which he had drawn a knife, a 
decapitated chicken, and the words “KFC”. JA 134-35, 148-49. 


The defense case in mitigation began first with evidence 
that Petitioner had no history of significant criminal convictions 
or juvenile adjudications. R. 1886, 1888. Petitioner's mother 
testified that he had a normal childhood and even did well 
academically until high school, when his grades went bad and he 
started getting into trouble. R. 1893-1903. 


A defense psychologist stated that Petitioner has average 
intelligence and no mental illnesses. The most the psychologist 
could say is that Petitioner might have been on his way to an 
alcohol problem. According to the psychologist, Petitioner is 
adaptable to prison, and Petitioner's possession of a shank, talk 
of escape, and drawing of a decapitated chicken was just the 
typical “puffing” often done by younger, smaller-built inmates. 
JA 178-93. On cross-examination, the state asked the defense 
psychologist how his opinion about adaptability and Petitioner's 
lack of mental illness was affected by reports in the mental health 
records that Petitioner had violent fantasies of getting away with 
killing people who have wronged him, and had tortured and killed 
small animals. JA 194-99, 218-19. 


9 


Former warden James Aiken also testified Petitioner was 
adaptable to prison life and easily managed and contained by the 
South Carolina Department of Corrections. Aiken theorized that 
Petitioner might be victimized in the harsh world of prison. JA 
220-42; R. 1971-73. 


Before closing arguments in the sentencing phase, the 
defense requested the judge charge from S.C. Code § 16-3-20(A) 
that a life sentence meant “until death of the offender”, and could 
not be reduced by parole, community supervision, any type of 
work or early release program, or any type of credits. After the 
solicitor stated that there were no Simmons v. South Carolina 
issues because he was not going to argue future dangerousness, 
the defense asserted the state had in fact raised future 
dangerousness through evidence of escape attempts, a plan to take 
a guard hostage, and cross-examination of the defense experts on 
jail records. In response, the solicitor stated that those only went 
to past conduct, and he did not bring in a mental health expert to 
testify Petitioner would be dangerous in the future. The solicitor 
also noted the distinction under Simmons between danger to those 
inside prison and danger to outside society. The trial court 
ultimately ruled that the future dangerousness prong of Simmons 
had not been met, and denied the request to charge. JA 243-48, 
249, 252-54. 


After the trial court denied the defense’s request for the 
verbatim charge from subsection |6-3-20(A), Petitioner asked for 
a charge that life imprisonment was to be understood in its plain 
and ordinary meaning. Petitioner also requested a charge that the 
state had stipulated future dangerousness was not an issue in the 
sentencing proceeding. The court denied the latter request. JA 
249-51. 


The solicitor began his closing argument by again stating 
that the death penalty “fit the crime” in this case. JA 256. As he

When discussing the aggravating factor of physical torture, the 
solicitor reminded the jury that Petitioner’s attack “was 
prolonged, unmerciful, intentional inflection of severe pain and 
abuse to another person”. JA 259. The solicitor stated that the 
victim was fighting for her life, but that Petitioner was “carrying 
out his plan”. The solicitor concluded his discussion of physical 
torture: 


He was a master murderer. And not 
instantaneous, but proionged, unmerciful, severe 
pain and abuse. All we've got to do is prove one 
[aggravating factor]. 


JA 259. 


After addressing the aggravators, the solicitor responded

The solicitor then referred to Petitioner’s plan to attack a

The solicitor next argued that Petitioner did not deserve

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


Since this Court decided Simmons v. South Carolina, 512 
U.S. 154 (1994), South Carolina courts as well as the courts of 
other jurisdictions have held that its due process rationale did not 
apply unless the prosecution affirmatively raised future 
dangerousness as a basis for the death penalty. The South 
Carolina Supreme Court so held in Petitioner’s case. 


Petitioner now seeks to expand on years of interpretation 
and application of Simmons, initially by arguing that future 
dangerousness to society can be raised by mere presentation of the 


15 


defendant’s other bad acts as well as the circumstances of the 
capital offense itself. Such a holding would make the future 
dangerousness to society requirement virtually meaningless and 
would conflict with the rationale of Simmons itself: a due process 
right of rebuttal. 


Although evidence of prior criminal record, bad acts, 
premeditation, and grisly circumstances of the capital crime itself 
has been held on appeal to support a finding of future 
dangerousness to society in jurisdictions that expressly submit 
that issue to the jury, it does not follow that such evidence 
automatically flags the issue in the minds of South Carolina 
jurors, to whom the issue is not mentioned at all. It is the express 
submission of and argument of the issue before the jury that 
makes such “routine” capital trial evidence raise future 
dangerousness to society. Indeed, some states hold future 
dangerousness is not an appropriate matter for consideration; yet 
they still allow evidence of prior criminal record or circumstances 
of the crime without concern that the jury is improperly focused 
on the issue. 


Thus, in order for a due process right of rebuttal to arise, 
the prosecution must affirmatively raise to the jury that the death 
of the defendant is the only way to ensure that society is 
protected. Future dangerousness to society is in issue if it is an 
expressly submitted aggravator, if the prosecution expressly 
argues it as a basis for death, or if the prosecution presents 
evidence for which there is no other inference but future 
dangerousness to society. \ 


Mere argument and evidence of a defendant’s other bad 
acts or the circumstances of his crime do not raise future 
dangerousness to society if the prosecution only uses them in the 
retributive context, that the defendant is undeserving of mercy 
because of what he has done. Retribution is a concept legally and 


16 


logically distinct from the concept of specific deterrence. 
Moreover, the state is entitled to introduce evidence of the 
defendant’s other bad acts to prevent an overly generous view 
when the defense presents its inevitable Eighth Amendment 
mitigation case on the defendant’s character and record. 


Moreover, Simmons is only triggered by argument or 
evidence that the defendant poses a future danger to free society, 
not argument or evidence that he poses a danger to other inmates 
or prison staff. Parole ineligibility does nothing to rebut claims 
that the defendant will be dangerous in prison or will be an escape 
threat. Unless the point is expressly made, the jury will not 
necessarily assume future dangerousness to society from evidence 
or argument about dangerousness in prison; indeed, the evidence 
in Petitioner’s case was that prison was causing him to 
misbehave. Further, evidence of and argument about the 
defendant’s misconduct in prison does not trigger Simmons if it 
is limited to addressing the defense’s adaptability case. 


Unlike the prosecutor in Simmons, who characterized a 
death sentence as a “response of society” to a “threat”, and an act 
of “self-defense”, the prosecutor in this case did not assert that 
Petitioner’s death was the only way to protect society. When the 
prosecutor’s argument is viewed as a whole, it is clear that the 
passages of which Petitioner complains were part of a retributive 
argument that the defendant was undeserving of mercy for the 
acts that he had committed. The passages also responded to the 
mitigating factors of age and lack of a prior criminal record. 


Even if the prosecution’s case did raise future 
dangerousness, Simmons was not violated. Unlike Simmons, the 
jury in Petitioner’s case did not inquire about parole eligibility, 
and the judge did not charge that parole was not for the jurors’ 
consideration. Also, defense counsel repeatedly argued to the 
jury that Petitioner would “never see the light of daylight again” 


17 


if sentenced to life, which satisfied the mandate in Simmons. 
Since there is nothing to indicate the jurors were concerned with 
or confused about the possibility of early release, there is no 
reason to think that the jurors did not accept that the “plain 
meaning” of life imprisonment is imprisonment for life.

As noted before, the Simmons rule is based in the due 
process right of a capital defendant to rebut the state’s 
presentation of future dangerousness as a basis for death. 512 
U.S. at 164-65 (discussing Skipper v. South Carolina, 476 U.S. | 
(1986), and Gardner v. Florida, 430 U.S. 349 (1977)). The 
rationale is obvious for distinguishing between argument about 
future dangerousness to the public, which would trigger Simmons, 
and argument about dangerousness while inside prison, which 
would not: whether or not the defendant may be released from 
prison on parole is irrelevant if the prosecutor is only arguing that 
the defendant will be dangerous to other inmates and prison staff. 
Since life without parole does absolutely nothing to respond to 
dangerousness inside prison, there is no corresponding due 
process right of rebuttal. 


31 


Despite its obvious logic, this distinction finds support in 
Simmons itself as well as the decisions of other jurisdictions. See 
Simmons, 512 U.S. at 165 n.5 (“Of course, the fact that a 
defendant is parole ineligible does not prevent the state from 
arguing that the defendant poses a future danger. The state is free 
to argue that the defendant will pose a danger to . . . inmates or 


\prison staff.”) (plurality); 512 U.S. at 177 (“Of course, in such 


cases the prosecution is free to argue that the defendant would be 
dangerous in prison.”) (concurrence). 


Petitioner contends that these passages only mean that the 
prosecution may continue to argue future dangerousness inside 
prison even though a parole ineligibility charge is given. 
Whatever merit Petitioner’ s interpretation may have from the way 
the above passages were phrased in Simmons, this does not 
change the obvious logic that ineligibility for parole does nothing 
to rebut future dangerousness to other inmates and prison staff. 
If, as noted by both Justices Blackmun and O’Connor, an 
ineligibility for parole charge does nothing to preclude the 
relevance of prosecution arguments that the defendant would be 
dangerous in prison, then the opposite is also true, that 
prosecution arguments about the defendant’s future 
dangerousness in prison do nothing to make relevant the 
defendant’s ineligibility for parole. Cf Ramdass v. Angelone, 120 
S.Ct. 2113, 2122 (2000) (parole ineligibility charge can be 
overcome if jury concludes defendant may pose risk to society in 


prison). 


In any event, the importance that the prosecution argued 
future dangerousness to society is readily apparent from the 
plurality and concurring opinions, even though Justice Scalia’s 
dissent states Justice Blackmun’s opinion may “appear” not to 
require future dangerousness argument to trigger Simmons. First, 
both the plurality and concurrence focused on the prosecution’s 
argument that the jury must decide “what to do with [Simmons] 


32 


now that he is in our midst”, and that a death sentence would be 
“a [self-defense] response of society to someone who is a threat”. 
512 U.S. at 157. Both the plurality and the concurring opinions 
made much of the testimony in Simmons that the defendant was 
only dangerous to elderly women and thus would not pose a threat 
to the men in prison. 512 U.S. at 157-58, 176. 


Second, the plurality opinion defined future 
dangerousness argument as “urg{ing] the jury to sentence the 
defendant to death so that he will not be a danger to the public if 
released from prison”. Simmons, 512 U.S. at 163 (emphasis 
added). Indeed, the Simmons plurality noted its due process 
concern was that “there may be no greater assurance of a 
defendant’s future nondangerousness fo the public than the fact 
that he never will be released on parole”. Id. at 164 (emphasis 
added). Justice Blackmun noted that the state raised future 
dangerousness “generally”, but then “thwarted” the defendant’s 
efforts to show that his parole ineligibility prevented him from 
“pos[ing] a future danger fo society”. Simmons, 512 U.S. at 165. 
Similarly, Justice O’Connor stated that due process required an 
ineligibility charge if life without parole was the only alternative 
sentence and “the prosecution argues that the defendant will pose 
a threat to society in the future”. Simmons, 512 U.S. at 177. 
Finally, the significance to this Court’s decision that the 
prosecutor in Simmons raised future dangerousness to society

in Justice Scalia’s dissent. 512 U.S. at 180-82 (arguing that the 
majority was incorrect in concluding that “future dangerousness 
outside of prison” was raised by the prosecutor in Simmons).*

Specific discussion is warranted here on evidence of

IV. South Carolina may remain true to its state law rule that 
disfavors introduction of collateral circumstances such as 
parole, as long as the state honors the due process rule of 
Simmons in the limited situations to which it applies. 


Subject only to the due process rule of Simmons, states 
retain “some discretion in determining the extent to which a 
sentencing jury should be advised of probable future custody and 
parole status in a future dangerousness case”. Ramdass v. 
Angelone, 120 S.Ct. 2113, 2119 & 2121 (2000). South Carolina 
has long reasoned that since the jury’s only responsibility is “to 
determine in the first instance whether punishment should be life 
or death”, the jury should not “speculate on the possible effect of 
pardon or parole”, or concern itself with “the time, place, manner, 
and conditions of execution or incarceration”. See, e.g. State v. 
Torrence, 406 S.E.2d 315, 322-23 (S.C. 1991). Indeed, even a 
defendant sentenced to life without parole under South Carolina’ s 
murder statute could still benefit from pardon or clemency. See 
S.C. Code Ann §§ 24-21-920 to -1000 (1977 & Supp. 2000). 
Moreover, a parole ineligibility charge is of “uncertain 
materiality”, and can be overcome if a jury concludes the 
defendant may escape, be pardoned, benefit from a change in state 
laws, or be dangerous to society in prison. Ramdass, 120 S.Ct. at 
2122. South Carolina seeks to avoid introduction of such 
collateral concerns into the sentencing proceeding. As long as 
South Carolina does not prohibit defendants from using their 
parole ineligibility for rebuttal when it is relevant, that is, when 
prosecutors make a case for death by affirmatively raising future 
dangerousness to society, then South Carolina may remain true to 
its state law rule. 


In this case, the prosecution did not expressly assert that 
Petitioner would be dangerous to society in the future, nor was it 
a necessary inference from the evidence. There is no indication 
that the jury was concerned wit or confused about Petitioner’s 
parole ineligibility, and defense counsel made the point that 
Petitioner would never leave prison if sentenced to life. The 
South Carolina Supreme Court correctly concluded that
