STATEMENT OF FACTS 
On January 1, 1996, a South Carolina statutory amendment took 
effect that abolished parole or other forms of early release for 
all prisoners convicted of murder. No. 83, § 10, 1995 S.C. Acts 


557, codified as $.C. Code § 16-3-20 (Supp. 1999), App. 21-24. In 


Capital cases, the same legislation limited the sentencing jury's 
options to death and life imprisonment without possibility of 
parole, so long as the jury determined the defendant to be death- 
eligible by finding the existence of one or more statutory aggra- 
vating circumstances. Id.’ 

Just five days after the no-parole amendment took effect, 17- 
year-old William Arthur Kelly killed a former co-worker at a 
Kentucky Fried Chicken restaurant during the course of a robbery. 
He confessed to the crime, and was duly convicted in a South 
Carolina state court of murder, kidnaping, armed robbery, and 
possession of a knife during the commission of a violent crime.

WHY THE WRIT SHOULD BE GRANTED 


1. The South Carolina Supreme Court’s holding 
that Simmons does not apply to the statutory 
scheme governing petitioner’s sentencing hear- 
ing has been overruled by this Court's inter- 


vening decision in Shafer v. South Carolina, 
532 U.S. _— (2001). 


Petitioner’s case is yet another in a series of decisions 
which recently led this Court to observe that “South Carolina has 
consistently refused to inform the jury of a capital defendant's 
parole eligibility status.” Shafer v. South Carolina, 532 U.S. 
__., Slip op. at 10 (March 20, 2001). This time, as related above, 
the state court’s refusal rests on alternative holdings. First, 
the state supreme court held that while the state’s evidence and 
jury argument “clearly” placed in issue petitioner’s future danger- 
ousness, they did so in a way that failed to trigger the due 
process rule of Simmons v. th ¢ lina, 512 U.S. 154 (1994). 


And second, the state court reiterated its holding, previously 


announced in State v. Starnes, 531 S.E.2d 907 (S.C. 2000), and 
State v. Shafer, 531 S.E.2d 524 (S.C. 2000), that Simmons is in any 


event inapplicable to the current South Carolina sentencing scheme. 
The second of these holdings has now been expressly rejected 
by Shafer v. South Carolina, 532 U.S. __ (2001). In Shafer, this 
Court held that the possibility of a less-than-life sentence in 
cases where the prosecution fails to establish the defendant's 
death-eligibility does not remove South Carolina's sentencing 
scheme from the ambit of Simmons. The state court's decision to

not place the defendant's future dangerousness in issue as required

important question that this Court found to be “not ripe for.

the issue as follows:

not argued or calling the evidence by another name. 

Slip op. at 17 (record citations omitted). Because the South 
Carolina Supreme Court had not explicitly focused on or resolved 
this question when it initially rejected Shafer’s Simmons claim, 
this Court remanded his case for further consideration. Here, 
however, the state court expressly held that petitioner’s future 
dangerousness, as that concept is employed in Simmons, was neither 
“ta logical inference from the evidence’ [nor] ‘injected into the

Carolina, supra, quoting State v. Kelly, supra, 540 S.E.2d at 857.

This holding was incorrect on both points: the issue of peti- 
tioner’s future dangerousness was both a logical inference from the 
state’s evidence and was hammered home in closing argument. The 
state supreme court majority’s holding to the contrary reflects a 
recalcitrant refusal to implement this Court’s decision in Simmons, 


and warrants correction. 


The state court acknowledged that the prosecutor "clearly 
presented" both evidence and argument to the effect that petitioner 
would pose a violent escape risk while incarcerated. 540 S.E.2d at 
855-57. According to the state court, however, this explicit claim 
of future dangerousness did not implicate the Simmons right of 
rebuttal because the fact of petitioner’s parole ineligibility did 
not directly rebut the state's implication that he would attempt to 
secrete weapons, take hostages and escape if he was not executed. 
Id. at 857. 

Of course, a dangerous prisoner can fashion shanks, take 
hostages and try to escape whether or not he is eligible for 
parole. What the state court overlooked, however, is that evidence 
of dangerousness in prison is also powerful evidence of dangerous- 
ness outside of prison, where the defendant will have much more 
freedom to indulge whatever violent impulses he may harbor. 


Evidence that for this particular defendant, not even prison was

effective in curbing his violent tendencies created a particularly 
strong inference of generalized future dangerousness. So while an 
accurate instruction on petitioner's parole ineligibility would not

serving a life sentence in prison, it would have allayed the jury's 
inevitable concern that petitioner would prove all the more violent 
should he ever be paroled back into society.‘

that executing him is the only means of eliminating the 

threat to the safety of other inmates or prison staff. 

But the State may not mislead the jury by concealing 

accurate information about the defendant's parole ineli- 

gibility.

ousness were just that: veiled references. Indeed, two dissenting 
Justices detected nothing at all in the prosecutor’s argument that 
even referred to Simmons’ future behavior: to the dissenters, the 
“in-our-midst” passage referred only to Simmons’ alleged attempt to 
blame his crimes on his childhood environment, while the “self- 
defense” reference was merely an appeal for a calm and dispassion- 
ate societal response to Simmons’ brutal crimes, rather than a 
sentence based on anger or emotion. 512 U.S. at 181-82 (Scalia, 
J., dissenting). If the prosecutor’s argument in Simmons sufficed 


to place Simmons’ dangerousness in issue, so did the much more 


explicit cross-examination and argument here. 


In denying petitioner’s request for a Simmons instruction, the 
trial judge ruled that the state’s evidence of future dangerousness 


actually went to petitioner’s “character and characteristics.” 


_——s 


This purported distinction between bad character and dangerousness 
does find support in prior South Carolina decisions, gee, @.g., 
State v. Young, 459 S.E.2d 84, 37 n.4 (S.C. 1994), but this Court’s 
cases are to the contrary. California v. Ramos, 463 U.S. 992, 
1006 n.20 (1983) (*“{A) jury’s evaluation of the defendant’s 
future dangerousness may rest on lay testimony about the defen- 
dant’s character and background and the inferences to be drawn 
therefrom”); Ramdass v. Angelone, 120 S.Ct. 2113, 2122 (2000) ("To 
demonstrate Ramdass’ evil character and his propensity to commit 
violent acts in the future, the prosecutor used Ramdass’ prior 
criminal conduct. .. .”). In this context, moreover, the dis- 
tinction is meaningless. When the prosecution seeks to prove that 
a teenaged murderer has exhibited unpredictable and extreme vio- 
lence, cannot be rehabilitated, lacks remorse, and behaves vio- 
lently in jail, it is trying to prove that his character is one of 
persistent and violent criminality--which is to say, that he is 
dangerous and will remain so. The defendant’s right to inform the 
jury that he will never be released from prison does not depend on 
whether this portrayal is labeled “dangerousness” or “character,” 
because in this setting the two terms mean the same thing. See 
Skipper v. South Carolina, 476 U.S. 1, 7 (1986) (rejecting South 
Carolina’s effort to distinguish between capital defendant’s “past 
good conduct to prove good character and use of the same evidence 


to establish future good conduct in prison.”). 


-18- 


South Carolina’s resistance to this Court’s due process 
holding in Simmons has continued too long. Virtually alone among 
the nation’s 40 death penalty jurisdictions, gee Shafer v. South 
Carolina, supra, slip op. at 10, n. 4, South Carolina continues to 
devise new rationales for its unfair and unconstitutional refusal 
to inform sentencing juries of the alternative to the death penalty 
that state law provides. By refusing to acknowledge the state’s 
obvious injection of the issue of future dangerousness into peti- 
tioner’s sentencing hearing, the South Carolina Supreme Court has 
sought, in effect, to vitiate the Simmons rule. Unless corrected 
by this Court, the precedent established by this case will autho- 
rize South Carolina prosecutors who wish to evade Simmons to do so 
by the simple expedient of giving a new name to the state’s case on 
the issue of dangerousness. The Court should not permit such 
persistent disregard of one of its constitutional precedents, in 


this or any other case.

[2](3] Evidence is relevant and admissible if it

prejudice means an undue tendency to suggest

implicitly overruled on other grounds by 
Casey v. State, 305 S.C. 445, 409 S.E.2d

3-50 (1985 & Supp.1999). " ‘Malice’ is the

502 S.E.2d 63, 69 (1998). 


FN2. Murder is “the killing of any

FN3. For example, the State

Compare State v. Garner, 304 S.C. 220, 403

authonty--whether judge or jury--be fully 
informed with regard to sentencing options, 
including the convicted offender's parole 
ineligibility. 


I would hold that where requested by the 
defense, a properly phrased parole ineligibility 
charge must be given to the jury in a capital 
case. 


The jury would be instructed that should they 
find the existence, beyond a reasonable doubt, 
of one of more aggravating circumstances, there 
would be but two potential punishments: life 
impnsonment or the death penalty. The jury 
would further be informed that life imprisonment _ 
means unt! death, and that the defendant would 
be ineligible for parole or other early release. 
This is the charge requested by appellant here. 


The jury would also be informed that should it 
fail to find the existence, beyond a reasonable 
doubt, of at least one statutory aggravating 
circumstance, the punishment would be solely 
within the province of the trial judge. The jury 
would be told that the sentencing options would 


App. 16 App. 17 


be limited to life impnsonment without the 
possibility of parole, or, assuming the defendant 
were a non-recidivist, a mandatory minimum of 
at least thirty years, also without the possibility 
of parole. 


I agree with Justice Blackmun’s plurality 
opinion in Simmons v. South Carolina, supra, 
wherein he discussed the perceptions of most 
jurors regarding parole: 

It can hardly be questioned that most junes 
lack accurate information about the precise 
meaning of life impnsonment as defined by 
the States.... An instruction directing junes 
that life impnsonment should be understowd in 
its plain and ordinary’ meaning does nothing 
to dispel the misunderstanding reasor able 
jurors may have about the way in which any 
particular State defines life impnsonment’. 

Id. at 169-70, 114 S.Ct. at 2197, 129 

L.Ed.2d at 146. The Simmons plurality 

noted that, at the time it was decided, twenty- 

Six States empowered junes in capital 

sentencing and provided for life impnsonment 

without parole as an alternative punishment. 

Of those states, seventeen required that the 

jury be expressly informed of the defendant's 

parole ineligibility. See id atn. 7. The 

Commonwealth of Virginia recently joined 

that number with its Supreme Court's decision 

in Yarbrough v. Commonwealth, 258 Va. 
347,519 S.E.2d 602 (1999). In my opinion, 


this State should likewise adopt the practice. 


In its search for an appropriate sentence, the 
jury should have the benefit of clear and 
accurate instructions regarding its options. A 
“plain meaning” instruction does not adequately 
inform the jury. Without the knowledge that, if 
aggravators are found. a life sentence is not 
subject to being reduced by parole, or any 
other method of early release, the jury ts likely 


App. 


to speculate unnecessanily on the possibility of 
early release, and impose *864 a sentence of 
death based upon “fear rather than reason.” 
Yarbrough v. Commonwealth, supra, citing 
Farris v. Commonwealth, 209 Va. 305, 307. 
163 S.E.2d 575, 576 (1968). A parole 
ineligibility charge would eliminate any 
speculation. Since such acharge was not given 
in this case despite appellant's request, I would 
reverse the sentence and remand for a new 
sentencing proceeding.
