ARGUMENT IN REPLY 


“1. The state’s proposed standard would effectively 
overrule Simmons. 


South Carolina concedes that the state’s evidence 
alone can, under some circumstances, place future dan- 
gerousness “at issue” under Simmons v. South Carolina, 
512 U.S. 154 (1994). Brief for Respondent at 15, 26. It 
further acknowledges that the prosecution’s evidence of 
petitioner’s violent character and behavior was more 
than sufficient to prove his future dangerousness beyond 
a reasonable doubt, had South Carolina law permitted or 
required the jury to make such an explicit finding. Brief 
for Respondent at 18-25. Nevertheless, the state insists 
that its evidence did not place petitioner’s future danger- 
ousness at issue because he has not shown that the jury 
could have drawn “no other possible inference but future 
dangerousness to society” from the state’s proof. Id. at 27 
(emphasis in original). 


This “dangerousness-only” standard, if adopted, 
would mean that most evidence of future dangerousness 
would fail to trigger the Simmons rule, since such evi- 
dence is usually also relevant to the separate issue of the 
defendant’s moral desert. Its practical effect would be to 
allow prosecutors to inundate capital sentencing juries 
with evidence of defendants’ likely future dangerousness, 
while evading Simmons by pointing out that the evidence 
also had retributive significance. Respondent cites no 
state or federal case adopting this highly restrictive stan- 
dard, and its sole motivation for proposing it appears to 


be that under any less grudging interpretation of Sim- 
mons, the state court’s judgment in this case would be 
impossible to defend. 


2. Other states do not rely on a narrow reading of 
Simmons. 


South Carolina lists state court decisions that “have 
identified the prosecutor’s express argument of future 
dangerousness as a necessary trigger for applicability of 
the [Simmons] rule.” Brief for Respondent at 20-21, n. 4. 
To the extent that this list is meant to imply that any 
appreciable number of other states share South Carolina’s 
reluctance to inform juries about parole ineligibility, the 
implication is false. Of the nine states represented, all but 
Pennsylvania now require trial courts to inform capital 
sentencing juries of the unavailability of parole in every 
case, regardless of Simmons’ dangerousness requirement,

and in most of the cases cited, the jury was so informed, 
either by instruction or the sentencing verdict itself. 
Whatever references to Simmons’ future dangerousness 
requirement may be found in these cases, the salient fact 
to be drawn from the practices of American state courts is 
that South Carolina now stands almost alone in refusing 
to enlighten capital sentencing juries concerning the 
meaning of their non-capital sentencing option. 


3. The prosecutor invoked both retribution and future 


dangerousness. 


Turning to the prosecutor’s argument, the state 
labors to show that its primary “focus” was retribution, 
and that the prosecutor did not “expressly raise” danger- 
ousness. Brief for Respondent at 37-44. But this claim, 
even if it were true, depends on the state’s additional 
contention that future dangerousness can only be put in 
issue by evidence or argument that is subject to no other 
possible interpretation but that of the defendant's future 
dangerousness. That this restriction would effectively 
overrule Simmons is illustrated by the facts of this case, in 
which the state interprets the prosecutor’s claim that 
petitioner wa “dangerous” and “more frightening than a 
serial killer” as an appeal for retribution. Be that as it 
may, such argument also implicitly invited the jury to 
protect society against petitioner. Accordingly, Simmons 
entitled him to show that this goal could be achieved by 
life imprisonment without parole as well as by death. 


Commonwealth v. Clark, 710 A.2d 31, 35-37 (Pa. 1998) (upholding 
denial of Simmons instruction). 


4. Defense counsel’s argument did not remove the 
need for a no-parole instruction. 


The state asserts that defense counsel's seemingly 
hyperbolic claim that petitioner would “never see the 
light of day again” if the jury sentenced him to life 
imprisonment was the equivalent of a no-parole instruc- 
tion. In Shafer v. South Carolina, 532 U.S. 36, 121 S.Ct. 1263 
(2001), this Court squarely rejected the same argument, 
although there defense counsel's argument was both 
more emphatic and more specific, 121 S.Ct. at 1273 
(“ ‘{hJe will die in prison’ ”), and although Shafer’s jury 
was instructed (as petitioner’s was not) that “life 
imprisonment means until the death of the offender.” Id. 


Nor does the failure of petitioner’s jury to request 
further instructions about parole distinguish this case 
from Simmons. Brief for Respondent at 47. Just as a jury’s 
question does not convert a constitutional instruction into 
an unconstitutional one, Weeks v. Angelone, 528 U.S. 225, 
234-37 (2000), so the absence of a jury request for further 
instruction does not cure a constitutionally inadequate 
charge. 


5. South Carolina may soon abandon its resistance to 
the Simmons rule. 


Although the state suggests that its reluctance to 
inform sentencing juries of the nonexistence of parole 
reflects a determination to “remain true” to longstanding 
state law, Brief for Respondent at 49, South Carolina’s 
judgment on this issue is by no means settled. On May 16, 
2901, the South Carolina House of Representatives 


approved legislation to require trial judges to read the no- 
parole provision of S.C. Code § 16-3-20(A) (Cum. Supp. 
2000) to capital sentencing juries upon request of either 
side, and the measure is now pending in the state Senate. 
H.R. 3819, 113th Leg., 1st Sess. (S.C.). If this legislation 
becomes law, as now appears likely, South Carolina will 
finally join the overwhelming majority of death penalty 
jurisdictions in which sentencing juries are informed of 
the unavailability of parole for capital murderers whose 
lives are spared.? 


6. South Carolina’s arguments do not explain its 
unwillingness to comply with Simmons. 


The state’s brief fills 50 pages, but sheds no light on 
the most puzzling aspect of South Carolina’s dogged 
resistance to Simmons. Why have South Carolina prosecu- 
tors gone to so much trouble to avoid letting juries know 
how the state’s legislature has defined life imprisonment? 
For if (as the state insists) the prosecution’s evidence and 
argument did not lead the jury to assess petitioner’s 
likely future behavior, a Simmons instruction would pre- 
sumably not have affected the jury’s deliberations, and 
the state would have no reason to care whether the 
instruction was given or not. 


In truth, the determination with which South Caro- 
lina continues to probe for loopholes in Simmons suggests

that it actually agrees with petitioner’s view of the real- 
ities of capital sentencing. When a jury convicts a defen- 
dant of a death-eligible murder, very little further 
prompting is needed to focus the jury’s attention on the 
danger that defendant may pose in the future if he is 
allowed to live. And once a sentencing jury focuses on 
that issue, a death sentence is much more likely if the jury 
is kept in the dark about its power to protect society by 
means of a true life imprisonment sentence. The state has 
no valid interest in increasing the number of death sen- 
tences by preserving and exploiting this “false dilemma,” 
Simmons, supra, 512 U.S. at 171, and the Court should 
reverse petitioner's death sentence in such a way as to 
ensure that this practice does not continue.
