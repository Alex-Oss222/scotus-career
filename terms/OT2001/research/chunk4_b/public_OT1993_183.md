### Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., No. 92-1292

Merits decision, 1994-03-07

Argued November 9, 1993; decided March 7, 1994. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit, 972 F.2d 1429, which reversed the defendants' summary judgment, 754 F.Supp. 1150, and held the use unfair. The questions concern commercial parody under §107, the relation between borrowing and criticism, and the proof of market effect.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Associate Justices Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg participated at argument and decision. All nine were seated at both milestones; no recusal or nonparticipation is established.

The Court reverses the Sixth Circuit's adverse fair-use judgment and remands for the integrated statutory inquiry. It does not reinstate summary judgment for 2 Live Crew or decide ultimate fair use.

Reversal and remand, 9–0: Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg. All nine reject the categorical commerciality and market-harm treatment, adopt the narrow lyrical application, and leave musical extent and the potentially material nonparody derivative market for further consideration. No Justice opposes this disposition. No final infringement, royalty, damages, or injunction determination is made.

Souter delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, and Ginsburg. All nine join every stated holding and remand limit. Kennedy also concurs, joined by no other Justice; his full join remains intact.

Section 107 requires the four fair-use factors to be considered together; commercial parody may qualify, and neither profit motive nor a parody label settles the inquiry or creates a dispositive presumption of unfairness. The work must reasonably permit perception of original-directed criticism to claim parody's particular justification for borrowing; satire directed elsewhere lacks that justification merely as satire but may still establish fair use under the actual factors, and requesting or being denied permission does not defeat an otherwise fair use.

The Court holds that the character of the use must be examined rather than inferred from its sale. Section 107 expressly includes commerciality within purpose and character, alongside the remaining statutory considerations. Sony's home-copying analysis and Harper & Row's protection of first publication cannot be detached from the uses and substitution those cases examined. A new work that comments on an earlier work may employ its expression for a different critical purpose. The reported contrast between the original's romantic presentation and the new lyrics reasonably permits perception of such criticism here; that conclusion does not certify taste or ultimate fairness. Recognizable borrowing may be needed to identify criticism's object, while borrowing for unrelated satire requires its own justification. Transformation informs purpose and character but is neither indispensable to every fair use nor a substitute for amount and market analysis. The owner's refusal to license criticism gives it no statutory veto over an otherwise fair use. No independent constitutional immunity is adopted.

Sony Corp. v. Universal City Studios, Inc., 464 U.S. 417: its contextual statutory inquiry is applied; its commerciality language does not establish a universal presumption against sold criticism, and its home-time-shifting result remains intact. Harper & Row, Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enterprises, 471 U.S. 539: distinguished as commercial appropriation in a protected publication market; its four-factor method remains binding, without a categorical rule against commercial parody.

The published original song's creative nature weighs within §107's second factor and is not erased by the claim of parody, but it contributes little to distinguishing fair from unfair parody because parody ordinarily must use recognizable expressive works. Neither publication nor creativity categorically determines fairness, and the four factors remain part of one inquiry.

The Court does not strip the original song of the protection afforded expressive composition. Harper & Row recognizes the importance of a work's character in the fair-use inquiry, and the original here falls close to copyright's creative core. But that observation cannot perform the same work in every setting. Parody commonly must confront expressive material recognizable to its audience; the source's creativity therefore does little by itself to separate lawful criticism from unlawful appropriation. Treating it as an automatic barrier would negate the critical-use inquiry the statute requires. Treating it as irrelevant would make the opposite mistake by excusing courts from considering an enumerated factor. The Court preserves both the original's protected character and the need to evaluate how it is used. Because the song was published, no claim concerning control of first publication is resolved. The statutory amendment providing that unpublished status alone does not bar fair use remains effective but does not decide this dispute.

Harper & Row, 471 U.S. 539: its attention to creative character remains; its first-publication concern does not determine this published-song parody, and no general unpublished-work rule is decided.

Under §107's third factor, both the quantity and qualitative importance of the borrowing must reasonably relate to the critical purpose; taking a recognizable opening or the original's core is not excessive solely because it is central. The brief identifying lyrical reference followed by substantially different critical lyrics takes no more than the parody reasonably requires, but whether the repeated musical borrowing is excessive remains for further consideration with purpose and potential substitution; no fixed allowance or sampling violation is established.

The Court holds that a parody may need the audience to recognize the work it criticizes. Harper & Row makes qualitative importance relevant, but its concern with taking valuable expression does not eliminate the need to ask why it was taken. A rule forbidding the original's recognizable core would often prevent criticism from identifying its object. Here the short lyrical reference performs that identifying function and is followed by a markedly different critical account; its extent is reasonably related to that purpose. This favorable application to the lyrics does not excuse every musical use. The recurring bass riff and broader musical borrowing require their own assessment of how much serves identification or criticism and how much may substitute for protected expression. Recognition supplies a reason for some taking, not a numerical license or blanket permission for repetition. The Court leaves that musical inquiry open and does not find an independent sampling violation. Ultimate fair use still requires the integrated statutory balance.

Harper & Row, 471 U.S. 539: qualitative substantiality remains important, but its application to valuable excerpts is distinguished where recognizable borrowing serves criticism; no automatic permission to take the whole or repeat the core follows.

For this transformative parody, commerciality alone does not raise a presumption of market harm; §107 requires examination of substitution for the original and legitimate potential derivatives, including the effect of widespread comparable conduct. Harm because criticism diminishes demand and foregone licensing of the critical attack itself are not cognizable substitutive injury, but a supported nonparody rap-derivative market remains relevant and this record proves neither its injury nor its absence.

The Court holds that the fourth factor protects copyright markets without granting control over criticism. Harper & Row protects an owner's valuable publication and derivative markets; Sony examines the likely economic consequences of the use rather than dispensing with proof in every setting. A critical parody can reduce demand because its audience accepts the criticism, and that effect differs from supplying a competing substitute. Nor can an owner establish a protected market merely by asserting a lost license for the attack itself. Yet the presence of criticism does not eliminate every ordinary derivative market. A nonparody rap adaptation may compete in a legitimate market distinct from the market for this critical work. Defense affidavits concerning the original's audience and popularity do not settle that separate question. The lower court therefore erred in presuming injury from commerciality, but correction of that error does not establish a complete absence of harm. The Court requires a record-based market inquiry and leaves the ultimate balance open.

Sony, 464 U.S. 417: commercial market-harm language is confined to its context; no universal presumption follows for this transformative use. Harper & Row, 471 U.S. 539: protection of legitimate existing and potential derivative markets remains; criticism injury and lost permission for criticism are distinguished from substitution.

Fair use remains an affirmative defense, and defendants seeking summary judgment must establish it on a record permitting judgment as a matter of law; rejection of the appellate commerciality and harm presumptions does not shift that burden. The Sixth Circuit's adverse fair-use judgment is reversed and remanded without reinstating defense summary judgment, because justified musical taking and the potentially material nonparody derivative market remain unresolved; no final infringement, royalty, damages, or injunction ruling follows.

The Court corrects legal shortcuts but does not replace missing evidence with a presumption favoring the defense. Harper & Row establishes the affirmative nature of fair use and permits appellate determination when sufficient facts support it. Here the original-directed critical purpose and the limited lyrical taking resolve important parts of the analysis. They do not answer how much recurring music is reasonably justified or whether that taking substitutes in an ordinary derivative market. The defendants' showing about the original's audience cannot do the work of an undeveloped inquiry into such a market. Conversely, the copyright owner cannot prevail merely because the parody was sold or permission was refused. Further consideration must combine all four statutory factors under the rules stated today, retaining the defendants' burden and the summary-judgment posture. The Court therefore reverses the appellate determination without restoring the district court's final defense judgment. It decides no amount, license obligation, or equitable remedy and leaves unappealed collateral claims undisturbed.

Harper & Row, 471 U.S. 539: applied to preserve the fair-use defense burden and the requirement of sufficient facts for a conclusive judicial determination.

Sony's time-shifting result and contextual statutory analysis remain; its commerciality and harm language does not establish the rejected universal presumptions for this critical use. Harper & Row retains its protection of creative expression, qualitative substantiality, legitimate markets, and the affirmative-defense burden; its first-publication and substitutive-excerpt setting does not decide every commercial parody. Feist's originality rule is unchanged. No independent constitutional exemption supplies the decision.

Effective March 7, 1994, §107 requires an integrated assessment of actual critical purpose, the protected work's nature, the relation between taking and purpose, and cognizable substitution. Transformation informs purpose and character but does not absorb the other factors. The Court finds reasonably perceptible original-directed criticism and a justified limited lyrical reference; the repeated music and possible nonparody rap market remain unresolved. The original's published creative character remains relevant, though of little help in separating fair from unfair parody. Neither a license refusal nor an allegation of parody decides the defense. Satire remains eligible to make its own statutory showing. Defendants retain their affirmative-defense and summary-judgment burdens.

Kennedy concurs, joined by no other Justice, and joins the Court's opinion in full. A parody claim must identify a reasonably apparent critical relationship to the original; altered lyrics or an asserted humorous purpose cannot automatically justify using valuable music as a sales device. He emphasizes examination of how the borrowing serves that criticism and remains cautious about ultimate fairness on this record. His concern is compatible with the Court's limited finding that criticism can reasonably be perceived and with its favorable lyrical application. It supplies neither a refusal to join nor a heightened mandatory test beyond §107, and it does not foreclose satire from making its own fair-use showing.

The extent of justified musical borrowing and evidence of a legitimate nonparody rap-derivative market require further consideration. The unappealed state-claim preemption determination and denial of leave to add a Lanham Act claim remain outside review.
