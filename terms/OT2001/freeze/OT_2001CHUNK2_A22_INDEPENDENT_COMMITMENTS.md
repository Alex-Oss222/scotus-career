## In re Cybernetic Services, Inc.

### Components and judgment

**C1 — Covered transaction and federal displacement.** Section 261's recording protection for assignments, grants and conveyances concerns transfers of patent ownership rights, including an actual title-transferring mortgage. This stipulated collateral security interest transferred no ownership and does not fall within that category. Its state-law perfection therefore does not conflict with the federal ownership-recording rule or intrude on a comprehensive federal system for perfecting every patent lien. The rights transferred, not the instrument's title, control. The decision does not classify every instrument called a security interest, declare that patent mortgages never fall within section 261, or convert all collateral interests into patent licenses for all substantive purposes.

Section 261 makes a covered assignment, grant or conveyance void against a subsequent purchaser or mortgagee for valuable consideration without notice unless recorded within three months of its date or before the subsequent purchase or mortgage. Both recording alternatives and the consideration/no-notice conditions retain their force. The statute does not declare an unrecorded transaction void against everyone or prescribe universal perfection for any encumbrance touching a patent.

Waterman's actual mortgage transferred title and carried ownership consequences; its rights-based distinction supports rather than defeats this nonownership boundary. Littlefield likewise distinguishes the absolute ownership conveyance from retained lesser rights. The national-register policy is substantial, but national patent protection does not itself displace ordinary secured-credit rules. O'Melveny's identified-conflict requirement defeats creation of a separate federal uniformity rule merely because national notice would be convenient. State filing here regulates credit priority and does not create patent-like rights in unpatented subject matter or alter patent validity, duration or exclusive scope. The particular conflict that would justify displacement is absent.

**C2 — Trustee's competing status, an independently sufficient federal ground.** Section 544(a)(1) places the trustee in the position of a hypothetical judicial lien creditor as of commencement, without regard to knowledge and whether or not an actual creditor exists. That status is not section 261's ownership-taking purchaser or title mortgagee. Consequently, even if transaction coverage were assumed, the trustee's preserved status supplies no federal recording protection against this creditor's earlier interest. The statutory independence from knowledge does not turn a judicial lien into ownership acquired for consideration. Section 544(a)(2)'s unsatisfied-execution theory was not preserved below and no exceptional circumstances were identified; purchaser status likewise was not argued below. Those theories are not newly adjudicated. Section 544(a)(3)'s text concerns real property and supplies no automatic patent-purchaser status.

**C3 — California's two independent step-back grounds.** Former California Commercial Code section 9104(a) withdraws Article 9 only to the extent a federal statute governs the rights of parties and affected third parties in the relevant transaction. Because section 261 does not govern this nonownership collateral interest against this judicial lien creditor, that provision does not withdraw state perfection. The state provision must be addressed on its own text; it is not merely renamed federal conflict preemption, and no universal decision about its precise outer reach is necessary.

Former sections 9302(3)(a) and 9302(4) separately govern substitution of an external filing system. Their reference to a national registration or another filing place must concern an adequate system for perfecting the relevant security interest. A national register of patents and ownership transfers, plus discretionary acceptance of collateral documents, does not itself establish a federal perfection system for this nonownership lien. Those provisions therefore do not make the stipulated California filing ineffective or require an additional PTO filing here. This construction gives effect to their transaction-specific perfection function and the Article 9 comments' description of an adequate outside filing system; it does not replace the governing former Article 9 with revised provisions or declare national registration irrelevant when a federal statute actually supplies the applicable security-interest system.

**C4 — Disposition.** Affirm the Ninth Circuit judgment sustaining the BAP and bankruptcy court's priority determination and relief from the automatic stay. The creditors' state filing compliance and its priority in time over the bankruptcy petition are stipulated. With no required federal filing and no applicable state step-back, their perfected interest has priority over the trustee's preserved section 544(a)(1) claim. The bankruptcy court's permission to pursue foreclosure remains in place; the Supreme Court does not itself transfer ownership, decree that foreclosure has occurred, establish a sale price, adjudicate patent validity or direct payment of a specified debt. No characterization remand is necessary because nonownership transfer and state compliance are stipulated rather than inferred.

### Adversarial paths and preserved limits

The **federal-recording path** would treat modern security interests as the functional successors to patent mortgages, read conveyance broadly and identify a single national notice system as essential to reliable patent transfers. Its difficulty is twofold: the present instrument transfers no ownership of the kind Waterman addressed, and the trustee relies on judicial-lien status rather than the statutory purchaser/mortgagee category. Thus transaction coverage alone would not yield this trustee's priority. A policy preference for broader filing cannot fix either statutory mismatch.

The independent **state-step-back path** could accept absence of federal preemption yet read section 9302(3)(a)'s national-registration language to make state filing ineffective. That is a real alternative, not redundant with C1. The eight Justices reject it because its function and subsection (4)'s perfection consequence require an outside system applicable to this security interest, while section 9104(a) withdraws Article 9 only within actual federal governance. An ownership register is not a security-interest perfection system merely because a collateral document may be accepted for recording. The Court need not decide the validity of PTO regulations to recognize that administrative availability is not statutory necessity.

The **characterization-remand path** would be appropriate if the instrument's rights or California filing compliance were unresolved. They are expressly stipulated here. No source-recovery gap authorizes contradicting that judicially reported stipulation or ordering production to recreate a contest the parties did not present.

The copyright comparison supplies no shortcut: the different statute expressly addresses hypothecation, and its own perfection merits are not before this Court. Neither J. E. M.'s coexistence holding nor its dissent decides patent collateral by analogy alone. The adequate conclusion rests on section 261, section 544, governing former California provisions and the actual instrument's stipulated effect.
