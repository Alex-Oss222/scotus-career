### Bell: rationale and limits

The majority applied Strickland to particular failures to present mitigation and give a closing argument, distinguishing Cronic's complete failure of adversarial testing across the sentencing proceeding. The three Cronic categories remain distinct: denial of counsel at a critical stage; complete failure of adversarial testing; and circumstances in which competent counsel very likely could not provide assistance. Footnote 3 treats the cited government-imposed denials separately. Applying Williams v. Taylor and section 2254(d)(1), the Court found neither a decision contrary to governing law nor an objectively unreasonable application of Strickland's performance standard. It did not establish a categorical rule that waiving argument is effective assistance; the particular alternatives and existing mitigation mattered. Reversal and remand removed the Sixth Circuit's conditional sentencing relief.

Stevens considered the combined failure to investigate, present available mitigation, and advocate after the State's case a complete failure under Cronic. His account distinguished insanity from mitigation, challenged counsel's stated tactical grounds, and explained why an inadequate record makes an ordinary prejudice inquiry difficult. The appendix reproduces counsel's explanation for waiving argument. The majority's footnote 4 expressly disputes using the later mental-health material: it was absent from the state proceedings, Cone did not argue that the Court could use it or that counsel was impaired at trial, and state postconviction experts praised counsel's competence. Stevens acknowledged that the illness could have begun later; his contrary inference is not an undisputed record fact. Footnote 5 neither decides Tennessee evidence law generally nor adopts a categorical rule excluding prior misconduct; the prior robberies were relevant to an aggravator.

Final-report corrections: Cornell's majority slip text erroneously describes avoiding unlawful arrest; the final opinion at 691 says lawful arrest. At 703, the final Stevens dissent states that Cone was accepted to law school for August/September 1980 but never enrolled, replacing the slip text's less specific account and removing its misleading 1972 reference to being before law school. The final opinion at 692 reports no mitigating circumstances substantial enough to outweigh the aggravators; do not turn the syllabus's abbreviated description into a finding that the jury had no mitigating evidence.

### Festo: rationale and limits

A narrowing amendment made to satisfy any Patent Act requirement, including section 112, may produce prosecution history estoppel; a genuinely cosmetic change that does not narrow scope does not. The unanimous Court rejected the complete bar, preserving the doctrine of equivalents and protecting reliance on the prior rule. The patentee bears the distinct burdens concerning the reason for amendment and the particular equivalent surrendered. A narrowing amendment presumptively surrenders the intervening territory. The presumption may be overcome through unforeseeability, a rationale only tangentially related to the equivalent, or another reason the patentee could not reasonably have described the insubstantial substitute. The final test addresses what a skilled person reasonably could have drafted at amendment; the opinion also uses application when listing unforeseeability. Preserve the actual wording and do not silently resolve that temporal formulation by later doctrine.

The concessions concerning the sealing rings and sleeve established a patentability reason. What territory those amendments surrendered, and whether Festo could overcome the presumption for these equivalents, remained for the Court of Appeals or District Court in the first instance. The decision did not find infringement or restore the earlier damages judgment as a final merits result. Warner-Jenkinson, Exhibit Supply, Goodyear Dental, Winans, and Graver Tank supply the principal historical framework. No additional substantive slip/final conflict was found; the final opinion clarifies the reference to alleged equivalents made or distributed by competitors and identifies SMC where the slip text says respondents.

### FMC: rationale and limits

The historical majority extended structural state immunity to the Commission's adjudication of a private complaint against a nonconsenting State. Hans, Alden, and Seminole Tribe supplied historical premises; Butz supplied the functional comparison of administrative and judicial adjudication. The assumed absence of Article III judicial power did not resolve the structural question. The rule covered both reparations and nonmonetary relief. Required agency participation to preserve effective judicial defenses supplied coercion even though agency orders were not self-executing. A possible later federal enforcement action did not transform the earlier private complaint into federal prosecution.

Preserved limits: SCSPA's arm-of-state status was uncontested (footnote 6); the Court did not decide proceedings allowing full de novo judicial review without prior agency participation (footnote 15). Private parties could still report violations. The FMC could investigate, initiate its own proceeding, or seek an injunction, and federal enforcement remained available. Direct private enforcement of reparations differed from federal enforcement of nonreparation orders and penalties. The majority considered the statutory availability of penalties for disobeying a reparation order; the United States disputed that construction. Do not present that disagreement as an adjudicated violation or a penalty actually imposed.

Stevens fully joined Breyer and separately disputed Alden and the dignity rationale, reading the Amendment's drafting history as restricting subject-matter jurisdiction rather than immunizing States from process. Breyer's four-Justice dissent treated agency adjudication as executive enforcement, rejected the historical and textual extension, and emphasized federal responsibility before legally coercive enforcement. He disputed both the procedural analogy and practical-compulsion theory, identified potential statutory or constitutional-review solutions, and would address review defects when an enforcement controversy actually arose. His objections survived even on an assumed acceptance of the earlier immunity cases. No merits finding of Shipping Act discrimination was made.

Source corrections: Cornell repeatedly spells In re Ayers as Ayres; use the final report's Ayers. Other inspected variants are report citations, editorial wording, or text-extraction artifacts. The comparator itself warns that the historical Article I premise differs from supplied current law; that warning requires later reconciliation, which this receipt does not undertake.

### Gisbrecht: rationale and limits

Section 406(b) preserves contingent-fee agreements within the 25-percent ceiling but requires independent judicial review of the resulting fee. It concerns claimant-funded court representation, distinct from fee shifting and section 406(a) agency representation. Counsel bears the reasonableness burden; footnote 17 quotes petitioners' express rejection of a presumption for the agreed amount. Reduction grounds include deficient representation or results, lawyer-caused delay, and disproportionate benefits compared with time. Courts may request time records and normal noncontingent rates as aids, without converting the starting point into lodestar calculation or requiring satellite litigation. District-court assessments ordinarily receive highly respectful appellate review. The remand requires recalculation, not automatic payment of each contract maximum.

The EAJA and section 406(b) can both produce awards, with the smaller amount refunded to the claimant. Section 406(a)'s then-current agreement cap of the lesser of 25 percent or $5,300 is not section 406(b)'s cap. Footnote 4 preserves EAJA hourly-rate increases for cost of living or special factors. Footnote 7 excludes Anderson: consolidated below, but certiorari denied. The Cornell caption's inclusion of Anderson does not establish his participation on the merits.

Scalia preferred lodestar compensation for work actually performed. He regarded the majority's combination of contingent-contract primacy and retrospective hourly review as internally inconsistent, difficult to administer, and insensitive to ex ante risk. His footnote 2 agreed that intentional delay can warrant reducing a contractual fee as breach of an implicit duty of prompt performance. He also distinguished agency agreements by their dollar cap and stressed the standardized, nonnegotiated character of the contracts. No material holding or lineup discrepancy was found. The final report corrects the incidental statutory pinpoint in majority footnote 11 to 31 U.S.C. section 3554(c)(4).

## Added matters: lower-court anchors only

**76. Prudential, 261 F.3d 355.** The [official Third Circuit opinion](https://www2.ca3.uscourts.gov/opinarch/001389.pdf), filed August 2, 2001 after December 5, 2000 argument, is McKee's opinion for McKee, Rosenn, and Cudahy; no separate writing. It affirms the settlement-enforcement injunction. The court relies on the incorporated broad release, retained enforcement jurisdiction, the All Writs Act, and the Anti-Injunction Act exceptions while acknowledging their narrow scope. The injunction binds the Lowes as class members for two policies and restricts use of shared released-policy facts in their excluded-policy case; it does not extinguish every excluded-policy claim. They may prove those claims using exclusively excluded-policy evidence, including direct dealings that neither establish a broader scheme nor call the class policies into question. Footnote 8 recommends clearer future notice without finding this notice defective. The Florida court's separate stay/inactive docket status is procedural history, not a final adjudication of the remaining claims. No Supreme Court merits vote, author, or holding exists in the supplied anchor.

**79. South Camden, 274 F.3d 771.** The [official Third Circuit opinion](https://www2.ca3.uscourts.gov/opinarch/012224.pdf), filed December 17, 2001 after September 25 argument, is Greenberg's opinion joined by Ambro; McKee dissents. The majority reverses the May 10 preliminary injunction and remands. It holds that regulations cannot independently create section 1983 rights absent a statutory right and treats the historical Sandoval analysis as defeating the asserted disparate-impact right. It assumes regulatory validity because the contrary challenge was not timely raised and was unnecessary to its analysis (footnote 6). It expressly reserves intentional-discrimination relief and most other procedural/substantive arguments; footnote 13 rejects the Eleventh Amendment retrospective-revocation defense without discussion. McKee would preserve preliminary relief because Powell's section 1983 holding remained binding after Sandoval's distinct direct-action holding and because Wright, Wilder, and the regulations supported a reasonable prospect of success. His footnote 7 does not independently decide the other preliminary-injunction factors. This is not a Supreme Court merits comparator, and the comparator's identified displaced Sandoval premise cannot be imported as current law.
