## 84. Devlin v. Scardelletti, No. 01-417

### Question, posture and record

Argument March 26, 2002; supplied decision event June 10. The question is whether a member of the mandatory pension class who timely objects to a settlement binding his own pension interest may appeal its approval without successful formal intervention. The original petition describes a member who both objects and moves to intervene. Review of the appellate-status barrier must be kept distinct from reopening the affirmed intervention denial.

The Fourth Circuit judgment is 265 F.3d 195. The Maryland district court conditionally certified a Rule 23(b)(1) defendant class with active-employee and retiree subclasses on February 9, 1999. The trustees sought a declaration concerning repeal of the pension plan's 1991 cost-of-living adjustment; the settlement declared that adjustment void from its adoption in exchange for other terms. Devlin was a retired participant whose own benefits were affected and who had declined to act as named representative. He pursued separate New York proceedings, which deferred the COLA dispute to Maryland.

His counsel sent informal intervention letters April 20 and May 12, 1999. After the August 27 request for preliminary settlement approval, he filed a formal intervention motion September 10. On November 12 the district court denied that motion as untimely, heard his settlement objections, and approved the settlement. The Fourth Circuit affirmed denial of intervention and separately rejected an appeal from approval absent intervention. The fact that the intervention application was late does not establish that the objections were late. The validity of the settlement's pension terms and adequacy of representation are not established by Devlin's disagreement.

The record's class is mandatory; no opt-out right supplies Devlin an ordinary exit from its binding disposition. The Court must distinguish Article III injury, actual class membership, representation, formal party status and the procedural capacity to appeal the specific approval order. The parties dispute the last question, not merely whether a pension interest is financially important. A direct appeal from settlement approval is not an appeal on behalf of all class members or a claim to become their representative.

### Current law and exact Standards

**Intervention before a shareholder’s derivative-settlement appeal — Present operation effective: 1999-01-20.** Felzen's five-Justice Court opinion requires an otherwise nonparty shareholder who objects to a derivative settlement to intervene before appealing; hearing objections alone does not create party status. It expressly distinguishes the corporation's represented claim from every possible personal-claim class or nonparty appeal and preserves review of an actual intervention denial. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas joined the full Court opinion. Stevens's dissent, joined by Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, would recognize a limited appellate role from invitation, appearance and heard objections within the Rule 23.1 representative procedure. It would not allow an unappearing shareholder to appeal or decide the settlement's merit. The complete writings in the public-positions slice supply each Associate's own date-eligible framework; neither coalition decides the different mandatory personal-interest case automatically.

**Constitutional authority to bind absent litigants — Present operation effective: Richards, June 10, 1996; Matsushita supplies the retained representation component.** A valid basis for representative binding effect is required; financial similarity and notice alone do not cure absent representation. That rule does not itself confer appellate status. **Certification of settlement-only classes — Present operation effective: Amchem, 1997-06-25** and **Mandatory limited-fund settlement classes — Present operation effective: Ortiz, June 23, 1999** preserve independent certification and representation requirements. No new challenge to certification or finding that this class fails them is supplied merely by identifying those rules.

Marino v. Ortiz, 484 U.S. 301, requires ordinary nonparties seeking review to intervene; the objectors there were outside the class. Karcher v. May, 484 U.S. 72, concerns litigating capacity after the office supplying it ends. United Airlines v. McDonald, 432 U.S. 385, recognizes timely postjudgment intervention in its actual class-appeal setting, not a general erasure of timeliness. Phillips Petroleum v. Shutts, 472 U.S. 797, supplies class-binding context with its own claim and opt-out limits. Neither fictional intervention nor unsupported reopening may be invented.

Prudential, May 28, 2002, now limits enforcement of a settlement and distinguishes released from expressly preserved claims. Its exact current-law section is included. It does not decide appellate party status or establish that Devlin's claim was excluded from this settlement. No conflict with Felzen is identified.

### Lawful paths and counterarguments

One path treats a timely objector who is necessarily bound on his own pension rights as a party for the limited purpose of appealing the settlement's approval. Its strongest objection is the ordinary intervention requirement and the need for orderly representative litigation: interest and binding effect do not invariably make someone an appellant. The answer must explain this Rule 23 setting and the actual objection, not assume all affected persons can appeal.

A second path requires intervention, applying Felzen's formal procedural prerequisite to the mandatory class as well. Its strongest objection is that Devlin's own rights are adjudicated without exit and his objection already informed the court of his opposition; forcing a separate entry step may add little protection while foreclosing review. A limited-intervention route is also raised in the supplied neutral materials as the United States' position. Its availability depends on the actual intervention posture and accepted question; the Court cannot deem an application timely merely to avoid the choice.

Reversal of the status barrier permits appellate consideration of preserved objections under ordinary requirements; it does not invalidate the pension settlement, find deficient representation, order new objections or decide ERISA merits. Affirmance leaves the attempted approval appeal unavailable. Reviewability of an actual intervention ruling and any independent action remain subject to their own governing requirements, not a newly promised reopening right. A broader rule for all absent members or all class-management orders is not needed to resolve this mandatory-class record, unless independently justified within the accepted question.

## 85. McKune v. Lile, No. 00-1187

### Question, posture and established conditions

Argument November 28, 2001; supplied decision June 10. Kansas officials seek reversal of the injunction affirmed by the Tenth Circuit, 224 F.3d 1175. The question is whether the consequences imposed for declining unprotected incriminating disclosures in the Sexual Abuse Treatment Program compel self-incrimination. This is prospective relief against threatened consequences, not suppression of statements already used at a criminal trial.

Lile was convicted in 1983 of rape, aggravated sodomy and aggravated kidnapping after testifying that the encounter was consensual. Treatment requires accepting responsibility and completing a detailed sexual-history form covering other sexual acts and victims; the program uses polygraph examination. Incrimination concerns include uncharged offenses and potential perjury exposure. No use or derivative-use immunity is supplied. Program confidentiality is qualified: child-abuse reporting, court-ordered release, protection of third parties, protection from self-harm or harm to others, escape planning and inappropriate staff relationships are exceptions. The State's reported lack of prior prosecutions is not immunity or proof that future use is legally impossible.

Refusal would reduce Lile from privilege Level III to Level I and move him to a cell in the maximum-security area. The privilege schedule limits personal property, activities, visitation, earnings and expenditures; the record gives $140 to $20 per payroll as the canteen change and restricts visitation to specified immediate-family and professional categories. Movement in the maximum-security area is more restricted. This event does not extend his sentence, remove earned good-time credit or eliminate the opportunity to earn such credit; the lower court also rejected the asserted loss of parole as an automatic consequence here.

Housing location, custody classification and earned privilege level are distinct. The State's evidence says a medium-custody inmate may be housed within the maximum-security area and that housing reflects several considerations. The warden disputes that the area's danger is categorically greater; Secretary Simmons acknowledges greater potential danger and the presence of more violent inmates. Those statements cannot be collapsed into an undisputed finding of a particular added risk of assault to Lile. The automatic privilege consequence and actual transfer, not a fabricated finding of inevitable violence, supply the presented pressure. The respondent disputes the State's characterization as ordinary incentives; the State disputes his equation of every withheld privilege with unconstitutional punishment.

The separate challenge to penile testing is not resolved by the question about compelled admissions. The district court rejected that privacy claim; the appellate court's treatment followed its Fifth Amendment disposition. A reversal must leave any resulting procedural question to its proper preserved posture, not silently declare the test valid on all grounds.

### Entering law and each Associate's relevant published authority

**State-created liberty in prison discipline — Present operation effective: 1995-06-19.** Sandin's controlling Breyer opinion, joined by Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg with Stone-Zsela, retains genuine substantive constraints and meaningful deprivation as a sufficient, nonexclusive source of protected prison liberty. It rejects mechanical mandatory-word analysis without adopting an exclusive atypical-hardship threshold. Ginsburg/Stevens preserve an independent retained-liberty ground; Breyer/Souter emphasize the complete disciplinary scheme. O'Connor's dissent, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, proposes the atypical-and-significant-hardship replacement. That remains their published position, not current law. The present Fifth Amendment claim is distinct even from the controlling due-process entitlement rule. Both the State's historical Sandin description and any response accepting that description require adaptation to current law.

**Protected silence concerning disputed sentencing facts — Present operation effective: Mitchell, April 5, 1999.** All nine joined Breyer's actual-incrimination/limited-waiver rule. Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer joined the Court's rule against adverse silence inferences proving the crime at sentencing. O'Connor and Thomas joined Scalia's contrary sentencing-inference position; Thomas separately questioned Griffin's foundation without overruling it. Mitchell does not decide every treatment incentive or extend the sentence here.

**Self-incrimination privilege despite professed innocence — Present operation effective: Ohio v. Reiner, 2001-03-19.** Innocence assertions do not eliminate a supported incriminating risk. Woodard, March 25, 1998, supplies the voluntary-clemency comparison: every Associate joined the optional-interview no-compulsion ground and the additional ordinary-civil-inference ground, while preserving the distinct question of an automatic privilege penalty. Its exact holdings and public writings are included. No immunity, waiver or automatic penalty was found there.

Pre-divergence authorities retain their proper settings: Minnesota v. Murphy, 465 U.S. 420, distinguishes ordinary required reporting from threats that penalize invocation; Baxter v. Palmigiano, 425 U.S. 308, allows an ordinary adverse inference in its civil disciplinary setting, where silence was not automatically sufficient for liability; Garrity v. New Jersey, 385 U.S. 493, Spevack v. Klein, 385 U.S. 511, Gardner v. Broderick, 392 U.S. 273, and Lefkowitz v. Turley, 414 U.S. 70, protect against governmental penalties attached to surrender of privilege in their respective employment, licensing and contracting settings. Kastigar, 406 U.S. 441, supplies the scope of immunity adequate to replace the privilege. Turner v. Safley, 482 U.S. 78, addresses prison regulations and legitimate penological interests; the parties dispute its relation to a separately established Fifth Amendment penalty, rather than agree that a generic rational connection answers compulsion.

### Competing paths and relief

A penalty-based path treats the combined automatic reduction and transfer as constitutionally impermissible pressure to give incriminating testimony without protection. Its strongest objection is prison administration's need to allocate limited treatment and use genuine incentives: the privilege is not a freestanding entitlement to particular housing or every earned privilege. The inquiry must identify the actual pressure attached to protected refusal and explain why it crosses the Fifth Amendment boundary.

A contrary path treats the specified consequences as insufficient pressure in the prison setting, relying on unchanged sentence and credits, administrative classification and the permissible use of rehabilitative incentives. Its strongest objection is the automatic consequence directed at invoking privilege, unlike merely declining Woodard's invitation or a supported Baxter inference. It cannot rely on historical Sandin's proposed hardship rule as controlling law. A Justice may advocate that or another Fifth Amendment analogy only by explaining it independently and respecting existing holdings.

Affirmance may preserve the restraint on the challenged consequences while leaving Kansas free to offer voluntary treatment, supply constitutionally adequate immunity or make classifications on supported independent grounds. Reversal permits only the challenged regime at its established scope; it supplies no categorical permission for sentence extensions, credit loss, compelled waiver or criminal use of compelled answers. No clinical finding that confession is the only effective treatment, no new grant of immunity and no independent dangerousness finding is reconstructed.

## 86. Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Echazabal, No. 00-1406

### Question, posture and source-verified limits

Argument February 27, 2002; supplied decision June 10. Chevron challenges the Ninth Circuit's rule, 226 F.3d 1063, excluding a threat-to-self qualification defense under original ADA Title I. The district court had granted Chevron summary judgment under the EEOC regulation. The principal question is statutory authorization for that regulation, distinct from whether Chevron proved an objectively sufficient individual risk.

Echazabal worked at Chevron's refinery through contractors, principally in or around the coker unit, and applied for direct employment. Conditional offers in 1992 and 1995–96 were withdrawn after company physicians evaluated his liver abnormalities, later diagnosed as hepatitis C, and exposure to hepatotoxic substances. Chevron asked Irwin to remove him from refinery work or place him where the exposure would be eliminated; Irwin removed him. The company relied on its physicians and communications with treating physicians. Echazabal submitted Fedoruk and Gitnick declarations disputing the alleged heightened risk and the foundation of the company's assessment.

The district court expressly recognized a genuine factual issue whether plant-helper work in fact posed greater danger to Echazabal than to other workers, but discounted the opposing evaluations because they were prepared later. Echazabal argued that these evaluations applied medical knowledge available at the time, rather than a later medical advance. The Ninth Circuit did not resolve that evidentiary question because it invalidated the own-health defense categorically. An actual significant risk, imminent death or a medically unanimous conclusion is therefore not an established Supreme Court premise.

**Material correction to the runtime's generic reservations:** The district court's JA p.184 n.6 states that Chevron did not dispute disability for purposes of its motion. It also states that Echazabal did not argue in opposition to Chevron's motion that reasonable accommodation could or should have been made, although accommodation was raised against Irwin. The Court must preserve accommodation as a condition of the statutory/regulatory defense without inventing a preserved accommodation claim against Chevron, commanding exploration of a new accommodation, or converting the assumed disability into a final adjudication. Irwin's distinct judgment and other causes are not swept into this review.

### Text, regulation and effective entering law

The original ADA supplies separate work for sections 12111(3) (statutory direct-threat definition directed to others), 12111(8) (ability to perform essential functions with or without reasonable accommodation), 12112(b)(6) (screening qualification standards), 12113(a) (job-related business-necessity defense with accommodation condition), 12113(b) (qualification standards may include no threat to others), and 12116 (EEOC rulemaking authority). Full official 2000-edition text is saved as `ada-2000-12111.txt`, `12112.txt`, `12113.txt` and `12116.txt`; these enactments originate in the pre-divergence 1990 Act. No later disability amendment is entering law.

The official July 1, 2001 CFR sections 1630.2 and 1630.15 are saved as PDFs and extracted text. Section 1630.15(b)(2) includes significant threats to the individual or others. Section 1630.2(r) requires a significant risk of substantial harm not eliminated or reduced by reasonable accommodation, an individual assessment of present ability to perform essential functions safely, reasonable medical judgment using current medical knowledge or the best available objective evidence, and consideration of duration, nature/severity, likelihood and imminence. Those qualifications constrain the defense if authorized; a diagnosis or sincere medical belief alone does not satisfy them.

**Objective assessment of an ADA direct threat — Present operation effective: 1998-06-25.** All nine joined Bragdon's objective individual method; Kennedy authored it. Its Title III holding addresses threats to others, not the contested Title I own-health authorization. Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer joined Bragdon's founded nonconclusive weight for public-health judgments; O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas withheld that distinct ground. **Mitigating measures in actual ADA disability — Present operation effective: Murphy and Sutton, June 22, 1999** remains the original-ADA underlying-impairment rule, not the historical mitigation exclusion. It is separate from this assumed-coverage motion. Murphy's all-nine nonreach of qualification and safety questions does not decide the present defense.

**Delegated Customs interpretation and independent tariff review — Present operation effective: Haggar, April 21, 1999 for promulgated regulations and own-record factfinding; Mead, June 18, 2001 for delegated-force threshold and classification-letter mechanism.** The agency needs actual delegated interpretive authority and action within that authority before controlling Chevron treatment. All Associates except Scalia joined Kennedy's Mead threshold; Scalia's published contrary position rests on authoritative institutional interpretation. Section 12116's express rulemaking charge and the formal regulatory mechanism are materially different from Mead's transaction-specific letters, but delegation does not authorize contradiction of statutory text. **Persuasive weight of a nonbinding agency advisory interpretation — Present operation effective: Christensen, May 1, 2000; Mead, June 18, 2001** remains relevant if controlling force is absent.

Toyota, January 8, 2002, supplies current manual-task coverage law without resolving safety; Waffle House, January 15, separates public enforcement and remedy; Barnett, April 29, distinguishes reasonable accommodation from particular undue hardship; Walton, March 27, sustains an actual delegated statutory choice while preserving statutory limits. Their exact holdings are included. Barnett's Breyer Court opinion was joined by Stevens, O'Connor and Kennedy with Stone-Zsela; Scalia/Thomas's disability-created-obstacle approach and Souter/Ginsburg's ordinary-inquiry view concern seniority, not an already decided own-health rule. Garrett preserves the enacted qualification, accommodation and hardship terms. None supplies an automatic answer from a physician's professional status.

### Lawful paths, arguments and remedial limits

One path reads section 12113(a)'s general qualification defense and subsection (b)'s permissive example as authorizing the bounded EEOC rule. Its strongest objection is that Congress repeatedly specified danger to others and sought to prevent paternalistic exclusion; the defense must not nullify that choice by treating every adverse health prediction as business necessity. Statutory delegation and the regulation's individual objective safeguards require analysis rather than assumption.

A contrary path reads the specific other-person limitation as excluding threats solely to the applicant. Its strongest objection is the breadth of subsection (a), the words may include, the EEOC's explicit rulemaking delegation and the practical employment-safety context. Workplace-safety duties do not independently create an unlimited ADA exemption; likewise a general autonomy principle cannot replace the enacted text. Chevron also advances a qualification/essential-functions alternative. Any such theory must be tested against the separation of plaintiff qualification from employer safety defenses and may not covertly shift the regulation's proof obligations.

Reversal of the categorical invalidation can return the case for the unresolved objective-evidence and application questions without automatically reinstating summary judgment. Affirmance preserves the rejection of this own-health defense without creating a constitutional right to every dangerous job. The Supreme Court need not decide the actual medical conflict to resolve statutory authorization. Any further proceedings must respect the actual motion assumptions and preservation limits identified above; general accommodation language does not revive an unargued claim.
