## Actual entering law and the comparator's displaced premise

Hendricks affirmed invalidation of its original commitment on an as-applied Ex Post Facto ground. It did not facially invalidate the Act, approve the Act generally, or make its due-process discussion controlling. Part I's bounded rejection of a diagnostic-vocabulary objection was non-dispositive; Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer joined it among the Associates, while Ginsburg reserved it. That discussion expressly depended on a serious recognized condition, evidence of impaired control and related dangerous conduct; a high burden or statutory verdict did not replace the substantive limitation. The O'Connor dissent, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, disagreed over punishment on that original record, not over an unqualified power to confine ordinary recidivists.

Seling unanimously preserves the demanding, cumulative actual-punishment inquiry and its distinct predicates and remedies. Hendricks's original findings do not become Crane findings. Foucha forbids continued psychiatric detention on danger alone after its conceded illness predicate ends, while preserving genuinely narrower preventive regimes. Addington's recognition of psychiatric uncertainty supplies no reduction in Kansas's reasonable-doubt burden.

The historical Crane writings relied heavily on a Hendricks decision sustaining this statutory application and treating its due-process explanation as controlling. That premise is absent. Neither the historical majority's description of an already established control holding nor the historical dissent's charge that the Court has withdrawn a previously conferred statutory victory may enter this case as current law. The new constitutional control question must be answered directly. The changed premise affects reasoning even though the eight Associate disposition positions remain the same as history.

## Final operative paths

**A — serious difficulty, vacate and remand:** Due process does not require total or complete inability to control behavior. It does require proof that the present qualifying condition causes serious difficulty controlling dangerous conduct, sufficient in light of the condition's nature and severity to distinguish the eligible person from a dangerous but ordinary recidivist. Diagnosis and predicted danger alone do not supply that functional limit. The determination need not take mathematical form, use a single clinical label, establish involuntariness of every act, or follow a universally prescribed jury-instruction formula. The legal inquiry is functional; psychiatric classifications inform rather than decide it.

Vacate the Kansas Supreme Court's complete-incapacity construction and remand for application of the serious-difficulty rule under Kansas's prescribed beyond-a-reasonable-doubt burden. The trial commitment is not automatically reinstated. The nature of the diagnosed condition and evidence of actual control impairment must be assessed together; no rule makes antisocial personality disorder automatically sufficient or insufficient. Labels distinguishing emotional, cognitive and volitional problems do not substitute for this functional inquiry. No general holding on confinement justified solely by an emotional abnormality without the functional showing is supplied by this record. Any independently preserved punitive-operation objection remains separate, with its own claim and proof; this grant supplies no new punishment adjudication.

**B — no additional independent control element, reverse and remand:** Reverse the complete-incapacity requirement and return for disposition under the statute's genuine present abnormality-or-disorder and causally related dangerousness predicates. The Constitution does not demand a freestanding additional control finding beyond those substantively limiting predicates. The separate issue of proved punitive operation remains governed by Hendricks and Seling. This position is not a diagnosis-only or dangerousness-only authorization and does not treat the high proof burden as creating a missing substantive predicate. It regards the actual qualifying disorder causing the danger as the substantive distinction, without adding a separately measured degree of volitional incapacity.

Both paths reject complete incapacity. That shared rejection does not make B a join in A's additional substantive requirement or in its application remand. An affirmance importing Hendricks's punishment facts is unavailable. A total-incapacity rule lacks controlling support and would exclude serious disorders merely because some volition remains.

**Historical departure:** The disposition alignments match history, but the governing premise differs: actual Hendricks invalidated its original punitive application and made Part I noncontrolling. Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer therefore support a new serious-difficulty holding on their actual present-custody and functional-limit grounds, while Scalia and Thomas retain their independent objection to an added control element without claiming that the Court has taken back a prior judgment upholding the Act. This line records a material premise departure, not an invented vote change.

The selected Scalia/Thomas position should explain its affirmative alternative. The legislature has required a genuine abnormality or disorder causally making repeat sexual violence likely, proved under Kansas's burden; those criteria distinguish eligible civil committees without a separately measured control requirement. They object that the added serious-difficulty standard supplies uncertain guidance and risks converting descriptions of one person's condition into a universal extra element. They reject total incapacity as well and would reverse the lower construction. Their disagreement does not dispense with the serious condition or causal relationship, adopt every diagnosis, negate Foucha's distinct ended-predicate holding, or restrict Seling's separate punitive-operation review.

The six A Justices answer that procedural protection and a diagnostic label cannot supply a substantive boundary when a diagnosis and prediction could encompass ordinary repeated crime. Functional difficulty provides that boundary without demanding an impossible measurement. Their common explanation must state that response and application rule, rather than present the holding as merely a reading of historical Hendricks language.
