## Verified material record and lower judgment

### Tying

The District Court's tying judgment rested on four forms of binding: a Windows 95/98 and Internet Explorer bundle at one price; prohibitions on original equipment manufacturers removing Internet Explorer or its desktop access; deletion of Internet Explorer from Windows 98's Add/Remove Programs utility; and override of the consumer's default-browser choice in specified situations. Microsoft did not dispute those forms of binding in the appeal. It disputed separate products, substantial browser foreclosure and operating-system power. The appellate court retained the operating-system power determination but vacated per se tying liability. Source: section IV introduction.

Commingling browser-only and operating-system-only routines in common library files was a separate supported finding and monopoly-maintenance basis. It was not an additional District Court tying basis, although plaintiffs had requested it. Co-residence in a file is not a finding that every line of code performs both functions. Microsoft disputed that finding; the appellate court relied on conflicting expert accounts and an identified internal document in sustaining it under clear-error review. Source: II.B.2 and IV introduction.

The court found separate browser demand, including corporate preference for one browser across different operating systems. Other operating-system vendors bundled browsers but allowed noninstallation or removal. Evidence conflicted about whether competing vendors offered a discount for a browserless system. No finding resolving that price conflict may be manufactured. The appellate court considered historical separate-demand and industry-practice measures potentially imperfect proxies when a new functional integration changes the product. It did not find Microsoft's particular integration efficient. Source: IV.A.

Microsoft's proposed efficiencies included shared functionality, reduced duplication and distribution cost, and making browser application interfaces reliably available to developers. The appellate court recognized a possible platform-specific benefit but expressly distinguished distributing needed interfaces from forcing an entire browser application or prohibiting later removal. A reason supporting one component does not automatically justify every contractual or technological restriction. Source: IV.B.

The lower holding is expressly confined to this combination, where the tying product serves principally as a platform for third-party applications and the tied product supplies complementary software functionality. It creates neither a software-industry immunity nor a rule that any efficiency assertion displaces per se treatment. Source: IV.B concluding paragraphs.

If plaintiffs pursue the tying claim on remand, the appellate mandate requires actual competitive harm in the putative browser market and assessment of the supported benefits. Plaintiffs must establish that anticompetitive effect outweighs the benefit; operating-system harm supporting section 2 does not itself establish browser-market harm under section 1. The refusal to allow removal and the omitted removal utility remain possible tying theories, although already sustained under section 2; additional remedy must be justified rather than presumed. The default-browser override failed as a section 2 basis because the technical explanation was unrebutted there, but the lower mandate permits plaintiffs to test its different browser-market effect for tying. Price bundling requires resolution of the conflict about a positive browser-attributable increment before weighing the relevant competitive consequences. Source: IV.C.

The lower mandate also forecloses renewed theories depending on a precise browser-market definition or independent barriers to browser entry that plaintiffs had the incentive and opportunity to establish on their failed attempt claim. It preserves what may be implicit in the tying arrangement itself. This is a case-specific mandate restriction, not a universal antitrust rule or proof that no browser competition can be harmed. The supplied Supreme Court scope does not automatically reopen the separate attempted-monopolization reversal. Any proposed change to this tying-remand limit must identify its relation to the granted tying question and the preserved appellate judgment. Source: III and IV.C.

### Liability and remedy posture relevant to disqualification

The appellate court affirmed section 2 monopoly maintenance on specified conduct and rejected other bases. Sustained conduct included most challenged equipment-manufacturer restrictions, omission of the removal utility, commingling, specified access-provider exclusivity, independent-vendor and Apple exclusivity, Java default agreements, deception of Java developers and threats to Intel. Rejected bases included the prohibition on automatic replacement of the entire desktop interface, the default-browser override on the presented section 2 record, free or attractive browser/access-kit offers without a preserved predatory-pricing claim, the insufficiently harmful content-provider arrangements, development of the faster incompatible Java implementation itself and the unsupported independent course-of-conduct ground. These are descriptions of the lower judgment, not newly adopted Supreme Court merits holdings. Sources: II.B.1–6.

Attempted browser monopolization was reversed without a new opportunity to prove the failed market and entry-barrier predicates. The tying judgment was vacated for rule-of-reason proceedings. The disposition extended to the corresponding state antitrust claims. Section 1 exclusive dealing had failed in the District Court and plaintiffs did not cross-appeal it; no new claim is created. Sources: introductory summary, II.B.3, III–IV.

The entire June 7, 2000 remedial decree, both the structural division and conduct provisions, was vacated. The appellate court expressly recognized that the published press accounts had not been admitted as evidence and might be hearsay. The lower court made disqualification retroactive only to the remedial decree and ordered assignment to another judge. ## Law entering the bounded questions

**Tying and depth of competitive inquiry.** Jefferson Parish supplies the surviving distinction between sufficient independent consumer demand, tying power, conditioning and a substantial affected volume of commerce; functional connection alone does not merge products. Kodak, June 8, 1992, applies those elements to a triable claim and rejects a categorical economic defense inconsistent with supported evidence. It makes no final liability finding and excludes its unpreserved rule-of-reason theory. Its actual holding, not a broad characterization of all integration, governs.

California Dental, May 24, 1999, requires an inquiry sufficient to assess the actual restraint and its substantial competing explanation. It authorizes abbreviated analysis when experience and the mechanism make the competitive tendency sufficiently clear; it does not require a full market-definition trial or fixed series of labels in every case. Its application found the employed analysis inadequate. Khan, November 4, 1997, changes only the vertical maximum-price per se category; NYNEX, December 14, 1998, rejects a vertical supplier-substitution boycott category and requires competitive injury, leaving genuine established categories intact. These authorities support inquiry into category fit; they do not already decide a software exception or absolve the challenged tie.

**Disqualification.** The operative Standards entry is “Objective appearance of judicial impartiality under §455(a),” with present operation effective Liteky, March 7, 1994. The informed, detached observer considers circumstances cumulatively; a reasonable question about impartiality suffices without actual bias or impossibility of fair judgment. Judicial origin supplies context, not immunity or an exceptional-intensity threshold. Outside origin is neither necessary nor sufficient. Ordinary rulings and administration alone do not establish partiality. The fuller exact rule and coalition are copied in the reading slice.

**Civil relief for a violation.** Liteky preserves Liljeberg's independent remedial analysis and adopts no automatic wholesale-vacatur or universal harmlessness rule. Liljeberg considers risk of injustice to these parties, risk of injustice in other cases from denying relief, and damage to confidence in judicial process. It arose under Rule 60(b)(6) after finality; this matter concerns a direct appeal, so its specific timing and procedural predicates cannot be mechanically transplanted. Its remedial interests remain materially relevant. The inquiry cannot make proof of actual bias a necessary condition of relief for an established appearance violation. Nor does the statutory violation alone decide which prior components must be vacated.

Rule 52(a) and the pre-divergence Anderson/Pullman-Standard principles distinguish review of findings that remain valid from vacatur requiring new findings. A reviewing court cannot cure the question by silently replacing ordinary factual review with its own trial. Pure legal conclusions, uncontested or independently established facts, contested expert assessments, witness credibility and discretionary relief may bear differently on the tailored remedy. This classification is an analytical inquiry, not an assertion that a particular finding has already been proved untainted.
