## Neutral operative reading and limits

The original common-carrier definition concerns public carriage of passengers or cargo between the United States and a foreign country for compensation, assumption of transportation responsibility and qualifying high-seas or Great Lakes vessels. The original marine-terminal definition covers terminal services in connection with a common carrier; section 102(8) of the 1998 Act expands its specified carrier connection. The original person definition lists individuals, corporations, partnerships and associations. These copied definitions do not expressly name States. California v. United States, 320 U.S. 577, as recited in the permitted government brief, supports substantive coverage of public terminal facilities under the predecessor Act. Whether the statutory structure and preserved interpretation unmistakably authorize this particular private adjudication and remedy against a State remains a distinct legal inquiry under actual simulated immunity law; a coverage recital is not that inquiry's answer.

The 1998 Act replaces section 10(b)(10) with the prohibition on unreasonable refusal to deal or negotiate and adds section 10(d)(4), forbidding a marine terminal operator's undue or unreasonable preference, advantage, prejudice or disadvantage with respect to a person. Section 109(c)(3) also changes the subsection (d)(3) incorporation to the redesigned (b)(10) and (13). These are the enactment sources corresponding to the charged provisions identified in the government statement. They neither establish that the port acted unreasonably nor decide the coverage of a particular voyage. The complaint's statutory carrier/terminal predicates remain distinct from immunity.

Original section 14(c) permits the Attorney General at Commission request, or an injured party, to seek judicial enforcement of nonreparation orders and subpoenas in a court having jurisdiction over the parties; after hearing, a properly made and duly issued order is enforced by appropriate process. Section 14(d) separately permits the award recipient to seek reparation enforcement, makes the Commission findings and order prima facie evidence, and supplies its particular costs, prevailing-petitioner fee and joinder conditions. Section 14(e) imposes the three-year enforcement period. Neither text says that an Article III court already has personal or subject-matter authority whenever an agency enters an order. The government brief corroborates these distinct channels and says the Attorney General is not authorized to collect the private reparation award. No enforcement action is before the Court.

Version caution: the government brief's reproduced section 1710(g) appendix carries older cross-references, while 1998 section 110 expressly replaces the identified references. It therefore must not be represented as an independently verified complete current codification. The 1998 amendments generally take effect May 1, 1999. Section 117 preserves suits filed before that date and specified suits concerning earlier conduct filed within one year afterward. The present permitted factual extracts do not fix the complaint's filing date or each alleged refusal date. No version-sensitive violation, enhanced award, retroactive application or transition conclusion is made here. If a chosen path depends on one, obtain the precise dates and any material intervening amendment rather than treating the modern codification or the source appendix as conclusive. The legal-method question whether this private administrative tribunal receives immunity can be assessed without awarding a remedy or resolving those substantive dates.

The 1984 Act predates divergence. The read text of the 1998 Act describes competition and maritime regulatory reform; no reviewed provision identifies codification, modification or abrogation of a displaced Supreme Court decision as its enactment predicate. This check supplies no substantive construction or anticipatory legislative response.
