### Issue 1: Judicial inquiry and conflict-related prejudice

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** Williams v. Taylor concerning Terry Williams applies ordinary Strickland prejudice without a separate unfairness requirement and preserves the distinct statutory gates. Williams v. Taylor concerning Michael Wayne Williams separates concealed-fact cause, factual-development eligibility, state-merits review, and actual constitutional entitlement. Swidler & Berlin v. United States preserves attorney-client confidentiality after death within its scope.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** The registers require the specific constitutional injury and a remedy responsive to it. Judicial failure to perform a safeguard is not automatically equivalent to complete loss of counsel; a former client’s death does not by itself eliminate continuing confidentiality obligations.
* **Other Governing Law:** The Sixth Amendment guarantees effective assistance. Strickland v. Washington ordinarily requires deficient performance and a reasonable probability of a different lawful result. Holloway v. Arkansas, Cuyler v. Sullivan, and Wood v. Georgia address particular divided-loyalty and inquiry situations.
* **Material Other Authority:** United States v. Cronic recognizes exceptional circumstances in which the denial of meaningful assistance justifies presumed prejudice. The different predicates in Holloway, Sullivan, and Wood must be identified rather than reduced to a generic conflict label.
* **Existing Rule's Reach:** Existing law supplies ordinary prejudice and established exceptions. The supplied simulation has not adopted a new automatic-reversal rule whenever a judge should have asked about a potential conflict, nor eliminated the need to distinguish potential division from an actual effect on representation.
* **Open Point:** Whether a known potential conflict and failure to inquire alone remove the requirement to establish an adverse effect, including where the conflict arises from representation of the deceased victim.

## C. Decision-material argument positions, as clarified in the complete official transcript

The November 5, 2001 argument materially qualifies the earlier neutral summary of the relief petitioner actually urged. At printed pages 23–24, counsel was asked whether he sought automatic reversal from the court's failure to inquire without any finding of an actual conflict. After the question distinguished notice to the court from counsel's timely objection in Holloway, counsel expressly answered that he was not advancing that no-actual-conflict position. At pages 24–25, he continued to treat the trial judge's duty as central but acknowledged significance in counsel's having raised the conflict and described Wood as requiring an actual-conflict showing. His discussion at pages 19–22 likewise invoked Wood's remand for an actual-conflict determination, rather than a finding that every potential conflict was itself sufficient.

At page 25, counsel maintained that the adverse-effect prong should not be required when the judge knew of the conflict, but expressly declined to concede that petitioner could not satisfy that prong. These are separate propositions: an actual-conflict inquiry remains in petitioner's proposed route; petitioner disputes a distinct adverse-effect requirement; and petitioner does not concede factual inability to prove an adverse effect. Acknowledging what the federal district court found does not concede the correctness of every finding or authorize reopening findings outside the granted question.

The rebuttal at pages 55–56 continued to argue from Holloway's protective purpose and the court's unperformed duty. It did not expressly retract the answer at page 24 or expressly renew a request to dispense with an actual-conflict showing. The supported characterization is an express oral-argument limitation on the no-actual-conflict theory. The source does not establish blanket abandonment of Holloway, abandonment of an actual-conflict-only route, abandonment of all factual adverse-effect contentions, or a formal waiver with consequences independently settled by the Court.

The warden and the United States urged that the failure to inquire does not dispense with an actual conflict adversely affecting representation. Their adverse-effect requirement was expressly distinguished from Strickland's ordinary reasonable-probability-of-a-different-outcome burden at pages 46–48 and 52–53. The United States described Holloway as a special situation involving counsel's contemporaneous representation of a disabling conflict and compelled continuation without the required inquiry; it did not contend that the duty to inquire arises only after an adverse effect has already been proved. The parties disputed what Wood's actual-conflict inquiry entails. Their labels cannot resolve that substantive difference by definition.

## D. Lawful alternatives, limits, and remedy

One supported path requires an actual conflict that adversely affected performance before dispensing with ordinary outcome prejudice, notwithstanding the inquiry failure. It must explain why the appointment and the judge's knowledge do not satisfy the different Holloway predicate. Within the granted legal question, affirmance would leave the adverse-effect determination operative rather than add an unsupported factual finding that no ongoing duty or confidential information existed.

A distinct supported path accepts petitioner's actual-conflict-only reading of the inquiry cases and rejects a separate adverse-effect showing in this setting. It must explain what actual conflict means, how that inquiry differs from adverse effect, why Wood supplies the relevant protection despite the absence of a timely counsel objection, and what remains to be resolved after the federal hearing. A remand under that rule requires an identified unresolved legal or factual component; a new trial requires the predicate and remedial conclusion the adopted rule actually demands. A third possible remand concerns a supported defect in the legal standard used at the hearing, not merely dissatisfaction with an adverse finding.

An even broader rule granting reversal solely for a known potential conflict and failure to inquire, without an actual-conflict showing, must not be represented as petitioner's maintained oral-argument request. If independently considered within the granted question, it requires its own doctrinal and party-presentation justification, with the express answer at pages 23–24 addressed. No neutral source verification determines whether the Court may or should adopt that broader rule. Collateral nonretroactivity and procedural default remain distinct if a proposed disposition depends on a genuinely new rule or a broader ground.

The Sixth Amendment question does not adjudicate professional discipline. Swidler preserves the relevant continuing confidentiality principle without proving what confidences existed or how counsel acted. The two Williams decisions preserve their separate constitutional, default, factual-development and state-merits inquiries; the existence of a federal hearing does not create a state-merits adjudication for §2254(d). Broad holdings governing every successive representation are unnecessary to the stated question.
