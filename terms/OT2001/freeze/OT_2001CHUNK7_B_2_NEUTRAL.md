* **Case Name:** Page v. Bartels.
* **Citation or Docket:** 144 F. Supp. 2d 346 (D.N.J. 2001); district No. 01-1733. The earlier procedural appeal is 248 F.3d 175 (3d Cir. 2001).
* **October Term:** 2001.

It rejected plaintiffs' contrary electoral projections, predicted chilling of participation and claimed discriminatory purpose. The opinion did not treat Districts 28 and 29 as remaining material disputes at the close of trial. The reported final judgment supplies the reviewable order. Personal exposure, traceability and redressability must be established for the relevant claim and remedy. ### Issue 1: Statutory electoral opportunity

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** Valdespino v. Alamo Heights Independent School District, June 17, 1999, expressly permits a single protected group to satisfy the first Gingles condition either through a lawful compact district-majority alternative or through rigorous proof of dependable crossover support sufficient to elect in a lawful compact alternative. Voinovich v. Quilter, March 2, 1993, preserves the claimant's burden and relevant majority-bloc requirement. Johnson v. De Grandy, June 30, 1994, preserves independent totality analysis, rejects maximization and rejects proportional opportunity as a conclusive defense.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** Identify the affected protected group and challenged arrangement. Establish a lawful, geographically compact electoral alternative under either Valdespino capacity route, the group's political cohesion, and opposing majority-bloc voting ordinarily sufficient to defeat its preferred candidates, with relevant special circumstances accounted for. Those necessary conditions do not themselves prove liability: the totality must establish unequal opportunity to participate and elect caused by the arrangement. Failure of a necessary condition can dispose of the claim without deciding the others.
* **Other Governing Law:** Voting Rights Act § 2(a)–(b), then 42 U.S.C. § 1973, including the results standard and disclaimer of proportional-representation entitlement. The 1982 amendment supplies an enacted results inquiry independently of a constitutional intent claim. Its historical response to an intent-based interpretation does not turn this statutory action into one requiring purpose. No post-2001 amendment or later statutory language governs this entry. Section 5's distinct nonretrogression benchmark is not substituted for § 2's inquiry.
* **Material Other Authority:** Thornburg v. Gingles, 478 U.S. 30, 50–51, 54–61 (1986), supplies the electoral causation framework subject to Valdespino's modification of numerical exclusivity. The election stage at which the alleged obstacle operates matters; success in a general election cannot automatically answer a demonstrated nomination barrier.
* **Existing Rule's Reach:** A demographic reduction alone establishes neither dilution nor preservation. The protected opportunity belongs to voters, not incumbents. A candidate of choice is one whom the relevant electorate actually prefers, regardless of the candidate's race. Crossover support means support from voters outside the claimant group for that group's preferred candidates. It is different from combining two protected groups into one claimant class. Effective opportunity means a realistic capacity to elect, not a promise of victory or merely sympathetic representation.
* **Open Point:** Distinct-minority-group aggregation and a statutory influence-only entitlement remain unresolved. Neither follows from Valdespino's single-group crossover rule. This case need not decide them if the independently necessary opposing-bloc showing fails.

### Issue 2: Constitutional dilution and political participation

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** Shaw v. Reno, June 28, 1993; Johnson v. Miller, June 29, 1995; and Easley v. Cromartie, April 18, 2001, hold that racial attention or predominance in a shared territorial district does not itself establish a constitutional deprivation. Actually presented exclusion, intentional dilution, materially unequal participation and other independent injuries retain their own elements. Easley rejects a universal requirement of electoral defeat, a second governmental act, financial loss or a prescribed statistical model. Voinovich leaves Fifteenth Amendment dilution coverage open while permitting disposition of an assumed claim on failed proof of discriminatory purpose.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** Distinguish Article III exposure from the merits. Then identify the protected interest, the governmental act that allegedly impairs it, the purpose or unequal treatment required by that claim, actual or sufficiently threatened material impairment, and causation. Racial awareness does not alone establish a discriminatory purpose. A political explanation neither automatically proves that purpose nor excuses an established constitutional wrong. Ordinary proof and stage-specific procedure govern.
* **Other Governing Law:** Fourteenth Amendment, § 1; Fifteenth Amendment, § 1. Their constitutional elements remain distinct from § 2's statutory results standard. A general due-process label supplies no developed claim or additional merits issue by itself.
* **Material Other Authority:** Brooks v. Barnes, April 22, 1999, supplies the governing purpose-based sequence: once prohibited purpose is established as a substantial or motivating factor, government bears the burden of establishing the applicable same-decision defense. Village of Arlington Heights v. Metropolitan Housing Development Corp., 429 U.S. 252 (1977), and Personnel Administrator v. Feeney, 442 U.S. 256 (1979), distinguish prohibited purpose from awareness of consequences. Their purpose analysis does not make racial predominance the required constitutional injury.
* **Existing Rule's Reach:** Deliberately unequal political access is not legally harmless merely because a preferred candidate can still win. Conversely, reduced numerical concentration or fear of reduced influence is not proof of material exclusion or obstruction. The absence of a territorial-design injury does not eliminate statutory protection or a properly supported independent constitutional claim.
* **Open Point:** Entering law does not adopt a comprehensive unified test for every form of coalition disruption, racial separation or partisan districting. The complete reach of those theories is unnecessary if no corresponding preserved claim and material injury are established. Fifteenth Amendment coverage of the asserted dilution theory remains reserved.

The court credited continuing district-specific opportunity and rejected the predicted participation decline. Its alternative rejection of the Fifteenth Amendment claim rested on absent discriminatory purpose.
* **Material Alternative or Unreached Grounds:** Preservation of opportunity in each affected district differs from a statewide offset. They relied on competing election analysis and testimony about political cooperation and chilling. The legally relevant claim seeks continued equal electoral opportunity and participation, not an automatic racial floor or ownership of District 27's former boundaries.
* **Appellees' Strongest Supported Position:** Electoral experience, relevant crossover, primary participation and district-specific projections support retained opportunity despite the population changes. No § 2 rule commands preservation of every numerical-majority district where equal opportunity remains, and no constitutional injury follows from racial consideration alone.

### Issue 1: Authority to bind nonparties

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** Richards v. Jefferson County, decided June 10, 1996, requires a constitutionally sufficient relationship before a judgment binds absent persons' rights. Common interests, vigorous advocacy, public importance, notice, and a virtual-representation label do not alone establish that relationship.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** Claim preclusion prevents relitigation of a claim by a person lawfully bound to a qualifying prior judgment. Privity means a legally sufficient relationship supporting binding effect, not merely political affinity. Consent, actual control, genuine substantive relationships, and authorized representative proceedings can supply a basis, subject to due process.
* **Other Governing Law:** The Fifth Amendment's due-process constraint applies to federal adjudication. Applicable federal preclusion principles govern the effect of the federal judgment. No identified statutory amendment changes the governing nonparty rule for this matter.
* **Material Other Authority:** Hansberry v. Lee, 311 U.S. 32 (1940), for the due-process requirements of genuine representation. Richards supplies the controlling rejection of a general duty to intervene.

* **Open Point:** No new legal rule of nonparty preclusion is necessary. Whether the record establishes an authorized binding relationship is an application of the settled rule; the alternative merits ground may independently resolve the judgment.

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** Shaw v. Reno, decided June 28, 1993; Johnson v. Miller, decided June 29, 1995; and Easley v. * **Other Governing Law:** Fourteenth Amendment equal protection; Fifteenth Amendment prohibition of racial denial or abridgment of voting. Voinovich v. * **Material Other Authority:** Anderson v. Martin, 375 U.S. 399 (1964), distinguishes official racial electoral treatment from geographical composition; Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242 (1986), and Rule 56 preserve favorable inferences at summary judgment.
* **Existing Rule's Reach:** Protection extends equally to voters of every race. ## Holdings

### Existing capacity rule retained

**Holding and operative rule:** For a preserved district-formation claim by one protected group, the first Gingles condition may be met through a numerical majority in a lawful, geographically compact district or through rigorous proof of dependable crossover support sufficient to elect the group's candidates of choice. Compactness, cohesion, relevant majority-bloc defeat, causation and unequal opportunity under the independent totality inquiry remain necessary; speculation, one unusual electoral victory, population arithmetic or mere influence does not establish dependable capacity, and a sufficient lawful ground independent of numerical exclusivity remains effective.

**Authority:** Valdespino v. Alamo Heights Independent School District, June 17, 1999, remains controlling. Stone-Zsela's concurrence, Part I, and Souter's dissent, Part I, joined by Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer, expressly accept that same rule and its limits. This is direct agreement by five Justices preserving existing law, not a new test or a majority rationale for today's affirmance. The four dissenters do not join the affirmance or its factual application.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 2 protects actual opportunity to elect the protected group's preferred representatives. Valdespino permits a single group to establish that opportunity through dependable support from other voters while preserving a lawful district, the other voting conditions and the ultimate inquiry into unequal opportunity. Gingles supplies the causal requirements; Growe carries them into districting claims; Voinovich requires a proved violation before a federal court compels a district; De Grandy makes the necessary conditions distinct from the ultimate statutory judgment and rejects a duty to maximize districts. A population percentage neither completes that inquiry nor forecloses the functional route. The same precedent also preserves independent factual and procedural rulings, so citing an obsolete numerical rule does not by itself establish reversible error. The Justices who accept this rule disagree here over whether the actual findings sufficiently resolve the claims carried into this congressional appeal. Their agreement on existing law supplies no common answer to that application dispute.

**Precedent treatment:** Valdespino is maintained within its single-group scope; the first Gingles condition remains modified as that decision held, while the remaining statutory conditions and independently sufficient lawful grounds retain their force.

### Congressional judgment affirmed without a majority rationale

**Judgment without controlling rationale:** The November 14, 2001 congressional judgment adopting Plan 1151C and rejecting the appealed request for additional Latino-opportunity districts is affirmed, 5–4. Stone-Zsela, O'Connor and Kennedy find the practical-effectiveness and geography grounds independently sufficient, with the qualifications in their separate writings; Scalia and Thomas reject the asserted statutory district-allocation entitlement. Neither ground commands five votes. The judgment binds the parties, but it does not adopt every premise of the lower opinion.

**Authority and limit:** The Court's unsigned summary order controls the disposition. The application rationale assumes and applies the statutory dilution framework; the noncoverage rationale denies that framework's application to the requested allocation. The latter supplies no finding that the former's elements were not met. These incompatible grounds do not yield a controlling narrower rationale under Marks v. United States, 430 U.S. 188 (1977). The Court establishes neither a numerical-majority safe harbor nor a rule that equivalent compactness scores, turnout differences, incumbent success or district shape alone resolve a Section 2 claim. The separate legislative judgments remain outside this congressional decision.

## Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in the judgment.** Part I maintains Valdespino's two capacity routes and the surviving statutory conditions. A single group's outside electoral support differs from aggregation of separate protected groups.

Part II finds the appeal's majority-registration, compactness and findings objections insufficient to require vacatur. Appellants offered election-performance evidence, including reaggregated contests and cross-examination; their case was not devoid of functional proof. But they sought seven registered-voter-majority districts and argued that those proposals exceeded the existing majority threshold. Evidence offered to prove those districts effective does not itself establish that a preserved claim was rejected solely because a protected group lacked a numerical majority. No particular terminology is required to preserve an issue; the material legal objection must actually be presented.

The lower court's findings about practical effectiveness and geography, read with the trial, independently sustain the judgment. Competing testimony addressed marginal districts, turnout and the proposed electorate; selected victories in Plan 1099C did not conclusively establish performance of every district in Plan 1123C. The geographic dispute included corridors, community relationships and subdivision boundaries, not shape alone. Comparative scores were relevant but did not compel a finding of workable geography. The findings are brief, yet identify grounds sufficient for review without adopting every appellate characterization of the testimony. The separate multigroup-cohesion finding does not answer a single-group crossover claim. Neither partisan convenience nor statewide gains could excuse proved local deprivation; the required violation was not established on the independent grounds here. Plan 1151C and the 2002 election under it remain undisturbed.

**O'Connor, concurring in the judgment; Kennedy joins Part I only.** Part I regards the trial court's practical-effectiveness and geography findings as sufficient when read with the record. The competing testimony concerns whether the additional arrangement would yield actual electoral opportunity, including marginality, turnout and the proposed electorate. Registration majorities do not answer those questions, and a geometric comparison does not by itself establish lawful compactness. Rule 52(a) requires an intelligible factual basis; it does not require discussion of every exhibit. The opinion's brevity presents a serious objection, but these findings permit review and sustain the judgment. This conclusion requires neither a numerical safe harbor nor rejection of Valdespino. Voinovich supplies the distinction between a State's choices and a federally compelled district; De Grandy supplies the need for proof beyond the necessary conditions.

In Part II, O'Connor also accepts the lower court's contextual totality determination as a sufficient basis for rejecting the requested additional district. That is an assessment of the actual opportunity shown in this record, not a conclusive defense based on statewide proportionality. Kennedy does not join this further account; his agreement is confined to the independent practical-effectiveness and geographic grounds in Part I.

**Thomas, concurring in the judgment, joined by Scalia.** Consistent with their positions in Holder v. Hall and De Grandy, they conclude that Section 2 does not authorize the asserted judicial allocation of districts to remedy this form of vote dilution. That antecedent statutory ground is sufficient to affirm. They accept the court's authority to replace an unconstitutionally malapportioned plan, but they do not join either capacity route or a finding that these plaintiffs failed the governing dilution framework. The actual electoral-performance evidence does not answer their statutory objection.

**Souter, dissenting, joined by Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer.** Part I expressly accepts the existing capacity rule and independent-ground limits stated in Stone-Zsela's Part I. Part II would vacate only the congressional rejection of the appealed additional Latino-opportunity arrangement and remand for application of that rule with sufficiently identified findings. The appeal affirmatively presents performance evidence, comparative geography and an objection to the adequacy of the findings. Its majority-registration framing does not eliminate the need to determine what the court decided about that evidence under current law.

The lower opinion links its conclusions to a majority-citizenship requirement and does not sufficiently explain why the proposed arrangement fails independently. The appellees' account of marginality and geography could support a lawful judgment, but cannot substitute for identifiable trial findings. Four selected victories do not prove dependable capacity, and a compactness score does not prove legal compactness; neither can be treated as absent. Plan 1099C testimony and Plan 1123C data must remain distinct. De Grandy requires an independent totality judgment, not repetition of a disputed capacity conclusion. The separate finding about cohesion between protected groups does not dispose of single-group outside support. The proposed remand would preserve supported findings and plaintiffs' burden, require explanation of any sufficient independent ground, and permit supplementation only under ordinary procedural rules. It would neither mandate an additional district nor reopen the entire trial or disturb the 2002 election under the operative plan.

The November 14, 2001 replacement-map judgment remains in force; Plan 1151C governs unless lawfully modified, and the 2002 election under it remains undisturbed. There is no congressional remand or release condition. Separate state legislative judgments remain outside this action.

## Robertson: incumbent-treatment ground, 148 F. Supp. 2d at 459

Count II of the plaintiffs’ complaint alleges that the redistricting plan unconstitutionally separates incumbents by their race and subjects them to disparate treatment on that basis. Although not expressly stated as such, this argument presents a variation on their first argument, namely that race — of incumbents, rather than voters' — was the predominant factor in the Commission’s decision to adopt the redistricting plan. Because we find that race, while considered, was not the predominant factor in the Commission’s redistricting plan, we also grant the defendants’ motion as to Count II.

In support of their position, the plaintiffs again point to statements in Bartels’ certification that he considered the reelection prospects of minority incumbent legislators in evaluating the plans. See Defs.’ Mot. for Summ. J. Ex. D (Bartels Cert. ¶¶ 6, 12, 22). But, as previously discussed, Bartels considered much more than simply the reelection chances of minority incumbents in assessing the redistricting plans. Indeed, Bartels expressly stated that he considered “minimizing voter disruption, so as not to deny too many New Jersey citizens the opportunity to vote for incumbents who had served them well or against incumbents who had not done so.” Id. ¶ 7. Bartels conceded that this goal conflicted to a certain degree with “the goals of creating a fair, unbiased, and responsive map and building new opportunities for minority representation,” but that the plan was a good compromise. Id. ¶ 21. Furthermore, Bartels sought to achieve statewide partisan fairness so that the party that receives a majority of the total statewide vote in a legislative election will obtain a majority in the legislature. See id. ¶¶ 17-18.

I It was central to achieving Bartels’ goals [in the redistricting to “unpack” the primarily African-American districts in Essex County, a process which necessarily made the surrounding districts, including District 34, more racially integrated. Of course, one consequence of the unpacking was that the chances for the reelection of the incumbents in those previously predominantly white districts was lessened, but this circumstance does not mean that the race of the incumbent was the predominant factor of the Commission’s consideration. In redistricting, the Voting Rights Act makes race a relevant factor to be considered, and strict scrutiny is not triggered necessarily where it is utilized. To the contrary, the Voting Rights Act expressly directs redistricters and courts to consider “[t]he extent to which members of a protected class have been elected to office” when analyzing the “totality of circumstances” confronting minority voters and, as we have indicated, the Supreme Court has found race to be a constitutionally appropriate factor, so long as it is not the predominant factor. 42 U.S.C. § 1973(b). It is therefore apparent that while Bartels and the Commission considered the race of incumbents in assessing the proposed redistricting maps, race was not the predominant factor in their decision to adopt the present plan. Accordingly, we grant summary judgment in favor of the defendants on Count II.
