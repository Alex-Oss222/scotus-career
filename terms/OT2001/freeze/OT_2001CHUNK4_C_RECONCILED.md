### Statutory components and common rule

### Available rules and exact burdens

**Rule B — ordinary seniority conflict, rebuttable in the actual case.** A claimant generally meets the initial burden by identifying an accommodation reasonable on its face or in the run of cases. Effectiveness alone does not answer reasonable. An employer's showing that the proposed assignment conflicts with an established seniority system ordinarily establishes that it is unreasonable, including where management imposed the system. The employee may show special circumstances making this exception reasonable. Examples are actual frequent exercises of reserved amendment power that sufficiently weaken expectations, or existing exceptions whose character makes an additional exception unlikely to matter. These are nonexclusive illustrations; bare reserved power is not automatically sufficient. After an adequate reasonableness showing, the employer retains the distinct statutory burden of significant difficulty or expense in its operations. Neither that defense nor every effect on coworkers becomes an element of the employee's ordinary first showing.

**O'Connor's preferred rule — vacancy and enforceable possession.** A position is not vacant if another worker presently occupies it or has a legally enforceable entitlement to it. A seniority policy with no enforceable claim to the position supplies no legal possessor merely through an expectation of future assignment. Under the supplied employer-created, noncontractual policy, seniority by itself therefore does not defeat Barnett's initial reasonable request; the employer may establish the actual statutory hardship. Her distinction is not a command to bump an incumbent. She accepts ordinary facial reasonableness followed by the employer's particular hardship burden and objects that Rule B's special-circumstances phase blends those inquiries.

**Rule A — ordinary individual reasonableness without a special unilateral-policy presumption.** Seniority matters to reasonable accommodation and actual hardship, but Congress omitted the explicit seniority protections found in other employment statutes. Souter and Ginsburg give reasonableness independent work through feasibility, suitability and the real requested change. They find Barnett's retention request facially reasonable on this record: he seeks continuation, not removal of another incumbent, and the supplied noncontractual reserved-power policy does not itself establish a disruptive entitlement. The employer remains free to prove actual hardship. This is not an automatic employee judgment or a holding that seniority expectations never matter. They preserve the distinct force of legally enforceable collective-bargaining obligations; no such obligation is established here.

**Rule C — disability-created obstacle.** Scalia and Thomas read sections 12112(a) and (b)(5)(A) together to require removal, within reason, of obstacles that prevent this worker from performing because of disability. Some neutral work rules must yield because disability makes them barriers, such as a standing requirement or inaccessible work station. A bona fide seniority allocation unrelated to disability is different; an exception gives priority over other workers rather than removing the disability-created barrier. Reassignment still does real work by requiring an employer to seek and offer a suitable vacant position within the existing lawful allocation instead of simply dismissing a worker unable to perform the old job. They reject the majority's vague special-circumstances exception even for bona fide systems. They do not adopt automatic immunity for every nominally neutral policy.
