## Validated question, record and below-stage consequences

The petitioner’s question asks whether knowingly allowing the charge period to expire is overcome by a sufficiently related timely incident. Respondent contests both the limitations rule and the evidentiary treatment. Review concerns Title VII’s continuing-violation rule. The parties’ merits questions, statements and summaries and the complete Joint Appendix confirm that this is a completed jury case following partial summary judgment. A November 19, 1998 special verdict rejected the submitted discrimination, retaliation and hostile-environment questions. The Ninth Circuit vacated both partial summary judgment and the jury judgment and ordered a new trial. It did not resolve the separate evidentiary objections.

The charge was filed February 27, 1995, making May 3, 1994 the beginning of the agreed 300-day window. It was amended March 8 to include the March termination. The verdict form describes the final adverse action as February 1995; the merits statements describe suspension in February and termination in March. Preserve the actual chronology and procedural labels without treating the form as a separate finding fixing the legal termination date. The October 1991 internal discrimination complaint and subsequent internal complaints support Amtrak’s knowledge argument, not automatic waiver or proof that later discrimination is lawful. The source record distinguishes individual discipline/training events, the alleged racial environment and retaliation. Common supervisors and kinds of conduct are Morgan’s continuity evidence, not adjudicated proof of one indivisible violation.

The timely-act jury verdict does not make the question harmless automatically: Morgan argues that the limitations ruling excluded or restricted environment evidence and affected the instructions. Conversely, rejection of the continuing-violation theory does not establish the independent evidentiary objections. Their preservation, effect and appropriate proceedings must be addressed or remanded.

## Live arguments and lawful paths

Amtrak proposes a clock beginning no later than notice of an actionable violation and a sparing equitable allowance where earlier events were not reasonably recognizable as actionable. Morgan argues that practice language, the backpay provision, employer notice and related recurring conduct permit broader recovery. His environment argument rests on the collective substantive wrong; his evidence argument does not necessarily require recovery for old discrete acts. The United States takes a third position: no extinguishment of new violations because the plaintiff knew of earlier discrimination, no revival of old violations by relation alone, and ordinarily only evidentiary use of older conduct even for a timely environment claim. Its position is not a concession to compensation for the entire connected environment.

The Court can reject revival of completed discrete actions while treating a connected environment as one practice if an act contributing to it occurs within the window; it can instead apply the charge period to compensable environment injury while admitting older evidence; or it can adopt the supported notice-based equitable approach, stating when a claim first exists and what later violation remains actionable. A broader related-act rule must explain how it preserves the filing command. Each path must distinguish a new adverse action from an old action’s present effects, identify what makes incidents one environment, and retain separately supported tolling, estoppel and prejudicial-delay defenses. No finding here establishes continuity, purpose, severity, attribution or damages.

The disposition must identify which continuing-violation components are affirmed or reversed and the further lower-court tasks. Background relevance is distinct from independent recoverability. A remand must preserve unresolved evidentiary objections and explain the effect on the vacated jury judgment rather than automatically entering judgment for either party.

* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The district court granted Amtrak partial summary judgment excluding liability for conduct preceding the applicable 300-day period. The Ninth Circuit reversed through its continuing-violation approach. Amtrak seeks reinstatement of the limitations ruling; Morgan defends consideration of the continuing conduct.
* **Relief Requested:** Amtrak seeks exclusion of untimely claims; Morgan seeks consideration of the alleged continuing environment and related conduct.
* **Material Record:** Abner Morgan, a Black employee, alleged discriminatory and retaliatory employment decisions and a racially hostile work environment at Amtrak. He filed with the EEOC and the California agency on February 27, 1995, later received a right-to-sue notice, and sued. The case reaches the Court from a limitations ruling, not a finding that every alleged event occurred or that all events formed one unlawful environment. Older acts may supply background evidence even when they cannot independently support recovery.
* **Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** The charge deadline and the later deadline to commence suit are separate. Classification of the alleged practice determines when it occurred; labeling every allegation a continuing violation does not decide the statutory issue. A claim’s timeliness does not establish discriminatory motive, severity or pervasiveness, employer responsibility, or damages. Equitable tolling and estoppel require their own grounds, not just similarity to a timely allegation.

### Issue 1: Identify the unlawful employment practice

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** Harris v. Forklift Systems, Inc. defines hostile conditions through the circumstances as a whole, with objective and subjective abuse and no psychiatric-injury prerequisite. Oncale v. Sundowner Offshore Services, Inc. preserves the required causal connection to protected status and the contextual severe-or-pervasive inquiry. Employer attribution remains a distinct question under the supplied agency and harassment decisions.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** A hostile environment may arise from a connected course of conduct, while an independently actionable employment decision has its own elements and consequences. Aggregate assessment of an environment does not itself establish a limitations rule for discrete decisions.
* **Other Governing Law:** Title VII requires filing a charge within 180 days, or within the applicable 300-day state-agency period, after the alleged unlawful employment practice occurred. The operative question is what practice is charged and when it occurred, not when a later related act makes earlier conduct seem more significant. Section 112 of the Civil Rights Act of 1991 revised the rule for intentionally discriminatory seniority systems after Lorance, adding §706(e)(2). That specific enactment must be distinguished from §706(e)(1)’s general filing language and supplies no automatic revival of every related employment act.
* **Material Other Authority:** United Air Lines, Inc. v. Evans and Delaware State College v. Ricks distinguish present effects from a new actionable act. Electrical Workers v. Robbins & Myers, Inc. addresses the statutory filing requirement without making a collateral grievance process automatically extend it.
* **Existing Rule’s Reach:** The supplied environment standard identifies the collective character of that wrong. It does not authenticate the Ninth Circuit’s general rule reviving all related discrete acts, or resolve the precise treatment of an environment extending across the charge window.
* **Open Point:** How to apply the charge deadline to each kind of practice and preserve valid equitable qualifications.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

**Reasoning below.** The district court barred recovery for conduct outside the 300-day window. The Ninth Circuit allowed older conduct under related-act and systemic continuing-violation theories. Its approach risked treating distinct completed decisions as timely simply because they related to later allegations.

**Amtrak.** Congress chose a specific charge window to provide prompt notice and reduce stale claims. Discharge, denied promotion, discipline, and retaliation do not acquire new filing periods merely because later acts reflect a similar motive. An employer must be able to determine which decisions are timely challenged.

**Morgan.** A hostile environment develops through repeated conduct and may be unintelligible if split at an arbitrary date. When the same environment continues within the charge period, its earlier components remain part of the charged wrong. He also argues for the broader continuing-violation treatment of linked employment actions.

**Limits.** The collective character of harassment supports consideration of its constituent events but cannot erase the deadline for every completed decision. Conversely, applying a separate clock to every comment or incident would ignore the very practice the hostile-environment cause addresses.

**United States as amicus:** The government rejects both an early-notice rule extinguishing later actionable violations and revival of old violations merely because they relate to timely conduct. Its proposed rule permits recovery for violations within the filing period, while ordinarily limiting older conduct to evidence supporting a timely claim, including a hostile-environment claim. It seeks reversal of the Ninth Circuit, leaving the separate evidentiary objection for possible consideration below. That position differs from treating the entire connected environment as a single timely compensable practice.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

The Court can distinguish discrete decisions from the connected environment, reverse the revival of stale discrete claims, and remand for determination of whether timely and older harassment belong to one practice. A stricter alternative would exclude older components once the claimant previously had a viable environment claim; a broader alternative would permit all related discrete acts, requiring an explanation of how that reading preserves the filing requirement.

**Plausible Holding Scope:** Each discrete act ordinarily starts its own charge clock. A hostile-environment charge can encompass a single continuing environment if an act contributing to it occurred within the window. A later unrelated incident or a materially separate environment does not revive old conduct. Equitable doctrines and proven prejudicial delay retain independent operation.

**Uncertainty:** The Court must not decide from the allegation list alone that all hostility had common actors, character, frequency, or continuity. Those matters, employer responsibility, and any equitable defense remain record questions on remand.
