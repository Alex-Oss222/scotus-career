## 27. Owasso Independent School District No. I-011 v. Falvo

### Components and final rule

**O1 — Coverage.** A student paper is an education record under 20 U.S.C. §1232g(a)(4)(A) only if it both directly relates to a student and is maintained by the educational institution or a person acting for it. During the described classroom exercise, classmates’ temporary handling and marking of papers, followed by immediate reporting of the marks, is instructional participation rather than maintenance of institutional records. Teacher direction does not by itself turn a peer grader into the institution’s recordkeeper. This rule concerns the temporary process before the teacher collects and retains the material; it does not establish that students can never perform an actual institutional recordkeeping function.

The strongest contrary argument treats the peer grader as the teacher’s agent and the production of a student-specific score as maintenance. That argument fails because student identification and compliance with instructional directions do not establish the distinct statutory recordkeeping function. The same limit answers the immediate oral reporting claim: reporting a mark in this process does not convert the preceding paper into a maintained education record.

The common formulation must **omit any requirement of a central repository, permanent storage, a single custodian, or a particular storage location**. FERPA’s access and custody provisions may illustrate recordkeeping; they do not add these elements. The sole-possession exclusion in §1232g(a)(4)(B)(i) remains an actual exclusion from coverage, rather than a reason to restrict all covered records to a central file. Retained teacher gradebooks and papers actually turned in for retention are outside the presented temporary process and are not resolved by extending this holding.

**O2 — Enforcement and scope.** Assume, without deciding, that a private §1983 action is available, and reject this claim on the statutory coverage ground. The private-action question is nonjurisdictional and outside the supplied coverage question; its resolution is unnecessary to afford complete relief on that question. Blessing’s right-specific inquiry remains intact. The entering-law Sandoval holding concerning its particular Title VI prospective action supplies neither a general FERPA cause of action nor a categorical bar. No later Gonzaga rule may enter. The retained-record question likewise concerns a materially different act, not an unresolved alternative ground necessary to decide the challenged peer-grading process.

**O3 — Disposition.** Reverse the Tenth Circuit’s coverage judgment and remand for proceedings consistent with O1 and O2. Do not decide that every school disclosure or every classroom document is exempt from FERPA.

## 28. Barnhart v. Sigmon Coal Co.

### Components and final rule

**S1 — Enumerated liability.** Under 26 U.S.C. §§9701(c)(2) and 9706, an asset purchaser does not become assignable merely because it succeeded directly to an original signatory operator. Section 9701(c)(2)(A) identifies: a member of the signatory’s controlled group under §52(a), including the signatory; a trade or business under common control under §52(b); and a person having a partnership interest or joint venture with a signatory in a coal-industry business employing eligible beneficiaries, excluding an interest held solely as a limited partner. Its successor sentence reaches persons described in those enumerated classes, rather than adding every direct successor of an original signatory as a new class. Section 9701(c)(2)(B) fixes the relationship at July 20, 1992, or immediately before the signatory’s earlier cessation of business. The ordered assignment tiers in §9706(a) do not independently enlarge those classes.

Section 9706(b)(2) separately permits a post-enactment transfer of an assignment to a successor when its conditions are met, retaining the assignor and related persons as guarantors and requiring notice to the trustees. That express mechanism does not make all earlier asset purchasers automatically liable. The 1973 purchase and assumption of the agreement do not, on the asserted statutory successor theory, make the purchaser an original signatory or an enumerated related person. The holding does not exempt an asset purchaser that independently satisfies an enumerated relationship or resolve an independent contractual obligation.

The express inclusion of successors to related persons, paired with the absence of a parallel addition for original signatories, supports the distinction. The strongest objection is that it is anomalous to reach a remote related-person successor while excluding the direct successor that continued the coal business and assumed an agreement. Congress’s choices about which entities bear a finite statutory liability may be imperfect without being an absurdity that authorizes insertion of an omitted class. Russello’s differential-language principle and Ron Pair’s insistence on the enacted text support that conclusion. The current simulated Gitlitz, Chickasaw, and J.E.M. holdings require contextual reading but do not license replacement of these specific classes with a general successor-liability policy.

**S2 — Administration and constitutional boundary.** The statute’s definite classes resolve this assignment theory. Administrative preference cannot enlarge clear statutory liability; no universal holding about the force of all informal agency interpretations is necessary. Mead and Christensen remain applicable according to their own rules. Eastern Enterprises does not invalidate the Coal Act as a whole, and its distinct retroactivity judgment is not a ground to decide constitutional liability here. Both final camps resolve this case on statutory grounds.

**S3 — Disposition.** Affirm the Fourth Circuit’s relief against the challenged 86 assignments on the asserted related-person theory. The Court does not decide whether the purchaser satisfies every other meaning of “successor” or every independent contractual ground. The reverse camp would reject categorical exclusion of a direct successor and remand for the remaining preserved application of the statute; it would not order every premium paid without resolving those matters. Historical footnote 10 confirms that the reviewed related-person question did not settle the independently disputed successor status.

## 29. Romeu v. Cohen

### Components and final alternatives

**Threshold.** All eight permit adjudication of the recurring prospective claim concerning eligibility for New York’s absentee presidential ballot. The challenged 2000 election is not reopened. The dates in the neutral packet establish Romeu’s former New York residence, move to Puerto Rico, and Puerto Rico registration. No missing Supreme Court docket number, argument date, or original petition is invented.

**R1 — Federal equality challenge.** UOCAVA supplies a federal minimum for qualifying former residents living outside the United States; it does not itself forbid New York to provide broader absentee eligibility. Nevertheless, the federal definition’s exclusion of Puerto Rico remains an independently challenged distinction and must receive its own equality disposition. It is not disposed of simply by observing that the State could go further.

**R2 — State equality challenge.** New York’s challenged overseas qualification must be analyzed separately. Section 11-200(1) requires the pertinent prior residence and overseas status and excludes a person having an abode or domicile, registration, or voting in another domestic district, State, or territory under its terms. The state distinction is not merely a rule against casting two presidential votes; Puerto Rico registration is relevant to the enacted domestic-membership limitation even though Puerto Rico supplies no presidential ballot.

The **affirming formulation** sustains both distinctions. A State may ordinarily tie membership in its electorate to bona fide residence. Congress and New York can accommodate former residents abroad, who are outside every domestic political community, without extending the same accommodation to persons who move into another domestic polity. Puerto Rico’s local elections and resident-commissioner election do not substitute for a presidential vote; they instead help identify the domestic political membership relevant to the residence line. The classification uses ordinary territorial residence, not a suspect racial classification. It is rationally connected to the limited overseas accommodation and legitimate membership rules. Burdick’s calibrated approach does not automatically apply strict scrutiny before the claimant establishes membership in the relevant electorate. Carrington and McDonald preserve important equality limits but do not create permanent former-state membership. Harris v. Rosario supports appropriate territorial distinctions in its setting, not a general suspension of constitutional rights or rational review for every election rule.

The **reversing formulation** holds that, once government preserves the former-state presidential franchise for otherwise qualified emigrants, total exclusion of the comparable former resident who moves to Puerto Rico severely burdens the opportunity to choose the same national officers. It requires a compelling justification and a fit adequate to that burden. Local political membership supplies no presidential vote; no comparable risk of two presidential ballots justifies this complete exclusion. Anderson and Burdick supply an inquiry into actual burden and justification, and Harper and Reynolds support meaningful equality within the franchise government has chosen to preserve. The state-election-membership question cannot be resolved by relabeling the selected group as outsiders. UOCAVA’s status as a floor supplies no independent answer to its own unequal preservation of that franchise. Neither judicial disagreement in Bush nor the sustaining of candidate-access rules in Schrader establishes that a total exclusion accompanied by no presidential alternative is valid.

**R3 — Independent travel, due process, and membership theories.** All eight reject a freestanding constitutional right to retain New York electoral membership permanently after moving away, a general Article IV right to vote in the former State, or a travel-based entitlement to that former-state ballot. Saenz’s protection of equal citizenship on arrival in a State does not itself create a former-state franchise in a territory. This conclusion does not say that moving can never be burdened; it distinguishes the independent entitlement claim from the R1–R2 equality claim. The Fifth Amendment equality challenge remains part of R1; a separate general due-process right supplies no additional remedy.

**R4 — Remedy.** The affirming camp sustains dismissal. The reversing camp reverses dismissal of both equality claims and remands for prospective relief addressing the federal and state provisions through the properly responsible officials and ordinary severability and implementation analysis. UOCAVA’s federal minimum remains operative unless lawfully conformed; New York cannot cure the problem simply by withdrawing ballots that federal law still requires it to provide. Reversal does not itself award an unconditional ballot, reopen the 2000 election, allocate electors to Puerto Rico, or adopt a proportional counting scheme.

## 31. Wisconsin Department of Health and Family Services v. Blumer

### Components and final rule

**B1 — Permissible income-first method.** Section 1396r-5 permits a State, at the pre-eligibility hearing under subsection (e)(2)(C), to assess the community spouse’s expected post-eligibility income, including income that may be transferred under subsection (d)(1)(B), before increasing the community spouse resource allowance. The statute does not compel a resources-first calculation that excludes that potential support. The holding permits this method; it does not require every State to adopt it or conclusively adjudicate an independently challenged resources-first program.

The relevant provisions must retain their separate operation. Subsection (a)(1) displaces inconsistent ordinary Medicaid rules; subsection (a)(3)’s preservation of ordinary income and resource methods is subject to the statutory exceptions and does not resolve this issue alone. Subsection (b)(1) prohibits attributing the community spouse’s income to the institutionalized spouse; it is not a reciprocal prohibition stated in the opposite direction. Subsection (b)(2) supplies post-eligibility income-attribution rules tied to ownership, including its distinct treatment of trusts, jointly payable income, instruments, and the applicable rebuttal rules. Subsection (c) governs the initial resource assessment and later separation of resources. Subsection (d)(1)(B) authorizes the community spouse monthly income allowance to be deducted from the institutionalized spouse’s care contribution **only to the extent actually made available to the community spouse**. That transfer remains optional; the statute does not command that every institutionalized spouse make it.

Subsection (e)(2)(C) requires an increased resource allowance when the community spouse establishes the statutory insufficiency under a lawful calculation. The income-first method may forecast the income available after eligibility in making that calculation; it does not transform an anticipated transfer into an actual deduction or a compulsory transfer. The hearing adjustment for exceptional circumstances producing significant financial duress under subsection (e)(2)(B) is distinct from this resource-increase provision. The court-ordered support treatment under subsection (d)(5) is another distinct rule. None may be silently substituted for another’s trigger.

The majority’s explanation reads the framework as protecting a minimum maintenance income while preserving resources for care, rather than guaranteeing the largest possible resource reserve. Some prediction is inherent in either method. The statute allows the anticipated support stream to be considered, and its nonattribution provision does not forbid that consideration. The principal contrary argument is that (e)(2)(C) directs an increase based on resources and that a voluntary future payment is not current income belonging to the community spouse. The majority answers that this pre-eligibility decision evaluates the spouse’s circumstances when eligibility and the support allowance operate, so it can include the contemplated allowance without declaring ownership or payment already accomplished. No additional consent, escrow, or payment guarantee is imposed as a judicial condition of the rule, and no later transfer is assumed as a fact.

**B2 — Agency interpretation.** The administrative history distinguishes the November 1993 instruction requiring income first from the March 3, 1994 clarification permitting reasonable alternatives pending a final rule. The September 7, 2001 proposed rule, 66 Fed. Reg. 46763–46768, remained a proposal at this event. Regional letters discussed in the historical opinion are additional communications, not a basis to erase that chronology. These materials may receive the persuasive weight their analysis earns; they do not bind merely because they state the agency’s preferred policy. The majority independently adopts the statutory construction and gives the agency’s experience respectful consideration. Mead and Christensen therefore do not remove a premise on which this result depends. Good Samaritan confirms that a change in agency position alone does not establish invalidity. No universal administrative-law proposition unnecessary to this statutory case is adopted.

**B3 — Disposition.** Reverse the Wisconsin appellate court’s categorical rejection of income first and remand for the ordinary eligibility and allowance determinations under the permissible method. Do not decide a particular benefits amount or an eligibility date not resolved by the supplied record. The dissenting camp would affirm the requirement of a resources-first recalculation under its reading of (e)(2)(C).

### Selected separate position

Stevens, O’Connor, and Scalia may jointly support a dissent that gives the specific resource-increase command priority over the agency’s broader maintenance policy. Subsection (e)(2)(C) directs substitution of enough resources to produce the required income when the community spouse’s allowance is insufficient. The optional post-eligibility transfer under (d)(1)(B) does not add a prerequisite requiring the community spouse to depend on the institutionalized spouse’s income first. Predicting a yield from resources attributable to the community spouse differs legally from predicting another person’s voluntary payment. The latter may not occur or may diminish; moreover, the eligibility denial can prevent the protected post-eligibility arrangement from commencing. These are objections to the legal calculation, not findings that a particular later transfer failed here.

The dissent recognizes that (b)(1) is not textually reciprocal and that (b)(2)’s attribution rules operate at the specified stage. It uses the scheme’s differentiated ownership and timing rules as context, not as a fabricated symmetric nonattribution command. It must confront the majority’s premise that both methods forecast future circumstances; its answer is that the statute authorizes a resource-based protection and does not make voluntary support from another person an antecedent condition of that protection. Administrative flexibility, federalism, and a policy preference for consuming assets cannot replace that command. An agency’s changed position is not alone fatal, but its explanation must confront this statutory objection. The dissent would affirm the resources-first recalculation rather than award an unestablished dollar amount. ## 32. Winn-Dixie Stores, Inc. v. Commissioner

### Components and final rule

**W1 — Statutory exception and substantive deduction.** Compliance with the four-of-seven exception in 26 U.S.C. §264(c)(1) removes the relevant §264(a)(3) bar on interest associated with the prohibited systematic borrowing pattern; it does not independently establish a deduction under §163 or immunize a transaction lacking economic substance. As accepted below, the relevant pattern satisfied the exception because no part of the annual premium was financed by policy borrowing in four of the first seven years. Compliance is accepted, not independently adjudicated. The distinction is between removing this statutory disallowance and satisfying the separate conditions for the claimed deduction.

Knetsch, 364 U.S. 361, requires examination of the substantive transaction notwithstanding the absence of a particular statutory prohibition; Gregory, 293 U.S. 465, distinguishes transactions meeting statutory substance from arrangements whose claimed tax form supplies no qualifying substance. Actual policies, payments, and contractual obligations do not by themselves establish the substance of the borrowing arrangement. The strongest counterargument invokes Congress’s detailed regulation of insurance borrowing and the current Gitlitz holding’s respect for expressly allowed tax consequences. The answer is that this exception removes one bar without affirmatively suspending the independent background requirements. The Court does not use generalized tax policy to cancel a deduction Congress has actually conferred on a qualifying transaction.

**W2 — Accepted findings and narrow application.** The appeal accepts the findings that the arrangement could not produce a pretax profit and had no nontax business purpose. Interest and fees exceeded the relevant benefits, and the claimed broad employee-insurance arrangement supplied no accepted business need for this borrowing program. Winn-Dixie expressly disclaimed a factual challenge to those findings. On that jointly negative record, the claimed interest and related fees are not deductible merely because the four-of-seven exception is met.

The decision does not choose a universal conjunctive or disjunctive economic-substance test for cases in which profit potential and business purpose point in different directions. That is outside the presented dispute because both relevant findings are accepted. Nor does it hold that every loss-producing investment is a sham or that tax motives alone defeat an otherwise qualifying transaction. A different transaction with an actual pretax return presents a different premise; no contrary outcome is imported from another taxpayer’s case.

**W3 — Disposition.** Affirm the Eleventh Circuit’s judgment sustaining the reviewed 1993 deficiency and disallowance of interest and fees. No new cash-flow trial, premium-timing inquiry, or reconstruction of missing tax facts is required. The lower appellate docket and Tax Court docket are not supplied Supreme Court docket numbers.
