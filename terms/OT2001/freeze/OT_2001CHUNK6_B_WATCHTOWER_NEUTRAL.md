## 93. Watchtower Bible & Tract Society of New York, Inc. v. Village of Stratton

## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

* **Case Name:** Watchtower Bible & Tract Society of New York, Inc. v. Village of Stratton
* **Citation or Docket:** 536 U.S. 150; No. 00-1737.
* **October Term:** OT2001.
* **Entry Stage and Event:** MERITS, decision-stage preparation under the closed supplied case list.
* **Simulated Event Date:** 2002-06-17. Historical opinion or disposition calendar date used as the simulated event date.
* **Question Presented:** Whether a village may require a permit and identifying registration before residents engage in door-to-door religious or political advocacy and distribution of literature?
* **Question Granted:** The merits questions stated above are paraphrased from the reviewed judicial record; no exact grant wording is asserted.
* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The district court upheld the ordinance subject to a narrowing construction, and the Sixth Circuit affirmed. The religious speakers seek reversal of the permit requirement as applied to protected noncommercial advocacy.
* **Relief Requested:** The speakers seek invalidation of the challenged permit prerequisite; Stratton seeks affirmance of the narrowed ordinance.
* **Material Record:** Stratton required canvassers entering private residential property to promote a cause to obtain a permit from the mayor. Registration included identifying information, and records were open to inspection. The permit was free, but speaking without it exposed a canvasser to criminal liability. Jehovah’s Witnesses objected to obtaining governmental permission before their ministry and to the loss of anonymity. The village asserted fraud prevention, crime prevention, and residential privacy. The ordinance operated beyond commercial sales and fundraising and reached ordinary religious and political canvassing. Residents retained the ability to refuse visitors and use no-solicitation notices; the case does not establish a right to enter a home over an owner’s objection.
* **Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** The challenged requirement affects planned and spontaneous protected advocacy before it occurs. A free and routinely issued permit still requires advance identification and official contact. The ordinance’s actual scope and the lower court’s narrowing construction control the challenge, not every hypothetical canvassing law. An owner’s exclusion of an unwanted visitor is distinct from a governmental licensing condition on approaching willing listeners.

### Issue 1: Prior registration, anonymity, and residential canvassing

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** McIntyre v. Ohio Elections Commission protects ordinary individual political leaflets against a categorical identification demand and requires narrow fit to an overriding state interest in its content-defined setting. Talley v. California protects anonymous publication. These rules distinguish truthful advocacy from fraud and preserve materially different disclosure settings.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** Examine the actual burden on protected expression, including identification, delay, spontaneity, and required official permission, and test the asserted interest against the activities actually regulated. Viewpoint neutrality and a zero fee do not eliminate those burdens.
* **Other Governing Law:** The First and Fourteenth Amendments protect religious exercise, political expression, and distribution of literature. The ordinance and its judicial construction define the challenged permit duty. Property owners may refuse entry, and valid fraud and trespass laws remain available.
* **Material Other Authority:** Martin v. City of Struthers and Schneider v. State concern door-to-door distribution and the relationship between public regulation and willing recipients. Cantwell v. Connecticut addresses governmental control of religious solicitation. Their different settings should inform, not replace, analysis of this broad registration requirement.
* **Existing Rule’s Reach:** McIntyre concerns anonymous individual issue leaflets rather than every type of canvasser. It does not by itself decide all registration systems, commercial solicitation, or organizational financial disclosure. No supplied simulated holding approves this ordinance merely because its issuance criteria are nondiscretionary.
* **Open Point:** Whether the asserted crime, fraud, and privacy interests justify prior identification and licensing of this broad category of noncommercial advocacy.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

**Reasoning below.** The lower courts viewed the permit regime, as narrowed, as a permissible means of protecting residents and identifying canvassers. They regarded the free permit and limited administrative process as reducing the burden.

**Speakers.** A religious minister or political advocate should not have to notify government and identify herself before approaching a willing listener. The rule prevents anonymous and spontaneous advocacy and reaches speakers who solicit no money. General crime and fraud interests do not explain licensing every peaceful advocate, especially when ordinary law can address misconduct.

**Village.** Identification helps residents and police detect fraud and investigate wrongdoing. Advance registration is modest, costs nothing, and does not authorize discrimination among messages. Residents have a strong privacy interest in their homes, and uniform rules are easier to administer than case-by-case distinctions.

**Limits.** The speakers’ claim does not remove an owner’s authority to refuse conversation or prohibit entry. The village’s public-safety purpose does not establish that every religious or political visitor presents the risk associated with commercial fraud. Public availability of identities and prior permission remain burdens even without a denial of a particular permit.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

Reversal can invalidate the permit duty for noncommercial religious and political advocacy while leaving resident-controlled exclusion and otherwise valid fraud, trespass, and commercial regulation for their own applications. Affirmance would require a concrete justification for the full range of protected activity burdened, not just the ordinance’s neutral wording.

**Plausible Holding Scope:** A broad advance-registration requirement on ordinary noncommercial advocacy may fail because it unnecessarily compromises anonymity and spontaneity while poorly matching the asserted risks. That conclusion need not make all identification requirements unconstitutional or decide which narrowly drawn commercial-solicitation rules would be valid.

**Uncertainty:** The Court should not invent a pattern of fraudulent religious canvassing, a discretionary denial practice, or a substantial fee absent from the record. The defect alleged is the broad legal prerequisite itself and its actual consequences for protected speech.
