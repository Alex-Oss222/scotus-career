## Barnes v. Gorman

**Presented question and posture:** Whether punitive damages are available under ADA Title II and Rehabilitation Act §504. The Police Board commissioners are sued officially; the unreviewed arm-of-State ruling, compensatory liability and $1,034,817.33 compensation remain intact. The Eighth Circuit held punitive relief legally available but required independent review of culpability and amount; that remand did not conclusively reinstate the $1,200,000 punitive award. The question must be resolved for both statutory channels.

**Statutory chain:** Section 12133 supplies Title II's remedies through §794a. Section 794a(a)(2), the §504 route implicated here, adopts Title VI remedies. Section 794a(a)(1)'s Title VII route concerns federal employment under §791 and is distinct. The 1991 Act's express punitive authorization, caps and governmental exclusion for specified employment claims do not directly govern these provisions. They inform competing structural arguments without acting as an enacted Title II exclusion. Title II reaches public entities regardless of receipt of federal funds; any use of the Title VI funding rationale must therefore operate through the common remedy Congress incorporated, not an invented finding that every Title II defendant signed a funding contract.

### Punitive-unavailability ground supported by five Associates

**Operative rule:** The Title VI remedial scheme incorporated into §504 and Title II permits appropriate compensatory and prospective relief but does not authorize punitive damages in these private actions. Notice of a nondiscrimination obligation and responsibility to compensate an intentional breach does not by itself provide notice of the additional exceptional punishment sought here. Title II adopts the same remedial scope through its express incorporation even when its substantive coverage does not depend on funding.

**Sufficient explanation:** Franklin establishes compensation and a presumption of appropriate relief; it expressly leaves punishment unresolved. Pennhurst's notice principle has independent work when the requested remedy exceeds redress for the violation. The funding arrangement's contractual character supplies a relevant remedial expectation: ordinary compensation for a known breach differs from punitive punishment, which ordinarily is not a contract remedy absent an independent basis. Congress did not supply the additional punitive remedy through Title VI's incorporated scheme. That conclusion does not make the statutory civil-rights obligation a literal private contract for every purpose or restrict every possible monetary remedy to one comprehensive contract-law catalogue.

The strongest contrary argument invokes Franklin's clear-direction requirement and the traditional availability of punitive damages for aggravated civil-rights wrongs. The five answer that “appropriate” remains a limitation requiring attention to this statutory funding-and-remedial arrangement; the general remedial tradition cannot supply notice of every type of relief. Compensation for the recipient's intentional violation remains fully consistent with Franklin. Searles preserves already available §1983 punishment against an injury-specific PLRA restriction, and Kolstad construes express employment punitive authority; neither establishes punitive availability in Title VI. Great-West reinforces attention to the remedy actually authorized, without importing ERISA's equitable-only language.

**Disposition:** Reverse the Eighth Circuit's punitive-availability ruling and remand with the categorical punitive component eliminated. The compensatory judgment survives. No culpability or amount review is required for a legally unavailable punitive remedy.

### Punitive-availability ground supported by three Associates

**Operative rule:** In the established actions supplied by the statutory incorporation, punitive damages are not categorically unavailable solely because Congress did not separately enumerate them. They may be considered for evil motive or intent, or reckless or callous indifference to federally protected rights, on independently established defendant responsibility; a liability verdict alone does not compel submission or an award. Punishment is discretionary and remains subject to meaningful review of eligibility and constitutional amount.

**Sufficient explanation:** Franklin's appropriate-remedies principle operates in an existing action; no new cause of action is created. The incorporated scheme supplies no express punitive exclusion, and the traditional remedy for aggravated rights violations can give the notice appropriate to that class of misconduct. The known discrimination prohibition plus the aggravated state of mind differs from punishment for an inadvertent breach of an ambiguous funding condition. Pennhurst must not become a categorical rule limiting an acknowledged statutory civil-rights action to private-contract damages. Title II's broader nonfunding prohibition further counsels against that literal model, although its remedial incorporation remains controlling. Congress's selective expansion of employment remedies in 1991 does not itself enact a prohibition for these different claims.

The strongest objection is the absence of a sufficiently established punitive expectation for recipients, despite their notice of compensation. The three answer that Franklin does not demand a separate express remedial authorization after a cognizable action exists; the traditional aggravated-culpability standard, statutory incorporation and amount safeguards identify punishment's limits. They do not treat all intentional discrimination as punitive culpability, transplant Kolstad's complete employment agency/compliance scheme, or turn existing compensation into entitlement to punishment.

**Disposition:** Affirm the Eighth Circuit's rejection of the categorical bar and its remand for the district court's independent review of the actual evidence and permissible amount. Preserve compensation. The Court should not itself restore the $1,200,000 award or find the contested transport facts sufficient for punishment.

## Watchtower Bible & Tract Society of New York, Inc. v. Village of Stratton

**Reach and disposition:** All eight Associates would reverse the Sixth Circuit's approval of the advance-registration and permit prerequisite for the reviewed noncommercial religious and political door-to-door advocacy and remand for relief against that enforcement. The review is limited to identification, anonymity and advance permission under the second petition question. No independent free-exercise, hybrid-rights or facial-vagueness holding is supplied.

**Complete operative rule:** The Village may not condition this ordinary noncommercial advocacy to willing household listeners on advance personal registration and obtaining and carrying its solicitation permit. Even treating the ordinance as content neutral and applying the Village's less demanding regulatory standard, its burdens on anonymous and spontaneous speech are insufficiently fitted to fraud prevention, crime prevention and residential privacy. A speaker's right to seek a willing listener does not authorize trespass or displace the resident's choice to exclude visitors. The unchallenged §116.07 resident-registration and posted-exclusion mechanism remains outside the invalidation.

**Actual ordinance and narrowed operation:** Sections 116.01 and 116.03 reach uninvited entry to promote or explain a cause as well as to sell products or services; invited visits are distinct. Registration requires name, current home address and five years' residences, purpose, organization/employer and credentials, requested duration, intended addresses and reasonable additional purpose information. The district court accepted a Village-provided address list and construed the additional-information requirement as satisfied by the stated ministry. It altered the original hours; the discarded 5 p.m. cutoff is not a basis for this decision. Automatic issuance upon a complete application and the lack of a fee are relevant limits on the burden, not proof that it is absent.

Section 116.04 requires carrying and exhibiting the permit on police or a solicited person's request. The actual form contains no separate personal-name field. The constitutional decision therefore does not depend on an invented name badge. Compelled prior identifying registration, public availability of that information and the permit prerequisite remain real burdens separately from the name printed on the permit. Section 116.06 permits denial or revocation for incomplete information, application fraud or misrepresentation, fraud or false statements during activity, violation of law, trespass or loss of required qualifications. No actual application, denial or revocation is found. Threatened enforcement against the intended ministry supplies the pre-enforcement posture. Section 116.99 makes violations of the no-permit and registration commands fourth-degree misdemeanors; separate resident-exclusion penalties remain distinct.

**Controlling-level explanation supported by seven Associates:** Martin and Schneider preserve direct dissemination and willing listeners while allowing laws against actual trespass and fraud. McIntyre protects anonymity without requiring a speaker to establish threats; Buckley's initiative-circulation decision examines when, to whom and why identifying information must be exposed. Those cases do not make every disclosure invalid. They require attention to its actual burden and fit. The obligation to visit an official before ordinary advocacy prevents spontaneous speech and forces identifying disclosure from speakers who wish to remain anonymous. Viewing the speaker's face does not disclose her legal name and residence or make government registration costless.

The Village has real evidence concerning commercial scams, vulnerable older residents, limited police resources and deterrence/investigation benefits. Its record is not empty. The defect is the reach of those reasons to all noncommercial religious and political advocacy, including speakers seeking no sale or contribution. Compulsory identification may assist some investigation, but the record does not justify making it a general prerequisite to every protected conversation. Fraud and trespass prohibitions address wrongdoing; resident-controlled exclusion directly serves household choice. An official permit does not make an otherwise unwanted visitor welcome, and withholding a permit from a peaceful advocate does not implement a willing resident's own preference.

Thomas v. Chicago Park District sustains actual management of shared public space through objective criteria, bounded time and review. This case presents neither competing reservations of a common facility nor that logistical predicate. The fact that the Village describes issuance as routine does not establish the fit that justified the park scheme. O'Connor and Breyer's defense of formal initiative registration and financial reporting in Buckley likewise concerns a legally operative election process and source information, not a general license to begin ordinary household advocacy. City of Ladue reinforces attention to a distinctive channel and inadequate substitutes without mechanically equating signs and canvassing.

**Adversarial alternative:** Sustaining the narrowed ordinance would rest on modest free registration, face-to-face entry onto private property, objective issuance and predictable investigative value. Those features are substantial arguments and defeat a characterization of arbitrary licensing. They do not remove prior identification, public disclosure and spontaneity burdens or tie the broad covered speech class to demonstrated commercial harms. A narrower law regulating an actual fraud or supported commercial solicitation risk may present another question; the Court does not design one or uphold an unpresented alternative. A ground limited solely to religious exercise would fail to decide the actual anonymity question, and a sweeping ban on all licensing would exceed the necessary rule.
