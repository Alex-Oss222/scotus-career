## 49. Mickens: legal snapshot and supported forks

The claim-specific state-merits predicate is absent on the supplied record. Williams (Terry) therefore supplies constitutional performance/prejudice rules without making §2254(d) an artificial gate. Williams (Michael) distinguishes concealed-fact cause, diligence, hearing eligibility, default prejudice and actual constitutional entitlement. The fact that a federal hearing occurred does not itself decide the constitutional standard. Swidler establishes that the victim's death does not extinguish attorney-client confidentiality; it neither determines the contents of confidences nor establishes an actual adverse effect here.

The primary alternatives remain: require an actual conflict adversely affecting representation before dispensing with ordinary outcome prejudice; extend the protective inquiry cases to a defined judicially created and undisclosed conflict; or identify a supported defect in the hearing's legal inquiry and remand on that narrower basis. The first path must answer why the court's own knowledge and appointment do not make Holloway's protection applicable. The second must explain the difference between potential conflict, actual divided loyalty and an effect on representation, and why a new prophylactic rule may govern this collateral case. The third requires a real defect in the inquiry rather than dissatisfaction with an adverse finding.

Holloway concerns a timely conflict objection to joint representation; Sullivan requires an actual conflict adversely affecting performance in the absence of that trigger; Wood requires inquiry into an apparent conflict in its own setting. Those different predicates must remain distinct. The supplied record does not authorize a blanket rule about every successive representation, a factual finding that no confidential information existed, or a new trial based solely on professional discipline. Nonretroactivity and default remain separate if the model adopts a genuinely new constitutional rule or a broader ground.

## 51. Steele: threshold before treatment merits

The complete official Ohio opinion was read, including its separate concurrence, footnote 8 and paragraphs 43–48. The source is saved as `entering-law/OT_2001CHUNK4_A_SOURCES/steele-lower.txt`. It confirms the material distinction between expert testimony and judicial findings: all three experts supported incapacity, treatment benefit and absence of an equally effective alternative, but the probate court's incapacity finding did not include the additional findings Ohio required for an involuntary-medication order. The Ohio court declined to remand because voluntary medication was reported. Its statement that the record contained no proof of recovery or release is an October 2000 record limitation, not a March 2002 status finding.

Ohio discusses both state constitutional and federal rights. Its autonomous public-interest jurisdiction does not supply Article III jurisdiction on review. Steele must possess a continuing remedial stake or satisfy the federal exception for recurrence involving the same person and likely evasion of review. Continued mental illness, current custody, renewed refusal, an operative authorization and an independently surviving damages claim are not newly established. Nor is permanent voluntary consent established. The procedural due-process challenge was not raised in the state appeal.

Steel Co. forbids assuming Article III jurisdiction to decide disputed merits but preserves appropriate nonmerits threshold dispositions. Erie requires an actual continuing legal disability and adverse defense; its business-closure rule does not supply missing treatment facts. Laidlaw's burden for a defendant's voluntary cessation is not transferred automatically to this patient's voluntary treatment. Bancorp makes vacatur equitable; it neither erases every state judgment nor authorizes disputed merits. Izumi preserves dismissal of an unsuitable writ without substantive endorsement. The pertinent exact holdings and lineups are copied.

A procedural disposition on the present vehicle is ready for modeling. A merits ruling requires supported present-stake facts or an expressly adopted material premise after the governing procedure. The existing record supports comparison of dismissal as improvidently granted with a limited, independently justified jurisdictional inquiry. Neither path permits a finding of recovery, discharge, continuing coercion or mootness by speculation. An inquiry may clarify an actual personal stake; it cannot exist solely to remedy an operator's missing research paper. If no supported personal stake can be shown, there is no treatment merits decision to model.

If the merits become reachable, Weston and Riggins establish refusal-interest protection, medical appropriateness, overriding justification and reasonable alternatives, but Weston's additional defense-effects inquiry concerns a prosecution-restoration setting. Crane, decided January 22, 2002, separates genuine disorder and functional control impairment in civil commitment; it neither establishes this patient's current condition nor turns valid commitment into permission to medicate. S.S. ex rel. Jervis, decided March 26, concerns actual custodial control and known serious danger; it supplies no universal medical substitute-decision standard.

The competing merits arguments are bounded. Ohio's protection of a treatment-incapable person's welfare can be distinguished from danger prevention and can be evaluated through the actual judicial, clear-and-convincing, individual-benefit and less-intrusive-treatment safeguards. The opposing argument is that benefit and incapacity cannot eliminate the bodily intrusion without an independently sufficient substantive justification. Neither commitment nor refusal proves treatment-specific incapacity, and neither a competent patient's disfavored choice nor emergency medication is the reviewed nonemergency question. No finding about Steele's actual March 2002 capacity is supplied.

## 52. Adams: the legal question, agency guidance and remand

The entire official Eleventh Circuit opinion, including Judge Barkett's concurrence and every footnote, was read and is saved as `entering-law/OT_2001CHUNK4_A_SOURCES/adams-lower.txt`. The majority expressly treated the certified question as categorical and said the district court made no factual finding or sufficiency assessment about the impact allegations. Barkett would affirm on inadequate pleading of a qualifying impact/class claim while rejecting categorical unavailability. Her assessment is a competing judicial position, not an undisputed fact finding adopted below. The separate disparate-treatment class ruling was not the certified appeal.

The current-term Swierkiewicz decision concerns the sufficiency of intentional-discrimination pleadings. It does not decide whether impact is a recognized ADEA theory and cannot itself establish the causation, identified-practice or reasonable-factor predicates for such a theory. The complete public-law entry is copied for its actual rule and limits. Hazen expressly reserved impact; Kennedy's public concurrence, joined by Thomas, emphasized substantial unresolved objections to importing Title VII impact doctrine. That published position is included in full in the slice and must not be replaced by an inference that either Justice already rejected every impact route.

The official 2000 Code text for §623 and the enacted Civil Rights Act of 1991 are saved in the source folder. Section 623(a)(2)'s employee-classification and adverse-status language is distinct from (a)(1)'s treatment language. Section 623(f)(1) separately contains the bona fide occupational qualification, reasonable-factors-other-than-age, and foreign-workplace legal-conflict permissions. The present issue invokes the reasonable-factor branch; it does not adjudicate the other permissions. Section 105 of the 1991 Act added Title VII's impact framework, including specific-practice causation, its inseparable-process qualification, business necessity and the alternative-practice route. It did not insert that framework into the ADEA. Section 112 concerns discriminatory seniority systems under Title VII and adds no ADEA impact provision.

The historical-predicate check finds no displaced predicate for the provisions actually used: the ADEA's relevant language predates October 1991; the 1990 benefits revision responded to Betts, a pre-divergence decision; and §105's expressly identified Wards Cove predicate is a 1989 decision, also pre-divergence. The 1991 Act's later enactment date does not by itself displace its preserved pre-divergence predicate. This check does not admit unrelated provisions or decide their construction.

The lower concurrence reproduces the then-effective EEOC interpretive guidelines, 29 C.F.R. §1625.7(d)–(f). They expressly contemplate adverse impact and propose a business-necessity justification; their existence is verified by that published quotation. Their asserted controlling force is the concurrence's legal position. Under Mead and Christensen, a model must identify actual delegated authority and exercise of it before granting controlling weight; official experience and consistency remain potentially persuasive without that authority. More demanding business necessity is not silently equivalent to the statute's reasonable-factor language. The quoted guideline supports an argument, not a foreordained allocation of ultimate burdens.

The alternatives are to reject the categorical bar and remand under a bounded impact rule, sustain the categorical statutory reading, or choose a genuinely supported vehicle disposition. Recognition requires explanation of the text, practice-specific causation and the statutory exception without importing later law; rejection must answer the independent function of (a)(2), the reasonableness qualification and Griggs's related language. A supposed inadequate pleading ground must be reconciled with the limited certification and the absence of adopted findings. No path determines that these layoffs were unlawful, restores a class automatically, awards damages or converts a lower concurrence into the judgment's alternative holding.

## 54. Venetian: fuller contract and actual attribution

The complete reported Ninth Circuit majority, addendum agreement, footnotes and Brunetti dissent were read. The agreement is mechanically copied from the retrieved opinion into `entering-law/OT_2001CHUNK4_A_SOURCES/venetian-agreement.txt`. It supplies all provisions, including private-property reservations and future replacement terms, rather than only the majority's selected clauses. The forum issue is not resolved by visual resemblance alone.

The agreement requires the connected sidewalk and unobstructed pedestrian access, binds successors and requires recording; it preserves private-property rights within constitutional limits and disclaims other state action; it requires a dedication and replacement sidewalk if the existing sidewalk is removed, altered or abandoned. The majority construes the arrangement as an existing public servitude. Brunetti treats the reservation and future-dedication clause as proof that no present dedication was conveyed, distinguishes the parcel's private history from the former adjacent sidewalk, and insists that state attribution precede forum classification. These are competing legal constructions. Neither is an undisputed factual finding that can be assumed away.

A correction to the original packet's description of party positions is required: footnote 4 states that the County describes itself as neutral about the sidewalk's forum status in its executive enforcement role, while acknowledging that the commissioners wanted public-forum treatment. The union and ACLU advocate that treatment. The County's refusal to arrest demonstrators is established; an affirmative County appellate endorsement of every union argument is not.
