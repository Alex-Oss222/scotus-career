## 69. United States v. Cotton — verified historical control

**Historical event and lineup:** No. 01-687; argued April 15, 2002; decided May 20, 2002. The Court reversed the Fourth Circuit's judgment, 261 F.3d 397, and remanded for proceedings consistent with the opinion. Rehnquist wrote for a unanimous Court: Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer joined the whole opinion. There is no component-specific nonjoin or separate writing.

**Components and grounds:**

1. Indictment defects do not remove the district court's subject-matter jurisdiction. Bain's broad jurisdictional vocabulary arose when habeas jurisdiction was the available route for reviewing constitutional criminal error. Lamar and Williams reject that premise under the modern distinction between authority to adjudicate and error on the merits. Bain is overruled only insofar as it held that a defective indictment deprives a court of jurisdiction. The rule against substantive amendment of an indictment without resubmission to a grand jury remains; a merely formal change is excepted. Stirone and Russell involved preserved objections and do not compel jurisdictional treatment of this forfeited claim. 535 U.S. at 629–631.
2. Rule 52(b), rather than automatic jurisdictional reversal, governs the unpreserved indictment objection. Error, plainness, effect on substantial rights, and serious effect on fairness, integrity, or public reputation are distinct requirements. The Government conceded error and plainness under Apprendi and Jones. The Court assumed, without resolving, the substantial-rights requirement and denied correction at the fourth requirement because evidence that the conspiracy involved at least fifty grams of cocaine base was overwhelming and essentially uncontroverted. This does not hold that every omission of an element is harmless, that every indictment defect fails prong three, or that trial evidence is a grand-jury finding. 535 U.S. at 631–634.
3. The Court's conclusion concerns the statutory fifty-gram threshold, not the larger quantities used in Guidelines calculations or every individual attribution dispute. The majority's judgment remains reversal and remand, not an independently entered new sentence.

**Complete footnote check:** Note 1 identifies the 1889 and 1891 expansions of direct criminal review, supplying the historical jurisdiction explanation. Note 2 records competing prong-three arguments: respondents' imprisonment above the twenty-year default maximum versus the Government's notice and hypothetical grand-jury finding arguments. Those arguments remain unresolved because the Court assumes prong three. Note 3 distinguishes objections to the 1.5-kilogram base-offense-level calculation from the absence of an argument that the conspiracy involved less than fifty grams, the relevant life-maximum threshold. The distinct status of a party argument, judicial assessment, and jury finding must be preserved.

**Comparator assessment:** The supplied comparator is accurate. Reconciliation must test whether the actual entering-law error-review framework and quantity record preserve each historical premise; unanimity alone cannot decide that issue. No historical authorship is transferable.

## 70. Verizon Maryland — verified historical control

**Historical event and lineup:** Nos. 00-1531 and 00-1711; argued December 5, 2001; decided May 20, 2002. The Court vacated 240 F.3d 279 and remanded both consolidated cases. Scalia wrote for all eight participants: Rehnquist, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer joined in full. O'Connor took no part. Kennedy separately concurred without a joiner. Souter separately concurred with Ginsburg and Breyer. These are full joins to the Court opinion plus additional writings, not judgment-only votes or partial joins.

**Historical scope:** The original grant was limited to section 1331; after argument the Court historically expanded the grant to the remaining three questions, 534 U.S. 1072 (2001), when the Mathias vehicle problem became apparent. 535 U.S. at 641. This later historical Court action cannot itself enlarge the questions actually granted in the simulated matter. Reconciliation must use the frozen packet's authorized scope rather than silently import the historical expansion.

**Controlling components:**

1. Section 1331 permits review of the substantial claim that the Commission order is preempted by the Telecommunications Act or FCC ruling. A federal issue is presented when the right to relief depends on the construction of federal law, unless the federal claim is immaterial and asserted solely to obtain jurisdiction, or wholly insubstantial and frivolous. The possible lack of a valid cause of action concerns merits, not the existence of subject-matter jurisdiction over an arguable claim. The Court does not decide whether the Act supplies the asserted private cause of action. 535 U.S. at 642–643.
2. Section 252(e)(6) does not remove section 1331 jurisdiction. It gives no distinctive exclusive review procedure or limited remedial scheme, does not purport to withdraw general jurisdiction, and contrasts with section 252(e)(4)'s express exclusion of specified state-court review. The Court does not decide whether interpretation or enforcement of an already-approved interconnection agreement is itself a determination covered by section 252(e)(6). 535 U.S. at 641–644.
3. The Young action against the individual commissioners may proceed on allegations of an ongoing federal violation and a request for prospective relief. The injunction restrains enforcement of the challenged order. Declaratory relief can affect private parties' past financial liability without imposing past monetary liability on the State or its officers. The threshold inquiry does not decide the merits of the preemption claim, the force of the vacated FCC ruling, or whether contract interpretation ultimately is governed by state or federal law. 535 U.S. at 645–646.
4. The Telecommunications Act lacks the detailed, restrictive remedial scheme that historical Seminole Tribe treated as displacing Young. Section 252(e)(6) specifies neither a limited set of remedies nor even the required defendant. Direct Commission waiver is unnecessary to decide because relief against commissioners suffices. This is the historical Court's sovereign-immunity reasoning; it is not automatically operative in the different simulated constitutional regime. 535 U.S. at 647–648.

**Kennedy's additional ground:** He fully joins because this complaint parallels Young itself: a challenge to a state regulator's enforcement of an allegedly unlawful order. He retains the need to consider sovereign interests alongside federal supremacy and distinguishes Coeur d'Alene's attempt to divest the State of territorial sovereignty. He does not adopt a separate, judgment-only theory or retract his earlier understanding of Young. 535 U.S. at 648–649.

**Souter's additional ground, joined by Ginsburg and Breyer:** Young supplies a common ground even assuming immunity, but the assumption itself is doubtful. A state commission deciding a federal issue under delegated federal authority may be only a nominal defendant in judicial review of its decision, unlike a State exposed to ordinary monetary or injunctive liability. Captioning should not determine constitutional immunity; federal review of the State's federal-law determination resembles review of state-court federal questions. He reserves the question whether this particular privately negotiated agreement presents a federal issue. These observations supplement his full join and do not become holdings of the Court. 535 U.S. at 649–653.

**Complete footnote check:** Court note 1 supplies the agreement's reciprocal-compensation and local-traffic definitions, including the exclusion of commercial-mobile-radio traffic. Note 2 records disputed state/federal sources of the Commission's interpretive power but no contention that it lacked authority to interpret and enforce this agreement. Note 3 rejects Rooker-Feldman: section 1331 does not confer appellate review of state-court judgments, but that doctrine does not apply to executive action, including agency determinations. Souter note 1 preserves his objection to historical sovereign-immunity doctrine; note 2 reproduces the Eleventh Amendment; note 3 distinguishes conventional liability from agency review; note 4 reserves the actual federal character of contract interpretation; note 5 identifies direct appellate review of FCC determinations; notes 6–7 document differing federal and state captioning practices; note 8 distinguishes statutory permission for Commission participation from Maryland decisional treatment of agencies representing public policy; note 9 rejects a categorical dignity-based requirement that only the Supreme Court may review a case involving a State. The reporter's transferred-brief notes identify historical Court procedure only.

**Comparator assessment:** Accurate, with scope and immunity caveats necessary for use. No merits holding about who owes reciprocal compensation, direct Commission waiver, or automatic section 252(e)(6) coverage may be inferred.

## 71. Alabama v. Shelton — verified historical control

**Historical event and lineup:** No. 00-1214; argued February 19, 2002; decided May 20, 2002. Judgment of the Alabama Supreme Court affirmed, five to four. Ginsburg wrote for Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, and Breyer. Scalia dissented with Rehnquist, Kennedy, and Thomas. Each historical join is complete; no partial join or additional writing appears.

**Controlling rule and remedy:** Absent knowing and intelligent waiver, an indigent misdemeanor defendant may not receive a suspended prison sentence capable of actual incarceration unless counsel was provided in the prosecution establishing guilt. Activation incarcerates him for the underlying offense, not for the later probation violation. Scott's fine-only line remains; the Court does not adopt appointed counsel whenever imprisonment is merely an authorized penalty. The affirmance leaves the conviction and monetary punishment standing while sustaining invalidation of the suspended imprisonment component. The State represented that loss of the suspended sentence also removed the tethered probation term; the Court does not independently construe state law to establish a freestanding probation sentence. 535 U.S. at 657–662, 672–674.

**Distinctions and limits:** Gagnon concerns the separate, more limited due-process right at revocation following a conviction for which the accused had the trial right to counsel. Nichols concerns a new, counseled felony conviction and sentence; use of an earlier valid fine-only misdemeanor does not imprison the defendant for that earlier offense. Alabama's ordinary informal revocation hearing neither permits a reliable new guilt determination nor permits attack on the underlying conviction, so it cannot cure the trial deprivation. 535 U.S. at 662–668.

The majority expressly does **not** foreclose probation conditioned on incarceration only after a complete counseled trial de novo: note 5 treats that option as functionally equivalent to permitted pretrial probation. It expresses doubt about lesser later safeguards but expressly leaves undecided whether or what safeguards short of complete retrial could satisfy the Sixth Amendment; Alabama's actual procedures suffice to resolve this case. Consent-based pretrial probation remains available. Whether probation alone independently requires counsel is not resolved. Alabama's late theory of independently enforceable probation through counseled contempt, with a proposed five-day/$100 limit, was not passed upon below and is left first to the state court. Those are distinct qualifications and cannot be collapsed into either a universal ban on later proceedings or an approval of freestanding probation. 535 U.S. at 668 n.5, 671 n.11, 672–674.

**Dissent's complete substantive position:** Scalia, Rehnquist, Kennedy, and Thomas read Argersinger and Scott to distinguish actual loss of liberty from its threat. Shelton had not been incarcerated, and future incarceration depended on a violation, discretionary revocation, and a finding that other sanctions were inadequate. They would resolve the present imposition question in the State's favor and reserve the constitutional sufficiency of future activation procedures until actually presented. A complete counseled retrial, and possibly lesser safeguards testing the conviction, might suffice. The dissent also rejects the majority's cost assumptions: counsel supplied in this particular assault case by thirty-four States is different from counsel for every suspended sentence; only twenty-four States were identified as following the latter rule. It disputes equating voluntary pretrial diversion with post-trial probation and cautions against fixing state practices constitutionally on uncertain cost evidence. 535 U.S. at 674–681. The dissent does not hold that every future uncounseled incarceration is valid.

**Complete footnote check:** Court note 1 addresses separate state-law appellate claims; note 2 records Justice Maddox's state-court dissent, including his waiver view; note 3 declines the late request to overrule Scott/Argersinger in favor of an authorized-imprisonment rule; note 4 records invited amicus participation; note 5 preserves counseled retrial and the open lesser-safeguards question; note 6 answers the dissent's speculation objection by reference to the judgment under review; notes 7–9 give the three categories of broader state-law counsel rights; note 10 qualifies those generalizations and addresses the limited uncounseled categories; note 11 identifies consent and reserves probation-alone restraints; note 12 distinguishes the cost of one trial from two; note 13 locates Alabama's no-activation concession first in its reply brief and distinguishes its position below. Dissent note 1 contests whether the state court considered later safeguards; note 2 disputes equivalence of pretrial and post-trial probation; note 3 distinguishes limited appellate review from possible retrial and notes this case's courts of record; note 4 identifies state-law exceptions and disputes the inference from the number of categories to the number of affected prosecutions.

**Textual variance:** Cornell's dissent note 4 refers to similarly phrased Michigan and Pennsylvania statutes, whereas the final Reports text refers only to Pennsylvania. Final Reports text controls. Other observed differences concern reporter citation formatting or editorial wording; no vote, operative rule, or reserved-question difference was identified. The extracted final text has OCR footnote-number errors, resolved against the complete Cornell note sequence and final page placement.

**Comparator assessment:** Accurate as a compact summary. The complete retrial exception, open lesser-safeguards question, valid-waiver condition, and preservation-based refusal to broaden Scott are material additions required for safe reconciliation.

## 72. Brown v. Gilmore — lower-court comparison only

**Historical event and lineup:** Fourth Circuit Nos. 00-2132 and 00-2400; argued May 8, 2001; decided July 24, 2001; reported at 258 F.3d 265. Niemeyer wrote, Williams joined, King dissented. The majority affirmed the district court's summary judgment sustaining Virginia Code section 22.1-203 against a facial Establishment Clause challenge. Majority note 4 states that the companion appeal from denial of preliminary relief is resolved by the merits decision. No Supreme Court merits comparator or Justice-level merits alignment exists for this added matter.

**Majority's grounds and boundaries:** The majority applied historical Lemon purpose, effect, and entanglement analysis and treated religious accommodation as permissible alongside a genuine secular purpose. The statute required daily seated silence without distracting displays, permitting individual meditation, prayer, or other silent activity that did not interfere with others' corresponding choice. Quiet reflection and classroom order were supported secular purposes; permitting prayer did not exclude nonreligious uses. In the facial, preapplication posture, the majority considered text, context, history, and inevitable effects without treating feared unconstitutional implementation as a proven fact. Silence disclosed no student's mental activity, and administrative instructions forbade directed prayer, audible prayer, or pressure to pray or refrain. Teacher conduct consistent with those limits created negligible entanglement. These are lower-court judgments about a particular text and record, not a rule that every silence law is valid.

Wallace was distinguished as a record of solely religious purpose, addition of prayer to a statute already serving asserted secular functions, and state-sponsored prayer practices. The majority recognized that naming prayer alone does not prove invalidity; making the rule statewide rather than leaving the same compulsory minute to each locality did not itself force religious exercise. The opinion's citations to later historical Supreme Court decisions are comparison material only if the corresponding simulated holdings differ.

**King's dissent:** King deemed the purported secular purposes insincere when assessed against the religious preamble, refusal to remove prayer, mandatory statewide expansion, defense provision, accompanying school-prayer resolution, and history. He recognized that appropriately framed silence laws can be valid but found this one materially like Wallace. He argued that the purported accommodation selectively facilitated silent seated practices without an established burden on silent prayer, that the Virginia enactment lacked Georgia's express secular purpose and disclaimer, and that students' constrained choice did not eliminate subtle official pressure. He rejected the majority's treatment of Good News Club: private after-school access was unlike compulsory classroom observance. Purpose was sufficient to invalidate the law; he expressly did not decide Lemon's other two requirements, although he disputed the majority's analysis of children's impressionability. His accounts of student affidavits and possible pressure remain evidence and argument, not findings of actual post-enactment coercion.

**Complete footnote check:** Majority note 1 reproduces the enacted changes, including the Attorney General's mandatory defense provision; note 2 expressly leaves the admissibility of press quotations undecided and says they do not change the legislative-record assessment; note 3 describes the contemporaneous constitutional-amendment resolution; note 4 resolves the companion preliminary-relief appeal; note 5 recounts plaintiffs' concession that reflection can benefit children. King note 1 addresses vertical precedent; note 2 confines his necessary holding to purpose; note 3 says an individual legislator's remarks illustrate an objective assessment, not independent proof of the law's invalidity; note 4 preserves the possibility of a valid silence law; note 5 gives student-affidavit examples of religious practices and fears; note 6 challenges the majority's asymmetric treatment of speculation; note 7 distinguishes administrative guidance from enacted language and recounts the Attorney General's clarification; note 8 rejects a per se argument based solely on the word prayer; note 9 preserves the after-hours, private-conduct limits of Good News Club and its contextual endorsement inquiry.

**Comparator assessment:** Accurate. The only numerical historical alignment here is the Fourth Circuit's two-to-one judgment; no Supreme Court vote, rationale, or authorship may be generated from it. Statutory requirements, implementation directions, majority findings, and dissent arguments remain distinct. The recovered appendix is complete; no decisive source gap appears in the comparator itself.

## 73. Mathias v. WorldCom Technologies — procedural comparison

**Historical event and participation:** No. 00-878; argued December 5, 2001; decided May 20, 2002. Per curiam dismissal of certiorari as improvidently granted. O'Connor took no part in consideration or decision. No separate writing or dissent is published. The source provides no numerical merits vote, and this validation supplies none.

**Ground and scope:** After full briefing and argument, the petitioning commissioners were prevailing parties seeking review of adverse statements that were unnecessary to the judgment and would not bind them in future litigation. Maltbie supplied the general rule that a party cannot appeal a favorable judgment merely to correct unfavorable findings. The Court did not decide the three granted substantive questions: section 252(e)(6) review of enforcement decisions, participation-based immunity waiver, and prospective officer relief under Young. It mentioned the separately decided Maryland cases as the vehicle for those questions; that reference does not convert the dismissal into a merits judgment, establish a universal constitutional standing rule, or vacate the Seventh Circuit's judgment. 535 U.S. at 683–684.

**Comparator assessment:** Accurate. The historical adverse-ruling/no-binding-consequence assessment is the premise to test against the frozen actual posture. It must not be treated as independent proof that a challenged collateral ruling has no present consequence. Nor does the historical dismissal alter the supplied module's classification or supply a simulated Court action.
