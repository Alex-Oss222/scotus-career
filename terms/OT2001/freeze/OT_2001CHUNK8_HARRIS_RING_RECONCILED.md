## Harris: common propositions and remedy

### H1. Statutory construction

All eight Associates accept that the amended §924(c)(1)(A) uses a completed basic-offense clause followed by graduated penalty directions. Brandishing is a sentencing condition as a matter of that structure; this description does not decide its constitutional treatment. Jones supplies attention to text, structure, tradition and consequence, and Castillo construed the materially different pre-1998 weapon-type provision. Neither statutory unanimity in Castillo nor placement after the word “shall” alone resolves this amendment.

The strongest contrary construction treats the defined intimidating conduct and two-year increase as an aggravated statutory element. The selected construction remains lawful because the statute does not itself prescribe judicial preponderance factfinding or dispense with constitutional charging protections. Applying Angle to the penalty condition therefore does not require invalidating the statute or inserting an exemption from its terms. Historical Harris's reliance on an undisturbed McMillan to reject constitutional doubt is unavailable. The current conclusion rests instead on the text's penalty structure and the independent operation of settled element protections. Thomas's historical dissent at 575–580 supports that separation: its constitutional conclusion did not depend on resolving the disputed statutory label in the defendant's favor. Acceptance of this construction by the four historical dissenters is not described as their historical statutory vote.

The governing greater-minimum exception and §924(c)(4)'s complete brandishing definition remain intact. Brandishing requires display of all or part of the firearm, or otherwise making its presence known to another person, **in order to intimidate that person**, regardless of whether the firearm is directly visible. The opening exception applies to the extent a greater minimum is otherwise provided by §924(c) or another provision of law. No dispute about that exception is decided here. The five-, seven- and ten-year clauses inform structure; Harris presents the seven-year brandishing condition, not an independently adjudicated discharge claim. No finding that intimidation occurred is supplied by construing the definition.

### H2. Constitutional floor, charging and proof

For Stevens, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg, Angle I-A directly governs a present-offense fact that legally raises the binding floor from five to seven years. Its constitutional effect does not vanish because the outer maximum is unchanged. Angle I-B independently requires applicable federal grand-jury charging protection. Brandishing was omitted from the indictment and was found at sentencing only by a preponderance. Harris's lawful bench-trial waiver changes the proper factfinder; it neither enlarges the indictment nor lowers the reasonable-doubt burden. No valid admission of brandishing as an aggravated element is supplied. The decision does not independently require another jury after the lawful waiver.

O'Connor, Kennedy and Breyer reject the floor-to-element rule, maintaining their published Angle disagreement and seeking its reconsideration. They cannot distinguish statutory floors from binding Guidelines floors merely by their source. Their asserted legislative authority to guide sentencing within an outer statutory range conflicts with current law; that is an express dissenting merits position, not an alternative description of controlling law. Their votes supply no support for the constitutional charging rationale.

### H3. Ordinary review permits sentence relief on this record

The statutory-element objection at sentencing and the constitutional argument added on appeal remain distinct. The five relief votes assume forfeiture of the constitutional theory and satisfy the stricter Rule 52(b) inquiry; they do not find every theory preserved or infer intentional waiver.

1. **Error:** The uncharged aggravator was used to compel a higher binding floor without a reasonable-doubt element determination. Angle supplies the floor and charging rules; Cotton confirms that this real charging defect is nonjurisdictional.
2. **Plainness:** McMillan supplied settled contrary minimum law at sentencing. Angle displaced that rule while direct review remained pending. Johnson makes the error plain at appellate consideration in this settled-law/intervening-law setting. No rule for every initially unsettled issue is added.
3. **Substantial rights:** Harris bears the ordinary effect burden. The contested brandishing issue was described by the sentencing judge as close, resolved under a lower standard, and used to impose the minimum that §2K2.4 made the guideline sentence. The judge considered seven years appropriate but expressly declined to choose it independently if the brandishing ruling failed. Taken together, those facts establish a reasonable probability of a different lawful sentencing outcome. This is an affirmative actual-record showing, not presumed prejudice or a transfer of the burden to the Government.
4. **Discretionary correction:** Compulsory additional imprisonment resting on that genuinely contested lower-standard finding, after an objection demanding charging and trial proof and without an independently selected unaffected sentence, seriously affects this proceeding's fairness and integrity. That additional conclusion is not inferred merely from the third condition. Johnson's clear materiality and Cotton's essentially uncontested statutory threshold differ from this record. Neither case creates a general evidence exception, an innocence prerequisite, or a requirement of a second unrelated institutional wrong.

The judgment affirming the aggravated firearm sentence cannot stand; vacate that sentence and remand for lawful sentencing, preserving the basic carrying conviction. The strongest competing remedy position points to the judge's approval of seven years and the possibility of a later lawful departure. Those facts do not establish an entered alternative sentence. The remand neither fixes five years nor authorizes a new aggravated trial, enhancement jury, indictment amendment or otherwise unavailable departure. The grant does not reopen the basic conviction or unrelated evidentiary rulings. No universal structural-error or preserved-error holding is needed.

## Ring: common propositions, distinct grounds and mandate

### R1. Sixth Amendment eligibility rule

Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg require jury determination beyond a reasonable doubt of a present-offense aggravating fact necessary to make death legally available, absent a valid element admission or lawful jury waiver. Neither exception is supplied here. The felony-murder verdict alone did not establish the additional statutory eligibility fact. The six would expressly overrule Walton only insofar as it permits the judge, sitting without a jury, to supply that necessary aggravator. Neither Apprendi's reservation nor Angle's binding-range rule is misdescribed as an earlier overruling of Walton.

The competing retention path invokes Walton's direct allocation holding, capital narrowing, procedural reliance and judicial-selection precedents. The answer is limited to eligibility: constitutionally required narrowing does not determine which factfinder establishes membership in the narrowed class. A nominal death maximum does not erase an enacted prerequisite without which death is unavailable. Apprendi's actual-authorization principle, reinforced by Angle, defeats the element/sentencing label at that boundary. The Court may respect reliance in lawful implementation without keeping an incompatible rule for the very fact authorizing the greatest punishment.

This does not decide a jury requirement for mitigation, weighing, the ultimate selection between authorized penalties, state grand-jury charging, prior-conviction facts, collateral retroactivity or a general procedure for new capital trials. Existing lawful selection and review powers remain at their actual scope; appellate reweighing cannot itself become a missing eligibility verdict. Breyer's different Eighth Amendment position below supplies no additional Sixth Amendment rationale vote. No combination of his rationale with R1 creates a broader common holding.

### R2. Reversal and remand; actual-record harmlessness remains distinct

The six R1 supporters reverse the Arizona Supreme Court's judgment insofar as it affirms the death sentence and remand for proceedings consistent with the jury-eligibility rule. The felony-murder conviction remains intact. They do not separately vacate the death sentence unconditionally, direct life, order a guilt retrial or authorize an otherwise unavailable new capital proceeding. The present record permits resolution of the legal allocation question while leaving the State's actual harmlessness contention for first proper application below.

Arizona contends that the robbery verdict implicitly supplies pecuniary gain. The lower court must examine the particular aggravator under Arizona law, the actual charge and necessary findings, and the applicable error-review rule; the word “robbery” is not itself an equivalent finding. The State bears any applicable preserved-error burden beyond a reasonable doubt. Strong evidence or a proposed later verdict cannot replace the required inquiry. The frozen packet does not include the complete actual charge needed to decide conclusively whether the State satisfies that burden.

Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas support applying the qualified Neder actual-verdict method to this omission: examine instructions, evidence directed to the jury, required findings and material conflicts; necessary equivalent findings are sufficient but not exclusive, while whole-record inference must establish noncontribution to the actual verdict beyond a reasonable doubt. An appellate court cannot newly resolve credibility or substitute proof sufficient for a hypothetical verdict. Scalia and Ginsburg support the reversal-and-remand judgment while limiting any harmlessness inquiry to genuine equivalent jury findings. Their published Neder qualification survives. The four affirmative Associate commitments to the broader method are not inflated by counting either an affirmance dissenter or a judgment-only vote.

This remand does not conclusively decide harmlessness or harmfulness. Failure of the State's applicable showing requires sentence relief; a legally sufficient showing may sustain the sentence against the Sixth Amendment omission. The governing actual-record limits differ from unqualified reliance on overwhelming evidence, but they do not require changing historical Ring's reversal into vacatur. Historical Ring, 536 U.S. at 609 n.7, already left the same harmlessness contention below. The prior provisional appellate-vacatur language is therefore not retained.

### R3. Breyer's independent Eighth Amendment judgment position

Breyer continues to reject the Sixth Amendment reclassification associated with Apprendi and Angle. He nevertheless concludes that the Eighth Amendment requires a jury to take responsibility for the ultimate decision to impose death. His ground concerns selection, not merely the finding that first makes death available. A jury aggravator finding with judicial final selection would satisfy R1's allocation rule while failing his additional requirement. His agreement in reversal cannot be counted as a join in R1 or the broader Neder method.

The comparator supplies a direct and specific answer to the provisional barrier. At 536 U.S. 614, Breyer expressly acknowledges joining Harris v. Alabama and says that he has come to accept Stevens's contrary view. He grounds that reconsideration in Gregg and Furman's special capital safeguards, Stevens's Spaziano and Harris v. Alabama writings, Witherspoon's community judgment, and the Eighth Amendment's proportionality concern. In his view, retribution is the principal justification for death and jurors better express the community's moral judgment whether the particular defendant should die. A single official's decision does not provide that safeguard, even if the official is elected. These are his legal reasons, not new findings about Ring or a wholesale adoption of empirical material cited in the historical concurrence.

The strongest contrary argument is his own earlier approval of judicial selection and the absence of a generally controlling jury-selection rule. But the historical writing expressly confronts that earlier join; repeating it does not supply a changed premise from history. The narrower simulated Harris v. Alabama holding also does not establish a stronger barrier: it approved the admitted advisory-weight and override challenge, preserving distinct sentencing defects. The intervening simulated capital Jones instruction decision concerns accuracy of a statutorily assigned jury's choice and neither commands nor forbids this Eighth Amendment reconsideration. Atkins addresses a separate categorical limit. None displaces Breyer's stated moral-responsibility basis. His ordinary sentencing objections in Angle are consistent with treating death differently under the Eighth Amendment.

Breyer therefore concurs in reversal and remand while maintaining this broader separate ground. Harmlessness of the missing eligibility finding alone would not answer his objection to judicial final selection. He does not adopt R2's broader error-review rationale or supply a vote to announce his Eighth Amendment rule for the Court. No historical authorship or join is imported by this commitment.
