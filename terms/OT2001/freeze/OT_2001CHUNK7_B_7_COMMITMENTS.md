## Atkins v. Virginia — No. 00-8452

**Neutral completion and refreshed law.** The preserved claim concerns categorical death eligibility, not guilt, competence or Virginia's ordinary comparative sentencing review. Penry v. Johnson (June 4, 2001) preserves effective mitigation but expressly does not decide this categorical rule. Henderson v. Norris (May 13, 2002) concerns narrow noncapital proportionality and establishes no existing disability exemption. The governing capital-mitigation standard remains effective June 4, 2001 at its actual scope. Penry v. Lynaugh's refusal of a categorical exemption must therefore be expressly retained or displaced.

The factual supplement establishes eighteen death-penalty States with disability-specific enactments, twelve without capital punishment and thirty-eight retaining it. Eleven disability enactments have offense, conviction or sentencing temporal limits; New York retains a correctional-custody murder exception; Kansas's capacity definition is not silently equated with every clinical diagnosis. Federal prohibitions also exist. The direction since 1989 is one-way among enacted exemptions. Recent enactment and limited experience qualify the evidentiary inference. Texas's vetoed bill and recommendations add no enacted prohibition. The reported five-State practice compilation concerns known IQ scores below seventy, not a verified census of fully diagnosed persons.

**Supported alternatives.** A categorical exemption can rest on the change in objective domestic evidence together with diminished culpability, attenuated retribution and deterrence, and the distinctive reliability problem; its principal counterargument is that many capital States still authorize execution and individualized mitigation can reflect variation within the category. Retaining Penry can rest on the absence of a sufficiently settled prohibition and the difference between diminished and absent responsibility; its principal counterargument is that Penry itself relied on the then-existing paucity of enactments and allowed for later evidence, while repeated mitigation does not eliminate the category's risks. Neither route permits an appellate finding of Atkins's diagnosis from a single score.

## Dadian v. Village of Wilmette

**Neutral completion and refreshed law.** The relevant record is a completed jury trial, so Rule 50 sufficiency and Rule 61 harmlessness govern, not a prima-facie employment burden formula. The lower opinion describes Mrs. Dadian's longstanding degenerative walking limitations, fracture history, medical testimony and comparative driveway evidence. A front driveway would be thirty feet; the rear alternative required eighty feet and a turnabout. The alternative's aesthetic and financial disadvantages do not alone establish necessity; their place in the complete comparison does not remove the concrete disability-related walking and turning limitations. Evidence suffices for a rational jury and is not conclusive factfinding by this Court.

Toyota (January 8, 2002) requires individual substantial limitation and rejects an occupational shortcut; the severe walking proof is independent of employment categories. Barnett (April 29, 2002) keeps reasonableness and the entity's defense separate; its seniority presumption is confined to its Title I allocation problem. Bragdon's objective individualized threat method, effective June 25, 1998, and Martin's necessary-modification/alteration separation, effective May 29, 2001, supply relevant statutory reasoning within their actual titles. Echazabal (June 10, 2002) places its own-health defense on the employer and requires objective proof; its own-health permission is not transplanted into housing or Title II. Barnes (June 17, 2002) concerns punitive damages under incorporated remedies; the permit injunction adjudicated here is not such an award.

**Supported alternatives.** Affirmance rests on a rational jury's finding of disability-related necessity and reasonable bounded modification, and the Village's failure to establish the asserted objective threat. The strongest alternative is a new trial because the threat instruction was attached only to intentional discrimination. On the published trial recitation, the jury resolved that same asserted threat for the Dadians under the properly burdened intentional theory; the placement error supplies no different unresolved safety determination. Judgment for the Village would require treating a rear turnabout as conclusively equally effective or reweighing trial credibility, which the evidence does not permit. The statutory accommodation duty does not depend on discriminatory motive.

## Rush Prudential HMO, Inc. v. Moran — No. 00-1021
