SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


Jose Castro entered this country illegally. He 
committed another illegal act when he obtained employment 
at Hoffman by presenting fraudulent immigration 
documents. And, he compounded his prior unlawful acts by 
remaining in this country illegally for at least four years 
following his discharge from Hoffman and by lying under 
oath about his true identity. Thereafter, the NLRB rewarded 
Castro for his illegal conduct by awarding him more than 
four years of backpay. 


The issue before the Court in this case is whether the 
NLRB acted properly in awarding Castro, an admitted illegal 
alien, backpay under the NLRA when he never was legally 
present or legally authorized to work in this country during 
the backpay period. To resolve this issue, the Court need 
look no further than its own precedent, which the NLRB and 
the D.C. Circuit ignored in this case, and the Second and 
Ninth Circuits have ignored in similar cases. 


In Sure-Tan v. NLRB, 467 U.S. 883 (1984), this Court 
directed the National Labor Relations Board that, when 
considering backpay for an undocumented alien, “the 
employee[] must be deemed ‘unavailable’ for work (and the 
accrual of backpay therefore tolled) during any period when 
[the employee was] not lawfully entitled to be present and 
employed in the United States.” /d. at 903. By affirming the 
NLRB’s award of backpay to Castro, the D.C. Circuit 
disregarded this Court's instruction in Sure-Tan. As the 
NLRB itself has previously advocated, Sure-Tan stands for 


6 


the proposition that a worker who is not both lawfully 
present and legally authorized to obtain work in the United 
States may not receive backpay as compensation for a 
violation of the NLRA.3 The D.C. Circuit's refusal to follow 
Sure-Tan’s instruction requires reversal. 


In affirming the Board’s backpay award to Castro, the 
D.C. Circuit also disregarded this country’s immigration 
laws and policies. With the passage of the Immigration 
Reform and Control Act (“IRCA”) in 1986, Congress made it 
unlawful for employers to knowingly hire undocumented 
aliens and reaffirmed that undocumented aliens who use 
fraudulent documentation to obtain employment in this 
country do so illegally. Since the passage of IRCA, no other 
court has required an employer who hired an undocumented 
alien without any knowledge of the alien's undocumented 
status to compensate that undocumented alien with backpay 
for a violation of the NLRA. By awarding backpay to Castro 
in contravention of IRCA, the D.C. Circuit erred and its 
order must be reversed.

A. Sure-Tan v. NLRB 


Whether undocumented aliens may recover backpay 
as compensation for an unlawful discharge was first 
addressed by this Court in Sure-!7an, Inc. v. NLRB, 467 US. 
883 (1984). In Sure-Tan, after a union was elected as the 
employees’ collective bargaining representative and in an 
apparent attempt to invalidate the election, the employer 
alerted the Immigration and Naturalization Service ("INS") 
that several of its employees might be undocumented aliens. 
467 U.S. at 886-87. The INS investigated and determined that 
several of Sure-Tan’s employees were “living and working 
illegally in the United States....” /d. at 887. Upon threat of 
deportation, these employees voluntarily left the country. /d. 
The NLRB later concluded that Sure-Tan’s report to the INS 
constituted an unfair labor practice and issued a cease and 
desist order. /d. at 887-88. The Board declined to issue a 
reinstatement and backpay order, however, because the 
Board could not determine whether the employees had 
legally reentered the country and because the Board had no 
evidence regarding the employees’ legal availability to work 
during the traditional backpay period. /d. at 889. 


On appeal, the Seventh Circuit modified the Board's 
remedial order. /@ at 889-90. The Seventh Circuit held that 
Sure-Tan was required to reinstate the employees once the 
employees were legally present and free to work in the 
United States. Jd at 889. With respect to backpay, the 
Seventh Circuit opined that the discharged employees 


should be deemed unavailable for work during any period 
when they were not legally entitled to be present and 
employed in the United States. /@ at 890. Recognizing that 
the employees would likely be entitled to no backpay under 
this standard, however, the Court of Appeals suggested that 
the Board order Sure-Tan to pay six months of backpay to 
each employee. /@ The Board agreed. /d@ Accordingly, the 
final order approved by the Seventh Circuit awarded six 
months of backpay to each affected employee. /Z. 


This Court reversed the Seventh Circuit’s remedial 
order. /d. at 898-906. In considering the remedy to which the 
undocumented aliens might be entitled, this Court held that 
the Board's traditional remedies of reinstatement and 
backpay "must be conditioned upon the employee's legal 
readmittance to the United States." /d. at 902-03. Indeed, this 
Court cautioned that in assessing remedies under the NLRA, 
"the Board is obliged to take into account another equally 
important congressional objective—to wit, the objective of 
deterring unauthorized immigration that is embodied in the 
[Immigration and Nationality Act]." /Z at 903. Given these 
competing objectives, the Court held that, with respect to 
backpay, "the employees must be deemed ‘unavailable' for 
work (and the accrual of backpay therefore tolled) during any 
period when they were not lawfully entitled to be present and 
employed in the United States." Id. (emphasis added). 


In so holding, this Court recognized that the 
discharged employees would very likely be left without any 
remedy whatsoever:

A. IRCA and the INA 


In 1986, Congress enacted the Immigration Reform 
and Control Act (“IRCA”) to control illegal immigration to 
the United States. H.R. Rep. No. 99-682 (I), 99 Cong., 2d 
Sess. 45, reprinted in 6 U.S.S.C.A.N. 5649, 5649-50. IRCA sets 
forth a comprehensive employer verification system. 8 
U.S.C. § 1324a. It is the goal of this system to deny 
employment to undocumented aliens, thus depriving them 
of the incentive to illegally enter the United States. To 
enforce this verification system, IRCA makes it unlawful for 
any employer to knowingly hire or continue to employ an 
unauthorized alien. 8 U.S.C. § 1324a(a)(1)-(2). Because 
Congress recognized that the employer verification system 
would very likely result in the increased manufacture and 
use of fraudulent immigration documents, IRCA also makes 
it unlawful for any person to obtain employment by 
presenting documentation that falsely represents his 
immigration status, 8 U.S.C. § 1324c(a), and makes it a crime 
to use an identification document “knowing that the 
document was not issued lawfully for the use of the 
possessor, [or] ... that the document is false.” 18 U.S.C. 


§ 1546(b).

Despite the Board’s prior acknowledgment that an
