## Identity, publication, and judgment

Department of Housing and Urban Development v. Rucker, No. 00-1770, together with Oakland Housing Authority et al. v. Rucker et al., No. 00-1781; certiorari to the Ninth Circuit. Argued February 19, 2002; decided March 26, 2002. The judgment below, 237 F.3d 1113, was reversed, and both cases were remanded for further proceedings consistent with the opinion. The Court reviewed the judgment sustaining a preliminary injunction; it did not itself enter tenant-specific eviction judgments. 535 U.S. at 125-126, 129-130, 136.

Rehnquist delivered the only opinion. Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg joined it in full: eight participating Justices, no dissent. Breyer took no part in consideration or decision. The sources give no reason; none should be inferred. Id. at 126, 136.

## Statutory text and construction

The opinion quotes 42 U.S.C. 1437d(l)(6) (1994 ed., Supp. V), directing public housing agencies to use leases providing that criminal activity threatening other tenants' health, safety, or peaceful enjoyment, or any drug-related criminal activity on or off the premises, by a tenant, household member, guest, or other person under the tenant's control, supplies cause for terminating tenancy. The two activity branches must remain distinct: the opinion does not impose the express threat element of the first branch on the drug-crime branch. Id. at 127, 130-131.

The historical holding is that the statute unambiguously requires lease terms giving local authorities discretion to terminate a lease for household-member or guest drug-related activity irrespective of the tenant's actual or constructive knowledge. It resolves the interpretive question at Chevron's first step, because Congress directly addressed it; it is not a holding that ambiguity merely permits HUD's interpretation. Id. at 130-131, 136.

The absence of a tenant-knowledge qualification, together with the expansive word "any," defeats the proposed knowledge requirement. United States v. Monsanto, 491 U.S. 600, 609 (1989), supplies the no-unwritten-qualification comparison; United States v. Gonzales, 520 U.S. 1, 5 (1997), supplies the broad reading of "any." Id. at 131.

The phrase "under the tenant's control" modifies only "other person," not household member or guest. Extending it across the entire series would also produce the nonsensical category of a tenant under the tenant's control. The Court accepts HUD's explanation that control means granting access to the premises; permitted access is implicit in household-member and guest status. This is not a requirement of practical control over another person's behavior or knowledge of that behavior. It also is not a ruling that an unrelated person's conduct automatically counts without the required relationship. Id. at 131.

The Court expressly contrasts 21 U.S.C. 881(a)(7), which contains a preexisting innocent-owner defense for acts committed without the owner's knowledge or consent. The 1988 Act added leasehold interests to that forfeiture provision while creating the housing provision. Congress did not copy the defense into the latter. Transferring private property to the Government through forfeiture differs from a public landlord enforcing its lease. The statutory contrast demonstrates that Congress knew how to create the defense. Id. at 132.

No legislative history is needed because the text is clear. Note 4 additionally explains that the 1990 Senate Report addressed language rejected in conference; the passages could instead advise authorities how to exercise discretion. A House Report about the 2000 forfeiture reform is not probative of this distinct housing provision. The 1996 amendment broadened "on or near" to "on or off" five years after HUD rejected a knowledge requirement, with no other change; Lorillard v. Pons, 434 U.S. 575, 580 (1978), supports the presumption of awareness. Id. at 132-133 & n.4.

Section 1437d(l)(2)'s ban on unreasonable lease terms does not invalidate the specifically authorized no-fault clause; the more general provision cannot override the specific one. Green v. Bock Laundry Machine Co., 490 U.S. 504, 524-526 (1989), supplies that general/specific principle. Id. at 133 n.5.

## Permission, obligation, and regulatory scope

The agency must use the specified lease clause. It need not evict every tenant whose lease has been violated. Local authorities retain the eviction choice and may assess, among other circumstances, the project's crime problem, seriousness of the conduct, and reasonable efforts to prevent or mitigate it. The opinion identifies these considerations without converting them into a judicially prescribed mandatory checklist. Id. at 129, 133-134.

The rejected absurdity argument fails because discretion permits attention to circumstances; no-fault eviction is also familiar in landlord-tenant practice. Pacific Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Haslip, 499 U.S. 1, 14 (1991), supports deterrence and enforcement rationales for strict liability. Congress could reasonably protect safe, drug-free housing where lack of tenant knowledge does not eliminate danger from household crime. Id. at 134.

The actual lease and the regulation quoted in note 2 use "on or near"; the amended statute quoted by the Court uses "on or off." These must not be silently made identical. Note 2 quotes 24 CFR 966.4(f)(12)(i) (2001): the tenant must assure that covered people do not engage in criminal activity threatening other residents' or housing employees' health, safety, or peaceful enjoyment, or drug-related criminal activity on or near the premises; violation supplies cause for termination and eviction. The opinion separately quotes 24 CFR 966.4(l)(5)(i) concerning discretion to consider all circumstances, and HUD's 1991 explanation extending discretion to unknown, unforeseeable, or uncontrollable conduct. Id. at 128-129 & n.2.

## Constitutional reasoning and Bennis verification

**Bennis v. Michigan is neither cited nor discussed anywhere in the historical syllabus or Court opinion, including all footnotes.** No historical Rucker reasoning, vote, or join may be attributed to reliance on Bennis on the strength of these sources. The opinion's actual forfeiture discussion is the statutory comparison to section 881(a)(7), at page 132. Whether changed law could alter the result is outside this source-only report.

Constitutional avoidance cannot change an unambiguous statute: United States v. Oakland Cannabis Buyers' Cooperative, 532 U.S. 483, 494 (2001). United States v. Albertini, 472 U.S. 675, 680 (1985), supplies the concern about displacing Congress's legislative power. The Court also finds no serious constitutional doubt about discretionary no-fault eviction, citing Reno v. Flores, 507 U.S. 292, 314 n.9 (1993). 535 U.S. at 134-135.

The due-process objection based on deprivation without personal wrongdoing invokes Scales v. United States, 367 U.S. 203, 224-225 (1961), and Southwestern Telegraph & Telephone Co. v. Danaher, 238 U.S. 482 (1915). The Court distinguishes both: they concern the Government acting as sovereign, through criminal punishment or regulation of the general public. Here it acts as landlord, enforcing an agreed lease clause Congress required. The opinion does not say governmental landlords are categorically outside the Constitution. Id. at 135.

Greene v. Lindsey, 456 U.S. 444 (1982), establishes the tenant's property interest and the need for proper notice before deprivation. Rucker expressly accepts that interest. Eviction occurs through the state unlawful-detainer proceedings; the record gives no indication of deficient past or future notice. Individual disputes about whether the lease was actually violated can be resolved there. Id. at 135-136.

Note 6 rejects two further constitutional objections. Lyng v. Automobile Workers, 485 U.S. 360 (1988), forecloses the freedom-of-association claim based on eviction of unknowing tenants. Termination is neither a cash nor an in-kind payment payable to the Government, so the Court adopts the panel's conclusion that it is not an excessive fine. Id. at 136 n.6 (citing 203 F.3d 627, 647-648).

## Reservations and all numbered notes

There is no express general reservation of every as-applied constitutional claim and no broad holding resolving hypothetical circumstances beyond those addressed. The specified matters remaining outside the decision are important: Rucker's and Walker's ADA claims and all respondents' state-law claims were not before the Court (note 3); actual violation of a lease is for the individual state proceedings (135-136). Do not describe these reservations as a retained tenant-knowledge defense.

1. At 128 n.1: OHA dismissed Rucker's unlawful-detainer action in February 1998 after her daughter's incarceration removed the daughter's threat to other tenants. This is an historical procedural fact, not an adjudicated statutory exception.
2. At 129 n.2: complete quoted regulation and its two activity branches, discussed above.
3. At 129 n.3: ADA and state-law claims not before the Court, as specified above.
4. At 133 n.4: rejected Senate language; possible discretionary reading; irrelevant forfeiture history; 1996 geographic expansion and Lorillard, as specified above.
5. At 133 n.5: unreasonable-lease-terms argument and specific/general rule, as specified above.
6. At 136 n.6: association and excessive-fine arguments, as specified above.

Additional neutral-sensitive record facts, if not already frozen: the complaints alleged marijuana use by Lee's and Hill's resident grandsons in the complex parking lot; cocaine and a pipe found on Rucker's resident daughter three blocks away; three cocaine incidents in Walker's apartment involving his caregiver and two others over two months, with notices after the first two incidents. They are expressly recited as allegations. The preliminary injunction covered drug activity outside the apartment when the tenant lacked actual and constructive knowledge. Nothing here converts those allegations into trial findings. Id. at 128-130.

## Additional predecision source review

The [November 2001 HUD merits brief](https://www.justice.gov/osg/brief/hud-v-rucker-oakland-housing-auth-v-rucker-brief-merits) was subsequently read in full, including its 17 notes and statutory/regulatory appendix (18,040 extracted words). Clean text is retained in `rucker_hud_merits_brief_official.txt`. It contains no First Amendment, association, or Excessive Fines discussion. It therefore cannot establish the precise presentation of those claims. It does expressly invoke Bennis in the summary and Part II.E: counsel argues that permission to forfeit privately owned property without owner fault supports, even more strongly, enforcement of the public landlord's agreed lease; counsel also disputes the lower court's instrumental-use distinction. These are counsel's arguments, not grounds adopted by the historical Supreme Court opinion.

The entire predecision panel opinion, 203 F.3d 627 (February 14, 2000), was then read, including the panel's 26 notes and W. Fletcher's dissent with nine notes. Its Part V separately identifies First Amendment association (the district court's ground), Fourteenth Amendment intimate association (tenants' express claim), and Excessive Fines (tenants' express claim). Exact predecision extracts and the district judge's caption appear in `../rucker_neutral_claim_source_supplement.md`, which contains no target Supreme Court outcome. These three branches must not be collapsed into an undifferentiated due-process objection merely because the en banc decision cited only due-process concerns.

The panel caption names Charles R. Breyer as the district judge. The original petition's Appendix D at 138a corroborates the court, district docket C 98-00781 CRB, June 19, 1998 order, and authorship by "BREYER, J."
