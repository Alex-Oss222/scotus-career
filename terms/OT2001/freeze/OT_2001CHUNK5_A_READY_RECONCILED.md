## 66. Ashcroft v. American Civil Liberties Union

### Disposition, controlling reach and preserved questions

Seven Associates — O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer — support setting aside the Third Circuit judgment and remanding. Stevens would affirm the preliminary injunction on the existing community-standards ground. The seven agree that COPA's reference to community standards does not, by itself, justify the appellate court's categorical disposition. That limited agreement does not establish seven votes accepting local standards on the Web or seven votes finally rejecting every community-standards challenge.

The existing preliminary injunction remains operative while the unresolved grounds are considered. This remedial commitment rests on the live, unadjudicated injunction grounds and present posture; it does not depend on an unverified Government concession about its prayer. No Justice here votes to declare the entire statute constitutional or direct immediate enforcement.

The remaining examination includes COPA's commercial coverage, the relevant whole work or communication, differences within the under-17 age range, the scope and placement of defenses, practical verification costs and adult anonymity/access, and less restrictive means insofar as properly presented. The finding that speakers cannot geographically exclude visitors remains available. An objective serious-value inquiry is distinct from community acceptance; serious value for minors is also distinct from the complete constitutional protection of adults. Neither proposition conclusively resolves the full challenge. The government retains the justification burden applicable to restrictions on protected adult speech under the supplied Reno and Playboy law.

Section 231(a)'s knowing commercial communication and penalties, subsection (b)'s provider exclusions, subsection (c)(1)'s good-faith access-restriction defense, subsection (c)(2)'s separate cause-of-action protection, and subsection (d)'s privacy/security duties and exceptions remain distinct. No remedy rewrites them or treats all profit-related protected expression as commercial advertising. This handoff adopts no common national-community construction and no generic mandate to gather new evidence merely because original research documents were unavailable.

### Historical comparison and correction of the modeled common rationale

The verified historical result is 8–1 vacatur and remand, Stevens dissenting, with the injunction continuing. The historical Thomas opinion had five joins only for Parts I, II and IV, four for III-B, and three for III-A, III-C and III-D. O'Connor, Breyer, and Kennedy joined by Souter and Ginsburg each supplied materially different separate grounds. Those divisions prevent converting the original model's seven-Associate agreement in a limited disposition into a single unrestricted explanation of why local standards satisfy the Constitution.

The model's disposition is retained. Its statement that Breyer treats national construction as merely a possible option is corrected: his verified historical concurrence treats the statute as employing a national adult-community standard and regards that construction as necessary to avoid the serious constitutional difficulty of geographic variation online. The same statutory text and technological premises remain here. His Reno and Free Speech Coalition joins do not forbid ordinary supported construction, and his Playboy dissent concerns protection of children through practical alternatives; none supplies a reason to abandon his national-standard condition. No historical departure is supported on this point. This is his interpretive ground, not a new neutral finding or an automatically controlling construction.

The model understates O'Connor's position when it says only that a national standard may be considered. Her verified separate writing affirmatively regards a national standard as constitutionally permissible and necessary for reasonable Internet obscenity regulation. She nevertheless supports this limited disposition because the present showing does not establish the requisite substantial overbreadth from geographic variation once the conjunctive limitations are considered. She does not foreclose a better-supported facial or an as-applied challenge. Her own Reno, Playboy and Free Speech Coalition positions reinforce attention to the medium and actual scope; they do not supply a changed premise for dropping that qualification.

Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg agree in judgment because the court must determine the statutory scope before deciding the ratio and extent of protected expression burdened. They do not finally reject a community-standards claim on the existing record. Commercial coverage, the unit of material, the ages of minors and other statutory questions may make that claim decisive after integrated analysis. Their simulated Reno, Playboy and Free Speech Coalition commitments strengthen this caution rather than justify a broader local-standard approval. The original phrase “reviewed rationale is completely resolved” is therefore limited to rejection of the categorical method, not elimination of the integrated constitutional claim.

## 67. Lapides v. Board of Regents of University System of Georgia

### Reconciled rule, disposition and remedial limits

All eight Associates support reversing the Eleventh Circuit's no-waiver determination. A State that authorizes voluntary removal of an action to federal court relinquishes its federal-forum immunity from the state-law claims for which it already permits adjudication in its own courts. The consequence follows from actual voluntary invocation of federal jurisdiction. It does not require a separate state-law authorization for the Attorney General to declare an abstract immunity waiver. The State's express reservation of federal immunity in its tort statute is real; it is the subsequent authorized removal, not enactment of the state-court waiver alone, that produces the federal consequence.

The verified historical decision is unanimous and formally states “Reversed.” Assembly should use reversal as the disposition and explain the ensuing ordinary jurisdictional and claim proceedings; it must not turn this into an order mandating remand to state court. The available scope is confined to state-law claims for which state-court immunity has been waived. No rule disposing of every removed federal claim, unwaived state claim, or other possible immunity is necessary.

Will's independent rule remains: the State is not a person subject to the pleaded §1983 damages claim. Removal waiver neither creates a cause of action nor cures that statutory defect. Failure on that ground does not automatically negate original federal jurisdiction at the time of removal; the individual defendants' claims and actual jurisdictional anchors retain their separate treatment. If original jurisdiction is absent, the applicable mandatory remand rule governs. If original jurisdiction existed and the federal claims are dismissed, ordinary supplemental-jurisdiction discretion, including §1367(c)(3), governs retention or remand of the remaining state claims. No automatic federal retention, mandatory state-court remand, or adjudication of tort liability is ordered.

The Georgia waiver's substantive scope, manner conditions and available tort defenses remain intact. Authorization to remove is supported; further consent, counsel motive, a factual merits concession and satisfaction of each tort condition are not invented. A uniform federal rule makes the chosen forum's legal consequence predictable rather than depending on case-specific motives or varying internal waiver rules.

### Retained independent components and rule

All eight Associates affirm the defense summary judgment on both statutory components. Under then-2 U.S.C. §§1 and 7, the federal election day fixes legally completed congressional selection; it does not require every act contributing to that selection to occur that day. Oregon's system leaves voting and receipt available through election day and does not make an earlier winner legally final. The unanimous simulated Foster rule against consummated October congressional selection remains controlling; it is not a prohibition of every preliminary ballot act.

Independently, then-3 U.S.C. §1 fixes the day of presidential-elector appointment. The State's completed selection on the prescribed day, rather than each earlier ballot transmission, supplies the appointment. Foster offers a useful completion distinction but did not decide this separate statute. The enacted appointment language is read with the separately enacted presidential absentee provisions: §1973aa-1(d)'s quoted provision protects qualified residents absent from their election district or unit on election day, and subsection (g) permits less restrictive practices. Those provisions do not become an unlimited express authorization for every early-voting scheme or every federal office.

The contrary single-day text and documented legislative rejection of multiday polling are addressed directly. A rule equating each voter's early act with the completed election cannot adequately accommodate Congress's permitted or required absentee participation. The supported Oregon scheme avoids an early final selection, allows continuing election-day participation and requires receipt by the statutory deadline. Delaying certification of an already final early election would not suffice. The share of ballots returned early does not independently determine legal completion.

Affirmance leaves the described receipt-by-election-day and election-day deposit procedures in place. It authorizes neither post-election receipt nor an earlier consummated result, replaces no electoral calendar, and decides no fraud, secrecy, equality or broad constitutional entitlement claim. Later counting or certification is not an extension of the receipt period.
