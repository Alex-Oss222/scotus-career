## 2. Material facts and lower proceedings

The primary lower-court materials establish the following account. Citations here use their printed petition-appendix pages in [the clean lower-court source](OT_2001CHUNK5_B_MATHIAS_LOWER_SOURCES.md).

1. Ameritech Illinois and competing local carriers had commission-approved interconnection agreements providing reciprocal compensation for transport and termination of local traffic. After initially making payments, Ameritech announced that it would no longer pay compensation for calls to Internet service providers because it considered those calls nonlocal. The competing carriers complained to the ICC. The ICC required reciprocal compensation under the agreements. The controversy concerned the existing agreements and their enforcement, not merely a proposed agreement awaiting approval. See 8a–10a, 25a–26a.
2. Ameritech sued the competing carriers and the commissioners in their official capacities. The ICC itself was not a named defendant. The district court describes claims under the Telecommunications Act and a separate state-law Count V, and reports invocation of federal-question and supplemental jurisdiction. The commissioners moved to dismiss for lack of federal jurisdiction and immunity, and separately raised preclusion and abstention. See 10a–12a, 23a–26a, 32a n.4, 34a.
3. The district court decided the agreement merits before disposing of the commissioners' dismissal motions. The Seventh Circuit quotes the Rule 54(b) explanation: the court required merits briefing because of novelty and urgency, while preserving the commissioners' right to pursue immunity objections. The court entered judgment for the competing carriers on August 4, 1998 and denied the commissioners' dismissal motions on August 25. Ameritech appealed, and the commissioners cross-appealed. See 11a–12a, 24a–25a, 35a.
4. The Seventh Circuit expressly states that no formal judgment was entered against the commissioners. It nevertheless treated the subsequent denial of their dismissal motions as making the favorable merits decision binding on them and held that the terminating-order defect did not prevent the consolidated appeal. That is the lower court's ruling about its own appellate jurisdiction and the decision's binding character; it does not decide the present Supreme Court appellate-aggrievement question or establish any particular future preclusion. See 11a–12a.
5. On June 18, 1999, the Seventh Circuit rejected the commissioners' statutory-jurisdiction and sovereign-immunity objections. It construed section 252(e)(6) to permit review of state commission action enforcing an approved agreement for compliance with federal law. It relied on its MCI decision for a waiver theory based on commission participation in the federal scheme. It then found no federal-law violation in the ICC's reciprocal-compensation decision and affirmed the district court's decision affirming the ICC. See 12a–15a, 18a–22a.
6. The August 19, 1999 amendment expressly leaves state-law issues open for the proper forum. It distinguishes a decision contrary to an agreement's terms, presenting a contract-law problem, from whether commission action departs from federal law. Its language says a carrier may move to state court after the federal case yields no federal violation; it does not impose an obligation to bring that second action. Rehearing was denied on August 9, 2000. See 1a–5a.

The district court's August 25 order is separately material. It held the ICC's enforcement decision reviewable under section 252(e)(6), expressly declined to decide the section 1331 objection because of that holding, and rejected immunity on two stated grounds adopted from its prior MCI opinion: voluntary participation and prospective officer relief for an alleged ongoing federal violation. It also denied the second motion's preclusion and abstention grounds. See 26a–35a. Those are lower-court grounds, not presumptively correct rules of this Court. The operative order denies both motions; it is not an immunity dismissal or a damages award against the commissioners.

The lower merits discussion describes the agency's then-existing interpretation and the parties' agreements. It does not authorize importing later FCC developments or a new Supreme Court determination of ISP compensation. The supplied questions do not by themselves place every compensation issue before this Court.

## 4. Entering law and questions if further review is available

The [B entering-law slice](../entering-law/OT_2001CHUNK5_B.md) supplies the full relevant simulated entries and their proposition-level authority. Selected exact register text and the exact Forney entry are copied below. These copies are reading aids; their sources remain authoritative.

- **Appellate aggrievement:** Forney v. Apfel, June 15, 1998, is unanimous, authored by Scalia. It permits an actual claimant to appeal the denial of an immediate award when she received only a final sentence-four remand. Its personal unmet request matters; fee prevailing-party status is not dispositive. It does not hold that every denial of a defensive motion creates an appeal, and it expressly reserves wholly favorable relief and requests only for another explanation. Its reliance on Roper preserves the distinction between complete relief and a continuing personal stake.
- **Constitutional power and redress:** Citizens for a Better Environment v. Steel Co., June 2, 1997, prohibits assuming disputed Article III power to decide contested statutory merits. Its separate redress holding requires requested relief likely to repair the injury or prevent supported continuing or imminent personal injury. The authority is proposition-specific: five direct joins on sequencing; six on redress, including Breyer. Ruhrgas, May 17, 1999, permits the stated bounded choice among nonmerits jurisdictional grounds and supplies no general permission to assume power over merits.
- **Ordinary commercial conduct versus actual consent:** College Savings Bank, June 23, 1999, Part III rejects consent inferred solely from continued ordinary commercial activity even under a clear prospective federal-suit condition. Florida Prepaid Part IV applies that rule unanimously. Actual voluntary waiver and genuinely distinct accepted-benefit choices remain different; this packet assumes neither that service as a state regulator is ordinary commerce nor that it constitutes acceptance of a specifically offered benefit. The statutory arrangement and the actual State choice must do that work if needed.
- **Congressional authorization and constitutional power:** Florida Prepaid, June 23, 1999, establishes that Congress may authorize private federal enforcement against States when the underlying obligation and enforcement measure fall within Article I and Congress unmistakably authorizes suit. Stevens, Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer adopt that rule; O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas oppose its extension. The Court's Seminole Tribe did not impose a categorical Article I prohibition or an abrogation-before-officer sequence. An express State-suit authorization, actual consent and an officer action remain separate routes with separate predicates.
- **Prospective officer relief:** The actual Seminole Tribe and Coeur d'Alene holdings preserve relief against connected officers for ongoing federal violations within the applicable statutory and genuinely prospective remedial limits. Pennhurst's state-law boundary and Edelman's retrospective-compensation boundary remain. A detailed statute does not automatically exclude an officer route, but neither decision creates one for every statute. Here the named defendants are officials; the lower court's characterization of ongoing federal violation and prospective relief must be distinguished from a merits finding that such a violation occurred.
- **Jurisdiction and immunity are distinct:** Schacht, June 22, 1998, does not allow immunity affecting one claim to eliminate otherwise valid federal jurisdiction over distinct claims. It supplies no federal basis for a claim that lacks one, and does not settle the general classification of immunity or all waiver questions.
- **Telecommunications allocation:** AT&T Corp. v. Iowa Utilities Board, January 25, 1999, recognizes federal implementing authority and retained statutory state tasks. Its agreement and national/state allocation holdings supply context, not a decision that every enforcement dispute arises under federal law or that section 252(e)(6) is exclusive. Gulf Power, January 16, 2002, concerns pole-attachment coverage and supplies no answer to this appeal's jurisdiction, immunity or ISP-service classification questions.

While it is true that “[a] party who receives all that he has

Id. at 334; see also Electrical Fittings Corp. v. Thomas & 
Betts Co., 307 U.S. 241, 242 (1939). The Court has thus 
permitted apneals to proceed from favorable judgments

Roper, 445 U.S. at 335 and n.7.

#### Forney v. Apfel, 524 U.S. 266

**Docket or dockets:** No. 97-5737.

**Decided:** June 15, 1998.

##### A claimant denied the immediate award she sought may appeal a final sentence-four remand

**Holding:** A sentence-four judgment under 42 U.S.C. §405(g) ends the district-court civil action and is a final decision reviewable under 28 U.S.C. §1291. A claimant who sought an immediate benefits award but received only renewed agency proceedings remains adversely affected by the denied relief and may appeal that judgment.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Scalia), unanimous.

**Material application:** The claimant obtained renewed agency proceedings but not the immediate benefits award she sought. Her remaining interest in greater relief supplies appellate aggrievement, independently of prevailing-party status for fees.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Allowing review decides no benefits question. The appellate court must assess the District Court’s remedy under the applicable review law; it receives no license to substitute unsupported factfinding. The Court does not decide appeals from sentence-six remands, general agency-remand finality, judgments granting all requested relief, or requests merely for a different explanation of wholly favorable relief. Sentence six retains its own conditions: a Secretary’s pre-answer motion for good cause, or new material evidence with good cause for its earlier omission, followed by retained jurisdiction and return to court.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The Ninth Circuit must entertain Forney’s appeal and consider the challenge to the remand remedy. The benefits merits remain undecided; no immediate agency award or other outcome on appellate review is directed.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- **Shalala v. Schaefer:** Its sentence-four termination rule is applied to claimant appeals; its separate fee and prevailing-party holdings are unchanged.
- **Sullivan v. Finkelstein:** Its final-judgment construction supplies the statutory structure; its government-appellant setting did not resolve this claimant’s denied request for greater relief.
- **Melkonyan v. Sullivan:** Its sentence-four/sentence-six distinction remains operative; the Court creates no third remand category.
- **Deposit Guaranty National Bank v. Roper:** Its distinction between complete relief and a continuing personal stake supports appellate aggrievement here.
- **Director, OWCP v. Newport News Shipbuilding & Dry Dock Co. and Ingalls Shipbuilding, Inc. v. Director, OWCP:** Their limits on general administrative interest do not prevent an actual claimant from appealing denied relief.

## Standards and Tests selection: Prospective officer relief for ongoing federal property violations

### Prospective officer relief for ongoing federal property violations

**Governing question:** Whether a federal tribal-property claim may proceed against responsible state officers despite disputed title or sovereign interests.

**Current rule:** An alleged ongoing violation of federal tribal property rights may support prospective declaratory or injunctive relief against officers actually connected to it. A disputed federal title question or important sovereign interest does not alone bar the claim. Relief must govern proved unlawful future official conduct; it may not bind the immune State's title, award damages or retrospective compensation, execute against state property or order the State itself to convey title. A general obligation to obey law does not supply the required officer connection.

**Authority by component:**
- Officer route and real remedial boundary: Idaho v. Coeur d'Alene Tribe, Idaho v. Coeur d'Alene Tribe of Idaho — An ongoing federal property-right claim may proceed against responsible officers within real remedial limits, Stone-Zsela's five-Justice opinion, 1997-06-23.

**Present operation effective:** Idaho v. Coeur d'Alene Tribe, 1997-06-23.

**Exceptions and limits:** State-law commands remain barred by Pennhurst. The rule decides neither title nor every requested remedy, and supplies no sovereign-lands exception or general balancing of state interests. The distinct IGRA officer route does not supply procedures or a general State-amenability ruling for this claim.

**Holdings navigation:** Idaho v. Coeur d’Alene Tribe of Idaho (1997-06-23) — Federal Courts / Prospective officer suits and disputed tribal property rights.

## Standards and Tests selection: Removal of federal claims alongside an immune state claim

### Removal of federal claims alongside an immune state claim

**Governing question:** Whether Eleventh Amendment immunity affecting some claims defeats removal of an otherwise federal-question action.

**Current rule:** An action containing substantial §1331 federal claims is removable under §1441(a) even when Eleventh Amendment immunity bars particular State or official-capacity claims. Proper assertion of immunity prevents adjudication of the affected claim; it does not eliminate jurisdiction over the other federal claims or require remand of the entire action under §1447(c).

**Authority by component:**

- Immunity affecting one claim does not eliminate removal jurisdiction over remaining federal claims: Wisconsin Department of Corrections v. Schacht — controlling Holdings, 1998-06-22.

**Present operation effective:** 1998-06-22.

**Exceptions and limits:** The rule decides neither whether removal itself waives immunity nor every unasserted-immunity issue, and does not supply jurisdiction over a claim lacking its own necessary foundation.

**Holdings navigation:** Wisconsin Department of Corrections v. Schacht, 524 U.S. 381 — Removal jurisdiction and claim-specific state immunity.



## Standards and Tests selection: Commercial participation and State consent to federal suit

### Commercial participation and State consent to federal suit

**Governing question:** Whether explicit prospective regulation and continued ordinary commercial activity establish State consent to private federal enforcement.

**Current rule:** A State’s continued operation of an otherwise lawful commercial undertaking is not unequivocal submission to federal jurisdiction merely because Congress clearly and prospectively subjects that activity to private federal enforcement, even if the undertaking is realistically optional. Unmistakable congressional intent remains distinct from the State’s voluntary act of consent; an opportunity to leave ordinary commerce does not itself constitute acceptance of a specifically offered federal benefit or voluntary invocation of judicial authority. A State’s continued commercial participation after Congress authorizes federal patent suit likewise is not consent. Actual consent through a distinct State choice, such as an express waiver or voluntary invocation of federal jurisdiction, remains available.

**Authority by component:**

## Standards and Tests selection: Article I enforcement against States in federal court

### Article I enforcement against States in federal court

**Governing question:** Whether Congress, acting within Article I, may subject a nonconsenting State to private federal-court enforcement of a valid federal obligation.

**Current rule:** Congress may authorize private enforcement against States when the underlying obligation and enforcement measure fall within its Article I authority and Congress unmistakably authorizes the State’s exposure to suit. The Patent Clause, together with the Necessary and Proper Clause, supports the express federal patent remedy of §§271(h) and 296. The Eleventh Amendment limits the citizen-State diversity grants, not jurisdiction over a federal question; an out-of-State plaintiff’s citizenship does not change the claim’s federal source, and Hans does not establish an immunity Congress cannot displace in this setting. The rule is claim-specific: it removes immunity from the authorized action without finding a violation or an entitlement to relief.

**Authority by component:**

- Florida Prepaid Postsecondary Education Expense Board v. College Savings Bank — Canonical Decision Record, Holdings, “Article I independently sustains the expressly authorized action”, 1999-06-23; Stevens Part III with Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Independently sufficient alongside the Section 5 holding.

**Present operation effective:** Florida Prepaid, June 23, 1999.

**Exceptions and limits:** The rule requires a valid enumerated-power obligation, an enforcement measure within that power and unmistakable congressional authorization; enacted clarity alone does not supply power. It does not rest on commercial-participation consent, which College Savings Bank rejects, and decides no actual litigation waiver. Validity, infringement, culpability and each remedy’s statutory and properly presented constitutional limits remain for ordinary adjudication; no compensation, injunction, fee or enhanced award is preapproved. The action is not converted into an officer suit; Young, Coeur d’Alene, Edelman and Pennhurst retain their boundaries.

**Former-rule note:** Union Gas’s retained five-vote constitutional conclusion supplies the foundation; the rule extends that conclusion without treating every proposition in its plurality rationale as binding. Seminole Tribe separated unmistakable statutory authorization from constitutional amenability and set aside the State-dismissal direction without a controlling constitutional rationale; it established no categorical Article I prohibition, overruling of Union Gas or abrogation-before-officer sequence. Florida Prepaid resolves the constitutional question Seminole left open.

**Related current rules:** “Congruent and proportional Fourteenth Amendment enforcement” supplies the independent Section 5 ground for the same patent remedy. “Express state-court FLSA remedies against public employers” separately governs the state-court forum. “Commercial participation and State consent to federal suit” remains intact.

## Standards and Tests selection: Article III power before disputed statutory merits

### Article III power before disputed statutory merits

**Governing question:** Whether a court may assume disputed constitutional jurisdiction to resolve contested federal statutory liability.

**Current rule:** A federal court may not assume disputed Article III power merely to decide a contested statutory merits question. Statutory enforcement-and-penalty jurisdictional wording does not automatically make every liability element jurisdictional; substantial federal claims remain distinct from success on their merits. A court may dismiss for lack of personal jurisdiction before resolving subject-matter jurisdiction when personal jurisdiction is straightforward and presents no complex state-law question while subject-matter jurisdiction is difficult. Ordinarily an easily resolved subject-matter question should come first. The choice must respect statutory remand commands and review bars, comity and relevant forum interests, and may decide no contested merits; an established absence of subject-matter jurisdiction retains its statutory consequence.

**Authority by component:**

- Citizens for a Better Environment v. Steel Co. — 1. Constitutional power before this disputed statutory merits question, 1997-06-02; Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; five, with O'Connor and Kennedy reserving other legitimate exceptional sequences

- Ruhrgas AG v. Marathon Oil Co. — Canonical Decision Record, Holdings, “A court may choose a straightforward nonmerits jurisdictional ground”, 1999-05-17; Scalia, all nine Justices.

**Present operation effective:** Ruhrgas, May 17, 1999, for bounded nonmerits sequencing in removed and originally filed cases; Steel Co.’s merits prohibition retains its June 2, 1997 date.

**Exceptions and limits:** The rule prescribes no exhaustive hierarchy among thresholds and authorizes no assumed jurisdiction over disputed merits. Norton and Avrech retain their own controlled-merits and jurisdictional settings. A particular statutory priority, such as Price-Anderson coverage before tribal exhaustion, remains operative.
