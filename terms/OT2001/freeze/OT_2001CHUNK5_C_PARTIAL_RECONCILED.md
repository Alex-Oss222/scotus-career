### Question, components and operative commitments

The review concerns the Third Circuit judgment affirming the March 29, 2000 federal restriction on the Lowes' retained Florida litigation. The Lowes remained in the settlement for two eligible policies and excluded two others through the policy-specific election expressly offered in the notice. The federal order reaches motion practice, discovery, presentation of evidence and other action involving the facts of released transactions. The Florida action is stayed and inactive subject to ordinary reopening; its retained claims have not been dismissed.

The following propositions define the scope to which the individual rows refer:

- **P1 — Enforcement jurisdiction.** Incorporation of the release and express retention of jurisdiction permit the district court to enforce the settlement within Kokkonen's rule. This answers the postdismissal-jurisdiction objection; it does not satisfy section 2283 or determine what was released.
- **P2 — Relitigation exception and the actual release.** The incorporated release extinguishes claims and relief within its lawful scope, including a genuinely indirect effort to obtain relief on a released transaction. It does not, consistently with the express policy-specific exclusions, turn every fact common to a released policy and an excluded policy into a released claim or a conclusively determined issue. Section 2283 therefore does not permit this blanket evidentiary restraint solely because proof overlaps. A supported injunction against an actual released claim or inconsistent determination remains available; the subject and use of the proposed proof must be connected to that protected adjudication, rather than identified by association alone.
- **P3 — Aid of jurisdiction.** Ongoing administration of a complex settlement is relevant but does not establish that the retained state claims or their overlapping proof seriously impair the federal court's ability to administer or dispose of its case. No such necessity sufficient for this order's breadth is established by the supplied reasons. Retained jurisdiction, settlement size and a general concern about future claims do not replace the statutory showing. The All-Writs Act does not remove section 2283's independent restriction.
- **P4 — Disposition and remedy.** Reverse the Third Circuit insofar as it sustains the categorical restriction and remand to remove that restriction. The settlement and release remain operative; any further request for protection must identify the actual released claim or issue, its lawful preclusive scope, the conduct requiring restraint and the applicable section 2283 exception. The Florida court applies its own reopening and evidentiary rules. The remand grants neither automatic admission of a particular item nor automatic recovery on any retained count.

The proposed rule is a statutory and release-specific answer to the order under review, not a categorical rule that settlement obligations can never affect evidence. The express exclusions and the distinction between adjudicated claims and evidence perform the necessary legal work. The Lowes' independent constitutional notice and vagueness objections need not be resolved to remove the present restraint on these sufficient statutory grounds. The Court does not use those objections to reopen certification or the settlement's completed approval.

### Adopted operative components: exact frozen copy

### G1. Competing methods and the selected rule

**Seven-Associate commitment:** When section 406(b)'s favorable-judgment and court-representation predicates are satisfied, the court begins with the lawful contingent-fee agreement and independently determines whether the requested fee is reasonable for the actual court representation, within the statutory ceiling. The agreement does not carry a presumption that the requested percentage is reasonable, and the attorney seeking payment bears the burden of establishing a reasonable award. A court may not replace the agreed arrangement at the outset with an exclusive hours-times-rate measure merely because that measure is usual in adversary fee-shifting cases.

Section 406(b) governs compensation paid out of the successful claimant's benefits under an existing representation arrangement. Its reasonable-fee requirement supervises that arrangement; it does not prescribe an hourly formula. The 25 percent limit fixes the outer boundary without fixing the award. Dague's categorical rule remains intact under its two environmental fee provisions, and Blanchard's separation of the private bargain from an adversary's statutory obligation remains intact. Neither already settles this distinct claimant-funded method. The present inference from those limits and section 406(b) is new to this question, not an earlier announced seven-Justice rule.

**Strongest objection and response:** The Commissioner emphasizes the same reasonable-fee language, judicial responsibility and protection of disability benefits. A percentage can increase because of a claimant's earnings, dependents or accumulated benefits, without a corresponding increase in legal work. Those concerns defeat automatic contract enforcement and a presumption for the maximum. They do not require treating every contingent return above an hourly figure as unreasonable; such a rule would deny the contingent arrangement a role without statutory language selecting that result. G2 supplies the necessary individual review, rather than assuming that all contingent contracts or all requested fees are sound.

**Scalia's competing commitment:** The court should determine a reasonable fee through reasonable hours multiplied by a reasonable market rate, subject to the statutory maximum and ordinary substantiation and nonduplication requirements. It should not add a multiplier for the risk of nonpayment. Dague's reasoning supports that method because a judicial reasonable-fee award need not reproduce all private payment economics. The source of payment changes who bears the fee, not the court's duty to value compensable services. The percentage cap retains work as an additional ceiling and supplies no statutory direction to begin at a contract percentage.

Scalia recognizes that Dague reserved different compensation schemes. His present extension rests on section 406(b)'s own judicial-determination language and claimant-protection operation, not on a claim that Dague's holding automatically governs. He answers the contract-first path by finding its protection against routine maximum awards less definite than an objective lodestar with the enacted ceiling. He would reject an EAJA-rate substitution: a reasonable market rate under section 406(b) and an EAJA limitation are different inquiries.

**Other available narrower method:** A rule treating the contract merely as evidence within an otherwise controlling lodestar would not correct the seven Associates' identified error: it would still substitute hourly valuation for review of this claimant-funded bargain. Conversely, presumptive enforcement of 25 percent would not preserve the attorney's burden or the statutory reasonableness decision. None of the eight adopts that presumptive-maximum alternative. O'Connor's earlier Dague market position supplies no mandatory multiplier here, and Stevens's join in the other Dague dissent supplies no automatic amount.

### G2. Independent review, proof and lawful reductions

All eight agree that counsel must substantiate the requested statutory fee and that the court must make an independent, explained reasonableness determination. Neither a signed agreement, a favorable judgment, the customary use of a percentage nor compliance with the numerical ceiling proves reasonableness by itself. The seven agreement-method Associates adopt the following implementation; Scalia does not join it as the governing method.

The court evaluates the actual services and results under the agreement. Demonstrated substandard representation, attorney-caused delay that inflated the benefit accumulation, or compensation disproportionate to the representation may justify reducing the request. These are grounds of statutory reasonableness, not findings about either lawyer here and not a mandatory numerical formula. The court may require and consider time records and rate information as an aid; it must explain why the actual request is or is not reasonable in this setting. A high effective hourly rate alone does not establish unreasonableness, and no preselected multiplier or universal hourly limit follows.

The ordinary contingent bargain may account for the possibility of no payment, but the decision creates no separate right to a risk premium or formula reimbursing counsel for unrelated unsuccessful representations. Nor does the lawyer satisfy the burden merely by asserting that all Social Security litigation is risky. The source's statement that no individual matter was argued unusually risky is preserved without treating it as a concession that no reasonable contingent compensation can exceed lodestar.

The court must address each reviewed representation separately. This commitment fixes no fee, permissible multiplier, market rate, delay finding, professional deficiency or universal list of required factors. It neither adopts all of the lower court's Kerr factors nor overrules an unpresented compensation rule. Buckhannon's distinct eligibility doctrine does not erase section 406(b)'s express favorable-judgment requirement; its separated reasonable-amount inquiry supplies an analogy at that scope only.

### G3. Statutory conditions and separate EAJA operation

All eight retain the following statutory boundaries, independently of the method disagreement:

- The court must have rendered the required favorable judgment to a claimant represented before it by an attorney. The permitted fee is for that court representation and cannot exceed 25 percent of the statutorily defined total past-due benefits due by reason of the judgment. A contract cannot authorize an award above that ceiling or substitute for the court's allowance.
- Certification is permitted out of the benefits, not as an additional government fee, and remains subject to any applicable subsection (d). Section 406(b)'s restriction on other payable or certified fees for the same court representation remains subject to the specific EAJA saving provision. Section 406(a)'s administrative-representation procedures and dollar limits are not imported into this method, and no aggregate administrative-and-court cap question is decided.
- Where subparagraph (b)(1)(B) applies under its favorable-judgment transition, continued benefits described in section 423(g) or (h) are excluded from the base; the benefit amount is determined before the applicable section 1320a-6(a) reduction. The 1994 addition applies to favorable judgments made after 180 days after August 15, 1994. No representation-specific date or benefit input is invented.
- The 1999 assessment amendment applies when a fee is required to be certified after January 31, 2000. It does not turn on the contract date or the date of services. Applicable subsection (d) assesses the attorney; collection may occur by offset, and the attorney may not obtain direct or indirect claimant reimbursement. Its stated pre-2001 percentage is 6.3 percent; after 2000 the Commissioner determines the cost-recovery rate, subject to the 6.3 percent maximum. The appropriations condition on collection and availability remains. This decision determines neither an applicable rate nor a deduction, certification date or individual assessment.
- Section 406(b)(2)'s restriction on charging, demanding, receiving or collecting more than the court permits remains; no violation or criminal liability is found here. Its stated maximum punishment is a $500 fine, one year of imprisonment, or both. The method dispute itself establishes no misconduct.
- Public Law 99-80 permits a separate award of fees and other expenses under EAJA despite the Social Security provision. When an attorney receives fees under both provisions for the same work, the attorney must refund the smaller fee to the claimant to satisfy the saving provision's condition. The comparison is between overlapping fees, not every expense. Receipt for the same work triggers that requirement; the existence of an EAJA award alone does not establish what has been paid or fix the section 406(b) fee.
- EAJA is government-paid and retains its separate eligibility, timing, substantial-justification and other requirements. It does not establish the section 406(b) amount or import its rate limitations into this claimant-funded fee. The 1985 amendment supplies the saving/refund rule, not a complete current EAJA codification.

Schaefer and Forney retain their separate remand, finality, fee-eligibility and appeal functions. Martin's service-date rule for its particular PLRA ceiling does not replace section 406's express favorable-judgment or certification transitions. Mathias's discretionary dismissal supplies no bar to review of actual unawarded fees merely because the claimants obtained benefits.

### Question, components and distinct paths

The Third Circuit reversed the preliminary injunction's section 1983 basis. The residents originally pleaded both intentional discrimination under section 601 and a direct effects theory under section 602; the later amendment added section 1983. The original theories were not shown to have been abandoned. The injunction was suspended on June 15, 2001. The present question includes both the asserted section 1983 right and the separate prospective Title VI route.

- **S1 — Section 1983, current-law path.** Powell's separate holding rejects the asserted broader individual effects right under section 1983. Section 601's intentional-discrimination right and section 602's agency instructions do not themselves create that additional individual right; regulatory elaboration cannot supply one that the statute lacks. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas adopt this ground and would retain the rejection of the section 1983 basis.
- **S1-A — Section 1983, supported reconsideration path.** Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer retain their published Powell position: the identified beneficiaries, statutory nondiscrimination protection, express implementation authority and a valid, sufficiently definite implementing requirement can together define the individual entitlement, with section 1983 supplying the enacted remedy. They would reject the categorical section 1983 bar and reconsider Powell to that extent. They do not treat every regulation or program-administration command as a right. This is their proposed legal change, not entering law or a controlling proposition of this handoff.
- **S2 — Direct prospective Title VI route.** Sandoval independently authorizes beneficiaries to obtain prospective compliance with valid, sufficiently definite section 602 implementing requirements from the proper covered defendant. The retained direct claim must receive that treatment. Section 7.35(b)'s criteria-or-methods rule requires the actual prohibited effect, including its alternative concerning defeat or substantial impairment of program objectives for protected persons. Section 7.35(c)'s site-selection provision has its own purpose-or-effect terms and is not automatically a pleaded, proved claim merely because it is in the copied regulation. Coverage, the particular requirement's validity and definiteness, causation and fitted relief remain actual adjudicative questions.
- **S3 — Jurisdiction, defendant and the distinct intentional claim.** The asserted substantial federal controversy is distinct from eventual entitlement. Under Verizon Maryland and Young, an actually connected officer may face prospective relief for an alleged continuing federal violation within the enacted remedy's limits; Commissioner Shinn is named, but his required connection and the relief's proper scope must be applied to this record. The remand does not resolve NJDEP's direct amenability by assuming consent. The original section 601 intentional-discrimination claim retains its separate intent requirement and actual procedural footing.
- **S4 — Disposition and remedy.** Vacate the appellate judgment and remand for consideration of the retained direct prospective claim under S2, the distinct intentional claim and the ordinary defendant and equitable inquiries under S3. The eight Associates support this disposition, but divide on S1 versus S1-A as specified below. Vacatur itself does not revive the previously suspended preliminary injunction; operative preliminary relief must rest on a valid route and the requisite findings. The existing findings retain their actual status for ordinary review; the Court requires no new hearing or wholesale reopening merely because the source excerpts do not reproduce every finding.

The S1 division is real. The common direct-action remand does not turn the four proposed section 1983 supporters into adopters of the contrary rule. Nor does agreement to apply Sandoval turn its four former dissenters into adopters of the rejected regulatory-right theory under section 1983.
