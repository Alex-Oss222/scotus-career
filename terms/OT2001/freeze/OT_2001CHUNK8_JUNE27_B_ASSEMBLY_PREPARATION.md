### Proposed holding I: The complete release showing governs every obligation proposed for termination

**Holding and operative rule:** A school district seeking release from an adjudicated desegregation obligation must establish full and satisfactory compliance, elimination of the relevant vestiges to the extent practicable, and demonstrated good faith toward the whole decree. A court may release a completed domain while retaining supervision elsewhere only after separately assessing whether continued control in that domain is necessary or practicable to achieve compliance elsewhere and whether release would obstruct the remaining remedy; satisfaction of the prerequisites permits, rather than compels, partial withdrawal.

**Authority:** Part I of the signed Opinion of the Court; all nine Justices directly join the rule, its qualifications and its application. Authorship remains unassigned.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that release turns on completion of the constitutional remedy. Freeman permits domain-specific withdrawal but requires the district's full showing, attention to whole-decree conduct and an examination of remedial connections. Jenkins applies that rule and distinguishes installing or funding programs from eliminating the injury those programs address. Dowell requires restoration of local control when the required work is complete; it does not substitute age or expense for completion.

Rockford's demonstrated progress in school assignments is substantial evidence for the domains it addresses. It does not alone determine whether retained educational measures still repair consequences of the adjudicated violation. Conversely, a disparity does not itself establish an unfinished constitutional duty. National achievement parity, maximum educational potential and every desirable program goal are not conditions of release. Courts must identify the remaining violation-linked work rather than supervise schools for general improvement.

Good faith is shown by conduct toward the whole decree, not compulsory enthusiasm. Elected officials may criticize the decree; criticism alone neither establishes noncompliance nor excuses it. Completed work and unfinished duties must be evaluated together where their operation connects. The court must decide whether control over a completed domain remains necessary or practicable for compliance elsewhere, assess nonobstruction independently, and define released duties and retained supervision. Rufo's modification rule does not replace this completion inquiry or add a changed-circumstances condition to release from fulfilled obligations.

### Proposed holding II: The district bears the causal showing, with ordinary protection for settled findings

**Holding and operative rule:** The district must show that remaining imbalance or educational conditions for which it disclaims remedial responsibility are not traceable to its adjudicated violation; the students need not establish that violation anew or supply a particular comparative study to preserve the existing burden. Existing findings receive clear-error review, but findings about one domain do not decide unexamined causal and remedial connections in another.

**Authority:** Part II; all nine Justices directly join the causal allocation and review standard.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the request for release carries the causal burden established in Freeman and reaffirmed in Jenkins. Genuinely independent causes do not impose perpetual racial balancing or make the district responsible for every social inequality. But the appellate demand that the plaintiffs partition all remaining achievement differences cannot replace the district's showing concerning the consequences of its own adjudicated discrimination.

The lower court must weigh the actual record. Testimony, records, program operation and sound inferences may establish the relevant causes; a new statistical comparison is neither invariably necessary nor invariably sufficient. Substantial assignment integration and open advanced-course enrollment deserve their supported weight, without automatically resolving every retained educational obligation. Neither general social causes nor a reported absence of newly identified decree violations alone completes the showing.

Freeman protects properly settled findings against appellate replacement absent clear error. It also requires resolution of causal and remedial questions those findings did not answer. The remand applies both requirements. It is not a general retrial of the 1994 liability determination or an instruction to substitute new appellate factual conclusions for supported findings.

On remand the lower courts must apply Parts I and II, identify any supported full or partial release and define the obligations and supervision that remain justified.

**Authority:** Part III; all nine Justices join the disposition and remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the termination command rests on an incomplete legal inquiry. Neither duration and expense nor successful assignment integration permits the district's burden to be replaced by a demand for new proof from the students. The displaced June 30 direction supplies no dissolution of the existing decree. The lower courts must make a timely, supported determination of what constitutional work remains and end supervision where that work is complete. The remand imposes no automatic additional supervision period and presumes neither that every retained measure remains necessary nor that every obligation has been fulfilled.

**Material precedent treatment.** Freeman is applied in full, including permissive partial withdrawal, the district's causal burden, whole-decree good faith, retained-control necessity or practicability, nonobstruction and clear-error review. Jenkins is applied to distinguish completed repair from spending and program installation. Green and Swann preserve the affirmative repair duty and effective, violation-linked equitable means. ### Proposed holding I: The actual announce clause fails strict scrutiny

**Holding and operative rule:** Minnesota's content-based prohibition of judicial candidates' views on disputed legal or political issues violates the First Amendment because the State has not shown that its actual restriction is narrowly tailored to a compelling interest in impartial adjudication or its appearance. A legal view does not, without more, establish litigant bias, a refusal to consider law and evidence or a promise of a particular result; the clause remains too broad in the speech it reaches and insufficiently fitted in the comparable speech it leaves unrestricted.

**Authority:** Part I of the signed Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; five direct joins in the complete sufficient rule. Authorship remains unassigned.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that judicial office creates no exemption from demanding review of a direct content-based restriction on candidate expression. Suster rejected such an exemption for campaign expenditures while independently examining impartiality. Kruse rejected justifying a direct restraint by the speech left available. Eu supplies the demanding protection for political expression. This different restriction requires its own strict-scrutiny inquiry.

The State's three meanings of impartiality must remain distinct. Absence of bias for or against a litigant is a compelling concern, but the clause regulates broad legal views rather than targeting party bias. Some prohibited statements can disclose bias against a class of litigants; that possibility does not make the whole prohibition narrowly tailored. An absence of all legal preconceptions is not a compelling interest: knowledgeable judges necessarily have views, and those views do not invariably disable fair judgment. Laird preserves that distinction.

Even assuming willingness to reconsider prior views and its appearance are compelling interests, the clause fails fit. Its campaign-focused coverage leaves analogous pre-candidacy statements and permitted judicial teaching, writing and speaking outside the prohibition. Minnesota has not established that nonpromissory campaign views uniquely produce closed-minded adjudication. Seeking electoral support can create pressure, as the dissent argues, but that concern does not establish that every prohibited view functions as a binding commitment. Voters have a substantial interest in knowing candidates' views, while the judge's duty remains responsiveness to governing law and the particular case.

The adopted construction permits some general discussion yet still bars current positions without a promise. The Court does not resolve that breadth by inventing a narrower state canon. Liteky concerns actual federal recusal under its objective appearance standard, not a categorical ban on campaign views. Distinct disqualification and conduct rules retain their own inquiry. Judicial and legislative elections need not be regulated identically, and no candidate is required to announce a view.

### Proposed holding II: Prospective relief addresses this clause alone

**Holding and operative rule:** The judgment sustaining the announce clause is reversed, and the case is remanded for declaratory and appropriate prospective injunctive relief protecting the plaintiffs' discussion of disputed legal and political issues against enforcement of that clause. The distinct pledges-or-promises provision and other unreviewed ethics restrictions are not adjudicated by this judgment.

**Authority:** Part II; the same five Justices join the disposition and the scope of prospective relief.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the continuing restraint on the plaintiffs' intended speech presents the prospective controversy; completed discipline is unnecessary. The constitutional defect defeats the enforcing officials' summary judgment. The lower court must provide relief against this clause consistent with Part I, without drafting a replacement canon. Separate promises, campaign-finance, partisan and recusal rules lie outside the question decided. Whether every formulation of open-mindedness is compelling also need not be decided, because the actual restriction fails tailoring even on that assumption.

**Material precedent treatment.** Suster's rejection of a general judicial-office speech exemption is extended from expenditures to this direct content restriction, without enlarging its own preliminary remedy. Kruse's insistence that remaining expression does not independently justify a restraint is applied, while distinct ethics rules retain separate review. Eu supplies strict review of direct political-expression restraints. Laird supplies the distinction between prior legal views and disqualifying bias. Liteky's informed-observer federal recusal rule remains independent and does not establish the validity of a campaign ban. Watchtower's licensing holding, BE&K's petitioning construction and Trans Union's confined commercial-data holding remain at their own scopes. No precedent is overruled.

### Proposed holding I: Restricted tuition payments remain subject to the financed-function rule

**Holding and operative rule:** Ohio may not finance demonstrated integrated devotional instruction through a restricted tuition payment redeemed by a provider merely because eligibility is neutral and a parent chooses and endorses the payment. Where devotional instruction is integrated into the financed work without reliable separation, parental routing and other educational options do not cure that purchase; genuine private benefits and independently controlled, reliably separated secular services retain their own protection.

**Authority:** Part I; Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer directly join this rule and its application. Authorship remains unassigned.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the challenged act is the public purchase made by restricted provider-redeemed tuition. Agostini retains Lemon as a unified inquiry into governmental authority and function: a specific prohibition or established permissible relationship controls when applicable; otherwise ordinary proof resolves attribution and function. Jackson addresses financed integrated devotional instruction, and Strout applies that rule to ordinary capped tuition. Neither neutral criteria nor the form of parental endorsement separates the instructional function bought by the payment.

The State's educational purpose is legitimate, and parents remain free to choose religious education. Their range of options can bear on genuine independence and coercion. The strongest contrary argument is that independently selected schools deliver an individual benefit to the child rather than governmental instruction. But this restricted payment purchases the integrated service identified in the record. Parental direction alone does not answer that distinct financing prohibition. Strout already rejects treating the cap on ordinary tuition as sufficient separation. Sustaining this purchase on the asserted choice ground would require reconsidering those holdings; the Court applies them.

Mueller and Witters retain their actual tax and individual vocational-benefit relationships. Rosenberger preserves its independently edited publication and controlled printing benefit, whose combined safeguards do not equate every expenditure with a speech forum. Mitchell likewise preserves genuine individual benefits while requiring correction of actual financed religious use. The Court does not attribute all activity of a religious school to government or infer the purchased function from the school's identity. Reliably separated secular work remains subject to its own supported analysis. Zobrest supplied no Establishment Clause merits holding, and cannot provide the claimed continuous line authorizing this tuition purchase.

### Proposed holding II: Actual funded function determines the supported scope

**Holding and operative rule:** The funded religious function must be established at the provider/payment scope necessary for relief; religious affiliation, participation percentages, possible diversion, fungibility or incidental institutional savings do not alone prove it. Existing record descriptions of integrated teaching must be used where they establish the purchased function, without demanding reproof of an established use or presuming that every participating provider operates identically.

**Authority:** Part II; the same six Justices directly join the complete proof rule and its record application. The three dissenters' agreement with the insufficiency of affiliation alone does not add them to this part.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that evidence of what tuition purchases does legal work that enrollment percentages cannot. Mitchell distinguishes demonstrated funded religious use from assumptions based on a recipient's character or the possibility of diversion. The present record includes descriptions of integrated instruction relevant to that inquiry. Those descriptions must receive their actual evidentiary force; they are not reduced to mere risk because the program includes other providers.

Conversely, high religious participation does not itself establish every provider's curriculum, the absence of reliable separation or the unlawfulness of every payment. The disagreement over available secular options need not be resolved through a new finding that all alternatives were inadequate or had unlimited capacity. Under Jackson and Strout, improving options alone would not cure a demonstrated integrated devotional purchase. Actual choice remains relevant to a genuinely different protected benefit, but cannot replace examination of the financed work here.

The Court requires neither separate corporate governance nor wholly distinct personnel as a categorical condition. Operational separation and reliable accountability must address the actual function. Whether a particular remaining provider or payment satisfies the governing rule is ordinary record application on remand, not a reservation of the governing constitutional standard.

### Proposed holding III: Effective relief must preserve lawful severable applications

**Holding and operative rule:** Relief must promptly prevent the demonstrated public purchase of integrated devotional instruction at the supported provider/payment scope while preserving lawful severable secular, tutorial and public-school applications under Ohio severability law. The lower courts may use the existing record where sufficient and must identify any material additional issue; uncertainty about unexamined applications neither authorizes continuation of a proved violation nor justifies categorical exclusion of every religious provider.

**Authority:** Part III; the same six Justices join the complete corrective-remedy standard. Their formal treatment of the established-use portion of the lower judgment differs as stated in Part IV and the judgment table.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that correction must be both effective and confined to the established conflict. Jackson requires provider/payment relief and state-law severability, and Mitchell forbids reducing a proved religious use to a hypothetical risk or requiring the parties to prove it again. Those decisions also preserve independently lawful applications. Dalton and Leavitt preserve the distinction between the federal conflict and the state-law consequences of severing it.

The blanket program disposition does not perform that work. The lower courts must use sufficient existing evidence to prevent established unlawful purchases and determine the consequences for affected payments. They must preserve lawful applications where supported and severable rather than treat religious identity as an exclusion rule. An unexamined provider does not excuse continuation of a demonstrated violation elsewhere. Conversely, a demonstrated use does not itself establish programwide prevalence or inseverability.

The remand resolves those application and severability questions without an automatic new trial or an indefinite transition. It does not judicially redesign Ohio's program. Any broader consequence must follow from supported dependence and Ohio law, not administrative convenience or a newly assumed constitutional entitlement to funding.

The majority therefore preserves the established invalidity and returns the remaining remedial scope for the precise inquiry required by the controlling expenditure precedents.
