## Electrical Fittings: decree correction despite the nonpreclusion view

The District Court dismissed the patent infringement claim in the defendants' favor but also included an express adjudication that patent claim 1 was valid in its decree. The defendants appealed that adverse validity adjudication. The court of appeals dismissed their appeal after concluding that they had obtained all the relief to which they were entitled and that the validity adjudication would not bind them in later litigation.

The Supreme Court distinguished an appeal merely to correct unnecessary findings from an appeal to eliminate an unnecessary adverse adjudication embedded in the decree itself. It permitted the latter. Its direction was to entertain the appeal and reform the decree, not to decide the patent's validity on the merits. The lower court's nonpreclusion conclusion therefore did not eliminate appellate authority to remove the adverse decree component. The short opinion creates no requirement that another suit already exist or that future preclusion be certain.

## Roper: the continuing stake and limited appellate function

Roper, 445 U.S. at 333–337 and notes 6–7, distinguishes the ordinary practice rule against a prevailing party's appeal from the independent constitutional requirement of a personal stake. Its class-certification holding rests on the named plaintiffs' asserted continuing economic interest in spreading litigation fees and expenses, notwithstanding the unaccepted tender and judgment entered over their objection. The Court limits the permitted appeal to the collateral certification ruling, rather than authorizing appellate resolution of the substantive damages merits. The unaccepted tender must not be described as an agreed settlement or payment and satisfaction of a final unappealable judgment. Procedural entitlement and favorable policy do not dispense with Article III.

Roper's account of Electrical Fittings is particularly important. It expressly notes that the Supreme Court did not question the lower appellate view that patent validity would not influence later litigation through collateral estoppel or otherwise, yet permitted reformation. It then identifies the defendants' concern about impairment of success in unspecified future litigation through precedent or collateral estoppel as the personal stake satisfied by eliminating the adverse adjudication from the decree. Note 7 identifies the procedural error as adjudging validity after finding no infringement; it preserves an adverse decision on a litigated issue and a continuing asserted interest, with policy supporting that confined procedural appeal.

These passages do not support requiring certain issue preclusion, a pending future suit, monetary liability or an immediately enforceable command in every collateral appeal. Nor can the distinction be reduced to a categorical claim that precedential consequences never count. The concern recognized in Roper's Electrical Fittings discussion was tied to an adverse adjudication actually left in a decree and was redressed by removing it. That is different from automatically allowing every prevailing party to appeal unfavorable reasoning because it might influence another court. Whether a particular denied motion or collateral adjudication creates the required continuing stake still requires analysis of that ruling and the relief an appeal could provide.

Stevens joins Roper's Court opinion and additionally treats absent members of a properly maintainable class as sufficiently present for the limited controversy inquiry; his concurrence also agrees that the named plaintiffs have a sufficient personal stake. His alternative class-party explanation neither establishes commissioners' injury nor dispenses generally with adversity. Blackmun's judgment concurrence and the Powell–Stewart dissent remain separate, noncontrolling positions at their stated scopes.
