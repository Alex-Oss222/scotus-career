## Validated record and statutory distinctions

The official Fourth Circuit opinion, 264 F.3d 391, was downloaded and read in full, including its September 6 publication order and September 19 citation correction. Review concerns only Gormley’s postconviction request for $80,000 from his IRA for appellate counsel. The government had not moved to amend the forfeiture judgment to forfeit substitute assets. The challenged order continues a section 853(e) restraint; it is not an IRA forfeiture order. The jury’s $1.2 million forfeiture verdict, indictment notice of substitute assets and probable cause concerning unavailable laundered funds are lower-court premises, not proof that every statutory substitute predicate has been adjudicated. Unrelated convictions, sentence calculations, restitution and excessiveness are outside this review.

Section 853(e) expressly concerns property described in subsection (a); section (p) separately directs forfeiture of other defendant property when the defendant’s act or omission causes one of its enumerated unavailability conditions, only up to the qualifying value. Subsection (g) operates after entry of an order declaring property forfeited and protects the property ordered forfeited. A conviction or money judgment is not itself an unstated order forfeiting the IRA. Section (o)’s liberal-construction direction and the anti-dissipation purpose support the government's broader construction but do not erase these textual distinctions. Georgia sections 18-4-22 and 18-4-20 address garnishment: the account is exempt before payment and paid benefits ordinarily retain protection except the garnishable quarter, subject to any greater exemption. These provisions cannot override authorized federal forfeiture; conversely, federal supremacy supplies no omitted federal restraint power.

The section 982(b)(2) intermediary limitation applies when the defendant merely handled but did not retain the laundered property, except when the offense conduct involved three or more separate transactions totaling at least $100,000 in twelve months. Gormley did not raise that theory in the district court. The appellate court reserved it for findings if the government later seeks substitute forfeiture. No facts satisfying it are inferred.

## Lawful paths and own Justice authorities

A statutory reversal can remove the section 853(e) premise and remand the refusal to release funds for lawful further proceedings without creating an appellate counsel-of-choice entitlement. Affirmance requires reading the preservation power to include substitute property despite the subsection (a) reference, as the court below did by analogy to Billman’s RICO rule. The strongest contrary arguments are the actual cross-reference on the narrow reading and the combination of mandatory substitution, anti-dissipation purpose and liberal construction on the broader reading. Any independent postconviction power requires its own source and predicates; none may be invented as an alternate finding.

**Criminal forfeiture, informed waiver and statutory property limits**, present operation effective Libretti, 1995-11-07, is the exact applicable Standards title. O’Connor’s Part I-B was joined by all eight other Justices: consent cannot enlarge the statutory power. Stevens’s separate Libretti position particularly requires statutory assurance when a concrete breadth issue appears. O’Connor, Scalia and Kennedy joined Monsanto and Caplin & Drysdale’s pre-divergence majority refusing a counsel-fee exemption for tainted forfeitable property; Stevens joined the contrary constitutional dissent. The legal distinction here is untainted substitute property and the source of current restraint, not the rejected exception for proceeds. Souter and Ginsburg’s Libretti concurrences, and Thomas and Breyer’s controlling Libretti joins, provide their own direct statutory-boundary positions without importing a separate counsel doctrine. All eight Associates joined the same no-enlargement proposition, but no vote follows automatically from it.
