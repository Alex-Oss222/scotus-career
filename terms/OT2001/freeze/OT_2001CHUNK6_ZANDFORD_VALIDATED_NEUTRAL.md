# SEC v. Zandford — validated neutral preparation

**Event:** June 3, 2002; argument March 18, 2002. These are scheduling fields, not an imported disposition.
**Research cutoff:** June 3, 2002. Only predecision filings, the judgment below, and effective public law inform this packet.
**Function:** Fresh neutral framing and source validation. This packet supplies no commitment, assignment, proposed coalition, or eventual disposition.

## 1. Scope, posture, and components

The issue is whether the alleged fraudulent liquidation of entrusted securities and diversion of the proceeds satisfies the connection required by §10(b) and Rule 10b-5. It is not whether Zandford committed wire fraud, whether every broker conversion violates the securities laws, or whether the conviction established every element of civil liability.

The Fourth Circuit, No. 99-1733, decided January 26, 2001, reversed the district court's summary judgment and directed dismissal. Its opinion is reproduced at petition appendix 1a–14a. The district court had enjoined future violations and ordered $343,000 disgorgement after treating the wire-fraud convictions as sufficient to establish the civil claim. The appellate court separately held that wire fraud did not necessarily adjudicate the legal securities-connection issue. The SEC does not ask this Court to prevent Zandford from disputing that legal conclusion.

The SEC seeks reversal of the categorical connection ruling. Its complaint also invoked Securities Act §17(a), but its merits brief expressly excludes the sufficiency of that claim from the requested review (p.4 n.1). The district court's final-judgment treatment followed an originally partial motion. Zandford's brief reports that excessive-trading allegations were not the basis of the motion and that the SEC had disclaimed further pursuit of them (p.4 n.3). Neither an unreviewed §17(a) holding nor renewed excessive-trading litigation should be silently supplied.

The contested legal issues are: (a) the required relationship between deception and sale; (b) the proper weight, if needed, of the SEC's statutory and regulatory interpretations; and (c) the remedy following the selected connection rule, preserving the distinction between sufficient allegations and established facts. These are not separate private-damages claims.

## 2. Supported record and its limits

William Wood and his daughter entrusted assets to Zandford in an account managed with trading discretion. The SEC alleges that Zandford promised conservative management but conducted sales to obtain proceeds for himself without disclosing that purpose. The complaint specifies checks against mutual-fund positions and other securities sales followed by diversion. The lower opinion describes thirteen wire-fraud convictions, one involving liquidation of mutual-fund shares and twelve involving checks. Those convictions establish neither a §10(b) conviction nor an automatic legal conclusion on this statutory nexus.

Trading discretion meant that particular purchases or sales did not require advance customer approval. The dispute is whether that discretion covered sales undertaken for concealed personal conversion, and whether its abuse is itself sufficiently transactional deception. Lack of transaction-by-transaction notice is therefore not independently treated as unlawful. Nor is the absence of a false market-price statement treated as absence of every possible deception.

Zandford contends that the SEC has not proved the sales themselves wrongful, that he possessed control over assets before liquidation, and that conversion was independent of routine market sales. The SEC contends that selling entrusted assets to generate the property to be stolen was an integral execution of a concealed scheme. These are competing characterizations of the pleaded scheme and the civil record, not two established descriptions to be combined into a finding. The broader investment account and the mutual-fund allegations must not be merged into a new transaction finding. The legal question can be resolved without deciding the exact scope of fact preclusion or reconstructing every trade.

## 3. Entering law and exact-text handoffs

Read the relevant selections in [A standards](../entering-law/OT_2001CHUNK6_A.md), [A opening holdings](../entering-law/OT_2001CHUNK6_A_AUTHORITIES.md), and [A current public law](../entering-law/OT_2001CHUNK6_A_CURRENT.md). The operative baseline includes effective decisions through May 28, 2002. The supplied runtime assertion that no OT2001 decisions exist is stale and is not adopted. An unrelated same-day decision is not entering law.

O'Hagan's June 25, 1997 controlling rule requires a duty, scienter, concealed disloyal use and the securities connection; it does not require the information source to be the trading counterparty. Its rule addresses the consummation of source deception by securities use. Whether this disposition of entrusted securities rather than information satisfies that principle must be reasoned, not presumed. Its separate criminal and tender-offer provisions do not supply this civil cause.

Central Bank's April 19, 1994 received private affirmative-assistance action remains in force in this timeline. Historical briefing cannot import a categorical rejection of that action. The present claim alleges Zandford's primary conduct in an SEC action; Central Bank's private accessory reliance, causation and severe-recklessness predicates are not automatically the elements of this claim.

Mead's June 18, 2001 controlling rule distinguishes an interpretation issued in exercise of delegated force-of-law authority from persuasive agency reasoning under Skidmore. Formal adjudication is relevant evidence of delegation, not a conclusive label. The SEC relies on §10(b)'s rulemaking grant, its broker-discipline adjudication authority and early administrative decisions including Kenneth Leo Bauer, 26 S.E.C. 770 (1947), and Southeastern Securities Corp., 29 S.E.C. 609 (1949). Zandford contests the interpretive content and relevance of the decisions relied upon. The SEC separately invokes deference to its own rule; that question must retain its own current-law boundary rather than being silently folded into Mead.

Bankers Life, 404 U.S. 6 (1971), is surviving authority concerning a securities sale whose proceeds were diverted; fair value received in the sale did not by itself dispose of the federal fraud question. Santa Fe, 430 U.S. 462 (1977), requires manipulation or deception rather than fiduciary unfairness alone. Chiarella, 445 U.S. 222 (1980), makes a relevant duty necessary to an omission theory. Naftalin, 441 U.S. 768 (1979), concerns §17(a) and is an analogy, not a substitute disposition of an unreviewed claim. Each party's use of those authorities remains contestable at its actual scope.

The companion source-extract file copies §10(b) and Rule 10b-5 from petition appendix 52a–53a, including the interstate means condition and all three rule clauses. OCR is identified as such; consult the original scan before publishing an exact quotation where recognition is doubtful.

[A public-position supplement](../entering-law/OT_2001CHUNK6_A_PUBLIC_POSITIONS.md) supplies two additional material authorities and their exact public explanations. Wharf (May 21, 2001), Thomas's unanimous opinion, holds that selling an actual option for value with concealed contemporaneous intent never to honor it is transaction-connected deception, while nonperformance alone is insufficient. Its purchased option and contemporaneous intent are essential distinctions, not a finding that these customers purchased a comparable option. Every Associate joined that holding; it is an important common anchor to test against both proposed paths. Thomas Jefferson University (June 24, 1994) retains controlling weight for an administering agency's own-regulation interpretation unless plainly erroneous or inconsistent with the regulation, with actual-ground, text, inconsistency and explanation limits. Kennedy, Scalia and Souter joined the controlling opinion; Thomas, Stevens, O'Connor and Ginsburg rejected the asserted regulatory exclusions on the substantive grounds preserved in the public supplement. Breyer was not then seated. Those grounds caution against turning agency power into an adopted operative rule; they do not predetermine this different SEC regulation.

## 4. Competing supported paths

**Integrated-deception path.** The SEC argues that feigned loyal management and sales made to obtain proceeds for personal use are one fraudulent course of conduct. The deceptive use of trading authority and the liquidation would therefore satisfy the connection requirement even without distortion of market value or a separate investor decision on each sale. Bankers Life and O'Hagan support the claimed relation. The strongest objection is that this formulation may collapse connection into fiduciary status or conversion: it must identify why the actual sales consummate deception, and distinguish later independent theft after an honestly completed authorized sale.

**Insufficient-connection path.** Zandford argues that the pleaded conversion is a completed nonsecurities wrong involving ordinary liquidations, not deception affecting a security or transactional decision. O'Hagan involved monetizing information through trading, whereas money already has independent value. Bankers Life involved inducement of the seller's transaction. The strongest objection is that treating discretionary execution as categorically separate from its concealed purpose could exclude deceptive uses of the very securities authority entrusted by the customer; neither fair price nor absence of individual customer approval is itself the statutory limitation.

**Agency-ground alternatives.** If independent interpretation resolves the issue, a broader deference holding is unnecessary. If agency reasoning is material, the model must separately test (i) actual delegated interpretive authority and the mechanism used; (ii) consistency and persuasive substance; and (iii) any independently applicable own-rule interpretation doctrine. The SEC's invocation does not prove delegation or persuasion, while its litigating role does not erase earlier adjudications.

**Remedy alternatives.** Rejection of the categorical connection barrier can permit further proceedings without reinstating every part of summary judgment or finding $343,000 presently recoverable. Adoption of the appellate connection rule can sustain dismissal of the reviewed §10(b)/Rule 10b-5 claim. Any narrower disposition must state which legal ground it resolves and why any remaining civil proof issue actually remains; no new hearing or unrestricted discovery entitlement follows automatically.
