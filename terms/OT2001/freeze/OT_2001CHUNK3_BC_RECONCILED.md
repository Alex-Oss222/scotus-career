## 39. United States v. Vonn — March 4, 2002

### Review scope and proposed controlling rules

The Court may decide the two review rules on certiorari from the Ninth Circuit. The Rule 11(c)(3) omission is an error despite current representation. The question is its appellate consequence, not whether a judge may omit the required advice. Defense silence is forfeiture rather than a proved intentional waiver. The later motion to withdraw on a different ground does not preserve the advice claim. The prosecutor's attempted correction at the second hearing is part of the actual record; it does not establish that Vonn himself sought relief on this ground or that the omission was corrected.

**Component A — review standard.** Seven Associates would hold that Rule 11(h) does not displace Rule 52(b) for an unpreserved claim. Such a defendant ordinarily bears the substantial-rights burden, and correction remains subject to the distinct discretionary condition. The mandatory trial-counsel warning and the general appellate review rule can both operate. No special reasonable-probability formula, constitutional-plea invalidity rule or collateral-review rule is introduced. Stevens would retain the Government's harmlessness burden under Rule 11(h) for this judge-controlled plea procedure.

**Component B — relevant record.** All eight would reject the colloquy-only limit. A reviewing court may consider relevant portions of the official record bearing on the defendant's knowledge and the omission's effect, including the earlier advisements. Such material does not substitute for the judge's required personal colloquy or become conclusive merely because counsel signed a form. No off-record conversation may be invented.

**Component C — application and remedy.** Seven would vacate the appellate judgment and return it for Rule 52(b) review using the relevant official record. Stevens would also return the matter for whole-record review but under Rule 11(h)'s Government burden. No Justice here commits to automatic reinstatement, immediate withdrawal of every plea, a finding of harmlessness, a quantified prejudice test or resolution of the unresolved perjury-warning contention. The Ninth Circuit must apply the correct standard and address other properly remaining issues.

## 40. Delaney v. DeTella — March 4, 2002

### Components and common lawful ground

**Component A — appellate reach.** All eight accept legal review of immunity on the assumed plaintiff-favorable facts. They would reject any attempt to obtain appellate resolution of disputed injury, awareness, causation or the guards' actual authority. Johnson v. Jones and Behrens distinguish that unavailable factual review from the legal issue properly before the Court. The appeal's factual objections may be dismissed or left outside the decision without dismissing the legal immunity issue.

**Component B — constitutional sufficiency.** Seven would hold that the assumed combination of six months without meaningful exercise, extremely cramped confinement, serious physical and psychological effects, actual awareness of the substantial risk and no reasonable response states a violation under Farmer. Thomas accepts that legal consequence under the controlling, unchallenged conditions framework without joining a broader independent constitutional endorsement of every deprivation characterized as harmful. **Component C — qualified immunity.** All eight would affirm rejection of the legal immunity defense on the proper assumed account. Searles's remedial distinctions remain for any actually presented compensation issue and do not decide this immunity appeal.

## 43. Edelman v. Lynchburg College — March 19, 2002

### Reconciled components and operative limits

**E1 — lawful cure:** All eight Associates uphold 29 CFR section 1601.12(b)'s relation back of verification to an otherwise sufficient charge filed within the applicable section 706(e)(1) period. Section 706(b)'s oath requirement remains mandatory. The regulation cures omitted verification; it neither initiates a new untimely charge nor deems every informal letter a charge. The employer must retain the oath's protection before being obliged to respond to the substance. The 180-day and applicable 300-day filing alternatives are not enlarged.

**E2 — independent statutory explanation:** Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer support the independent interpretation that verification and timely filing are distinct requirements whose performance need not be simultaneous. The statute does not define charge as necessarily verified when first filed. Filing prevents stale claims; verification protects the employer against the expense of answering an unsupported accusation. The regulation gives both commands effect. The six find the construction independently persuasive without deciding every question about the degree of Chevron deference. Thomas joins on the express understanding that section 713(a) delegates procedural rulemaking, this procedural regulation is reasonable and not proscribed by the statute, and it conforms to the Administrative Procedure Act. This is not unrestricted equitable cure of an enacted deadline.

**E3 — alternative delegated-choice explanation:** O'Connor and Scalia support the operative cure and judgment, but withhold the assertion that the regulation supplies the most natural independent construction. The rule is reasonable because it preserves timely initiation and later verified accountability in a lay-initiated process, consistent with established verification practice. ## 44. Zahrey v. Coffey — March 19, 2002

### Components and alternative paths

**Z1: Constitutional injury and attribution.** The affirmative path recognizes a narrow due-process injury when an investigating official deliberately creates materially false evidence for use in legal proceedings and thereby causes an unjustified physical detention. The injury is the resulting deprivation, not preparation of a false statement by itself. The official's later use of his own product in a protected advocacy capacity does not retrospectively immunize the earlier investigative act or necessarily sever its causal connection. This is a completed wrong only when the deliberate material falsehood causes the deprivation.

The strongest objection is that the arrest and detention belong to the Fourth Amendment, while Mooney, Pyle and Napue govern false evidence resulting in conviction. Buckley decides immunity, not a substantive right; Albright does not constitutionalize malicious prosecution. The affirmative answer is limited to intentional corruption of the process used to procure an actual loss of liberty, rather than a new right against prosecution unsupported by probable cause. Albright's fractured judgment leaves independent procedural injury open. Nothing here adjudicates an independently presented Fourth Amendment component or declares its protection to end at indictment.

**Z2: Notice and qualified immunity.** The affirmance path holds that the favorable knowing-fabrication, material-use and detention allegations defeat the asserted categorical immunity defense under 1996 law. Mooney, Pyle and Napue establish that the government cannot obtain a liberty deprivation through its deliberate evidentiary falsehood; Franks and Malley make material official deception and resulting detention constitutionally significant and reject the proposition that judicial process necessarily launders that deception. White v. Frank and Jones v. City of Chicago reinforce the notice. No identical investigator/advocate sequence is indispensable under Anderson. Later Clanton and Fletcher confirm present functional and causal distinctions, but furnish no retrospective notice.

The strongest contrary path grants qualified immunity because the link between investigative fabrication, the same official's absolutely protected presentation and pretrial rather than postconviction confinement was materially unsettled in 1996. Buckley's express merits reservation, Albright's distinct approaches and the adverse Buckley-IV and Rhodes decisions supply more than a missing factual twin. The response cannot be merely that lying is wrongful. It must explain why that source and functional uncertainty did not permit deliberate procurement of detention through known falsehood. The dissenting commitments below find that response insufficient for this pleaded due-process claim.

**Z3: Function and remedy.** On either path, the assumed investigative function remains the appellate concession, not a merits finding about every interview. ## 48. Hoffman Plastic Compounds v. NLRB — March 27, 2002

### Statutory paths and material distinctions

**H1: Limited monetary-remedy path.** Section 10(c) permits this backpay award, ending when the employer discovered the disqualification, as a consequence of the independent unfair labor practice. It orders no reinstatement, legalizes no employment and requires no future document fraud. The employer's verified lack of knowledge matters because section 1324a separately regulates knowing hiring/retention and document verification. ABF and McKennon require a legal connection between the worker's wrongdoing and the remedy, rather than an automatic misconduct forfeiture. Sure-Tan's actual problem involved departed workers, lawful reentry and speculative minimum awards; this supported period and actual mitigation record do not present those predicates.

The strongest objection is that the award compensates work Castro was not lawfully authorized to perform and would have performed only through the continued effect of his unlawful documents. The employer's innocence of an immigration violation does not confer authorization on the worker. The affirmative answer is that Congress specified immigration prohibitions and sanctions without withdrawing this distinct monetary labor remedy; the award addresses the employer's antiunion act, and the discovery cutoff accommodates the point at which continued employment would violate the employer's statutory duty. Deterring retaliation against a vulnerable workforce is a statutory remedial reason, not authority to excuse the worker's immigration violation.

**H2: Statutory-conflict path.** The Board cannot award replacement wages for unperformed work during a period of unauthorized employment obtained through fraudulent verification documents. IRCA changes the pre-1986 employment premise underlying Sure-Tan; section 10(c)'s labor-policy delegation cannot be used without regard to that independent federal scheme. Southern Steamship requires accommodation of the actual prohibition, and Sure-Tan's work-availability principle is not confined to physically absent employees. ABF involved a lie at the remedial hearing rather than legal ineligibility for the work underlying the award. McKennon concerned otherwise lawful employment and an independent sufficient discharge ground, not work authorization imposed by federal law.

The strongest objection is that neither statute expressly prohibits paying damages, Congress retained labor coverage, and denying backpay can weaken both schemes by reducing the cost of antiunion targeting. The contrary answer is limited to this remedy and its unlawful-work predicate: coverage, an established violation and eligibility for every remedy are separate. Section 10(c)'s discretion is bounded even where a particular order would improve labor-law deterrence. The response must not claim that the backpay order literally commands rehiring, that Hoffman knowingly violated IRCA, that Castro committed new fraud during mitigation, or that every monetary labor protection is displaced.

**H3: Existing violation and relief limits.** Every Associate preserves the established unfair-labor-practice finding, the denial of reinstatement and the absence of post-June-14-1993 backpay. H1 supporters affirm enforcement of the existing $66,951 plus interest award. H2 supporters reverse enforcement of that award and remand only for a decree consistent with its elimination, leaving other lawful Board relief intact. Neither path requires a new mitigation or calculation proceeding on an unpreserved defect. Earned wages, another employment statute's remedy and the separate case of an employer knowingly using unauthorized labor are not resolved here.
