### Issue 1: Federal replacement authority and remedial limits

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** Growe v. Emison, February 23, 1993, gives timely state legislative and judicial redistricting priority while permitting federal action when the State will not provide a lawful plan in time. Voinovich v. Quilter, March 2, 1993, distinguishes a State's permissible districting choices from a federal court's authority to compel districts as relief for a proved violation.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** The federal court may remedy unconstitutional malapportionment without acquiring general authority to optimize partisan or racial outcomes. The remedy must comply with applicable population and voting-rights law. Judicial authorship does not eliminate statutory review.
* **Other Governing Law:** Article I, § 2 governs congressional representational equality; 2 U.S.C. § 2c requires single-member congressional districts in the applicable setting. Voting Rights Act § 2, then 42 U.S.C. § 1973, constrains the replacement arrangement. No claim requiring a separate amendment-history adjustment is identified.
* **Material Other Authority:** White v. Weiser, 412 U.S. 783 (1973), and Connor v. Finch, 431 U.S. 407, 414–426 (1977), distinguish judicial remedial responsibility from unrestricted legislative choice and require a legally justified map.
* **Existing Rule's Reach:** A timely lawful state plan receives priority; failure to produce one permits necessary federal relief. That authority does not decide whether each feature of the resulting plan complies with § 2.
* **Open Point:** No unresolved rule of federal replacement authority requires decision. Whether a preserved objection establishes a statutory defect in this plan is an application of the governing law, not a reason to reconsider state priority.

### Issue 2: Electoral capacity, unequal opportunity, and independent grounds

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** Valdespino v. Alamo Heights Independent School District, June 17, 1999, expressly modifies numerical exclusivity in the first Gingles condition for a single protected group. A lawful, geographically compact district may satisfy that condition through a numerical majority or through rigorous proof of dependable crossover support sufficient to elect the group's candidates of choice. Growe retains cohesion, relevant majority-bloc voting, and causation. Johnson v. De Grandy, June 30, 1994, requires independent totality, rejects maximization, and treats proportional opportunity as evidence rather than a conclusive defense.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** Electoral capacity means an actual ability to elect preferred representatives in the proposed lawful district, not merely an increased chance to influence others. Crossover support means support from voters outside the single protected group for that group's demonstrated preferred candidates. Dependability requires support grounded in relevant voting behavior and electoral conditions; it is not established by racial arithmetic, one unusual victory, or a prediction detached from the proposed electorate. A distinct-group coalition claim instead treats two or more protected groups as a combined claimant electorate; its cognizability remains reserved, and adding their populations cannot substitute for common cohesion.
* **Other Governing Law:** Section 2's results standard, enacted in its governing form in 1982, asks whether political processes are equally open under the totality; discriminatory purpose is not an additional statutory element. The statute disclaims a right to representation proportional to group population. Its enacted results inquiry remains distinct from constitutional intentional-discrimination claims.
* **Material Other Authority:** Thornburg v. Gingles, 478 U.S. 30, 50–51 (1986), supplies the causal conditions, subject to Valdespino's express modification. Nomination procedures matter where they are a decisive electoral barrier. Candidate race is not a substitute for voter preference.
* **Existing Rule's Reach:** Both capacity routes require lawful population and geography; compactness asks whether the relevant voters can form a geographically workable district, not whether a numerical target can be reached by any configuration. Political cohesion concerns a sufficiently common electoral preference demonstrated by voting behavior. Relevant majority-bloc voting concerns ordinary defeat of preferred candidates in the challenged arrangement, allowing for special circumstances. Independent totality means the further statutory judgment about unequal opportunity after the necessary conditions are addressed; no automatic liability follows from satisfying those conditions. Local deprivation cannot be excused by opportunity elsewhere.
* **Open Point:** The single-group functional-capacity route is settled. Preservation, support in this record, and the independence of compactness and totality findings present application and review questions. A universal population denominator, mathematical crossover formula, distinct-group entitlement, and influence-only entitlement remain unresolved; they are not necessary merely because the record mentions minority percentages.

#### Voinovich v. Quilter, 507 U.S. 146 (1993)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 91-1618.

**Decided:** March 2, 1993.

##### Section 2 does not make a State prove remedial necessity before choosing majority-minority districts

**Holding:** Section 2 does not prohibit a State from choosing majority-minority districts merely because no federal violation compels their creation; challengers bear at least the initial burden of showing, under the totality of circumstances, unequal electoral opportunity caused by the challenged arrangement. A federal court may order such districts only when their creation is necessary to remedy an established federal-law violation, but that limit does not become a categorical restriction on state redistricting authority; independently applicable federal constitutional and statutory requirements remain enforceable.

**Proposition-level authority:** O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, Part II.B, joined by the other eight Justices. All nine adopt the complete proposition; it directly controls the rejection of the lower court's statutory prohibition and burden allocation.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Thornburg v. Gingles, 478 U.S. 30 (1986): followed for the challengers' burden and the totality-of-circumstances inquiry; no district form is automatically unlawful under Section 2.
- Growe v. Emison, decision of February 23, 1993: applied to distinguish the State's primary districting authority from federal judicial remedial authority; its actual majority holdings remain controlling.

##### The assumed influence-dilution claim fails for lack of the required majority bloc voting

**Holding:** Assuming that Section 2 reaches the asserted loss of minority influence districts, the claim fails because challengers did not establish sufficiently cohesive white majority voting usually to defeat the minority's preferred candidates. The Gingles prerequisites apply with attention to the claim's nature: an influence claim would require modification or elimination of the first prerequisite that the minority could constitute a district majority, but the Court need not select that adaptation or decide whether influence claims are cognizable because the independently necessary bloc-voting showing is absent.

**Proposition-level authority:** O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, Parts II.A and II.C, joined by the other eight Justices. All nine adopt this independently dispositive rejection of the presented Section 2 claim and each stated reservation.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Thornburg v. Gingles, 478 U.S. 30 (1986): applied to the necessity of majority bloc voting and the causal mechanism of dilution; its reservations about influence claims remain unresolved.
- Growe v. Emison, decision of February 23, 1993: followed for application of the threshold framework to single-member district challenges, with its influence-claim reservation preserved rather than silently decided.

##### The particular finding of intentional discrimination is clearly erroneous

**Holding:** Assuming that the Fifteenth Amendment reaches the asserted vote-dilution claim, the finding that Ohio intentionally injured black voters cannot stand on this record: attention to perceived federal-law requirements and possession of an opponent's speculative documents, without evidence of reliance, do not establish the prohibited purpose the District Court inferred. The Court reverses that finding as clearly erroneous without deciding the Amendment's coverage of dilution or its relationship to race-conscious districting generally.

**Proposition-level authority:** O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, Part III, joined by the other eight Justices. All nine adopt this record-specific constitutional disposition; none adopts a general immunity for political objectives or racial awareness.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Beer v. United States, 425 U.S. 130 (1976): followed in leaving unresolved whether the Fifteenth Amendment reaches vote-dilution claims; the assumption made here creates no new coverage holding.
- Mobile v. Bolden, 446 U.S. 55 (1980): its plurality and separate writings are background to the alleged intentional-discrimination claim, not treated as a single majority rationale or a new categorical rule governing every race-conscious map.

##### Population deviations above ten percent require a genuine justification inquiry

**Holding:** A state legislative plan whose maximum total population deviation exceeds ten percent establishes a prima facie case requiring the State to justify the disparity; crossing that threshold does not make preservation of county boundaries legally unavailable as a justification. The District Court must determine whether the actual plan reasonably advances that rational state policy, including its substantiated connection to the deviations and consistency of implementation, and whether the resulting disparities nevertheless exceed constitutional limits; the Court does not decide that Ohio has met its burden.

**Proposition-level authority:** O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, Part IV, joined by the other eight Justices. All nine adopt this standard and the population-only remand; neither the State's success nor a particular remedial map receives a controlling vote.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Brown v. Thomson, 462 U.S. 835 (1983): followed for the prima facie threshold, the required connection to a rational state policy, and the separate constitutional limit; departures below ten percent remain generally minor, not categorically immune.
- Mahan v. Howell, 410 U.S. 315 (1973): followed in allowing a substantiated political-subdivision policy to justify departures above ten percent; it does not automatically validate Ohio's particular departures.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The State remains subject to independent federal constitutional and statutory limits. Influence-claim cognizability and adaptation of the first Gingles prerequisite, Fifteenth Amendment dilution coverage and the distinct unpresented racial-classification claim remain unresolved. Neither political aims nor racial awareness receives categorical immunity.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Reverse and remand only the population-equality claim for substantiated county-preservation justification and independent constitutional limits. The statutory and intent grounds do not return for retrial; no map or election date is ordered.

#### Growe v. Emison, 507 U.S. 25 (1993)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 91-1420.

**Decided:** February 23, 1993.

##### Timely state redistricting includes the State's courts

**Holding:** When a State's legislature or judiciary is timely undertaking redistricting, a federal court must defer its own redistricting action unless the record shows that the State will not produce a lawful plan in time for the election. The federal court may set a reasonable deadline and retain jurisdiction as a safeguard, but may neither obstruct timely state work nor displace it merely because it prefers another plan; completion of every state appeal before the election is not required.

**Proposition-level authority:** Scalia's opinion on state priority, joined by the other eight Justices; all nine adopt this rule. The separate eight-Justice dismissal direction concerns this completed record, not the general availability of retained jurisdiction.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Scott v. Germano, 381 U.S. 407 (1965): applied to both legislative and congressional redistricting through state courts; its timely-action condition and reserve federal jurisdiction remain.
- Reynolds v. Sims, 377 U.S. 533 (1964): applied for primary state responsibility and the obligation to remedy unconstitutional apportionment; population-equality requirements are unchanged.

##### A superseding state judgment must receive legal effect

**Holding:** Once a state court's final judgment replaces the challenged legislative plan, the federal court must give the replacement legal effect rather than treat it as one optional proposal. A challenge to the old plan becomes moot unless it also reaches the superseding plan; allegations against the old plan alone do not establish a violation in the replacement.

**Proposition-level authority:** Scalia's opinion on the live-plan issue, joined by the other eight Justices; all nine adopt the stated rule. No party-preclusion rule beyond that necessary legal effect is announced.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Atlantic Coast Line Railroad Co. v. Brotherhood of Locomotive Engineers, 398 U.S. 281 (1970): applied for respect for state judgments and the federal-state judicial relationship; no enlarged preclusion rule adopted.
- Wise v. Lipscomb, 437 U.S. 535 (1978): applied for the need to evaluate the legally operative replacement before federal remedial displacement.

##### Fragmentation claims require proof of the causal voting conditions

**Holding:** A §2 claim that single-member districts fragment a minority group's potential electoral majority requires proof that the group is sufficiently large and geographically compact to form a majority in a single-member district, is politically cohesive, and faces majority bloc voting ordinarily sufficient to defeat its preferred candidate, absent special circumstances such as an unopposed minority candidate. Without the required cohesion and majority-bloc evidence, the court may not order a minority district by adding distinct groups' populations; the Court assumes without deciding that a supported coalition claim is permissible and leaves influence-only claims and the population denominator unresolved.

**Proposition-level authority:** Scalia's statutory opinion, joined by the other eight Justices; all nine adopt the stated single-member extension and record application. No fractured-opinion or Marks inference is required.

**Current-force modification:** Valdespino v. Alamo Heights Independent School District, June 17, 1999, modifies the exclusivity of the district-majority condition for one protected group. The group may instead prove a lawful, geographically compact district in which cohesive voting and dependable crossover support give it actual ability to elect its candidates of choice. The numerical-majority route survives; speculation, isolated electoral success, near-majority arithmetic and influence alone do not establish the alternative. Cohesion, the relevant majority-bloc showing with its special-circumstances qualification, causation, independent totality and constitutional limits remain necessary. The distinct-group coalition and influence-only reservations are not resolved.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Thornburg v. Gingles, 478 U.S. 30 (1986): extended from multimember dilution to the single-member fragmentation claim at issue; its causal prerequisites and totality inquiry remain distinct, and its influence-only reservation remains open.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Gingles prerequisites are necessary, not sufficient; the totality inquiry remains. No proportional representation, mandatory statistical method, filing-date priority or enlarged party-preclusion rule is adopted. Coalition permissibility is assumed; influence claims and population denominator remain open.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Reverse the federal redistricting judgment. Eight Justices—White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas—direct dismissal of this action; Stone-Zsela supports reversal but would allow conditional retention. That case-specific mandate does not abolish the unanimous general rule permitting reserve jurisdiction.

**Later-authority backlinks:** Valdespino v. Alamo Heights Independent School District, lower docket 98-50227, June 17, 1999: first-condition functional ability-to-elect alternative for a single protected group.

#### Johnson v. De Grandy, 512 U.S. 997

**Docket or dockets:** Nos. 92-519, 92-593 and 92-767.

**Decided:** 1994-06-30.

**Companion or consolidated matters:** Johnson v. De Grandy, No. 92-519; De Grandy v. Johnson, No. 92-593; United States v. Florida, No. 92-767. Holder v. Hall, decided June 30, 1994, precedes this decision in the expressly coordinated same-day sequence.

For the other questions in Johnson v. De Grandy, 1994-06-30, Nos. 92-519, 92-593 and 92-767, see Federal Courts — The state validation does not preclude the reserved factual claims; Federal Courts — The United States brings an original enforcement suit.

##### Necessary voting conditions do not require maximization

**Holding:** This Section 2 fragmentation claim requires a minority sufficiently large and geographically compact to form a district majority, political cohesion, and majority bloc voting ordinarily sufficient to defeat its preferred candidate, absent special circumstances such as an unopposed minority candidate; the claimant must also establish unequal opportunity to participate and elect chosen representatives under the totality of circumstances, without proving discriminatory purpose. Even when those necessary conditions are satisfied, Section 2 does not require every feasible additional majority-minority district; the disputed first condition is assumed here, leaving citizenship, population-denominator and coalition or influence-claim questions unresolved.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Ginsburg; seven Justices directly adopt the complete necessary-versus-sufficient distinction and rejection of maximization. Thomas and Scalia's statutory noncoverage position is a different rationale and supplies no join.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Thornburg v. Gingles, 478 U.S. 30: follows its causal conditions, special-circumstances qualification and independent totality inquiry; the conditions do not establish liability by themselves.
- Growe v. Emison, February 23, 1993: preserves local cohesion and majority-bloc proof for single-member fragmentation and the unresolved coalition, influence and population questions.
- Voinovich v. Quilter, March 2, 1993: follows the challengers' burden and the distinction between a State's authority to choose districts and a court's power to remedy a proved violation.
- Holder v. Hall, June 30, 1994: applies its separation of coverage, comparison, proof and remedy; a permissible comparison creates no maximization command.
- Shaw v. Reno, June 28, 1993: preserves the independent statutory elements; its constitutional injury rule adds no further element to Section 2.

##### Proportional opportunity is relevant evidence, not a safe harbor

**Holding:** The relationship between a group's share of the relevant population and the share of districts in which it has an effective opportunity to elect its preferred candidates is relevant evidence within Section 2's totality inquiry, whose weight depends on the other evidence; it is neither a conclusive safe harbor nor a burden-shifting affirmative defense, and disproportionality alone likewise does not establish liability. The inquiry concerns demonstrated electoral opportunity, not nominal district labels or the race of officeholders; it guarantees no proportional election, racial floor or ceiling, statewide offset, or universal geographic or population denominator, and cannot excuse proved local packing, fragmentation or another barrier merely because opportunities exist elsewhere.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Ginsburg; six Justices directly adopt this contextual rule and its explanation. Kennedy separately accepts the exact limited propositions that proportionality is relevant, that the necessary conditions alone do not establish liability, and that proportionality supplies no safe harbor; his concurrence does not join Part III's full rationale or Parts IV and V's affirmative applications. No combined rationale under Marks is needed.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Gingles, 478 U.S. 30: follows its contextual inquiry and treatment of electoral success; adopts no conclusive proportional-success presumption.
- Voinovich, March 2, 1993: preserves the need for proof of actual unequal opportunity rather than automatic condemnation or approval of a district form.
- Holder, June 30, 1994: preserves independent totality proof after an adequate comparison; its local institutional comparison supplies neither a quota nor an evidentiary safe harbor.

##### The House liability and dependent remedy cannot stand on this record

**Holding:** Assuming the disputed first Gingles condition in the challengers' favor and retaining the supported findings of discrimination and polarized voting, the tried House record does not establish unequal opportunity under the totality beyond the erroneous inference that every feasible additional Hispanic district must be created. The liability judgment and dependent substitution of Modified De Grandy Plan 268 are reversed; no new liability trial, Supreme Court replacement map or numerical district minimum is ordered, and implementation must follow lawful election administration.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part IV, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Ginsburg; six Justices adopt this full contextual application. Kennedy supports reversal and the mandate under Parts II and VI on the narrower rejection of maximization, without joining Part IV; the House disposition therefore has seven votes, against Thomas and Scalia's vacatur-and-dismissal alternative.

**Material application:** The Court considers the distinct eighteen- and twenty-district House frames, actual effective opportunities, accepted discrimination and polarization evidence, and testimony about neighborhood splitting and concentration. No finding of inconsistent treatment of comparable minority and other neighborhoods is made or imposed as a new liability element. Supported findings retain Rule 52(a) protection; the legal error lies in making feasible additional districts obligatory.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Gingles, 478 U.S. 30: follows the totality standard and Rule 52(a)'s protection of supported findings; corrects a legally infected liability determination without creating a ratio presumption or new intent requirement.
- Voinovich, March 2, 1993: applies the need for an established federal violation before judicial imposition of remedial districts.
- Holder, June 30, 1994: distinguishes a sufficient comparison from the separate proof of unequal opportunity; its established liability and remedy remand do not supply missing liability here.

##### The Senate judgment stands because no violation was proved

**Holding:** On the tried Senate record, the asserted unequal opportunity is not established under the totality merely because an additional Hispanic district and a different additional Black district are each feasible; the judgment leaving the State's Senate plan operative is affirmed for lack of a proved violation, not because a proved violation may remain when proposed remedies conflict. The existing Black electoral opportunity with crossover support is relevant evidence, not a second Black-majority district, proof of general cohesion between Hispanic and Black voters, or a legal entitlement to a crossover district.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part V, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Ginsburg; six Justices adopt this full contextual application. Kennedy supports affirmance under Parts II and VI on the narrower no-maximization ground without joining Part V; the Senate disposition has seven votes, against Thomas and Scalia's vacatur-and-dismissal alternative.

**Material application:** The operative July 2 Senate judgment found no violation; the July 17 explanation's competing-remedies account cannot excuse a proved wrong. The seven-district, five-wholly-Dade and broader South Florida frames remain distinct, and cohesion within each group does not establish a combined Hispanic–Black coalition.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Gingles, 478 U.S. 30: applies contextual unequal-opportunity proof without inferring liability from separately feasible district increases.
- Growe, February 23, 1993: preserves the need for proved common cohesion before distinct groups can be treated as one electoral coalition.
- Voinovich, March 2, 1993: preserves the unresolved influence-claim question and ties compelled relief to an established violation.
- Holder, June 30, 1994: preserves the obligation to formulate lawful relief for an established wrong; no competing-remedies exception to liability or relief is recognized.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The House liability judgment and dependent Modified De Grandy Plan 268 remedy are reversed in No. 92-519; the Senate judgment is affirmed in Nos. 92-593 and 92-767, each 7–2. Implementation must follow lawful election administration. No new liability trial, extra Senate district, Supreme Court replacement map or special election timetable is ordered. Congressional proceedings, the Escambia settlement, dismissed claims and preclearance determinations remain undisturbed.

#### Valdespino v. Alamo Heights Independent School District

**Docket or dockets:** lower docket 98-50227.
**Decided:** 1999-06-17.

##### The first condition includes a proved functional ability-to-elect route

**Holding:** For a claim by one protected group, the first Gingles condition may be met either through the retained district-majority route or through rigorous proof that a lawful, geographically compact proposed district would provide the cohesive group dependable crossover support sufficient to elect its candidates of choice. Speculation, an isolated favorable election, near-majority population arithmetic or mere influence does not suffice; the relevant majority-bloc showing, causal connection and independent totality remain necessary.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stone-Zsela's Part I is joined by Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; five Justices adopt this express modification and its limits.

##### The population figures do not resolve functional opportunity

**Holding:** The accepted 47.9% and 48.3% Hispanic citizen voting-age shares neither prove nor foreclose dependable ability to elect. The judgment resting exclusively on their being below fifty percent must be vacated for application of the changed rule, with all remaining statutory conditions and totality to be established before relief.

**Proposition-level authority:** The same five Justices join Stone-Zsela's Part I and this application and remedy.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The proposed district must remain lawful in population and geography; the 8.1% underpopulation is not approved. No mathematical crossover formula, racial floor, proportional-election right, distinct-minority-group combination or influence-only entitlement is adopted. Supplementation under ordinary procedure is neither automatically required nor forbidden; the excluded survey is not automatically admitted.

##### The independent demographic and disclosure rulings stand

**Holding:** The credited proof of concrete post-census housing, occupancy and growth changes supports the present demographic findings under clear-error review. Excluding the survey disclosed on the eve of trial for surprise under the applicable disclosure rule was within the trial court's discretion; changing the capacity rule does not itself make either independent ruling erroneous.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stone-Zsela's Part II is joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; seven Justices adopt this rule and explanation. Scalia and Thomas do not join it unconditionally.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Thornburg v. Gingles: expressly modifies numerical exclusivity in the first condition for the defined single-group functional route; preserves compactness, cohesion, relevant majority-bloc proof, causation and independent totality.
- Growe v. Emison: modifies the same exclusivity while preserving its causal framework and the distinct-group reservation.
- Voinovich v. Quilter: preserves the claimant's burden, relevant bloc-voting requirement and distinction between lawful state districting choice and a court-compelled remedy; the present rule resolves only the defined functional-capacity question.
- Johnson v. De Grandy: applies the rejection of maximization and conclusive ratios; preserves independent totality and actual opportunity without attributing today's change to that earlier decision.
- Brooks v. Barnes: applies its insistence that proof address the actual electoral barrier; its nomination holding did not already decide this district-formation question.
- Department of Commerce v. United States House of Representatives / Clinton v. Glavin: preserves the national enumeration ruling; it neither freezes local demographics at the census date nor validates every projection.

**Limits and questions not reached:** A single protected group below a numerical majority may prove the first condition through dependable actual ability to elect in a lawful compact district. A majority-population route remains available, but neither route establishes every other condition or liability. Cohesion, relevant bloc-voting causation with applicable special circumstances, totality, independent constitutional limits and ordinary evidence procedures retain their force. Mere influence and speculative near-majority calculations do not create a right to districting. The present record establishes no functional success or completed violation.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Partial vacatur and remand are 5–4. Supporting: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Opposing: O'Connor and Kennedy would affirm under a numerical citizen-voting-age majority requirement; Scalia and Thomas would affirm on statutory noncoverage grounds.

The demographic and disclosure rulings are sustained by seven direct votes: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Scalia and Thomas express only conditional agreement if the dilution framework is applied. Those positions do not supply direct joins in the evidence holding or the numerical rule.

Vacatur concerns the categorical sub-fifty bar. The lower courts must assess the functional route on the preserved admissible record and any supplementation independently permitted under ordinary procedure. ## Issues positively presented in the congressional appeal

The jurisdictional statement presents three linked issues: rejection of proposed Latino registered-voter-majority districts under the first Gingles condition; the compactness standard applied to proposed districts compared with districts the court adopted or preserved; and the adequacy of findings supporting the rejection of unequal-opportunity claims after trial. It challenges the rejection of an additional South Texas Latino opportunity district as unlawful packing of the existing Latino electorate. It argues for appellate review of the governing standard and findings, not merely for an abstract choice between population denominators. Sources: Jurisdictional Statement, pp. i, 5–6, 8–28.

Appellants' principal numerical argument is that their proposed seven South Texas districts have Latino registered-voter majorities and therefore meet even the Fifth Circuit's citizen-voting-age-majority requirement. They argue that Latino registration rates are lower than Anglo rates, so a Latino registered-voter majority implies a citizen-voting-age majority. They expressly resist making registered-voter majority a new minimum threshold. Those propositions are their contentions; they are not findings by this supplement. Sources: Jurisdictional Statement, pp. 14–17 and nn. 4–6.

Appellants also make a positive electoral-performance argument: they say voting behavior in the proposed districts would permit election of Latino-preferred candidates, that the court did not evaluate that evidence, and that the term effectiveness cannot supply unstated findings resolving the dispute. Sources: Jurisdictional Statement, pp. 23–26; Reply to Manley, pp. 1–8. Thus, the appellate materials actually present the role of functional opportunity and the adequacy of the trial court's findings. They should not be described as an appeal containing only population arithmetic, nor as an established below-fifty-percent case identical to Valdespino.

The new sources do not themselves establish a distinct additional claim demanding a particular sub-fifty-percent citizen-voting-age district. The point supported here is narrower: electoral capacity and the factual grounds for rejecting the proposed Latino-opportunity arrangement were disputed in the congressional appeal. Whether the effective Valdespino rule changes the disposition requires application to these actual issues and findings.

## What the lower court decided

The congressional opinion describes a full trial, competing maps, and its own plan-making process. It retained protected districts, located the two new seats near population growth, considered compactness, contiguity and subdivisions, and reported checks on incumbency and partisan consequences. It found no need to change its map after the incumbency check. These explanations occupy App. 1–10 of the jurisdictional statement.

The court then rejected requests for additional minority districts. It separately found inadequate common cohesion between Latino and African-American voters, particularly in Democratic primaries, for the combined-population proposal. That finding must not be converted into a finding about dependable support from other voters for one protected group's candidates in every South Texas district. Sources: congressional opinion, App. 11–14.

For Latino opportunity districts, the court found that the population was insufficiently compact or numerous to support another effective Latino citizenship-majority district in Texas, Dallas County or South Texas. It then found that failure to create seven districts of that description would not prevent full and equal Latino political participation under the totality. Its explanation cites the Fifth Circuit Valdespino rule in the preceding discussion. Sources: congressional opinion, App. 12 and nn. 25–27, App. 14–15 and n. 32.

The judgment declares the existing congressional map unconstitutionally malapportioned and adopts Plan 1151C; it denies relief not granted. Source: Jurisdictional Statement, App. 17–18. The mandate under review therefore concerns an operative replacement map. Any appellate action must distinguish correcting the rejection of the statutory claim from reviving the obsolete map, prescribing a replacement district, or ordering a new election.

The adequacy and independence of the compactness, capacity, and totality determinations are disputed legal questions. The source account does not decide that the court made no findings at all, that all its findings are independent of numerical exclusivity, or that every finding is invalid because it cited the former numerical rule.

## Electoral-performance record: both sides

**The affirmative material exists in the record account.** Appellants' reply identifies reaggregated results of statewide racially contested elections for the precincts in their proposed districts. They say Latino-preferred candidates received majorities in elections involving Pete Benavides, Dan Morales, Victor Morales and Hector Uribe. Their appendix reproduces cross-examination of Allan Lichtman concerning Plan 1099C and Balderas Exhibit 105, a summary sheet with supporting reaggregated election sheets. Lichtman agrees that the selected Latino-preferred candidates won four of six elections in proposed District 20 and four of six in proposed District 23; for District 23, he qualifies that the most recent three elections included two results near fifty percent and one loss. He agrees that the listed contests were won by Latino-preferred candidates in proposed Districts 15, 16, 27, 28 and 32. He also acknowledges his report's description of an additional seventh opportunity district. Sources: Reply to Manley, pp. 7–8 and nn. 7–9; App. 5–7, reproducing October 23 afternoon testimony at trial pp. 156–160.

**That affirmative material is not equivalent to an appellate finding of dependable capacity.** The exhibits and testimony concern particular plans and selected elections. Plan 1099C must not silently become Plan 1123C. The later plan, the relevance and representativeness of the selected elections, voter preferences, turnout, the proposed electorate and any relevant primary barrier must remain identified. Four selected victories do not, by this source account alone, establish dependability or prove every other statutory condition.

**Manley's contrary account is concrete and qualified.** His motion contends that appellants offered no adequate effectiveness proof, noting that their expert Engstrom discussed polarized voting rather than the effectiveness of proposed districts. Footnote 4 acknowledges that their proposed findings cited two exhibits without expert sponsorship and cross-examination of Lichtman. Thus the motion's broader absence language is an argument about the adequacy and form of proof; it cannot accurately be repeated as an established complete absence of relevant material. Sources: Motion to Affirm, pp. 14–16 and n. 4; Reply to Manley, p. 8 and nn. 8–9.

Lichtman's reproduced direct testimony places proposed District 23 in a marginal category even though the proposed configuration is stronger than the existing one, and discusses the risk of exchanging six effective districts for seven districts including marginal ones. Murray's testimony says turnout and actual performance of the proposed territory must be examined, and describes lower Latino turnout. Congressman Gonzalez describes why a reduction to approximately half of registered voters, coupled with the proposed removal of heavily Latino areas and addition of an Anglo Republican voting bloc, may make proposed District 20 ineffective. Sources: Manley Motion, App. 2a–9a, reproducing October 23 and 25 testimony.

Appellants respond that Gonzalez had acknowledged earlier electoral success at lower Latino registration shares and that his own candidacy would require more work, rather than necessarily become unwinnable. His reproduced cross-examination confirms earlier victories and identifies the absence of substantial opposition in his 2000 election. Those special circumstances matter; candidate race, party label and incumbent success are not substitutes for identifying the group's preferred candidates. Sources: Reply to Manley, pp. 5–6, App. 7–8.

Alford's reproduced testimony recognizes that the additional South Texas district could make some districts more marginal; he describes a risk, rather than treating all such proposals as necessarily impossible. Other excerpts discuss existing District 23, Latino turnout, support for Bonilla among some Latino voters, and his conclusion that Bonilla was not the candidate preferred by most Latino voters. Sources: Reply to Manley, pp. 3–6; Reply to Texas, pp. 1–2 and 9–10. Neither party's interpretation of the opinion is adopted here as a finding.

## Compactness, community evidence and competing maps

Manley and Texas argue that proposed Districts 15 and 32 stretch north from the border through long corridors, divide subdivisions, and subordinate traditional criteria to a numerical target. Manley's appendix reproduces Ratliff's testimony explaining why he declined to add another South Texas district, and Weber's testimony discussing the proposed corridors and allocation of Latino and Anglo precincts. Sources: Manley Motion, pp. 17–20, App. 12a–16a; Texas Motion, pp. 3–5.

Appellants supply more than an assertion about district shapes. Their reply includes maps of the old Plan 1000C, proposed Plan 1123C and court-adopted Plan 1151C; Texas Legislative Council reports supply two distinct compactness measures. Those measures do not tell the same story for every district. Under the report's scale, smaller values mean greater compactness. Proposed District 32 has a perimeter-to-area score of 7.0 and a smallest-circle score of 5.6; the latter is the least compact ranking within Plan 1123C. The court's District 15 has scores of 8.5 and 5.0, while proposed District 15 has scores of 8.3 and 4.7. These are reported geometric comparisons, not determinations of lawful compactness. It would be inaccurate to declare every proposed district better under every measure. Sources: Reply to Texas, App. 1–3 and 16–20; Manley Motion, pp. 18–20 and App. 1a. The rotated reports were visually read.

Tijerina's reproduced testimony connects the relevant Rio Grande, Nueces and San Antonio–Guadalupe areas through settlement, family, trade, labor and migration patterns, expressly discussing proposed Plan 1099C's Districts 27, 15 and 32. Weber's reproduced cross-examination limits his claimed expertise about geographic barriers in the San Antonio–Austin area. Alford's cross-examination identifies a substantial Latino population in the I-35 corridor and similarities between boundaries in the State's plan and appellants' plans, with the Travis County difference preserved. Sources: Reply to Texas, App. 4–15, reproducing October 22 and November 1 testimony.

The geographic dispute therefore has evidence on both sides. A numerical compactness score alone cannot establish community coherence, but neither does a long north–south shape by itself disprove it. Testimony about Plan 1099C, Plan 1110C, Plan 1123C and the State's alternative must remain attached to the plan actually examined. The source account does not infer that revised plans are interchangeable.

The parties invoke constitutional redistricting decisions and distinguish a State's choices from a federal court's remedial power. Their invocations are advocacy, not authorization to import any historical post-divergence Supreme Court proposition into current law. The validated entering-law slice governs the force of each authority. Sources: Manley Motion, pp. 17–20; Texas Motion, pp. 3–5; Reply to Texas, pp. 4–8.

## Supported competing appellate paths for renewed validation

**Possible affirmance:** the congressional court's compactness and totality determinations, read against the actual testimony and maps, may independently establish that the proposed arrangement does not prove unequal opportunity or a lawful additional district. The current capacity rule does not remove geography, relevant voting proof, causation, independent totality, or the claimant's burden. A short explanation can satisfy Rule 52 if it permits meaningful review; the losing side's proposed findings need not all be adopted. Appellees make those arguments in Manley Motion, pp. 20–23, and Texas Motion, pp. 11–12. Their strongest record support includes the district-specific marginality evidence and geographic testimony. The opposing objection is that their explanation attributes findings to the court that the opinion did not identify and overlooks affirmative contrary material actually presented.

**Possible limited vacatur:** the appeal specifically challenges the adequacy of the first-condition, compactness and totality reasoning, and its appendices establish actual contested electoral-performance and geographic evidence. If the opinion's majority-linked conclusions do not reveal an independent lawful ground under the current capacity rule, a remand could require the lower court to apply the proper inquiry and explain the dispositive findings without an appellate ruling that any proposed district satisfies the statute. The strongest objection is that the proposals asserted registered-voter majorities, and the trial included evidence of functional inadequacy; changing the former numerical exclusivity rule does not automatically undermine a judgment resting on actual ineffectiveness or lack of compactness.

Neither path may rest on an invented missing filing, a finding that all relevant evidence was absent, a requirement to maximize Latino districts, or a presumed district entitlement from registered-voter percentages. Numerical-majority, functional-capacity, the other causal conditions and totality remain distinct tasks under entering law. The source account does not decide whether the existing findings are sufficient for affirmance or inadequate enough to require remand.

Any remand remains limited to the supplied congressional review scope and the issues and proof properly presented. It need not order new evidence, new maps, a special election, or relief on distinct coalition claims. Existing election arrangements require lawful treatment; a statutory reconsideration is not itself an order to reinstate the obsolete map. No present source gap is transformed into litigant default or a condition on the scheduled appeal.
