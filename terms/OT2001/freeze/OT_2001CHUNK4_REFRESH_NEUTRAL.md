## 50. Barnhart v. Walton — duration, entitlement and trial work

### Record and reviewed judgment

The complete December 18, 2000 Fourth Circuit opinion, 235 F.3d 184, supports a more precise sequence than the intake's compressed account. Walton applied for disability insurance benefits and supplemental security income on April 12, 1995, alleging a schizophrenic disorder with depression. The amended onset date was October 31, 1994. He began part-time work in May 1995 and full-time work on December 10, 1995. After the first hearing in July 1996, the ALJ initially awarded a closed period ending with full-time work. On administrative remand, the ALJ found substantial gainful activity in October 1995 and denied benefits because inability to perform that activity did not persist for twelve months. The circuit affirmed the substantial-gainful-activity finding, but reversed the duration and trial-work rulings and remanded.

The Commissioner did not dispute that the impairment itself lasted at least twelve months. The circuit reasoned that the duration clause modifies impairment alone; entitlement could begin after the five-month waiting period without a prior award; trial-work protection then prevented use of the October 1995 activity to show cessation. Independently, it regarded the agency construction as unreasonable even if the text were ambiguous. Both grounds require attention. The circuit's conclusion about entitlement is its disputed statutory holding, not a fact that neutral preflight may assume. The October 1995 substantial-gainful-activity finding is the affirmed factual component; the present questions do not authorize recalculating it from later earnings thresholds.

### Complete statutory and regulatory setting

## 58. Michigan v. Environmental Protection Agency — interstate NOx budgets

### Record, scope and lower reasoning

The complete March 3, 2000 D.C. Circuit opinion, 213 F.3d 663, including Judge Sentelle's dissent and footnotes, establishes that the judgment under review addresses the one-hour ozone standard. The eight-hour findings had been stayed by EPA and the circuit stayed that part of review. The current Whitman decision does not automatically reactivate the stayed portion or enlarge these three supplied questions.

EPA first identified contributing States through the magnitude, frequency and relative amount of modeled interstate effects. It then required elimination of the amount removable by highly cost-effective controls, using a $2,000-per-ton benchmark; the remaining budget was projected from emissions and required controls. The inquiry concerns the legal permissibility of that role for cost. No party in the lower judgment claimed that benefits must exceed costs, that EPA was authorized to use that balance here, or that the word significant requires cost consideration. A permissive-cost conclusion would not establish any of those propositions or independently validate every numerical assumption.

The majority relied on text and the circuit's presumption favoring consideration of cost absent clear exclusion. Sentelle rejected that presumption in this setting: the enacted phrase concerns amounts of pollution contributing to another State's nonattainment, while control expense measures something different. He would invalidate the rule on statutory grounds and did not reach the subsidiary issues. This is a material alternative legal ground, not a disagreement with EPA's mathematical models.

The majority's state-choice ruling rests on Train v. NRDC, 421 U.S. 60 (1975), Union Electric and Virginia v. EPA. EPA's rule set the required result, while States retained actual choices among stationary and mobile sources and other control measures. They could opt into EPA's model trading program, use their own trading program, or use none; the model's source requirements govern participation in that option. The rule did not let a State substitute VOC controls for its NOx budget, because EPA and OTAG concluded VOC controls would not adequately address the long-distance transport problem. States could add VOC controls for associated local disbenefits. The budget's pollutant specificity and the retained means choices must both be considered; statutory discretion is not proved solely by saying that States may spend more.

The lower nondelegation account materially differs from controlling Whitman. It acknowledged that EPA did not explain a statutory stopping point for the particular dollar figure and relied in part on the provision's relatively confined practical exercise to date. Whitman forbids curing absent congressional guidance by agency self-limitation. A present judgment sustaining the delegation therefore requires sufficient limits in Congress's statute, independently of this agency's modesty, geographical reach or chosen price. The source's description of the exact price's rationale does not itself decide a different, unreviewed arbitrary-and-capricious challenge to that figure.

The circuit vacated the rule as to Wisconsin, Missouri and Georgia and remanded; it separately remanded inadequate notice concerning the electric-generating-unit definition and the control level for large stationary internal-combustion engines. The supplied review does not reopen those separate corrections. The circuit denied the remaining petitions. ## 61. Thompson v. Western States Medical Center — compounding and truthful promotion

### Complete provision and record

Section 503A, enacted in FDAMA §127 on November 21, 1997 and effective after one year, exempts qualifying compounded drugs from §501(a)(2)(B)'s manufacturing-practice requirement, §502(f)(1)'s adequate-directions requirement and §505's new-drug approval requirement. It is not an exemption from every federal or state drug rule. The complete enacted text is copied in the source appendix.

The intake's shorthand about unsolicited prescriptions must be read with §503A(a)'s actual alternative: the statute also specifies a notation on the prescription order, approved by the prescribing practitioner, that a compounded product is necessary for the identified patient. It separately permits limited advance compounding based on the prescribed history within the specified established relationships. Section 503A(c) restricts promotion of a particular drug, class of drug, or type of drug, while permitting promotion of the compounding service. All those categories matter; the provision does not merely regulate mass-media advertising or only named brand products.

The remaining enacted controls govern permitted substances and their sources and certificates, monographs, withdrawn unsafe or ineffective products, and regular or inordinate compounding of essentially commercial copies. A patient-specific change producing a significant difference, as determined by the prescribing practitioner, is excluded from that copy category. Another condition addresses drugs presenting demonstrable compounding difficulties. The interstate condition is either a State memorandum of understanding addressing inordinate interstate quantities and complaint investigation, or, without that State agreement, out-of-state distribution not exceeding five percent of total prescription orders dispensed or distributed by the pharmacy or physician. The statute does not prescribe a twenty-percent MOU ceiling; that figure in the lower opinion must not become statutory text. The agreement is with a State, not an individual pharmacy. The five-percent denominator is total prescription orders, not only interstate sales or compounded products. PET compounded drugs and radiopharmaceuticals are excluded from §503A, and manufacturer-directed mixing under approved labeling lies outside its compounding definition.

The complete February 6, 2001 Ninth Circuit opinion supports the summary-judgment posture, prepared promotional materials mailed or distributed at medical conferences, and the Government's noncontention there that the speech was unlawful or misleading. The circuit accepted health and approval-integrity interests as substantial but rejected the separately described interest in balancing availability against widespread compounding on the offered showing. It found advancement and fit insufficient and reversed the district court's severability ruling, leaving §353a invalid in its entirety. The supplied review excludes that separate severability issue. Neither the appellate court's assessment nor its paraphrase of the statute is an independent factual finding that compounding at any scale is safe.

## 62. US Airways, Inc. v. Barnett — reassignment and the employer's seniority system

(b) Construction
As used in subsection (a) of this section, the
term ‘‘discriminate’’ includes—
(1) limiting, segregating, or classifying a job
applicant or employee in a way that adversely
affects the opportunities or status of such ap -
plicant or employee because of the disability
of such applicant or employee;
(2) participating in a contractual or other
arrangement or relationship that has the ef -
fect of subjecting a covered entity’s qualified
applicant or employee with a disability to the
discrimination prohibited by this subchapter
(such relationship includes a relationship with
an employment or referral agency, labor
union, an organization providing fringe bene -
fits to an employee of the covered entity, or
an organization providing training and ap -
prenticeship programs);
(3) utilizing standards, criteria, or methods
of administration—
(A) that have the effect of discrimination
on the basis of disability; or
(B) that perpetuate the discrimination of
others who are subject to common adminis -
trative control;
(4) excluding or otherwise denying equal jobs
or benefits to a qualified individual because of
the known disability of an individual with
whom the qualified individual is known to
have a relationship or association;

(5)(A) not making reasonable accommoda -
tions to the known physical or mental limita -
tions of an otherwise qualified individual with
a disability who is an applicant or employee,
unless such covered entity can demonstrate
that the accommodation would impose an
undue hardship on the operation of the busi -

(B) denying employment opportunities to a
job applicant or employee who is an otherwise
qualified individual with a disability, if such
denial is based on the need of such covered en -
tity to make reasonable accommodation to
the physical or mental impairments of the em-
ployee or applicant;
(6) using qualification standards, employ -
ment tests or other selection criteria that
screen out or tend to screen out an individual
with a disability or a class of individuals with
disabilities unless the standard, test or other
selection criteria, as used by the covered en -
tity, is shown to be job-related for the position
in question and is consistent with business ne -
cessity; and
(7) failing to select and administer tests con-
cerning employment in the most effective
manner to ensure that, when such test is ad -
ministered to a job applicant or employee who
has a disability that impairs sensory, manual,
or speaking skills, such test results accurately
reflect the skills, aptitude, or whatever other
factor of such applicant or employee that such
test purports to measure, rather than reflect -
ing the impaired sensory, manual, or speaking
skills of such employee or applicant (except
where such skills are the factors that the test
purports to measure).
