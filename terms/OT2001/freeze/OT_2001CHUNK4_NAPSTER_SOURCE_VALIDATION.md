## Posture and operative order

The two consolidated appeals concern the preliminary injunction issued in July 2000 and explained and modified in August 2000. The appellate court stayed that injunction pending appeal. It affirmed in part, reversed in part and remanded on February 12, 2001. Part X continues the stay of the original injunction until the district court modifies it to conform to the appellate opinion and issues an immediate limited remand for that task. This source proves no later replacement order, compliance event, business closure or subsequent adjudication. The reviewed appellate judgment also awards its appellees appellate costs.

Part VIII requires a narrower order. The plaintiffs must identify copyrighted works and files containing them available on the system before the duty to disable access arises. Napster also has an affirmative policing duty within its actual system capabilities. The system indexes user-chosen names and can terminate access, but does not inspect the contents of users' files. Misspellings and approximate titles matter to administration; neither perfect detection nor complete inability to act is established. The source's statement that relief is required is the lower court's conclusion on this preliminary record, not an independently established general Supreme Court rule eliminating injunction prerequisites.

## Preliminary liability and distinct grounds

The prima facie direct-infringement conclusion was not appealed; the disputed user defense was fair use. The court applies all four statutory factors to full, generally permanent copies available to other users. Its discussion distinguishes the record's exchange from personal time-shifting or transfer confined to the original owner. Sampling and the asserted space-shifting theory fail on this record; permissive artist distribution, chat rooms, message boards and the New Artist Program are outside the requested prohibition. Copying a whole work is not declared invariably unfair. Evidence of possible increased CD sales does not dispose of the separate supported licensing and digital-download markets. Footnote 3 expressly avoids deciding who ultimately bears the affirmative-defense burden at this preliminary stage: the result stands even assuming the plaintiffs must show likely success against the defense.

Contributory liability requires knowledge connected to actual infringement and material assistance. Sony prevents imputing knowledge merely from distributing a technology capable of substantial noninfringing uses. The appellate court rejects the district court's reliance solely on the ratio of current uses; future noninfringing capabilities also matter. It nevertheless finds the preliminary record sufficient because specific notice, actual availability, blocking capacity and failure to remove were shown. The knowledge account includes the notice identifying more than 12,000 files and continuing availability after purported terminations, as judicial findings on the preliminary record. The source does not establish strict liability for architecture alone.

Vicarious liability is separately supported by the right and ability to supervise and a direct financial interest. Infringing availability can draw users who support expected revenue; immediate sales revenue is not the only supported form of benefit. The right to terminate users and search the index supplies actual supervisory capacity, bounded by the architecture. The knowledge requirement and Sony's protection against imputed contributory knowledge are not substituted for these distinct elements. No final trial liability or damages were entered by this appeal.

## Statutory and other defenses

The AHRA defense fails for the actual computer-hard-drive copying because the statutory device and recording definitions do not encompass it as claimed. This is not a declaration that section 1008 has no application to any noncommercial copying or that the Act matters only when a complaint pleads an AHRA violation.

The appellate court expressly rejects categorical exclusion of secondary infringers from section 512. Eligibility remains for fuller proceedings, including the service-provider category, knowledge/notice requirements and compliance policy. It sustains preliminary relief on the shown serious questions and hardship balance; it does not adjudicate ultimate safe-harbor ineligibility. The enacted limits on permissible relief must be considered separately from common-law liability. The saved section 512 text, rather than an inferred lower-court command, supplies those limits.

Waiver requires an intentional surrender of the right; promoting lawful licensed downloading does not establish it here. The record supports rejection of an implied license, particularly given the actual objection to the unauthorized availability. The court rejects misuse on the present showing that the owners sought to control reproduction and distribution within their rights; it does not erase every possible misuse defense. Footnote 7 also affirms denial of further discovery and an evidentiary hearing under abuse-of-discretion review. These are preliminary record rulings, not new findings in the Court's proceeding.

The narrower infringement order answers the asserted directory and user-expression objections; the lower court does not make every restriction involving software constitutional. Its refusal to address additional original-order scope objections rests on remand for a revised order.

Part IX upholds the five-million-dollar security as within discretion on the competing evidence. It rejects replacing relief with a compulsory royalty: neither novel technology nor the asserted public injury establishes the exceptional basis claimed, and the identified statutory compulsory-license circumstances do not apply. The judicial disposition creates no compulsory license, final damage award or predicted criminal liability.
