## 1. Rebuttal of background mitigation

**Governing source and operative limit.** Cozzolino v. State, 584 S.W.2d 765, 767–768 (Tenn. June 11, 1979), reads section 39-2404(c) to admit punishment evidence only when relevant to a statutory aggravating circumstance or a mitigating factor raised by the defendant. Rebuttal must explain or controvert a proposition actually advanced. A premature introduction might become harmless if later defense proof actually made the evidence relevant in rebuttal; in Cozzolino that did not happen. The defendant sought to trace his crimes to a troubled childhood, and proof of present criminal proclivities did not controvert that explanation. The majority reversed the sentence because the irrelevant subsequent-crime evidence could not be held harmless.

That is a relevance rule with an application to the mitigation actually offered. It is not blanket permission to cross-examine every background witness about all other bad acts. It is also not categorical immunity for every statement a background or character witness might make. Whether a particular question actually rebuts a mitigating proposition remains distinct from counsel's fear that a witness might be damaging. The statutory text reproduced in the separate writing also preserves the defendant's fair opportunity to rebut admitted hearsay and excludes evidence obtained in violation of the federal or state constitution. The dissent's broader argument for admission is not the majority's rule.

```text
The defendant has raised another claim that we consider dispositive of the case. He contends that it was error for the trial judge to permit the State to introduce, in its case in chief, evidence that the defendant committed crimes subsequent to the murder, in that this evidence was not relevant to the proof of the presence or absence of any statutory aggravating circumstance or mitigating factor, the only issues properly before the jury. In opposition, the State has argued that TCA § 39— 2404(c) permits the introduction of this evidence regardless of its relevance to those issues. On its face section (c) would seem to permit the introduction of evidence “relevant to the punishment” in addition to that “tending to establish or rebut the aggravating circumstances” or “tending to establish or rebut any mitigating factors.” However, this interpretation is one that we cannot accept. When the statute is considered as a whole, it is clear that the only issues that the jury may properly consider in reaching a decision on the sentence to be imposed are whether the State has established one or more of the aggravating circumstances beyond a reasonable doubt and, if so, whether any mitigating factors have been shown that would outweigh those aggravating circumstances. Any evidence that does not go to the proof of one or the other of those issues is irrelevant to the jury’s deliberation. We cannot believe that the legislature intended that irrelevant evidence be placed before the jury, fraught as such a procedure would be with the “substantial risk that [the death penalty] would be inflicted in an arbitrary or capricious manner,” Gregg v. Georgia, 428 U.S. 153, 188, 96 S.Ct. 2909, 2932, 49 L.Ed.2d 859 (1976), i. e., on the basis of factors other than those deemed by the legislature to be proper predicates for the sentencing determination. We think that a better interpretation of TCA § 39-2404(c), and one more in keeping with both the sense of the entire statute and the mandate of the United States Supreme Court, see, e. g., Gregg v. Georgia, supra; Lockett v. Ohio, 438 U.S. 586, 98 S.Ct. 2954, 57 L.Ed.2d 973 (1978), is that evidence is relevant to the punishment, and thus admissible, only if it is relevant to an aggravating circumstance, or to a mitigating factor raised by the defendant.
At the outset, it is apparent that this evidence of subsequent crimes was not admissible to establish any of the aggravating circumstances set forth in TCA § 39-2404(i), for it is relevant to none of them. This was recognized by the trial judge. From the limitations placed on the use of this evidence in the court’s instructions, it appears that the trial judge permitted its introduction on the theory that it was relevant to rebut evidence of mitigating circumstances that might be advanced by the defendant. In this, he was in error. “Rebutting evidence” is that which tends to explain or controvert evidence produced by an adverse party. See, e. g., State v. Anderson, 159 N.W.2d 809 (Iowa 1968); Hutchinson v. Shaheen, 55 A.D.2d 833, 390 N.Y.S.2d 317 (1976). One cannot rebut a proposition that has not been advanced. While this error admittedly might have been made harmless by the later introduction by the defendant of evidence to which the State’s proof of subsequent crimes was relevant in rebuttal, that did not occur in the instant case. The defendant’s proof was limited to an attempt to show the origin, in a troubled childhood, of the defendant’s criminal acts. This proof was not controverted by the State’s demonstration of his present criminal proclivities.
As this evidence was relevant to neither the statutory aggravating circumstances, nor to any mitigating factors raised by the defendant, its admission was error. Given the nature of the issue on trial, we are unable to hold that this error was harmless. Therefore, the verdict of the jury imposing the death penalty is reversed, and the case remanded for a new sentencing hearing. Our decision pretermits the other assignments of error raised by the defendant.
```

## 2. Invalid aggravator: the trial-date rule and the later direct appeal

**What was available by trial.** State v. Pritchett, 621 S.W.2d 127, 139–140 (Tenn. Aug. 3, 1981), rejected one of two aggravators and ordered resentencing. Its majority relied on State v. Moore, 614 S.W.2d 348 (Tenn. 1981), as having followed the same course with one of two aggravators invalid, and said it could not know or speculate whether the jury would still impose death after weighing the sole remaining aggravator against mitigation. Pritchett is direct pretrial support for an expectation of resentencing in that situation. Its actual reasoning and setting should be carried forward; it does not itself decide every possible case involving a greater number of remaining aggravators. Moore is cited here only for Pritchett's own published account, not as an independently fully reviewed opinion.

**What happened later.** The January 23, 1984 direct Cone appeal, 665 S.W.2d 87, reproduced at petition appendix pages 84–100, treated the disputed great-risk aggravator as, if erroneous, harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. It stressed three valid aggravators found by the jury and a fourth established by the record; it expressly distinguished Pritchett's two-aggravator setting. That is the later case-specific disposition and the court's stated distinction. It does not establish what counsel should have known from a decision that did not exist in April 1982. Conversely, Pritchett does not make Dice's desired reversal guaranteed. The legal support for an expectation, the wisdom of trying to obtain appellate error, and the separate reason for avoiding rebuttal argument must remain separate questions.

Respondent's footnote 7 says that Tennessee had never announced a per se automatic-new-sentencing rule. That claim is party argument, not the pretrial rule supplied by the source review. The bounded conclusions above rest on the actual opinions rather than adopting that broad historical assertion.

## 3. The district court's actual grounds

**Answer to the source question.** The May 15, 1998 order supplies an express performance rationale in addition to prejudice reasoning. At appendix page 80 it distinguishes total abdication in Austin because counsel had already introduced mitigating evidence and rejected further proof through specific tactical analysis. It says that a miscalculation does not make the strategic decision deficient and invokes a rule against second-guessing tactics. It then concludes that Cone did not establish an unreasonable application of clearly established law. This is the court's own reasoning, not merely Tennessee's argument in its later brief.

For closing argument, pages 80–81 independently describe the choice to prevent the more experienced prosecutor's rebuttal and rely on the state finding of a tactical choice. The court also finds no demonstrated persuasive effect from a possible defense statement and concludes that the state decision was a reasonable application of Sixth Amendment standards. Thus the closing discussion contains both strategy/performance and effect-based grounds.

**Limits on what this establishes.** The mitigation discussion expressly confines itself to evidence available to counsel because the district court treated separate investigation/development allegations as procedurally defaulted. Its page 77 factual account and page 80 tactical rationale must be assessed with that restriction, rather than generalized to a finding that every possible investigation claim was decided on its merits. At pages 78–80 the court both recites ordinary reasonable-probability prejudice and additionally invokes Fretwell's fairness/reliability formulation; it demands the latter showing in application. The existence of the separate performance rationale does not itself resolve any challenge to its legal correctness, evidentiary premise, reviewability, or independence from the challenged restriction on the record.
