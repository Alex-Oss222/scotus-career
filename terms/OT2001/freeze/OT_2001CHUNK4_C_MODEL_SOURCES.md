### Hudson v. McMillian, No. 90-6531

Merits decision, February 25, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 13, 1991. On writ of certiorari to the Fifth Circuit, 929 F.2d 1014. Review follows reversal of an $800 damages award entered after a consensual trial before a magistrate.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

The question is whether the Fifth Circuit applied the correct Eighth Amendment test when it rejected the claim arising from this incident of force for lack of significant injury. The accepted question does not require a general definition of punishment for every unauthorized assault.

Hudson seeks removal of the significant-injury bar; respondents seek affirmance, including on an asserted isolated/unauthorized-conduct ground. The October 30, 1983 beating occurred while Hudson was handcuffed and shackled. Undisturbed findings establish unnecessary blows by McMillian and Woods and express supervisory condonation by Mezo. Bruising, facial swelling, loosened teeth and a cracked dental plate accompanied the blows. The court of appeals found the force clearly excessive and wanton but treated the injuries as minor. No new injury, motive or damages finding is made.

#### Judgment

The Fifth Circuit is reversed, 7–2, and the case is remanded for further disposition consistent with the undisturbed trial findings and the rejection of the significant-injury bar.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Limited injury does not defeat this established nontrivial malicious beating

**Controlling proposition:** A nontrivial beating inflicted maliciously and without disciplinary justification on a restrained convicted prisoner violates the Eighth Amendment even when the resulting physical injuries are limited, where the established conduct satisfies even Whitley’s demanding malicious-and-sadistic inquiry. Injury remains relevant evidence and bears on compensation; accidental harm or a merely technical battery is not enough.

**Authority:** Part A of O’Connor’s opinion, joined by Stone, White, Stevens, Kennedy and Souter: six Justices adopt this rule and its application. The general extension proposed in Part B has only four votes.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Constitution does not make protection from this beating depend on how much lasting injury the prisoner suffered. Whitley v. Albers distinguishes a good-faith effort to preserve discipline from force maliciously and sadistically applied to cause harm; the findings here satisfy even that demanding inquiry. Hudson was restrained, no force was needed, the guards repeatedly struck him, and their supervisor condoned the assault. His dental and facial injuries also establish that the force was not trivial. Wilson v. Seiter requires an objective constitutional deprivation, but its treatment of confinement conditions does not impose a separate significant-medical-injury requirement on these established acts of violence. Estelle v. Gamble supplies the prohibition on unnecessary and wanton pain while retaining its distinct medical-care requirements. The contrary injury floor would excuse serious custodial violence because the victim happened to withstand it. The Court leaves a comprehensive mental-state rule for other force settings, psychological injury alone, individual defenses and lawful measurement of damages undecided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Whitley v. Albers*, 475 U.S. 312 — applied as a sufficient ground on these findings; its disturbance rule remains in force, but a universal extension receives no majority here.
- *Wilson v. Seiter*, 501 U.S. 294 — distinguished by the character of the constitutional wrong; the objective-deprivation requirement survives without becoming a significant-injury floor for this beating.
- *Estelle v. Gamble*, 429 U.S. 97 — applied for unnecessary and wanton pain; serious medical need and deliberate indifference remain the medical-care rules.

#### Limits and Questions Not Reached

Part C rejects the alternate characterization on this record: the undisturbed supervisory-condonation finding and the granted question prevent affirmance on an assumed isolated, unauthorized assault. It announces no general rule defining punishment. Part B’s proposed universal Whitley standard is a four-Justice position. Combining the separate writings under Marks supplies no universal mental-state rule: the six-Justice narrow holding already controls this case, and the other writings do not adopt a common broader rationale. The ordinary exclusion of trivial force is not converted into an exhaustive rule for all unusual forms of cruelty.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Effective February 25, 1992, significant injury is not an additional necessary element for the established nontrivial malicious custodial beating described in Part A. Before this decision, Whitley directly governed disturbance-related force and Wilson governed conditions; afterward, the six-Justice application resolves this nontrivial beating without adopting a universal mental-state standard. The existing rules for medical care, conditions, legitimate discipline, personal defenses and compensatory proof remain distinct. No new mandatory test for every prison-force setting is created.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

O’Connor, joined by White, Kennedy and Souter in Part B, would apply Whitley’s malicious-and-sadistic standard to all prison force claims. That formulation has four votes. Stevens would reserve that heightened formulation for serious disturbances and otherwise apply the unnecessary-and-wanton-pain inquiry, although he agrees the heightened standard is met here. Blackmun likewise rejects a universal extension and rejects an injury floor that would shield intentional cruelty. Thomas, joined by Scalia, would require a serious objective deprivation as well as culpable intent; his view does not require permanent or visible physical injury in every case.

Dates and procedural background: [503 U.S. 1 (1992), pp. 1–29](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-503/pdf/USREPORTS-503-1.pdf).

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court merits review ends with reversal and remand. The Fifth Circuit must dispose of the case consistently with the intact findings and without its significant-injury bar. No amount above the existing $800 award is ordered; no immunity defense or broader injunction is adjudicated. No Supreme Court implementation jurisdiction is retained.

**End of entry: Hudson v. McMillian, merits decision, February 25, 1992.**

### Sutton v. United Air Lines, Inc., No. 97-1943

Merits decision, June 22, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 28, 1999; decided June 22. On certiorari to the Tenth Circuit, 130 F.3d 893, which affirmed dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6). The questions concern mitigating measures in the original ADA's actual-disability definition, the proper weight of the EEOC Appendix guidance, and the sufficiency of the pleaded regarded-as-working theory.

The twin applicants allege uncorrected right-eye vision of 20/200 or worse and left-eye vision of 20/400 or worse, corrected vision of 20/20, commercial experience and first-class FAA medical certificates. United requires uncorrected visual acuity of 20/100 or better for the hiring at issue. The complaint alleges actual disability substantially limiting seeing and a perceived disability substantially limiting working. The lower court's assumption that United rejected the applicants from all its pilot positions is maintained for this appeal. Those allegations and assumptions are not adjudicated findings of unsafe flight, employer intent or universal airline qualification.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed in part and vacated in part.**

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Actual disability — seeing (Parts I–II) | Vacated and remanded, 5–4 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Reassessment without beneficial correction eliminating the underlying limitation; individual substantial limitation remains to be established |
| Regarded as substantially limited in working (Part III) | Affirmed, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | — | Regarded-as-working dismissal stands |

The judgment is affirmed in part and vacated in part. The actual-disability claim returns for reassessment without treating beneficial correction as eliminating the underlying limitation; the applicants must establish individual substantial limitation. Qualification, accommodation and legitimate safety defenses are resolved on their own elements. The regarded-as-working dismissal stands. No automatic hiring, reinstatement, accommodation, damages or new evidentiary proceeding is ordered, and no airline hiring criterion is categorically validated.

#### Opinions and Joins

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|

| Opinion of the Court, Part III | Stone-Zsela | Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | Affirms the regarded-as-working dismissal | Perceived-class rule and dismissal application |
| Opinion concurring in Part III and dissenting from Parts I–II | Kennedy | O’Connor, Scalia and Thomas | Would affirm actual-disability dismissal; joins regarded-as affirmance | Actual-measures-used rule and actual-disability application; agrees with Part III’s rule and application |
| Concurrence | Stevens | Breyer | Joins the Court in full | Persuasive agency guidance without making deference a ground of the Court’s decision |

Parts I–II have five direct votes; Part III has nine. No plurality or narrowest-ground synthesis is necessary.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Holding 1— Beneficial mitigation does not eliminate underlying-impairment coverage

**Holding and operative rule:** Under the original 42 U.S.C. §12102(2)(A), successful mitigating measures do not erase the underlying impairment: whether it substantially limits a major life activity is assessed without treating beneficial correction or medication as eliminating that limitation. The protections of the Americans with Disabilities Act do not disappear because an individual has successfully treated or mitigated an impairment. The existence of a qualifying disability must be distinguished from the individual’s capacity to perform particular employment duties. Effective treatment does not itself establish that the underlying impairment is outside the statute’s protection. Individual proof of substantial limitation remains necessary; this is not a categorical rule that every eyeglass wearer is disabled.

**Authority:** Part I of Chief Justice Stone-Zsela’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. All five adopt the rule, individualized-proof requirement and separation of coverage from qualification, accommodation and safety.

**Controlling explanation:** The statute asks whether the underlying impairment substantially limits a major life activity of the individual. A person does not cease to have an impairment because lenses make ordinary activity possible. Making protection disappear when treatment enables work would merge disability with the separate question whether a person can perform the employment duties with accommodation. The original Act separates those inquiries. It still requires a substantial limitation in this individual, not merely a diagnosis or the use of glasses.

Arline’s functional protection supports examining the impairment without confusing coverage with qualification or justified exclusion. Bragdon supplies the individualized sequence of impairment, major activity and substantial limitation; it expressly reserved mitigation, so its recognition of a substantial treated reproductive risk does not resolve this question against the underlying-impairment rule. The twins’ severe uncorrected myopia and dependence on lenses must therefore be assessed without corrected acuity eliminating the underlying limitation. Their individual substantiality remains for reassessment.

The present-function argument properly insists on individual proof, but it treats the effectiveness of an aid as the absence of the limitation the aid addresses. Actual corrected performance belongs in the separate qualification, accommodation and safety inquiries. The Court adopts this construction from the original statute, without making agency deference its ground. The EEOC Appendix’s interpretation does not require a ruling on general definitional authority or the binding force of every regulatory or Appendix statement; Haggar’s actual-authority and statutory-fit requirements remain intact.

##### Holding 2 — The actual-disability claim requires reassessment under the underlying-impairment rule

**Holding and operative rule:** The dismissal of the actual-disability claim is vacated and the claim remanded for reassessment without beneficial mitigation eliminating the underlying visual limitation, with individual proof that the impairment substantially limits seeing. Qualification, reasonable accommodation and legitimate safety defenses must be resolved on their own elements; the remand awards no automatic hiring, reinstatement or accommodation.

**Authority:** Part II of the Court’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. These five adopt the vacatur and bounded reassessment; O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas dissent from this component.

**Controlling explanation:** The complaint’s allegations of severe uncorrected myopia and continued dependence on corrective lenses must be considered under the statutory rule just stated. Normal corrected seeing does not itself defeat the claim. The Court vacates the dismissal so the lower courts can assess individual substantial limitation under the proper rule; it does not conclusively determine disability or the sufficiency of every allegation.

The FAA certificates and commercial experience concern the applicants’ performance and qualifications; neither establishes the separate disability element nor invalidates every employer criterion. United may contest qualification, accommodation and a supported safety defense, but those distinct inquiries cannot erase the coverage question. Arline supports the separation of disability from qualification and justified exclusion; Bragdon supplies individualized assessment and preserves the distinct objective-risk inquiry within its own setting. No record-of claim, regarded-as-seeing theory, finding of dangerousness or ultimate employment entitlement is supplied.

##### Holding 3 — The alleged perceived restriction is confined to a specialized job category

**Holding and operative rule:** Assuming working is a major life activity, a regarded-as-working claim requires an alleged perceived restriction in a class or broad range of jobs; exclusion from the specialized global-airline-pilot category alleged here does not suffice. The pleaded exclusion remains insufficient even under the assumption that United rejected these applicants for all of its pilot positions.

**Authority:** Part III of the Court’s opinion commands all nine Justices: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Each joins both the perceived-class rule and the dismissal application.

**Controlling explanation:** Perceiving an impairment is not necessarily perceiving a substantial limitation of working. The distinction reflected in 29 C.F.R. §1630.2(j) and (l) separates a class or broad range of jobs from a particular specialized occupation. The alleged perception concerns the global-airline-pilot category, while other pilot work provides context for why that category is specialized. The Court does not hold that actual employment elsewhere conclusively proves United's belief, require rejection by every employer, or decide disputed facts about its mental state. The complaint fails because the alleged perceived limitation does not reach the required breadth, even accepting the favorable scope assumption below.

This is a coverage ruling on the theory actually presented. It does not decide whether working invariably qualifies as a major life activity, whether every airline position shares the same qualification, whether United's criterion would be justified against a covered applicant, or whether a different properly presented perceived limitation would satisfy the Act.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **School Board of Nassau County v. Arline:** Applies its functional protection and individualized assessment while keeping disability distinct from qualification and supported exclusion; its actual setting and limits remain intact.
- **Bragdon v. Abbott:** Applies its individualized disability framework and resolves the reserved mitigation question through the underlying-impairment rule. Its reproduction holding, substantial residual-risk application and distinct Title III objective direct-threat rules remain intact.
- **United States v. Haggar Apparel Co. and Chevron:** Preserve actual delegation and statutory fit as conditions of agency deference. The Court rests on its construction of the original ADA and makes no general decision about ADA definitional authority or the binding force of every EEOC regulation or Appendix statement.

#### Law After Decision

Under the original ADA, beneficial mitigating measures do not eliminate an underlying impairment’s substantial limitation for actual-disability coverage. Individual substantiality remains required, and coverage is distinct from qualification, reasonable accommodation and legitimate safety standards. This resolves Bragdon’s express mitigation reservation without automatic coverage for every corrected condition. The actual-seeing claim returns for reassessment under that rule.

Assuming working is a major life activity, the regarded-as theory presented here also fails because the alleged perception concerns a specialized global-airline-pilot category rather than a class or broad range of jobs. Separate coverage branches, individual proof, qualification and lawful defenses retain their distinct elements. No precedent is overruled; no general delegation or airline-safety rule is decided.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Kennedy, joined by O’Connor, Scalia and Thomas, concurring in Part III and dissenting from Parts I–II.** They would assess substantial limitation with the measures the individual actually uses, including beneficial and adverse effects. The statutory requirement concerns this person’s present limitation; treating effective lenses as absent substitutes a hypothetical untreated condition for actual function. This does not erase an impairment or exclude every treated condition: residual limitations and treatment burdens can still be substantial. Bragdon found substantial reproductive risk even with treatment and reserved the general mitigation question. Here the complaint alleges normal corrected seeing and no additional substantial limitation inconsistent with that function. Constant dependence on lenses does not supply the missing present limitation under their construction. The protection and accommodation structure cannot eliminate the threshold Congress separately enacted, and contrary Appendix guidance cannot override that statutory reading under Haggar. They would affirm the actual-disability dismissal without deciding general EEOC power, qualification or safety. They join Part III’s perceived-class rule and regarded-as affirmance.

**Stevens, joined by Breyer, concurring.** They join the Court in full. The agency guidance and legislative explanations persuasively reinforce the underlying-impairment reading because the statute separates protected status from the ability to perform work with assistance. Successful management should not itself defeat protection against impairment-based exclusion. Haggar’s requirements of actual authority and statutory fit do not prevent consideration of that persuasive reasoning, and no conclusion that all Appendix language has binding force is needed. Individual substantiality, the bounded reassessment, the independent regarded-as affirmance and ordinary qualification and safety defenses remain as the Court states them.

#### Record Limits

The governing provisions are the original 42 U.S.C. §§12102(2), 12111(8) and 12112, with the relevant 29 C.F.R. §1630.2 definitions and Appendix guidance. FAA certification does not establish the validity of every employer qualification, and an employer's safety contention is not a finding of actual risk.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The actual-disability portion of the Tenth Circuit’s judgment is vacated and remanded for reassessment under the underlying-impairment rule. The regarded-as-working dismissal is affirmed. Supreme Court merits review is complete, subject to ordinary mandate procedure. The lower courts address individual substantial limitation and any properly presented qualification, accommodation or safety issues on their own elements; no employment award, automatic trial, new-evidence entitlement or amended pleading is directed.

**End of entry: Sutton v. United Air Lines, Inc., Merits decision, June 22, 1999.**

### Olmstead v. L.C., No. 98-536

Merits decision, June 22, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 21, 1999. On writ of certiorari to the Eleventh Circuit, reviewing 138 F.3d 893.

The question is whether Title II of the Americans with Disabilities Act requires community treatment and habilitation when appropriate treatment and habilitation can also be furnished in a state institution. Review is limited to petition question 1; the separate question concerning Congress's Fourteenth Amendment §5 power is outside the grant.

L.C. and E.W. sought prospective relief after remaining in a state psychiatric hospital when the State's treatment professionals concluded that appropriately supported community care could meet their needs. Both sought that care. They had received community placements by the appeal; the continuing injunction and disputed service obligations preserve the controversy. The Eleventh Circuit sustained the integration determination and remanded the fundamental-alteration defense. This is a suit by two individuals, not a class action.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and the other eight Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

| Component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing or other positions | Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Statutory integration determination and supported relevant-time suitability premise | Affirmed, 6–3 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Kennedy would vacate for a distinct discrimination inquiry; Scalia and Thomas would reverse the asserted statutory basis | The bounded integration determination remains; no new finding of present medical condition |
| Defense-related portion of the reviewed appellate judgment | Vacated and remanded, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Scalia and Thomas would remove the statutory remedial basis on their contrary liability ground | Reconsider the defense and consequent prospective relief under the controlling resource rule; Stevens agrees in this judgment only, and Kennedy relies on his separate ground |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. Unjustified isolation can be disability discrimination in the administration of existing services

**Holding and operative rule.** Unjustified institutional isolation of a qualified person with a disability is discrimination under Title II. Community care is required when the State's treatment professionals determine that it is appropriate, the individual does not oppose it, and it can reasonably be accommodated considering available resources and the needs of other persons with mental disabilities, without fundamentally altering the State's service system. An actual nondisabled person needing the identical service is not an indispensable comparator.

**Authority.** Part I, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer, directly controls the rule, statutory explanation, and individual limits.

**Controlling explanation.** Section 12132 governs how a public entity administers its services. Yeskey forecloses an institutional exception while preserving each liability element. Congress expressly identified isolation and segregation and, in §12134, directed regulations consistent with an existing integration requirement. Sections 35.130(d) and (b)(7) implement that direction through appropriate integration and reasonable modification with a fundamental-alteration limit.

The disadvantage here is unnecessary isolation imposed as the condition of obtaining existing needed services. Such isolation perpetuates assumptions that affected people cannot or should not participate in community life and restricts ordinary family, social, educational, work, and cultural participation. This explanation requires neither individual animus nor proof that every employee embraced a stereotype. It does not condemn medically appropriate institutional care.

Choate recognizes that discrimination can arise without hostile intent and that meaningful access may require accommodation, while rejecting a general guarantee of adequate health benefits. Traynor's refusal to extend every benefit across disability categories does not decide the segregated delivery of existing services. O'Connor and Oncale reinforce the distinction between a protected population and prohibited treatment; their employment proof frameworks are not transplanted here.

The Department's consistent, reasoned construction warrants respect within actual statutory authority. Auer and Haggar preserve textual fit and delegated limits; interpretation establishes no Georgia fact. The Court need not decide an unpresented facial-validity challenge or make full Chevron deference indispensable to this result.

##### 2. Professional suitability and individual choice constrain the integration obligation

**Holding and operative rule.** The supported relevant-time finding that appropriately provided community care could meet these respondents' needs remains intact. The ADA does not require an individual to accept an unwanted accommodation or require a placement contrary to a reasonable professional assessment of that person's needs; appropriate institutional services remain available when those needs require them. This decision guarantees no particular residence and displaces no neutral eligibility requirement.

**Authority.** The same six Justices join Part I's application and limits.

**Controlling explanation.** Earlier differing assessments, inadequately supported placements, and an intervening physical illness do not erase the lower court's supported finding at the relevant times. Conversely, that finding does not establish uninterrupted professional agreement, suitability for every community setting, or present clinical conditions. Bragdon and Arline support individual assessment grounded in actual needs; their principles do not make professional title or sincere belief a conclusive statutory justification. No case involving continuing contrary treating-professional recommendations is decided.

Section 35.130(e)(1) preserves the individual's choice to decline an accommodation. Its separate guardian provision does not authorize refusal of food, water, medical treatment, or medical services. The Court neither orders compulsory deinstitutionalization nor requires community treatment unsupported by necessary care. Actual placements and the continuing service obligations determine the appropriate prospective relief below.

##### 3. Fundamental alteration requires consideration of the relevant service system

**Holding and operative rule.** The State may demonstrate under §35.130(b)(7) that an otherwise necessary modification would fundamentally alter its service, program, or activity. The inquiry must consider available resources, the range of services provided to others with mental disabilities, and equitable administration of those services; it cannot be reduced to the additional expense for these two claimants compared with the entire mental-health budget. A bare shortage assertion does not establish the defense, and a lower average community cost does not defeat it.

**Authority.** Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer, directly controls the full rule and corrective remand. Kennedy expressly adopts only the narrower non-isolated-cost proposition identified in Opinion Topology. Stevens accepts the remand judgment without joining this rationale.

**Controlling explanation.** Davis distinguishes reasonable accommodation from fundamental alteration. The regulation makes that distinction an actual limit on the integration duty, with the demonstration assigned to the public entity. Fixed institutional overhead, transition expense, continuing institutional needs, suitable community capacity, available funding and lawful transfers, and legitimate priorities can affect what a requested modification does to the relevant service system. An award to particular plaintiffs may impair equitable obligations to others even when their isolated expense is modest. Conversely, defining the existing arrangement as the program cannot immunize avoidable segregation.

The Eleventh Circuit acknowledged other relevant factors and did not expressly exclude all wider effects. Its operative formulation nevertheless repeatedly centered the inquiry on these plaintiffs' additional expenditure measured against the overall budget. That formulation does not adequately account for the distribution and nature of services. Defining the duty's express limitation is fairly included in the granted question; the correction concerns the appellate judgment under review, not independent review of the later district ruling.

The State's concession about the two plaintiffs viewed alone does not waive the broader defense. Federal matching authority is not itself funded capacity, and a per-person cost comparison is not complete marginal accounting. Georgia must establish its defense; the Court makes no finding that it has done so. A placement plan and actual priorities may bear on the inquiry, but no majority adopts the separate concurrence's proposition that its specified plan and queue conditions alone are sufficient.

**Reserved questions.** The Court decides no §5 validity question, independent immunity issue, damages claim, unresolved constitutional claim, general entitlement to every desired medical benefit, or classwide institutional-closure remedy. It prescribes no numerical budget test, compulsory planning procedure, universal waiting period, or automatic placement priority.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Pennsylvania Department of Corrections v. Yeskey:** Applies comprehensive public-entity coverage while preserving the distinction between coverage and actual discrimination, modification, and relief.
- **Alexander v. Choate:** Applies meaningful-access and non-animus principles; its rejection of a general guarantee of adequate health benefits remains.
- **Southeastern Community College v. Davis:** Applies the distinction between reasonable accommodation and fundamental alteration through Title II's express regulatory defense.
- **Traynor v. Turnage:** Distinguishes extension of a benefit across disability categories from unjustifiably segregated delivery of existing services; no rule equalizing every benefit is adopted.
- **School Board of Nassau County v. Arline and Bragdon v. Abbott:** Apply individual assessment and objective support; neither supplies an automatic placement entitlement or an unreviewable professional veto.
- **Auer v. Robbins and United States v. Haggar Apparel Co.:** Preserve textual fit, actual delegated authority, and the separation of legal interpretation from factual proof; no broader deference or facial-validity question is decided.
- **O'Connor v. Consolidated Coin Caterers Corp. and Oncale v. Sundowner Offshore Services, Inc.:** Supply bounded analogical support against an indispensable outside-group comparator while preserving independent proof of the prohibited treatment and cause; their employment standards retain their distinct scope.
- **Cedar Rapids Community School District v. Garret F.:** Its refusal to add an affordability exception to IDEA's specified duty does not eliminate Title II's express fundamental-alteration defense.
- **Youngberg v. Romeo and Heller v. Doe:** Their constitutional treatment and commitment-procedure rules remain distinct; neither is converted into the complete statutory integration standard.

#### Law After Decision

Title II reaches unjustified isolation in the administration of existing state services under the professional-suitability, individual-choice, and reasonable-modification conditions stated above. The State bears the regulation's fundamental-alteration demonstration, assessed against its relevant service obligations and resources. The reviewed expenditure-centered formulation cannot govern that inquiry.

The four-Justice sufficient-plan rule and Stone-Zsela's additional plan-proof qualification are noncontrolling. No majority decides that a working plan and reasonably moving queue alone settle the defense. Kennedy's distinct discrimination theory does not replace the Court's directly majority-supported integration ground, and his limited resource agreement supplies no additional join in the full plan proposition. No fractured-opinion combination is needed for the controlling holdings.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**O'Connor, joined by Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer, concurring on plan sufficiency.** A State meets the reasonable-modification standard in the stated circumstances by demonstrating a comprehensive, effectively working plan to place qualified persons in less restrictive settings and a waiting list moving at a reasonable pace not controlled by an effort to keep institutions full. A court then may not advance a litigant by displacing persons properly ahead. The four require actual operation and reasonable movement, not a paper plan or inert list; they require no separate additional disruption showing once all stated conditions are proved. They find no Georgia compliance and prescribe no universal waiting period or required planning procedure. Their position supplies an important proposed limit but lacks majority support.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring.** He joins both Court parts and both component judgments. A working plan and lawful queue may supply substantial evidence, but the State must connect them to fundamental alteration from the requested modification. Plan compliance alone does not conclusively resolve each person's entitlement. His position preserves actual resource constraints and proper priorities without accepting generalized fiscal preference or indefinite postponement; this additional qualification is not the Court's holding.

**Stevens, concurring.** He joins the integration holding and accepts the confined mixed judgment, although he would prefer affirming the existing resource remand. Its nonexclusive factors already allow relevant program evidence, while the later restriction and defense ruling belong in their own appeal. His judgment agreement does not adopt the Court's corrective rationale or the separate sufficient-plan rule.

**Kennedy, joined by Breyer in Part I only.** Their clinical and administrative cautions warn against inadequately staffed placements and pressures to discharge people into settings unable to provide necessary care. In Part II, Kennedy alone would vacate the liability determination for an inquiry into discriminatory treatment, potentially comparing treatment of medical problems of comparable seriousness and unjustified institutional conditions imposed on people with mental disabilities. They nevertheless reject categorical immunity for disability-only programs and an indispensable actual nondisabled comparator. Their objection concerns the asserted service obligation. They would reverse that statutory ground, remove its dependent remedial support, and preserve unresolved claims and lawful treatment of existing relief. They find neither a legitimate Georgia queue nor exhausted resources and authorize no unlawful confinement.

### PGA TOUR, Inc. v. Martin, No. 00-24

Merits decision, May 29, 2001

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 17, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, which affirmed the District of Oregon’s cart-accommodation injunction. The Court decides whether Title III covers Martin’s tournament access and whether his necessary cart fundamentally alters the competition.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 7–2.** The Ninth Circuit judgment sustaining Martin's cart injunction remains effective.

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Scalia and Thomas.

Martin may use the required cart while remaining subject to ordinary skill qualifications and other valid competition rules. No damages, automatic tour membership, or new trial is ordered.

#### Opinion Topology

Stone-Zsela delivered the opinion of the Court, joined throughout by Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Thomas filed a dissent, joined by Scalia.

#### Holdings

##### Competitors receive covered golf-course services

**Holding and operative rule.** A golf-course operator offering paid qualifying opportunities and tournament participation provides covered services and privileges to competitors as well as spectators under Title III of the Americans with Disabilities Act. An eligible entrant does not lose that recipient relationship because his play also contributes to the entertainment provided to others; employment status is unnecessary to this Title III holding.

**Authority.** The signed opinion by Stone-Zsela is joined throughout by the other members of the seven-Justice coalition identified in Opinion Topology. All seven directly join both holdings and the remedy.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the statutory golf-course category and the protection of goods, services, facilities, privileges, advantages, and accommodations encompass the opportunity the Tour offers here. Section 12181(7)(L) includes golf courses affecting commerce. Section 12182(a) protects an individual's full and equal enjoyment of the operator's offered services. Martin pays for access to a qualifying process and seeks access to the resulting competition. Providing athletic performance to spectators does not make those offered opportunities disappear.

The clients-or-customers language in section 12182(b)(1)(A)(iv) expressly applies to clauses (i) through (iii) of that subparagraph; it is not an enacted definition of every protected relationship throughout Title III. Even under the Tour's proposed recipient limitation, Martin receives the paid qualifying opportunity. The Court therefore need not decide a universal rule about workers, independent contractors, or every sporting relationship. Bragdon v. Abbott supplies individualized examination of the statutory conditions; Cleveland v. Policy Management Systems Corp. supplies the requirement to address those conditions rather than substitute a categorical label. Coverage leaves the separate accommodation requirements to be satisfied.

##### Necessary modification and fundamental alteration

**Holding and operative rule.** Under section 12182(b)(2)(A)(ii), an operator must make a reasonable modification necessary for a disabled individual's access unless the operator demonstrates that the modification would fundamentally alter the offered service. In a competition, alteration can arise by changing an essential competitive feature, even uniformly, or by giving the particular competitor a material competitive advantage through an otherwise peripheral change; the supported findings establish neither effect from Martin's cart, so the modification is required.

**Authority.** The same seven Justices directly join this rule, its two distinct alteration routes, its individual application, and affirmance of the injunction.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that an existing uniform rule is evidence of how a competition operates, but its existence does not conclusively establish fundamental alteration. Otherwise the enacted duty to modify policies would have no work to do. The Tour's judgment deserves consideration; it does not replace the statutory defense. The court found after trial that shot-making is the essential competitive task, walking is not essential in this tournament setting, carts are used in related qualifying and tour settings, and Martin experiences greater fatigue even with a cart than other players ordinarily experience walking. These findings are reviewed for clear error and support the conclusion that a cart changes neither the essential task nor the competitive balance to his advantage.

Bragdon supplies objective, individual evaluation rather than unsupported generalization. Albertson's, Inc. v. Kirkingburg preserves a particular federal safety criterion; it grants no automatic exemption to a private tournament rule. Olmstead v. L.C. preserves genuine program limits, but its Title II resource defense does not replace Title III's enacted alteration inquiry. Sutton v. United Air Lines, Inc. preserves individual substantial-limitation analysis; Martin's disability and need are established under that inquiry. The Court does not prescribe favorable outcomes, equal stamina, automatic membership, or waiver of essential skill qualifications. Martin must satisfy the ordinary qualifications and other valid rules. The operator bears its alteration defense after necessity and reasonableness are established; no auxiliary-aid undue-burden standard is imported.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Bragdon v. Abbott: applied for objective individual statutory assessment; its direct-threat inquiry is not substituted for the distinct fundamental-alteration defense.
- Cleveland v. Policy Management Systems Corp.: applied for separate examination of statutory conditions rather than categorical labels.
- Sutton v. United Air Lines, Inc.: preserved as the governing individualized disability rule; this case does not change it or decide disability from diagnosis alone.
- Albertson's, Inc. v. Kirkingburg: distinguished because its federal safety-standard holding does not exempt private sporting rules from Title III.
- Olmstead v. L.C.: used as a bounded comparison preserving genuine program limits; its Title II defense is not transplanted into Title III.
- Board of Trustees of the University of Alabama v. Garrett: concerns Title I enforcement against state employers and supplies no limit on this private Title III claim.

#### Law After Decision

Title III reaches the Tour's offered qualifying and tournament opportunities for competitors. Necessary reasonable modification of competition rules is required unless the operator demonstrates an alteration of an essential feature or a material competitive advantage in the individual application. The cart injunction is sustained on the trial findings; normal skill qualification, genuine essential rules, and other valid conduct requirements remain enforceable. This is a new application of the enacted access and alteration rules, not a general entitlement to carts or tournament admission.

#### Separate Writings

**Thomas, dissenting, joined by Scalia.** The dissent would reverse because the professional tournament is the service provided to spectators, while competitors produce that service. Payment to enter a qualifying process does not, in its view, turn entry into a profession into consumption of the professional contest. It acknowledges the limited textual scope of the clients-or-customers clause and relies instead on the particular relationship and service identified by the statute. Alternatively, even assuming coverage, it would find fundamental alteration: a uniform walking rule is part of the contest being offered, and excusing one player changes that contest. Relying on the distinction between access to an existing service and a compelled different service reflected in its Olmstead position, the dissent rejects judicial identification of a sport's essence and comparative fatigue as the means of defining the offered competition. It disputes neither Martin's impairment nor the trial finding of substantial fatigue. Its grounds do not decide an employment cause or make every private rule immune from the ADA.

#### Source Notes

[The official report](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-532/pdf/USREPORTS-532-661.pdf) supplies the argument date, proceedings below, medical and trial findings, and competition practices. [Title III’s enacted accommodation provision](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2000-title42/pdf/USCODE-2000-title42-chap126-subchapIII-sec12182.pdf) supplies the distinct modification obligation, alteration defense, and limited customer clause. Findings about Martin’s fatigue and advantage are individual trial findings, not universal statutory facts.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The affirmed injunction remains operative. The Tour must permit Martin’s necessary cart use under the injunction, while he must meet ordinary qualification and other valid competition conditions. No additional appellate remand is directed.

**End of entry: PGA TOUR, Inc. v. Martin, Merits decision, May 29, 2001.**

### Graham v. Collins, No. 91-7580

Merits decision, January 25, 1993

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 14, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the Fifth Circuit sitting en banc, 950 F.2d 1009, affirming denial of federal habeas relief. The questions are whether Teague bars Graham’s claim and whether Texas’s three submitted special issues permitted effective use of youth, background and positive-character mitigation. This decision and Herrera v. Collins are issued on the same day, without a sequence between them.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses denial of sentencing relief on the youth and background grounds and remands for a conditional writ directed to the death sentence. The murder conviction remains undisturbed.

| Component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Teague and the youth/background sentencing claim | Reverse denial of relief, 5–4 | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter | White, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Conditional writ: Texas must provide an adequate new sentencing proceeding within a reasonable district-court period or replace the death sentence with a lawful noncapital sentence |
| Positive character as a separately sufficient ground for relief | No independent relief on this component, nine supporting disposition on different grounds | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | No opposing votes | Youth/background relief remains; no separate additional remedy. Stone-Zsela supports this component in the judgment only |

The new sentencer must have an effective route for all relevant mitigation, but no particular weight or favorable sentence is dictated. No guilt retrial or release from independent lawful custody is ordered.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Parts I–III and V | Souter | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor | Reverse denial of youth/background sentencing relief and order a conditional writ | Teague, youth, background and conditional sentencing relief; five join these propositions in full |
| Part IV, positive-character application | Souter | Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor | Deny independent relief on this component | Four find no independent material culpability use requiring further relief on this record; no fifth rationale join |
| Concurrence | Stevens | Author alone | Joins Souter throughout | Individual mitigation complements safeguards against arbitrary and discriminatory sentencing |
| Dissent | White | Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Would affirm under Teague | No new-rule exception applies on their view |
| Separate dissent | Thomas | Author alone | Also joins White | Would reject Penry’s broader mitigation requirement and preserve reasonable legislative structuring |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The youth and background claims apply law settled before finality

**Controlling proposition:** Teague does not bar Graham's claim that Texas's submitted sentencing questions prevented effective use of his youth and unstable background as material mitigation independent of deliberateness or future danger. Jurek's conditional approval, Lockett and Eddings dictated that functional requirement before his conviction became final on September 10, 1984; no new procedural-rule exception is created.

**Authority:** Parts I–III and V of Souter's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens and O’Connor; five Justices adopt this prefinality-law holding, its application to youth and background, and the conditional sentencing remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that applying an existing protection to these facts does not announce a new rule. Teague and Butler protect reasonable reliance on law at finality; they do not require an earlier case with identical evidence. Jurek approved the Texas scheme on the understanding that relevant mitigation could receive effective consideration. Lockett rejected legal barriers to mitigating aspects of character, record and offense, and Eddings expressly protected youth and disturbed background. Those decisions preceded Graham's final judgment. Penry later applied the same duty when the special issues failed to express a material mitigating judgment; it did not create that duty. The State's reliance on Jurek is substantial, but facial approval subject to effective mitigation cannot authorize its material exclusion in a particular application. Franklin's treatment of prison conduct and Saffle's rejection of unrestricted sympathy do not negate the preexisting requirement. Stringer confirms that a later application need not create new law merely because judges disagree. The Court reaches these claims without invoking either Teague exception.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S. 288, and Butler v. McKellar, 494 U.S. 407 — applied; the relevant result must be dictated at finality, not merely supported by a later plausible extension.
- Jurek v. Texas, 428 U.S. 262 — preserved as qualified approval; it does not immunize every application of the special issues.
- Lockett v. Ohio, 438 U.S. 586, and Eddings v. Oklahoma, 455 U.S. 104 — applied as the pre-1984 effective-mitigation rule.
- Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302 — applied as recognition of that existing rule, not as law inserted retroactively into 1984.
- Stringer v. Black, March 9, 1992 — applied at its existing Teague scope; disagreement alone does not establish novelty.
- Franklin v. Lynaugh, 487 U.S. 164, and Saffle v. Parks, 494 U.S. 484 — distinguished; evidence whose mitigating use fits the questions, and unrestricted sympathy, differ from material culpability mitigation excluded by them.

##### Youth requires an effective route for its independent mitigating force

**Controlling proposition:** When the record supports a material mitigating judgment about culpability that the authorized sentencing answers cannot express, a reasonable likelihood that the submitted mechanism prevents that use requires an effective corrective instruction or procedure. Graham's youth at seventeen has that independent significance notwithstanding deliberate conduct or predicted future danger, and the three submitted special issues provide no adequate route for it here.

**Authority:** Parts II and V of Souter's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens and O’Connor; five Justices adopt this independently sufficient ground for sentencing relief and its conditional remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a prediction of future violence does not answer every question about a young offender's culpability. Eddings recognizes immaturity, inexperience and undeveloped judgment as material mitigation. A juror could accept those considerations while finding that Graham deliberately killed and might remain dangerous. Jurek's approval therefore does not end the inquiry: the permitted answers must actually allow the material mitigating judgment. The provocation question supplies no route for youth's independent significance either. Texas correctly observes that youth may predict rehabilitation, and the jury could consider that prediction. But permitting one material use does not authorize exclusion of another. Penry confirms this distinction between admitting evidence and allowing its mitigating effect. Boyde's reasonable-likelihood formulation assesses the submitted mechanism; the substantive duty remains the prefinality rule. The Court requires an effective sentencing vehicle, not favorable weight, acquittal or an unrestricted mercy question. It announces no categorical prohibition on executing all seventeen-year-old offenders and does not decide the constitutionality of Texas's later statute.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Eddings v. Oklahoma, 455 U.S. 104 — applied to youth's independent culpability significance; jurors may assign weight but cannot be legally prevented from giving it effect.
- Jurek v. Texas, 428 U.S. 262 — its conditional framework remains; this defective application does not establish facial invalidity.
- Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302 — applied beyond a permanent-handicap limitation; a mitigating trait need not have only aggravating relevance to future danger to require an effective vehicle.
- Boyde v. California, 494 U.S. 370 — applied as the reasonable-likelihood method of assessing instructional operation, not as the source of a new substantive rule at 1984 finality.

##### The submitted background evidence also requires effective consideration

**Controlling proposition:** A capital sentencing system may not prevent a jury from acting on the material culpability significance it finds in a defendant's disturbed upbringing merely because that evidence also bears on future dangerousness or lacks a uniquely severe permanent handicap. The evidence of Graham's mother's recurring mental illness and hospitalizations and his shifting family care supports such a mitigating judgment, which the submitted special issues cannot adequately express; this independently requires the same sentencing remedy.

**Authority:** Parts III and V of Souter's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens and O’Connor; five Justices adopt this independent background-evidence ground and the conditional sentencing remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the sentencing jury must have a lawful way to act on the material mitigating significance of this upbringing. Eddings protects consideration of disturbed family background, particularly for a young defendant; it does not demand proof of a uniquely severe permanent handicap. Graham's grandmother described his mother's repeated hospitalization and his movement between family caregivers. The jury could find that history reduced his culpability without denying that the killing was deliberate or that future violence was probable. The provocation question does not express that judgment. The State argues that the favorable testimony about his behavior shows no lasting harm and that the evidence can support rehabilitation. Those arguments properly address weight, but cannot establish that every material mitigating use fits the authorized answers. Penry requires an effective means of expressing the supported culpability judgment; admission alone is insufficient. The Court neither finds a psychiatric disability nor requires jurors to infer a particular causal effect. It requires a lawful opportunity for sentencing assessment and leaves the weight to the new sentencer.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Lockett v. Ohio, 438 U.S. 586 — applied to relevant background proffered as a basis for less than death.
- Eddings v. Oklahoma, 455 U.S. 104 — applied; disturbed upbringing cannot be excluded from mitigating effect as a matter of law.
- Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302 — applied; neither a uniquely severe permanent handicap nor complete absence of relevance to the special issues is an exclusive trigger.

##### Positive character as an independent ground

**Judgment without controlling rationale:** The separate contention that Graham's household assistance, religious observance and financial support of his children independently require additional sentencing relief is rejected by all nine Justices, on different grounds. Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor and Souter regard its asserted independent culpability significance as insubstantial on this record and its future-behavior use as available under the second issue; White, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would bar the requested mitigation relief under Teague. Stone-Zsela joins the denial of this independent component in the judgment only. These grounds do not yield a common general rule or a Marks subset; the youth and background holdings independently require sentencing relief.

**Limits:** No universal rule about positive character evidence or its value apart from the asserted culpability and future-danger uses is decided. The jury remains free to hear constitutionally relevant evidence at the new sentencing proceeding. Neither denial of this independent ground nor the sentencing remedy disturbs the murder conviction.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

No precedent is overruled. Jurek's qualified approval and the prefinality Lockett/Eddings rule are applied. Penry is not confined to uniquely severe permanent disabilities or evidence wholly lacking mitigating relevance to the special issues. Franklin's prison-conduct example and Saffle's unrestricted-sympathy claim remain distinct. Teague retains its dictated-by-prefinality-law requirement and exceptions; neither exception supplies this judgment. Sawyer's gateway and Stringer's separate weighing-cure law are unchanged.

Effective January 25, 1993, Texas's former special-issue mechanism is unconstitutional as applied to the material culpability significance of Graham's youth and disturbed background, and relief on those grounds is not new law under Teague. Lower courts must ask whether supported material mitigating use can actually receive effect, using the reasonable-likelihood instruction inquiry; mere admission or some relevance to future danger is insufficient. This is no categorical fourth-question requirement, entitlement to favorable weight, facial invalidation, or juvenile-execution prohibition. The positive-character component yields no new general controlling rule.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

White, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, would affirm because reasonable jurists in 1984 could read Jurek, Lockett and Eddings as allowing Texas to channel this mitigation through deliberateness and future dangerousness. In their view a new instruction requirement is not dictated by prefinality law; it neither removes a class of conduct or persons from punishment nor supplies a watershed accuracy procedure, so neither Teague exception saves it. They distinguish Penry's disabling evidence from Graham's evidence supporting rehabilitation and do not adopt the Court's as-applied merits rule.

Thomas also writes separately. He regards Penry's expansive account of mitigating effect as inconsistent with Furman's requirement that capital discretion be structured against arbitrariness and discrimination. He would permit reasonable state definitions of relevance and sentencing criteria while allowing defendants to present constitutionally relevant information. He would overrule Penry to that extent; the Court does not do so. His view is separate from White's sufficient Teague ground.

Stevens concurs while joining the controlling opinion. He regards narrowing eligibility, avoiding vague aggravators, proportionality limits and individualized mitigation as complementary protections. Requiring a sentencer to act on relevant individual circumstances reduces resort to irrelevant considerations such as race; it does not demand an unreasoned preference. His discussion responds to Thomas without creating an additional test or changing the conditional remedy.

Souter's Part IV, joined by Blackmun, Stevens and O’Connor, explains the limited character-evidence disposition. Ordinary chores, religious observance and support for children can inform future dangerousness through the second special issue. On the asserted theory here, their separate bearing on culpability for this offense is insubstantial. The four do not decide the value of such traits apart from the submitted culpability/future-danger arguments. Stone-Zsela joins only the denial of independent relief on that component.
