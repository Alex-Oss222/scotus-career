## Mathias: commissioners? appellate-aggrievement argument

Although the position taken by the ICC on the underlying 
complaints was upheld below, its members have been re- 
quired to defend its ruling in federal courts at every level, 
despite their claims of the absence of jurisdiction and con- 
stitutionally-grounded immunity. Throughout this litigation 
Petitioners have asserted their rights to be free from such 
federal court proceedings and from the entry against them of 
resulting judgments, whether favorable or not. Those claims 
implicate distinct but important interests of Petitioners, par- 
ticularly as officials of a sovereign State. Policy considera- 
tions thus favor deciding these questions in this case despite 
the “favorable” outcome of the proceedings below. 


While it is true that “[a] party who receives all that he has 
sought generally is not aggrieved by the judgment affording 
the relief and cannot appeal from it,” that principle is not a 
limitation grounded in Article III of the Constitution. Deposit 
Guaranty Nat'l Bank v. Roper, 445 U.S. 326, 333 (1980). 
The Court has recognized that appellate courts have discre- 
tion to consider an appeal “from an adverse ruling collateral 
to the judgment on the merits at the behest of the party who 


has prevailed on the merits, so long as that party retains a 
stake in the appeal satisfying the requirements of Art. III.” 
Id. at 334; see also Electrical Fittings Corp. v. Thomas & 
Betts Co., 307 U.S. 241, 242 (1939). The Court has thus 
permitted apneals to proceed from favorable judgments 
where “policy considerations” supported hearing the appeal, 
as long as dismissal was not required under Article III. 
Roper, 445 U.S. at 335 and n.7. 


This case presents legal and policy considerations that fa- 
vor reaching the merits of the questions presented. In this 
case Petitioners have not sought “a review of findings [they] 
deem erroneous which are not necessary to support the de- 
cree,” Electrical Fittings, 307 U.S. at 242; instead, they ap- 
pealed rulings on jurisdiction and immunity upon which the 
district court’s ability to enter judgment depended. Although 
that judgment was favorable to Petitioners on the merits of 
the ICC ruling, it did not grant the relief Petitioners sought in 


their motions to dismiss the action, the denials of which are- 


the subject of this case. See Forney v. Apfel, 524 U.S. 266, 
271 (1998) (district court order gave petitioner “only half a 
loaf. . . some, but not all, of the relief she requested; and she 
consequently can appeal the District Court’s order insofar as 
it denies her the relief she has sought.”). 


In addition, because of the manner in which the district 
court proceeded in this case, Petitioners could not have 
posed the questions presented herein without the entry of a 
judgment on the merits. The district court essentially com- 
pelled Petitioners to litigate the merits of the underlying 
complaints over their objections to being in federal court by 
deciding to rule on the merits before it considered Petition- 


ers’ motions to dismiss based on jurisdiction and immunity. 
See Jt. App. 150a. This effectively required Petitioners to 
defend their ruling without a determination of jurisdiction 
and prevented them from pursuing any interlocutory appeal, 
at least on the immunity issues under Puerto Rico Aqueduct 
& Sewer Auth. v. Metcalf & Eddy, Inc., 506 U.S. 139 (1993). 
Thus, as this case proceeded, it was impossible for Petition- 
ers to obtain a ruling on the federal courts’ power over them 
prior to that power being exercised in the issuance of a 
judgment on the merits. Cf Roper, 445 U.S. at 335 n. 7 (ap- 
peal from favorable judgment should be permitted where 
both favorable and unfavorable rulings were presented due to 
district court’s “procedural error” of considering both issues, 
citing Electrical Fittings). 


Petitioners, as officials of an agency of a sovereign State, 
have a clear stake in this appeal, well satisfying Article III. 
While the merits of the case in the lower courts turned on 
whether the ICC properly construed the underlying intercon- 
nection agreements, the questions presented in this case in- 
volve entirely collateral but equally important issues of 
statutory federal court jurisdiction and sovereign immunity. 
Petitioners maintained below that their decisions should not 
have been reviewed in federal court at all and that neither 
they nor the ICC should have been subject to a federal suit 
brought by a private party. As state officials, Petitioners 
have distinct and separate interests in being free of the bur- 
den of defending themselves or their state agency from suits 
that have been brought by private parties in federal courts 
and that seek relief potentially in excess of those courts’ ju- 
risdiction, even in cases in which that defense is ultimately 
successful. The importance of maintaining the balance be-
