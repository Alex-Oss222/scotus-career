## 23. Gulf Power — supplemental neutral law and source map

Section 224(a)(4) defines attachments by a cable television system or a provider of telecommunications service to a pole, duct, conduit or right-of-way owned or controlled by a statutory utility. Section 224(b)(1) requires just and reasonable rates, terms and conditions; subsection (b)(2) expressly confers rulemaking authority. Section 224(d)(3) makes the cable formula applicable to attachments used solely for cable service. Section 224(e) separately prescribes the telecommunications formula. The former interim rule in (d)(3), pending promulgation of the subsection (e) regulations, is not an additional presently operative coverage restriction. Sections (a) and (b) do not state that these formulas exhaust covered attachments.

The definition of telecommunications service covers offerings to the public for a fee regardless of facilities used. The wire-communications condition in the definition of utility identifies covered facilities and owners; it does not expressly exclude wireless equipment attached to an otherwise qualifying facility. The utility exclusions for railroads, cooperatively organized persons and federal or state ownership, the exclusion of incumbent local exchange carriers from the subsection (a)(5) carrier definition, and subsection (c)'s qualifying state-regulation allocation retain their actual statutory scope. No issue about those exclusions is established in this controversy. The present question does not extend coverage to every freestanding wireless tower or to property that fails the utility and attachment conditions.

Coverage, classification and lawful rate selection are distinct. The government's brief, note 10, identifies use of the cable formula for commingled attachments as a separate rate ruling not challenged in these questions. Recognizing coverage therefore need not command classification of Internet service or affirm a particular reasonable rental. The strongest contrary argument is structural: the sole-cable limitation and telecommunications formula might be read as exhaustive choices, requiring agency classification before lawful regulation. Its difficulty is that it turns limitations governing the method of regulation into an unexpressed amendment of the broader definition. A Chenery objection would be material to an actually presented rate calculation; it does not erase the agency's express reliance on (a)(4) and (b)(1) for coverage. No invented new rate challenge supplies a third judgment component.

AT&T Corp. v. Iowa Utilities Board, January 25, 1999, supplies actual FCC implementation authority alongside express statutory constraints, not approval of every communications rule. Mead, June 18, 2001, requires delegation carrying legal force before controlling interpretive deference; subsection (b)(2) and the implementing order provide the relevant mechanism here. The text can resolve coverage without depending on deference. J. E. M., December 10, 2001, supplies a current example of reading broad coverage and specialized statutory regimes together while preserving each regime's requirements; it decides no communications question. TRW and Chickasaw Nation reinforce giving precise limiting language its actual work rather than enlarging it beyond the provision containing it.

Lawful path A reverses both categorical exclusions while preserving actual rate and classification limits. Path B treats the formulas as exhaustive and requires classification or sustains an exclusion. Path A's strongest objection is that broad subsection (b) authority might defeat the word solely or prescribed cost formulas; the answer must be that mandatory formulas remain mandatory when their terms apply. Path B's strongest objection is that neither formula is written as a coverage definition and the 1996 amendment broadened (a)(4). For wireless attachments, a separate exclusion requires reading the utility's wire-use condition as a restriction on each attached service, despite the distinct definition of telecommunications service. Reversal on that component is limited to qualifying attachments.

Rule 24.09 requires a written continuance motion supported by affidavit unless the adverse party consents to an oral application. Rule 24.10 addresses absent witnesses separately: materiality and diligence; reasonable grounds to expect attendance within reasonable time; facts to be proved and absence of an available equivalent witness; good faith rather than delay; and absence not procured or consented to by the applicant. The oral-request exception in 24.09 does not dispense with 24.10's substantive information. No opponent consent is presumed from silence.

The witnesses were subpoenaed family alibi witnesses who had traveled to court and been present earlier. Counsel described the alibi in opening and sought a short continuance when they unexpectedly disappeared after the recess. Counsel believed they remained locally because two planned to attend a religious activity that evening. The judge referred to his daughter's surgery the next day and another scheduled trial the following Monday, rather than demanding written papers. The state appellate court invoked the rules. The relatives later attributed their departure to a security officer, but no tribunal had found that explanation true; it is an allegation, not a premise establishing state-caused absence or cause for default. Their prospective testimony is not accepted as true and no innocence finding follows.

Coleman v. Thompson, 501 U.S. 722 (1991), retains independent and adequate default rules and cause/prejudice or miscarriage-of-justice exceptions. The 1992 stay ruling does not enlarge that doctrine. Adequacy remains a federal question; ordinary state-law compliance remains a state question. Osborne v. Ohio, 495 U.S. 103, 123–25 (1990), supplies the distinction between a useful omitted objection and a futile repetition after adequate presentation: https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/495/103 . Neither makes procedural regularity turn on locating a prior factually identical case. Henry, James and Douglas inform the legitimate-state-interest inquiry as discussed in Osborne. Trest's December 9, 1997 unanimous opinion by Stevens preserves default as a nonjurisdictional defense and requires its actual predicates. O'Sullivan's June 7, 1999 controlling ordinary-round rule and Stevens's dissent joined by Ginsburg and Breyer distinguish real opportunities for state consideration from unnecessary repetition.

Path A finds the combined application inadequate in the exceptional midtrial circumstances and vacates/remands the federal default judgment. ## 25. Crane — supplemental neutral law and source map

Hendricks affirmed invalidation of that original commitment on an as-applied Ex Post Facto ground. It neither facially approved nor facially invalidated the Act. Breyer's non-dispositive Part I, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas among the Associates, rejected a categorical objection to diagnostic vocabulary on a record of serious recognized condition, impaired control and related dangerous conduct. Ginsburg reserved that discussion. It is evidence of their positions, not a controlling functional test. The dissent of O'Connor joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas disagreed about punitive operation while preserving genuine present predicates. Seling was unanimous on the continuing availability and demanding proof of punitive-operation claims; it did not transfer one detainee's findings to another.

Foucha's present-lawful-predicate rule prohibits indefinite psychiatric restraint on dangerousness alone after the admitted illness basis has ended. Its reservation of narrower preventive regimes matters. The strongest civil-control path requires a qualifying condition that causes sufficiently serious difficulty controlling dangerous conduct, without demanding total inability. It must distinguish the eligible committee from a dangerous but ordinary recidivist, allow assessment of the condition's nature and severity, and avoid converting a diagnosis into proof of the functional requirement. It does not demand a numeric measurement of control, a particular clinical term or proof that every act is involuntary. Kansas's statutory proof burden remains intact; no new universal jury-instruction form is selected.

The complete-incapacity alternative protects against continuing punishment through diagnostic labels, but its strongest difficulty is that complete inability is neither required by the governing holdings nor a realistic measure of civil disorder in every case. The no-separate-element alternative treats the statute's present condition, causal dangerousness and statutory procedures as sufficient without a new independent volitional finding. Its strongest objection is that diagnosis and predicted recidivism may leave no substantive line between civil detention and ordinary preventive incarceration. That dispute is separate from whether the evidence supports the necessary rule in Crane's particular proceeding.

Vacatur and remand can reject a complete-incapacity construction while requiring application of serious functional impairment under Kansas's burden. It does not automatically reinstate the trial commitment. A separate statutory-predicates disposition would reverse the complete-incapacity rule without demanding the new independent control determination. Affirming merely by importing Hendricks's punishment facts is unavailable. The grant requires answering control; a preserved punitive-operation claim retains its distinct proof and remedy and cannot displace that answer or be decided on an assumed later program. No such claim is newly created here.

The present inquiry is whether due process requires complete inability or a serious impairment linked to the qualifying condition and danger. **State:** Total inability to control behavior is neither medically realistic nor required by the statutory mental-abnormality and dangerousness predicates. Serious dysfunction may justify civil treatment without proof that every voluntary choice is impossible. **Crane:** Dangerousness and a diagnosis can otherwise become a means of continuing punishment after the ordinary criminal sentence. A substantial demonstrated impairment of control is necessary, and a control formulation cannot cure a separately proved retrospective-punishment violation. **Limits:** Neither side may assume the facts establishing the temporal reach of the prior invalidity ruling. Nor may the Court treat antisocial personality disorder as automatically sufficient or insufficient for every civil scheme. The inquiry requires actual condition, functional impairment, danger, and lawful statutory authority.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

**Complete-incapacity path:** Affirm the control requirement only if due process makes inability to control behavior the necessary distinction between civil commitment and ordinary criminal responsibility. **Plausible Holding Scope:** The rule would govern the necessary constitutional predicate; it would not adjudicate punitive operation on facts outside the grant. **Civil-control path:** For a genuinely lawful civil scheme, require proof of serious difficulty controlling conduct without demanding complete inability. **Plausible Holding Scope:** The State must establish substantial impairment connected to the qualifying condition and danger, not a diagnosis alone. **Record path:** Vacate the complete-incapacity construction, state the necessary serious-impairment rule, and remand to apply it to the commitment evidence under Kansas’s prescribed burden. **Plausible Holding Scope:** No automatic reinstatement follows; a preserved independent objection remains distinct and cannot postpone answering the control question.
