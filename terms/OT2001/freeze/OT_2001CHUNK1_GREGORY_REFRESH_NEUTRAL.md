## Bounded additions

1. Section 1201.118 of the 2001 Board regulations permits the Board, on its own motion, to reopen an appeal and reconsider the administrative judge's decision at any time. The Postal Service's Statement, paragraph 3, and Summary of Argument B and D cite that provision. The brief argues that the Board can reconsider a decision after a relied-on prior action is set aside or modified. This is reopening authority, not a mandatory reopening rule or a finding that reopening occurred. The reported fact remains that Gregory did not notify the Board of the arbitral reversal while review was pending; the brief further reports that she had not moved to reopen.

2. The statutory framework identifies a choice between Board appeal and the negotiated grievance procedure for a covered serious adverse action under 5 U.S.C. §7121(e)(1). Gregory elected Board review of her removal. The choice concerning removal is distinct from her already filed grievances concerning the earlier discipline. No assumption is made that she could personally compel union-controlled arbitration, that a particular arbitrator would rule for her, or that her other grievances were finally terminated.

3. The Postal Service itself relied on the adequacy of the Bolling framework to defend consideration of prior actions during pending grievances. Summary of Argument B and D states that collateral review accounts for the procedures accompanying the earlier action and that the framework supplies ample process; its argument section develops that defense. The brief separately invokes reopening if a prior action is later overturned or modified. These are the Postal Service's asserted grounds for rejecting categorical exclusion, not an adjudication of Bolling's statutory or constitutional sufficiency.

# Gregory — complete reported Bolling review framework

The first passage is copied mechanically from the contemporaneous Postal Service merits brief’s description of preexisting Board practice. The second is copied from an authenticated judicial recitation solely to specify the higher-level challenge requirement. These describe Board practice and establish no Supreme Court judgment, constitutional approval, Justice position or expanded grant.

In determining the proper scope of review, the MSPB looks to whether the employee received notice of the prior action in writing and had an opportunity to challenge it, and whether the action is a matter of record. Id. at 659, 660-661. If one or more of those procedural protections was not observed, the MSPB undertakes "a full, de novo review" of the earlier disciplinary action as part of its review of the later disciplinary decision. Id. at 659. When all of those procedural protections were afforded, by contrast, the MSPB will credit the agency's reliance on the prior action unless the employee can show, based upon the existing record from the earlier proceeding, that the earlier action was "clearly erroneous." Id. at 660-661. As the MSPB reasoned in Bolling, the foregoing analysis "strikes a reasonable and workable balance between the competing interests involved": "an appellant is not allowed to relitigate issues that either were, or could have been, thoroughly litigated previously," but "agencies are not able to utilize clearly erroneous prior actions as aggravating factors so as to enhance the penalties imposed." 8 M.S.P.B. at 660.
