## Decisions and Dispositions

### Brown v. City of Oneonta

Merits decision, November 13, 2001

#### Chronology and Posture

On review of the Second Circuit's amended judgment, 221 F.3d 329. The question is whether deliberate investigative selection by race escapes racial-classification review when the racial feature comes from a victim's description and additional hostility is not shown, and what constitutional standard governs that selection.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Judgment

**Reversed in part and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The reversal reaches the rejection of the equal-protection claim insofar as it rests on a categorical victim-description exemption. The affected claim is restored for application of the governing racial-classification standard to the actual selection, scope and burdens. Independently resolved claims, existing Fourth Amendment dispositions and the earlier qualified-immunity judgment protecting university officials for release of the list remain undisturbed.

#### Opinion Topology

O'CONNOR, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, SCALIA, KENNEDY, SOUTER, THOMAS, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**Deliberate racial selection requires justification.** When officials intentionally select people for investigation by race, they use a racial classification even if a victim supplied that feature and no hostility is shown. The government must establish an important lawful investigative objective and a substantial, evidence-supported relationship between that objective and the actual selection, its coverage and burdens, including a supported response to concrete practicable alternatives offering comparable investigative value with materially less racial disadvantage.

**Authority:** O'Connor's opinion for all nine Justices controls the classification rule, its standard and the bounded remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the source of identifying information can justify an official racial choice; it does not erase that choice. Adarand Constructors, Inc. v. Peña supplies the common justification standard; Engineering Contractors and City of Dallas require examination of the evidence connecting the objective to the actual measure and burden. Solving the reported violent attack is an important lawful objective. A concrete description linking race to age, sex, an injured hand, location and timing may support appropriately limited measures. The Court therefore rejects the contention that classification review requires police to ignore useful descriptions. It also rejects a categorical permission to investigate a racial community without examining the description's other features and the practical scope of the operation. The wider sweep and alleged encounter with a woman require that examination. Testimony, investigative records and reasonable inferences can establish fit; no commissioned study or universal least-restrictive-means requirement follows. Consensual questioning and a Fourth Amendment seizure are distinct, but consent alone does not remove an intentionally racial burden from equal-protection review. Membership in a described group does not establish an individual encounter, injury or defendant's responsibility.

**Precedent treatment:** Peña's common standard is extended to purposeful investigative selection; its evidentiary and remedial limits remain.

**Remand rule.** The lower courts must examine the actual selection criteria, scope and burdens under that standard, and address personal responsibility, coercion where relevant, immunity and relief through their distinct governing rules. Immunity remains measured against the law at the September 1992 conduct; this decision does not retrospectively supply clearly established notice.

**Authority and explanation:** The same unanimous opinion controls. Lesage separates unequal-process injury from consequential loss and appropriate relief. The categorical exemption prevented the necessary examination; removing it restores the affected claim without reopening independently sustained dispositions.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Adarand Constructors, Inc. v. Peña (June 12, 1995): extended to deliberate investigative selection; the common substantial-justification standard governs rather than strict scrutiny.
- Engineering Contractors (1998) and City of Dallas (1999): applied for evidence-supported fit, actual burdens and concrete practicable alternatives.
- Eisenberg (May 30, 2000): its acknowledgment of the binding common standard is preserved; its distinct educational component is not extended to criminal investigation.
- Lesage (November 29, 1999): applied to keep the classification inquiry separate from individual causation, immunity and consequential relief.

#### Law After Decision

Purposeful investigative selection by race is subject to the common substantial-justification standard. Victim-supplied identifying information may justify fitted measures, but its source and the absence of hostility create no categorical exemption. The rule governs the classification and its justification; individual liability and remedies remain subject to their existing requirements.

#### Source Notes

U.S. Const., Amdt. 14; Brown v. City of Oneonta, 221 F.3d 329 (2d Cir. 2000).

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The affected equal-protection claim returns for proceedings under the stated rule. Unchallenged and independently sustained dispositions retain their force.

**End of entry: Brown v. City of Oneonta, Merits decision, November 13, 2001.**

### Nebraska v. Wyoming, No. 108, Original

Modified North Platte Decree entered, November 13, 2001

#### Chronology and Posture

On the Special Master's final report and the parties' joint request to approve the Final Settlement Stipulation dated March 13, 2001 and presented March 15, 2001, and to replace the decree entered October 8, 1945, as modified June 15, 1953.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Public Action

The Special Master's Final Report is received and ordered filed. The Court approves the parties' Final Settlement Stipulation, enters the complete Modified North Platte Decree below in place of the October 8, 1945 decree as modified June 15, 1953, and dismisses with prejudice all claims, counterclaims and cross-claims brought in this case, including the settled claims for which leave to file was or could have been sought. The parties shall share litigation costs as the Court orders after entry of the modified decree. No individual tally is published.

Paragraph XIV confirms costs apportioned and paid under previous orders; the order preserves a further allocation of litigation costs. Paragraph XIII retains decree jurisdiction and its committee-first procedure.

#### Opinion Topology

The Court enters the order and modified decree. No individual joins are published.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**Operative rule and authority:** The Court enters the following complete Modified North Platte Decree. Each injunction, limit, definition, exception, accounting requirement, reserved right and retained power operates according to its own terms. The Court's order and decree bind the parties; they establish no new general equitable-apportionment standard.

**Controlling explanation:** The parties have agreed to resolve this existing interstate controversy through specified obligations. The earlier Nebraska decisions distinguish enforcement of an established entitlement from contested alteration of an allocation; they do not prevent this lawful consensual modification. The decree preserves independent storage and other enumerated rights, makes its terms superior to committee procedures, and retains judicial authority over implementation, amendment and unresolved committee matters. Those limits answer the concern that administrative implementation could become an uncontrolled power to change allocations. Consent settles the claims without converting their disputed allegations into findings.

**Precedent treatment:** Nebraska v. Wyoming (April 20, 1993 and May 30, 1995) remains governing for its enforcement, priority and modification distinctions; the present decree supplies the parties' exact agreed replacement obligations.

### TRW Inc. v. Andrews, No. 00-1045

Merits decision, November 13, 2001

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 9, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, which reversed partial summary judgment treating the first two disputed credit disclosures as untimely. The question is whether the then-operative Fair Credit Reporting Act supplies a general discovery extension beyond its express misrepresentation exception.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The first two disclosure claims cannot proceed on the rejected general discovery theory. The mandate supplies no entitlement to reopen the newly presented actual-damages theory, amend to allege misrepresentation, or obtain an accrual or exception hearing. Any request in further proceedings remains subject to the mandate and ordinary preservation, waiver and procedural rules. Independent judgments outside this review retain their force.

#### Opinion Topology

GINSBURG, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, KENNEDY, SOUTER, and BREYER, JJ., joined. STONE-ZSELA, C. J., filed a concurring opinion. SCALIA, J., filed an opinion concurring in the judgment, in which THOMAS, J., joined.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Breyer | Supports the judgment | Entire confined statutory opinion |
| Concurrence | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Joins the Court's opinion and judgment | Element-specific accrual; no affirmative reopening entitlement |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Scalia | Thomas | Supports the judgment on a distinct ground | Traditional complete-cause rule and rejection of a general discovery presumption |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**No general discovery extension under the governing FCRA.** The version of 15 U.S.C. §1681p governing these claims requires suit within two years from when liability arises. Its express extension permits suit within two years after discovery only when the defendant materially and willfully misrepresented information the Act required it to disclose to the individual and that information was material to establishing that defendant's liability to the individual; ignorance of a violation alone satisfies neither those conditions nor an additional general exception.

**Authority:** Ginsburg's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer, controls this statutory construction, the exception's application and the limited nonreach below. Scalia and Thomas agree with the judgment on their separately stated ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the specified discovery exception must retain its restrictive operation. A general discovery extension would allow a claimant to bypass its conditions and make it largely redundant. Andrus supports giving an enumerated exception its limiting effect; Menasche requires the enacted parts to perform their assigned work. The words identifying when liability arises do not transform liability into discovered liability. The practical difficulty of detecting identity misuse therefore cannot supply an exception Congress did not enact. Holmberg's fraud and concealment rule and Urie's latent-disease setting do not establish a universal discovery presumption overriding this text. Rotella and Klehr retain their distinct RICO questions and qualifications; they do not rewrite the FCRA. Andrews's complaint does not allege the material and willful misrepresentation of required information specified by §1681p, so that express extension supplies no basis for these claims.

**Precedent treatment:** Holmberg and Urie are distinguished at their stated scopes; Andrus and Menasche are applied to the statutory structure; the distinct Rotella and Klehr rules are preserved.

**Question left undecided.** The Court does not decide Andrews's argument that liability first arose upon actual loss rather than disclosure, because that distinct argument was first presented here and was not raised or briefed below. This nonreach does not resolve the substantive requirements of every willful claim, establish the asserted loss date, or authorize reopening the theory on remand. The confined statutory ruling corrects the general-discovery judgment without deciding a universal federal accrual rule.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Andrus v. Glover Construction Co., 446 U.S. 608 (1980): applied for the significance of an expressly enumerated exception.
- United States v. Menasche, 348 U.S. 528 (1955): applied to preserve operative work for the exception's enacted conditions.
- Holmberg v. Armbrecht, 327 U.S. 392 (1946): its fraud/concealment rule is preserved; it does not establish the universal discovery rule urged here.
- Urie v. Thompson, 337 U.S. 163 (1949): its latent-disease setting does not govern this express FCRA structure.
- Rotella v. Wood (February 23, 2000) and Klehr v. A. O. Smith Corp. (June 19, 1997): their distinct RICO accrual, new-injury, unprovable-damage and concealment components remain at their actual scope.

#### Law After Decision

The governing version of §1681p has no general discovery extension. Its precise statutory misrepresentation exception remains available when its conditions are met. The Court's ruling does not decide the first-presented actual-loss theory or create a universal accrual rule for federal statutes.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Stone-Zsela, concurring.** Stone-Zsela joins the Court's statutory ruling and judgment. He adds that liability arises when the elements necessary for the particular statutory claim exist: rejection of general discovery does not erase the need for an element-specific inquiry or an independently applicable preserved exception. Rotella rejects substitution of a later discovery event for the governing accrual inquiry, but does not make disclosure, discovery and actionable injury interchangeable. Properly preserved questions remain subject to ordinary adjudication. The newly presented theory receives no affirmative permission to reopen merely because the Court leaves it undecided.

**Scalia, joined by Thomas, concurring in the judgment.** They begin with the traditional rule that a limitations period ordinarily runs when the cause of action is complete and enforceable, rather than when the claimant learns of it. A general discovery presumption does not follow from Congress's failure expressly to reject discovery. Congress can prescribe a different start and did enact a confined exception here. Traditional fraud/concealment protection and the latent-injury setting remain separate rules. They object that leaving the general background uncertain invites uncertainty under other statutes. Their agreement with reversal and remand does not establish that every disclosure completes every FCRA claim or decide the newly raised actual-loss theory.

#### Source Notes

15 U.S.C. §§1681e(a), 1681n and 1681p (versions governing the challenged disclosures); TRW Inc. v. Andrews, No. 00-1045; Andrews v. TRW Inc., 225 F.3d 1063 (9th Cir. 2000).

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns for proceedings consistent with rejection of the general discovery theory, subject to the stated mandate and ordinary procedural limits. The independent dispositions concerning other claims remain intact.

**End of entry: TRW Inc. v. Andrews, Merits decision, November 13, 2001.**

### United States Postal Service v. Gregory, No. 00-758

Merits decision, November 13, 2001

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 9, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Federal Circuit. The question is whether an agency and the Merit Systems Protection Board may consider prior discipline while contractual grievances challenging it remain pending. The judgment below imposed a categorical pending-grievance exclusion.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Judgment

**Vacated and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The Federal Circuit must reject categorical exclusion based solely on grievance pendency and address the available consequences of the warning's completed arbitral expungement, including its procedural availability and material effect on penalty review. Where necessary, the penalty must return to the Board for reconsideration without an invalidated predicate. The mandate does not automatically restore the removal, require reopening, or select a replacement penalty.

#### Opinion Topology

BREYER, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, and SOUTER, JJ., joined, and in which THOMAS, J., joined except as to the rationale for leaving statutory sufficiency undecided. THOMAS, J., filed a concurring opinion. GINSBURG, J., filed an opinion concurring in the judgment.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Breyer | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter; Thomas except the nonreach rationale | Supports the judgment | Breyer, Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter join the entire limited opinion; Thomas joins statutory authority, incidental review, conditional adequacy and the remand, but not the reason for leaving statutory sufficiency undecided |
| Concurrence | Thomas | No other Justice | Joins the Court's opinion at the specified scope and the judgment | Would additionally decide and uphold Bolling's statutory sufficiency |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Ginsburg | No other Justice | Supports vacatur and remand on separate grounds | Reopening power, removal election, adequacy reservation and rejection of an absolute agency prohibition |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**Pending grievance review does not itself exclude earlier discipline.** When reviewing a removal or other serious adverse action, the agency and the Board may consider earlier discipline while a contractual grievance remains pending, provided the independent review affords a meaningful opportunity to challenge the predicate and its material use. The Board's authority over the later adverse action includes incidental examination of relevant earlier discipline even when that minor action was not itself directly appealable to the Board.

**Authority:** Breyer's opinion on these propositions is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, supplying eight direct supporters. Ginsburg agrees with the judgment on her separate ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that neither the adverse-action statutes nor the review statute makes completion of contractual grievances a condition of considering disciplinary history. The statutory channels may proceed in parallel. Reviewing the later removal necessarily includes examining the discipline relevant to its penalty; absence of a direct appeal from a minor action does not defeat that incidental authority. Darby supports, by analogy, refusal to add a procedural prerequisite absent from the enacted scheme. The strongest contrary concern is that unreliable prior discipline may magnify a removal penalty. That concern requires adequate review, not categorical exclusion of every action still under challenge. Independent review may satisfy the agency's obligations without awaiting the grievance. The conditional premise matters: this holding does not pronounce Bolling's particular mechanism adequate in every statutory or constitutional application.

**Judicial review and proof have different work.** Under §7703(c), a court reviews the Board's administrative choice for arbitrary or capricious action, abuse of discretion, legal error, required procedures and substantial evidence; it may not replace a permissible review policy with its preferred categorical rule. That standard does not diminish the agency's own statutory proof obligation or make agency expertise controlling on statutory meaning.

**Authority and explanation:** The same eight Justices join this proposition. Overton Park and State Farm supply the narrow review of administrative judgment while requiring lawful reasoned action. Greenwich Collieries keeps the §7701(c)(1)(B) preponderance obligation distinct from judicial review and from the Board's collateral-review formula. Respect for disciplinary administration and the presumption of regularity do not prove every predicate valid. Brown v. Gardner, Christensen and Mead preserve judicial interpretation within their respective limits.

**Adequacy reserved; the completed reversal receives lawful effect.** The Court leaves Bolling's actual statutory and constitutional adequacy undecided because Gregory did not seek another mechanism below or here and the categorical judgment can be corrected without resolving it. Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer adopt that nonreach rationale; Thomas does not join it. The completed arbitral expungement is distinct from mere pendency and must receive its legally required effect through the governing agreement and review mechanisms. Section 1201.118 permits discretionary Board reopening on its own motion; it does not compel reopening after every changed predicate. The remand requires examination of the warning's material role and the procedural consequences of its late presentation, rather than a judicial finding of dependence or automatic waiver.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Darby v. Cisneros (June 21, 1993): applied by analogy against an extra procedural prerequisite; its APA provision is not directly imposed on this personnel statute.
- Citizens to Preserve Overton Park v. Volpe, 401 U.S. 402 (1971), and Motor Vehicle Manufacturers Assn. v. State Farm, 463 U.S. 29 (1983): applied to the bounds of judicial review of administrative judgment.
- Director, OWCP v. Greenwich Collieries (June 20, 1994): applied to keep proof burdens distinct from reviewing deference.
- Brown v. Gardner (December 12, 1994), Christensen v. Harris County (May 1, 2000), and United States v. Mead Corp. (June 18, 2001): preserved at their actual limits on administrative interpretation; no general conclusive-deference rule is created.
- United States v. Chemical Foundation, 272 U.S. 1 (1926): regularity does not establish that a particular predicate or collateral-review formula is valid.

#### Law After Decision

A pending contractual grievance creates no categorical statutory bar to considering earlier discipline in later adverse-action review. Adequate independent review remains essential, and the agency's proof burden is separate from judicial review. Bolling's actual adequacy remains undecided by the Court. A completed reversal must receive the effect required by the applicable agreement and review framework through the available procedural mechanisms.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Thomas, concurring.** Thomas joins the limited statutory opinion at the scope shown in the table, but would also decide statutory sufficiency because the Postal Service's reliance on Bolling makes that ground part of the presented dispute. Under Bolling, full fresh review applies if written notice, a recorded action, or an opportunity to challenge above the original imposing authority was absent. If all three exist, the employee may show clear error on the earlier record. Thomas considers that framework consistent with §7503's protections for minor discipline and its absence of a direct Board appeal. The statute does not require retrying the facts of every earlier action merely because it bears on a later penalty. The agency must establish the earlier action, while the appealed charge and penalty retain their own proof requirements. The contractual and Board routes protect different rights; one route's conclusion is not automatically a prerequisite for the other. He accepts remand concerning the overturned warning without commanding reopening. His affirmative statutory view neither becomes the Court's holding nor resolves every constitutional application.

**Ginsburg, concurring in the judgment.** Ginsburg questions whether restricted clear-error review can satisfy the statutory preponderance requirement when the earlier merits remain unadjudicated. The opportunity to contest a recorded action does not itself prove adequate review. She therefore withholds assent to the Court's independent-review rationale, without holding Bolling invalid or requiring fresh review of every final disciplinary action. Her remand rests on the Board's actual reopening power, Gregory's election of Board review of the removal, the reservation of Bolling's adequacy, and rejection of an absolute prohibition on agency consideration of pending discipline. Reopening provides a route to request consideration of the changed disciplinary record, not guaranteed relief. A refusal to address a material invalidated predicate could present an arbitrary-and-capricious question. The removal election does not abandon earlier grievances, establish personal control over union arbitration, or substitute for adequate Board process. Her distinction between final discipline and an action still being challenged remains a noncontrolling qualification.

#### Source Notes

5 U.S.C. §§7503, 7512–7513, 7701(c)(1)(B), 7703(c), 7121; 5 C.F.R. §§1201.117–1201.118 (2001); Bolling v. Department of Air Force, 8 M.S.P.B. 658 (1981); United States Postal Service v. Gregory, No. 00-758.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Federal Circuit resumes review under the stated rule, addresses the expungement and available procedural consequences, and returns the penalty to the Board where legally necessary. The Board's reopening and procedural options retain their statutory and regulatory limits.

**End of entry: United States Postal Service v. Gregory, Merits decision, November 13, 2001.**

### Chickasaw Nation v. United States, No. 00-507

Merits decision, November 27, 2001

#### Chronology and Posture

The case encompasses the Chickasaw and Choctaw Nations. Argued October 2, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Tenth Circuit's judgments rejecting the tribes' claimed exemption from federal wagering excise and occupational taxes. The question is whether 25 U.S.C. §2719(d)(1) carries chapter 35's state wagering-tax exemption into covered tribal gaming.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Judgment

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Chickasaw Nation's asserted chapter 35 exemption | Affirmed, 6–3 | Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Souter | The judgment rejecting the asserted IGRA exemption stands. |
| Choctaw Nation's asserted chapter 35 exemption | Affirmed, 6–3 | Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Souter | The judgment rejecting the asserted IGRA exemption stands. |

#### Opinion Topology

KENNEDY, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STEVENS, SCALIA, THOMAS, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined. SOUTER, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and O'CONNOR, J., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**IGRA's reporting and withholding direction does not confer the claimed substantive tax exemption.** For Indian gaming conducted under IGRA or an effective Tribal-State compact entered under §2710(d)(3), §2719(d)(1) directs state-equivalent treatment of the Code provisions concerning reporting and withholding of taxes on gaming or wagering winnings. Its parenthetical chapter 35 reference does not extend that defined subject to chapter 35's substantive wagering excise and occupational taxes. Section 2719(d)(2) makes the subsection govern notwithstanding other law unless that law specifically cites the subsection; this priority applies only within (d)(1)'s scope.

**Authority:** Kennedy's opinion, joined by Stevens, Scalia, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies six direct votes for the complete construction and both affirmances.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court reads the operative description and its examples together. National Bank of Oregon permits the complete enactment to establish meaning rather than making an isolated textual feature decisive. Here the parenthetical illustrates the class defined by the reporting-and-withholding words; it does not create a second, broader class. The tribes' strongest objection is that this reading deprives the express chapter reference of its apparent substantive work. That is a real textual difficulty, but an inconsistent example cannot expand the enacted subject it purports to illustrate. State-equivalent treatment still governs the reporting and withholding provisions within the defined class. The Indian-law canon resolves genuine remaining ambiguity; it does not require the opposite construction after the whole clause fixes that scope. This conclusion rests on text and context, not a claimed power to delete any inconvenient reference, a fabricated drafting sequence, or hostility to protective tribal canons. The state-tax and treaty rules of Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Chickasaw Nation and Mille Lacs do not supply an independent federal tax exemption.

**Precedent treatment:** National Bank of Oregon is applied for contextual construction; the distinct state-tax and tribal-instrument precedents remain confined to their own predicates. Chapter 35's independent definitions, exclusions and liability-linked provisions remain operative; today's construction rejects the asserted IGRA exemption rather than erasing those provisions.

#### Precedent Treatment

- United States National Bank of Oregon v. Independent Insurance Agents of America (June 7, 1993): applied to read the whole enacted provision; no general authority to rewrite statutory text is adopted.
- Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Chickasaw Nation (June 14, 1995): its state-tax incidence, residence, source and treaty rules are distinguished from this federal statutory exemption.
- Minnesota v. Mille Lacs Band of Chippewa Indians (March 24, 1999): its protective interpretation of retained tribal rights remains; it creates no exemption outside the scope of this resolved federal text.

#### Law After Decision

Section 2719(d)(1) does not give covered tribal gaming chapter 35's state wagering-tax exemption. Reporting and withholding parity operates within the provision's described subject. Other Code exclusions, the actual tax base and the occupational tax's independent linkage remain governed by their own terms; no general tribal federal-tax immunity is recognized.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Souter, joined by Stone-Zsela and O'Connor, dissenting.** The dissent would give the expressly named chapter operative effect. The reporting-and-withholding description and the substantive chapter reference pull in different directions; calling the reference an accident is not the only possible reading. Punctuation does not make text inside parentheses legally inferior, and specific inclusion may show that the general description is too narrow. National Bank requires integrated interpretation, not a license to discard the inconvenient side of an enacted conflict. At least genuine ambiguity remains after ordinary reading, and the Indian-law canon resolves it in favor of the tribes; tribal economic-development purposes support that reading without independently granting immunity.

The dissent would apply state-equivalent treatment to chapter 35's pertinent exemption for a sweepstakes, wagering pool or lottery conducted by a state agency under state law, where the wager is placed with that agency or its authorized employee or agent. Section 4411's occupational tax would then follow its own liability-linked terms. This is a construction of the IGRA cross-reference for covered operations, not an exemption for every tribe, member, game or federal tax. The dissent would reverse and remand for application to each tribe's actual assessments and relief, leaving transaction coverage and calculations to the tax record.

#### Source Notes

25 U.S.C. §§2719(d)(1)–(2), 2710(d)(3); 26 U.S.C. §§4401–4402, 4411, 4421; Chickasaw Nation v. United States, No. 00-507 (including the Choctaw Nation).

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Tenth Circuit judgments rejecting the asserted IGRA exemption are affirmed. No Supreme Court remand is ordered.

**End of entry: Chickasaw Nation v. United States, Merits decision, November 27, 2001.**

### Church of Scientology International v. Behar

Merits decision, November 27, 2001

#### Chronology and Posture

On review of the Second Circuit judgment, 238 F.3d 168, concerning the May 6, 1991 Time article. The question is whether the First Amendment supplies an independent subsidiary-meaning immunity for a challenged statement because actual malice was not proved for other accusations in the same publication.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Judgment

**Vacated in part and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The affected statements are 5, 6 and 7 in the appellate opinion. The remand permits statement-specific adjudication of material falsity, materially distinct defamatory meaning, identification and actual malice, together with properly available alternative defenses. Independently supported dismissals of other statements, unappealed matters and the amendment request's independent procedural limits remain undisturbed. The remand guarantees neither trial nor additional discovery.

#### Opinion Topology

KENNEDY, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, SCALIA, SOUTER, THOMAS, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**Contextual materiality does not create automatic immunity for a distinct accusation.** A challenged statement may be dismissed when, read in context, it conveys no materially false defamatory meaning. But the First Amendment does not immunize a materially distinct factual accusation merely because it is subsidiary to other serious accusations for which actual malice was not proved; the required fault inquiry concerns the distinct accusation itself.

**Authority:** Kennedy's opinion, joined by all eight other Justices, controls the complete rule and bounded remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court preserves the demanding protection New York Times v. Sullivan supplies: a public figure must prove a materially false defamatory assertion of and concerning it, published with knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard, with clear-and-convincing proof of actual malice. Context remains essential. Masson permits immaterial differences to be excluded from actionable defamation, but rejects a constitutionally compelled incremental-harm defense and keeps meaning, fault and injury distinct. The strongest defense of subsidiary-meaning immunity is that a supported general portrayal leaves an additional accusation with no meaningful defamatory work. Ordinary contextual analysis may establish that premise in a particular case; protection of one accusation does not itself establish it for another. Nor does absence of actual malice about one assertion prove the publisher's belief about a separate assertion. The Vancouver statements concern a source of funds and particular takeover, stock and property conduct capable of adding a distinct charge. The panel's reliance on the article's general portrayal did not resolve all their separate elements. Statement-specific adjudication is therefore required through the ordinary procedures, including an adequately supported alternative summary-judgment ground.

**Precedent treatment:** Sullivan's fault burden remains unchanged; Masson's separation of materiality, fault and incremental injury governs. The Second Circuit's Herbert subsidiary-meaning rule is rejected only insofar as it supplies the independent constitutional immunity described here.

**Limits and remaining adjudication.** Ordinary state rules governing injury, causation and damages retain the role Masson left them. The of-and-concerning defense and other undecided statement-specific elements remain for the lower courts because the panel did not resolve them and the record does not compel their resolution here. Nominal damages do not eliminate the conceded public-figure fault requirement. The distinct libel-proof-plaintiff doctrine lies outside the question decided.

#### Precedent Treatment

- New York Times Co. v. Sullivan, 376 U.S. 254 (1964): reaffirmed for the public-figure requirement of knowing falsity or reckless disregard and constitutionally sufficient proof.
- St. Amant v. Thompson, 390 U.S. 727 (1968), and Harte-Hanks Communications v. Connaughton, 491 U.S. 657 (1989): applied for subjective fault rather than mere hostility or poor journalism.
- Masson v. New Yorker Magazine, Inc., 501 U.S. 496 (1991): applied to separate material falsity, contextual meaning, fault and injury, reject a constitutionally compelled incremental-harm defense, and preserve ordinary state tort rules.
- Bartnicki v. Vopper (May 21, 2001): its accuracy-dependent protection at its stated factual scope supplies no article-wide immunity for distinct false accusations.
- The Second Circuit's Herbert subsidiary-meaning rule: displaced to the extent it insulates a materially distinct accusation from the required fault inquiry solely because other accusations are protected.

#### Law After Decision

The First Amendment supplies no automatic subsidiary-meaning immunity for a materially distinct defamatory factual accusation. Context may establish absence of actionable material falsity, and the public-figure burden remains demanding and statement-specific. Protected criticism elsewhere in a publication cannot replace those inquiries. State-law injury, causation and damages rules remain separately operative.

#### Source Notes

Church of Scientology International v. Behar, 238 F.3d 168 (2d Cir. 2001); New York Times Co. v. Sullivan, 376 U.S. 254 (1964); Masson v. New Yorker Magazine, Inc., 501 U.S. 496 (1991).

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The three affected Vancouver statements return for ordinary adjudication under the stated rule and any properly available alternative defenses. Independently supported dispositions and amendment limits retain their force. The counterclaims dismissed without prejudice retain their recorded conditional ability to be refiled upon reinstatement of the complaint; no counterclaim merits are decided by this remand.

**End of entry: Church of Scientology International v. Behar, Merits decision, November 27, 2001.**

### Correctional Services Corp. v. Malesko, No. 00-860

Merits decision, November 27, 2001

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 1, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Second Circuit, which reversed dismissal of the constitutional damages claim against the private operator of a federal halfway house. The question is whether a private federal custodial corporation may answer for an Eighth Amendment injury caused by its own unconstitutional policy, practice or authorized decision.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed at the stated scope and remanded, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

The lower courts must determine whether the pleading identifies a corporate policy, established practice or authorized corporate decision causing deliberate indifference and injury in the exercise of federal custody, permitting the ordinary pleading process under applicable rules. Automatic responsibility for an employee's wrong is excluded. The independent limitations judgments concerning individual employees remain in place.

#### Opinion Topology

STEVENS, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and SOUTER, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined. O'CONNOR, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which SCALIA, KENNEDY, and THOMAS, JJ., joined. SCALIA, J., filed a separate dissenting opinion, in which THOMAS, J., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**A private federal custodian answers for its own constitutional misconduct.** A private corporation entrusted with federal custody may be sued in damages for an Eighth Amendment violation caused by its own policy, established practice or authorized corporate decision in performing that custodial function. The claim requires deliberate indifference, direct corporate attribution and causation; employee negligence or automatic vicarious responsibility alone is insufficient.

**Authority:** Stevens's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies five direct votes for the full cause, its limitations and the remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court recognizes a limited extension of the custodial damages remedy preserved by Carlson. Custody gives the operator control over basic conditions and access to necessary care; when the company itself makes the unconstitutional decision, an employee-only remedy can miss the responsible actor. Meyer does not mechanically resolve this different defendant. It rejects direct agency liability and a new public fiscal obligation; a private operator's own decision lacks that agency and fiscal premise. The Court nevertheless accepts Meyer's distinction between having a constitutional duty and having a cause of action. The extension rests on Carlson's established protection for persons in federal custody and on direct private responsibility, not on a waiver of immunity or federal contracting alone. The dissent correctly identifies a new entity remedy and asks why useful compensation should authorize it. Here the established custodial injury, the corporation's own decision and the absence of the agency obligation provide the bounded remedial justification. State negligence remedies and prospective relief remain relevant, but neither necessarily addresses the corporation's own completed, deliberate constitutional wrong. Richardson distinguishes private custodial operation from automatic public immunity; it does not itself create this cause. The own-conduct requirement prevents the remedy from becoming liability for every employee mistake or every federal contractor.

**Precedent treatment:** Carlson's custodial remedy is extended to the private operator's own culpable conduct; Meyer remains controlling for federal agencies and is distinguished at its actual grounds; Richardson's separate immunity rule is preserved.

**Application and limit.** The elevator allegation must be assessed for an actual corporate policy, practice or authorized decision, deliberate indifference and causation. The seriousness of an alleged injury does not establish those elements. The recognized cause is confined to direct corporate responsibility in federal custody; individual-employee liability is outside the question presented. Ordinary pleading and proof govern the remand, and the time-barred employee claims are not revived.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents, 403 U.S. 388 (1971), and Davis v. Passman, 442 U.S. 228 (1979): their recognized individual constitutional damages settings remain operative.
- Carlson v. Green, 446 U.S. 14 (1980): its federal custodial damages function supports the confined extension to a private custodian's own unconstitutional policy, practice or authorized decision.
- FDIC v. Meyer (February 23, 1994): its independent agency-cause and public fiscal/remedial grounds remain; they do not automatically determine the different private own-conduct claim.
- Richardson v. McKnight (June 23, 1997): its immunity rule is distinguished from the separate cause-of-action question and is not converted into a holding on corporate liability.
- Alexander v. Sandoval (April 24, 2001): its limited prospective statutory remedy remains distinct; it supplies neither a universal prohibition nor automatic authorization of constitutional damages.

#### Law After Decision

A confined federal constitutional damages cause is available against a private corporation exercising federal custody for its own deliberate constitutional misconduct through policy, established practice or authorized decision. Corporate attribution and causation must be proved; ordinary negligence and automatic vicarious liability do not suffice. Meyer's federal-agency bar and existing individual-action rules remain in force.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**O'Connor, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, dissenting.** The dissent would reverse and dismiss the corporate constitutional claim. Meyer gives independent force to the distinction between an established officer action and judicial creation of a new entity remedy. A federal custodial duty, useful compensation and the absence of sovereign immunity do not themselves supply that cause. The private defendant weakens the public-fisc concern, but does not eliminate the separate question of who may create a corporate constitutional remedy. Richardson addresses immunity and expressly does not resolve corporate liability or cause creation. Individual actions, state tort remedies, prospective relief and contractual oversight retain their actual scope and reinforce remedial restraint; the dissent does not need to assume that tort relief exactly duplicates every constitutional injury. Limiting the proposed action to corporate policy narrows liability but still adds the disputed defendant. The dissent's objection concerns creation of that cause, not a finding that the company complied with the Constitution or a grant of sovereign status to every contractor.

**Scalia, joined by Thomas, dissenting separately.** They join O'Connor's dissent and add that the Court should not extend implied constitutional damages beyond the recognized Bivens, Davis and Carlson settings even when a proposed extension can be analogized to their reasoning. That distinct objection reaches the Court's claim of a sufficiently close custodial analogy: accepting the analogy does not, in their view, authorize another judicially created damages action. Existing recognized causes remain operative. They do not rely on treating Sandoval as a controlling general abandonment of statutory implication, because its actual statutory holding supplies no such premise. Their separate ground neither decides private employee liability nor overrules the recognized constitutional actions.

#### Source Notes

U.S. Const., Amdt. 8; Correctional Services Corp. v. Malesko, No. 00-860; Carlson v. Green, 446 U.S. 14 (1980); Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents, 403 U.S. 388 (1971); Davis v. Passman, 442 U.S. 228 (1979).

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns for application of the confined cause to the actual pleading and, if the claim proceeds under ordinary rules, proof of its elements. The independent employee-limitations dispositions retain their force.

**End of entry: Correctional Services Corp. v. Malesko, Merits decision, November 27, 2001.**

### J. E. M. Ag Supply, Inc. v. Pioneer Hi-Bred International, Inc., No. 99-1996

Merits decision, December 10, 2001

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 3, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Federal Circuit. Pioneer asserted utility patents concerning corn varieties against unauthorized seed sellers. The question is whether the Plant Patent Act and Plant Variety Protection Act categorically exclude newly developed plants from utility-patent protection under §101. The accepted defense concerns statutory eligibility and exclusivity, with patent-specific and transaction-specific issues remaining distinct.

All nine Justices participated at argument or submission and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg.

**Opposing:** Stevens and Breyer.

The categorical ineligibility defense is rejected. Patent litigation may continue under the independent requirements of the protection actually invoked.

#### Opinion Topology

SOUTER, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, THOMAS, and GINSBURG, JJ., joined. SCALIA, J., filed a concurring opinion. STEVENS, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which BREYER, J., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The plant statutes permit concurrent utility-patent protection

**Holding and operative rule:** Newly developed plants, including the corn varieties at issue, are not categorically excluded from 35 U.S.C. §101 because the Plant Patent Act or Plant Variety Protection Act also covers the subject. A claimant invoking utility protection must meet that statute's independent requirements; certificate-specific exceptions govern their own statutory right and do not create a general immunity from a valid utility patent.

**Authority:** The Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg; seven direct joins support the complete eligibility and coexistence rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress's plant-specific protections can operate alongside the broadly expressed utility-patent categories. Chakrabarty rejects a categorical living-subject exclusion and gives effect to human invention within the statutory language. The Plant Patent Act's limited reproduction right and relaxed description requirement address obstacles to protection without declaring that a plant satisfying the more demanding general statute loses that protection. The Plant Variety Protection Act likewise supplies a distinct right with distinct requirements and exceptions.

The contrary argument has practical force: utility rights can restrict seed saving or research that the certificate regime permits. But different rights and eligibility requirements are not an irreconcilable statutory conflict. Neither plant statute expressly makes its protection exclusive, and enactment or recodification does not require exclusion from §101. Each statute retains work; a court may not create a hybrid by importing certificate exceptions into utility-patent law.

Asgrow construes the former certificate saved-seed provision. It does not answer utility-patent exclusivity or supply a universal agricultural exemption. Its former-law limits and the enacted transition remain effective within the certificate regime. Administrative practice and legislative silence are not treated as binding interpretations. The present category-wide answer leaves novelty, nonobviousness, disclosure and other patent requirements independently operative; it establishes neither validity of every asserted claim nor enforceability of every sales restriction.

**Precedent treatment:** Chakrabarty's broad statutory reading is applied; Asgrow remains confined to its certificate setting; Deuel and State Street's independent requirements and limits remain intact.

##### Certificate protections retain their own statutory version and exceptions

**Holding and operative rule:** Concurrent utility eligibility does not rewrite the protection supplied by a plant-variety certificate. Former-law saved-seed protection and the amendments effective April 4, 1995 retain their enacted conditions and transition; no specific certificate, seed transaction or exception entitlement is adjudicated here.

**Authority:** The same seven-Justice Opinion of the Court preserves these boundaries as part of the coexistence rule.

**Controlling explanation:** Under Asgrow, former §2543 permits reproductive sale only of seed genuinely saved for the farmer's own planting, produced from seed obtained with the owner's authority or its descendants. Seller and purchaser must primarily farm for nonreproductive crop sales, and the sale must comply with state seed-sale law. Changed planting plans may permit disposition of genuinely retained seed; intentional multiplication for marketing is not exempt. The independent protection for bona fide nonreproductive sales in customary channels remains; a buyer who diverts seed sold through those channels to planting is deemed to have section 2567 notice that the diversion infringes. Section 2544's exemption for use and reproduction for plant breeding or other bona fide research likewise belongs to that certificate regime.

The 1994 amendments delete the former farmer-sales proviso for the amended regime from April 4, 1995. Except as section 14 itself provides through the refiling rule of section 14(b) and the notice rule of section 14(c) described next, section 14(a) keeps varieties with certificates issued before April 4, 1995, or with applications pending on that date, under the Plant Variety Protection Act as in effect the day before. Section 14(b) permits refiling under amended terms while retaining the original filing date for §42. Section 14(c) requires identification of amended protection in a notice claiming it and retains sanctions for false or misleading claims or labels. These rules continue to govern the certificate right; none is converted into an exception to a separate utility patent. The Court makes no transaction-specific exception or transition finding.

**Precedent treatment:** Asgrow's saved-seed construction and former-law force are preserved; the statutory amendment's express transition remains unchanged.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Diamond v. Chakrabarty, 447 U.S. 303: its broad reading of §101 applies to qualifying human invention rather than a categorical exclusion of living subject matter.
- Asgrow Seed Co. v. Winterboer, No. 92-2038: its former certificate saved-seed construction and transition limits remain; no utility-patent exception is derived from it.
- In re Deuel, January 14, 1997: ordinary supported nonobviousness requirements remain distinct from category eligibility.
- State Street Bank & Trust Co. v. Signature Financial Group, Inc., January 14, 1999: broad category language does not remove genuine statutory limitations or independent proof requirements.

#### Law After Decision

Plants meeting the utility statute's requirements may receive utility patents alongside the plant-specific regimes. Each right carries its own requirements and exceptions. The certificate amendments and Asgrow's surviving former-law operation retain their actual reach; the decision supplies no generalized saved-seed or research exemption from utility-patent rights.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia concurs and joins the Court in full. Chakrabarty already authoritatively construed the general subject-matter language to reject the supposed ambiguity based on living subject matter. The plant-specific statutes therefore cannot merely be used to clarify that language into the opposite categorical exclusion; a claimed change must satisfy the more demanding inquiry into displacement of the existing rule. This canon explanation supplements rather than narrows the Court's eligibility and coexistence rationale.

Stevens dissents, joined by Breyer, and would treat the plant-specific enactments as the exclusive protection for their covered plant categories. The 1930 Act expressly amended the general patent provision, conditioned protection on asexual reproduction and limited the reproduction right; the later variety statute supplied seed-reproduction protection with saved-seed and research limits. In their view, the utility route bypasses those particular congressional choices, and recodification did not undo them. Different eligibility thresholds do not explain why increased originality should eliminate protections Congress deliberately conferred on agricultural users. Chakrabarty's human-made microorganism lay outside those specialized categories, so the dissent preserves its holding and does not exclude all living inventions or independently qualifying processes. The dissent would reverse and remand on the category defense.

#### Source Notes

35 U.S.C. §§101–103, 112 and 161–164; 7 U.S.C. §§2543–2544; Pub. L. 103-349 §§10, 14–15; J. E. M. Ag Supply, No. 99-1996, 534 U.S. 124, for the patent dispute and statutory setting.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Federal Circuit's rejection of the categorical defense stands. Remaining patent-specific validity, exhaustion, infringement, competition and contract issues proceed only within their ordinary preserved scope; this decision resolves none of them by the eligibility answer alone.

**End of entry: J. E. M. Ag Supply, Inc. v. Pioneer Hi-Bred International, Inc., Merits decision, December 10, 2001.**

### Schrader v. Blackwell

Merits decision, December 10, 2001

#### Chronology and Posture

Review of the Sixth Circuit judgment reported at 241 F.3d 783, reversing relief for the requested Libertarian ballot designation. Schrader qualified for the 1998 congressional ballot through independent nomination; the party had actually nominated him but lacked state recognition. A preliminary injunction permitted the label for that election. The party later regained recognition. The question is whether the First and Fourteenth Amendments permit Ohio to condition the official party designation on recognized-party status. The continuing party plaintiff, recurring recognition requirement and short election cycle keep the dispute reviewable; the earlier filing-deadline objection is outside review.

All nine Justices participated at argument or submission and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg.

The Sixth Circuit's judgment remains in force at the reviewed ballot-designation scope. The four dissenters would vacate and remand for the actual burden, affiliation wording, verification and administrative fit, with appropriately bounded prospective relief.

THOMAS, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, and BREYER, JJ., joined, and in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, SOUTER, and GINSBURG, JJ., joined as to the recurring-controversy holding. STEVENS, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and SOUTER and GINSBURG, JJ., joined.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Thomas | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Breyer throughout; Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg as to the recurring-controversy holding only | Supports affirmance on the merits; all nine support reaching the dispute | Five join the recognition-linked merits rule; all nine join the threshold holding |
| Dissent | Stevens | Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg | Would vacate and remand | Verified affiliation, separate justification, supported administrative limits and bounded prospective relief |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The recurring controversy remains reviewable

**Holding and operative rule:** Completion of the election and the party's later recognition do not end this challenge to a recognition requirement capable of recurring against the continuing party plaintiff in elections too short for full review. The Court reaches the ballot-designation dispute under the capable-of-repetition exception.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join this threshold proposition in the Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** The ongoing party is subject to a periodic recognition requirement and has previously lost and regained recognition. The same challenged designation restriction can therefore recur during an election cycle that ends before full judicial review. Review does not require a new inquiry merely because the 1998 ballot is complete. The earlier filing-deadline objection is outside the accepted issue.

**Precedent treatment:** Timmons's preservation of review in a continuing nomination dispute is applied consistently with its distinct conditional-enactment facts.

##### Ohio may retain the official nomination designation within its reasonable recognition system

**Holding and operative rule:** Under Anderson-Burdick, Ohio may condition this requested official party designation on compliance with its neutral party-recognition system where the complete nomination scheme leaves meaningful independent ballot access and opportunities for endorsement and campaigning. The actual burden is less than severe, and coherent nomination categories and reasonable preventive administration proportionately justify this application; empirical proof of prior disruption is not indispensable.

**Authority:** The Opinion of the Court on the merits, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer; five direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that support sufficient to place a congressional candidate on the ballot does not itself establish the separate statewide party status represented by Ohio's official nomination cue. Anderson and Burdick require attention to the actual burden and the fit of the State's interests, rather than treating the designation as immune from review. Timmons permits proportionate regulation of official nomination machinery within a system that leaves meaningful political participation.

Schrader's verified nomination and affiliation make his associational interest real. They do not compel Ohio to redesign its official designation to distinguish affiliation from recognized-party nomination whenever an alternative wording is feasible. Independent access and campaigning reduce the burden; they do not make it disappear. The State may use reasonable preventive categories without waiting for actual disruption.

Norman protected authorized geographic party expansion and independently qualified candidates from disqualification. This candidate remains on the ballot through a functioning nomination route, and the different party-recognition designation serves a distinct purpose. Tashjian and Eu's associational protection does not settle every official ballot format. The Court adopts neither pure rational-basis review nor a presumption validating every affiliation prohibition. More restrictive access systems and unsupported burdens remain subject to the governing calibrated inquiry.

**Precedent treatment:** Anderson and Burdick supply the calibrated framework; Timmons's nomination-system reasoning is applied to this distinct designation burden; Norman is distinguished by the preserved candidate access and different state-status cue; Tashjian and Eu retain their associational protections.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Anderson v. Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780, and Burdick v. Takushi, 504 U.S. 428: the actual burden, precise interests and proportional fit remain controlling; no write-in presumption is transferred to labels.
- Timmons v. Twin Cities Area New Party, No. 95-1608: its contextual nomination-system reasoning supports this bounded application, without universal approval of recognition restrictions.
- Norman v. Reed, 502 U.S. 279: authorized expansion and independently qualified slate access remain protected; the present official status designation is distinguished.
- Tashjian v. Republican Party of Connecticut, 479 U.S. 208, and Eu v. San Francisco County Democratic Central Committee, 489 U.S. 214: associational protection remains, without compelling every requested ballot format.

#### Law After Decision

The official party designation may remain linked to Ohio's recognition system in these circumstances. The holding applies burden-and-fit review to a designation restriction; it supplies no categorical approval of every recognition threshold or prohibition of truthful affiliation.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stevens dissents, joined by Stone-Zsela, Souter and Ginsburg, and would distinguish verified affiliation from the privileges and official representation of recognized-party nomination. Because the candidate has independently qualified and the party actually nominated him, the State must explain why every accurately qualified affiliation cue must be suppressed. Anderson-Burdick requires that fit even when the burden is less than severe; campaign speech does not eliminate the distinct ballot burden. Norman, Tashjian and Eu give weight to the real association and supported standard bearer. Timmons concerned fusion nomination and does not decide this separate informational burden. The dissent would permit the State to verify affiliation and choose accurate wording, and would recognize demonstrated administrative or effective-governing interests rather than impose a universal preferred-label right. It would remand for bounded prospective relief, preserving party-recognition thresholds and leaving the completed election intact.

U.S. Const. amends. I and XIV; Schrader v. Blackwell, 241 F.3d 783 (6th Cir. 2001).

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The reviewed Sixth Circuit judgment stands. The completed election remains intact, and this decision confers no additional recognized-party privileges.

### United States v. Knights, No. 00-1260

Merits decision, December 10, 2001

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 6, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, which affirmed suppression because the residence search pursued ordinary criminal investigation. The question is whether a known probation search condition and individualized reasonable suspicion justify the search notwithstanding that purpose. The written condition permitted searches by probation or law-enforcement officers, and the detective had reasonable suspicion connecting the residence to criminal activity.

All nine Justices participated at argument or submission and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The categorical investigative-purpose ground for suppression is rejected. The lower courts apply the condition-and-reasonable-suspicion rule; evidence is admissible if no independently preserved defect remains. Any genuinely open scope or factual issue must be resolved on its own record.

#### Opinion Topology

SCALIA, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, KENNEDY, SOUTER, THOMAS, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The known search condition and individualized suspicion justify this probation search

**Holding and operative rule:** A warrantless search of a probationer's residence within a known, lawful search condition is reasonable when supported by particularized reasonable suspicion of criminal activity in the circumstances presented. Ordinary criminal-investigative purpose does not independently invalidate that combination; neither the condition alone nor reasonable suspicion alone supplies this holding.

**Authority:** The unanimous Opinion of the Court; all nine Justices directly adopt this rule, its justification, and its limits.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the probation relationship and notice of the express condition reduce the privacy interest implicated by this residential search, while individualized suspicion supplies an objective justification for this intrusion. Griffin establishes that lawful probation supervision changes the constitutional setting; it does not make its special-needs rationale the exclusive ground for every probation search. Knights's proposed purpose restriction would invalidate a search supported by this combined justification merely because the officer investigates crime. The Fourth Amendment does not require that result here.

Whren supports examining the objective justification after authority for the intrusion has been established. Its ordinary probable-cause traffic rule does not itself confer residential search power or decide motive doctrine for every search based on reasonable suspicion. Edmond and Ferguson retain their purpose inquiries in their suspicionless and special-needs settings. Scott governs the consequence of an unconstitutional supervisory search, not the antecedent validity of this condition-and-suspicion search; its exclusion rule remains intact. Any ultimate suspicion question properly remaining is independently reviewed under Ornelas, with its protection for historical findings and due weight to grounded contextual inferences.

The holding rests on reasonableness, not complete contractual consent or forfeiture of Fourth Amendment protection. Suspicionless residential searches and blanket consent are outside the question necessarily resolved because individualized suspicion supplies the sufficient ground here.

**Precedent treatment:** Griffin's supervisory-relationship principle is applied without making its rationale exclusive; Whren's objective method is applied within its source-specific limits; Edmond and Ferguson are distinguished; Scott's exclusion rule and Ornelas's review allocation remain operative.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Griffin v. Wisconsin, 483 U.S. 868: the lawful supervisory relationship informs diminished privacy; special needs is not the exclusive possible justification.
- Whren v. United States, 517 U.S. 806: objective assessment follows a demonstrated source of search authority; its traffic holding is not generally extended to every reasonable-suspicion search.
- City of Indianapolis v. Edmond, 531 U.S. 32, and Ferguson v. City of Charleston, 532 U.S. 67: purpose remains material in their distinct suspicionless and special-needs settings.
- Pennsylvania Board of Probation & Parole v. Scott, 524 U.S. 357: exclusion for the institution's unconstitutional supervisory search remains unchanged; this decision finds the bounded search reasonable.
- Ornelas v. United States, 517 U.S. 690: the division between factual and ultimate legal review is applied unchanged.

#### Law After Decision

The Fourth Amendment permits the defined probation-condition and individualized-suspicion combination despite ordinary investigative purpose. Existing scope, execution and exclusion rules retain their separate operation; the decision creates no general suspicionless-search or consent-waiver rule.

#### Source Notes

U.S. Const. amend. IV; Griffin v. Wisconsin, 483 U.S. 868; United States v. Knights, No. 00-1260, 534 U.S. 112, for the probation order and procedural record.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns through the Ninth Circuit for proceedings under the stated rule and resolution of any independently preserved issue actually remaining. The purpose-based suppression ground cannot sustain exclusion.

### Dusenbery v. United States, No. 00-6567

Merits decision, January 8, 2002

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 29, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Sixth Circuit, which sustained the forfeiture-notice procedure. The FBI sent certified notice to the federal institution holding Dusenbery; notice mailed November 7, 1988 identified the forfeiture, contest process and December 19 claim deadline. An employee acknowledged postal receipt. Testimony concerning the relevant period described collection, certified-mail logging, receipt by the responsible unit employee and ordinary delivery to the inmate. The old log was unavailable under a one-year retention practice, and actual personal receipt was not established. The question is whether this process satisfied due process without proof of actual receipt.

All nine Justices participated at argument or submission and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

**Opposing:** Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The challenged notice determination stands on the reasonably calculated institutional route. The dissenters would vacate and remand for the consequences of the insufficiently demonstrated final custodial handoff.

#### Opinion Topology

STONE-ZSELA, C. J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, and THOMAS, JJ., joined. STEVENS, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which SOUTER, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### A proved reasonable custodial route can satisfy due process without proof of actual receipt

**Holding and operative rule:** Due process requires means reasonably calculated in the known circumstances to convey forfeiture notice and afford a meaningful opportunity to object, not an absolute guarantee of actual receipt. For a known confined owner, certified delivery coupled with an established, reasonably functioning internal distribution route to that inmate can satisfy the obligation; a signature at the institution's entrance alone is not universally sufficient.

**Authority:** The Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; five direct joins support the standard, its application and affirmance. The four dissenters accept Mullane and reject a guaranteed-receipt requirement but do not join the adequacy application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the proved route was reasonably directed to Dusenbery. Mullane asks prospectively whether the means reasonably communicate in the actual circumstances. The government demonstrated more than dispatch to a prison address: employees collected certified mail, logged it, obtained the receiving unit employee's signature and ordinarily delivered it to inmates. The district court credited that relevant-period practice evidence. The missing old log does not establish an unreasonable system, and testimony about ordinary operation can establish the route without proof of this item's final receipt.

The contrary argument correctly emphasizes the government's exclusive custodial control. The inquiry therefore follows the onward route rather than ending at employee receipt. But neither Mullane nor Mennonite makes a particular additional verification measure or guaranteed success indispensable on this evidence. A feasible improvement alone does not invalidate the earlier reasonable procedure. Other attempted addresses supply no independent cure if the custodial route were deficient.

West Covina's protection of usable notice remains; its rejection of an individualized guide to published law does not excuse a deficient delivery method. Mathews's general calibration does not displace the settled notice-specific standard. This decision concerns the established procedure for the relevant period and the absence of an established known failure; it does not validate every prison mailing or resolve a different record involving unreliability or known nondelivery.

**Precedent treatment:** Mullane and Mennonite are applied to the actual route; West Covina's distinction between usable notice and legal instruction remains; Good's different pre-seizure requirement is not altered.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co., 339 U.S. 306: reasonable communication in the known circumstances governs; neither ritual dispatch nor guaranteed receipt is sufficient as a universal formula.
- Mennonite Board of Missions v. Adams, 462 U.S. 791: the practical-notice principle is applied without an absolute successful-receipt requirement.
- City of West Covina v. Perkins, No. 97-1230: usable notice and adequate access remain distinct from individualized legal instruction; that decision does not settle this delivery application.
- Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S. 319: its general procedural calibration does not replace Mullane's notice-specific standard.
- United States v. James Daniel Good Real Property: its distinct civil real-property pre-seizure protections remain intact.

#### Law After Decision

A known prisoner must receive notice through a reasonably calculated custodial route. The proved certified-mail and internal-distribution process satisfies that standard without proof of actual receipt. The holding is procedure-based and leaves genuinely unreliable or known-failure situations to their own records.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stevens dissents, joined by Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, and agrees that due process does not guarantee receipt. The dissent concludes that the government has not sufficiently demonstrated the final custodial handoff. A confined owner cannot supervise or replace the government's intermediary, so Mullane requires practical attention to how notice reaches that person, including reasonably available safeguards. Evidence of transmission between employees does not adequately establish the final inmate-directed protection. Greene and Mennonite emphasize practical communication; Good and West Covina preserve a meaningful opportunity to respond. The dissent does not infer nonreceipt from the denial or the missing old records, invent a failure rate, demand one particular signature form, or apply later prison practices retroactively. It would set aside the notice judgment and return for the proper consequences of the defect, without treating inadequate notice as proof of ownership or automatic entitlement to the property.

#### Source Notes

U.S. Const. amend. V; Dusenbery v. United States, No. 00-6567, 534 U.S. 161; J.A. 18–21, 36–38, 46–54, 59–62 and 67–71, as identified in the United States' merits presentation, for the relevant-period mailing practice. Personal receipt is not established.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Sixth Circuit's determination sustaining the challenged forfeiture notice stands. The judgment establishes constitutional adequacy of the proved procedure, without finding Dusenbery's actual receipt.

**End of entry: Dusenbery v. United States, Merits decision, January 8, 2002.**

### Great-West Life & Annuity Insurance Co. v. Knudson, No. 99-1786

Merits decision, January 8, 2002

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 1, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, which affirmed rejection of the plan's reimbursement claim. The question is whether this demand for repayment of medical benefits after a tort settlement is appropriate equitable relief under ERISA §502(a)(3). The settlement property was distributed to a special-needs trust and an attorney rather than held by the named beneficiaries in the manner required by the asserted property remedy. The claim seeks personal payment from those beneficiaries; the plan's contractual entitlement and the available federal remedy are distinct questions.

All nine Justices participated at argument or submission and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

**Opposing:** Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The particular reimbursement demand is unavailable under §502(a)(3), and its dismissal stands. The four dissenters would vacate the statutory-remedy rejection and remand for plan construction and the necessary restitution predicates.

#### Opinion Topology

THOMAS, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and O'CONNOR, SCALIA, and KENNEDY, JJ., joined. SOUTER, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which STEVENS, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### A contractual payment obligation does not establish an equitable remedy

**Holding and operative rule:** ERISA §502(a)(3) authorizes appropriate traditionally equitable relief, not personal payment of a past-due contractual debt merely because a claimant calls its demand an injunction, specific performance or restitution. A constructive trust or equitable lien ordinarily requires specifically identifiable property or proceeds, an equitable entitlement and the required connection to the defendant holding that property, subject to applicable defenses.

**Authority:** The Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; five direct joins establish the entire rule and its application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the remedy's actual predicates, not its label, determine whether it is equitable. Mertens gives the statutory limitation independent force by distinguishing traditional equitable categories from all relief once obtainable in a trust court. An injunction to pay an accrued debt does not avoid that distinction. Equity's exceptional use of specific performance to prevent otherwise inadequately remediable future losses does not supply this demand for an accrued sum.

Restitution has legal and equitable forms. The property-based form ordinarily restores identifiable property or proceeds from the defendant who holds them; an obligation to repay a benefit from other assets is different. Here settlement property went to the separate trust and attorney. The named beneficiaries' asserted contractual debt cannot substitute for the connection required by the remedy invoked against them. A charge against a beneficiary's trust interest also differs from the personal money judgment sought here. This is not a universal tracing formula for every equitable remedy; an accounting for profits has distinct predicates and is not this claim.

The contrary gain-restoration argument correctly recognizes that some restitution is equitable. It does not establish that this particular demand satisfies the traditional remedy's conditions. Harris Trust confirms genuine recipient equity within its own predicates and defenses, while Varity preserves appropriate individual relief within statutory categories. Neither turns every omitted remedy into authorized equity. The statutory ground sustains dismissal without deciding the alternative interpretation of the plan terms.

**Precedent treatment:** Mertens's traditional-category construction governs; Harris Trust's recipient equity is preserved rather than converted to personal collection; Varity retains its individual-equity channel and ordinary adequacy limits.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Mertens v. Hewitt Associates, 508 U.S. 248: its traditional-equitable-category construction controls; neither its compensation exclusion nor preserved genuine monetary equity is displaced.
- Harris Trust and Savings Bank v. Salomon Smith Barney Inc., 530 U.S. 238: equitable recipient relief remains available on its actual predicates and defenses; it supplies neither this missing property connection nor a universal tracing formula.
- Varity Corp. v. Howe, 516 U.S. 489: appropriate individual equity remains within statutory categories and ordinary adequate-remedy limits.

#### Law After Decision

Section 502(a)(3) does not authorize this demand for personal payment merely from the reimbursement clause. Genuine property-based equity remains available when its defendant, property and defense requirements are met. A different claim against another holder requires its own proper pleading, jurisdiction, process and equitable basis; the present judgment decides no such claim.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Souter dissents, joined by Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer, and would recognize the asserted return of benefits from a third-party recovery as the traditionally equitable restitution category. The claim is measured by alleged gain rather than the plan-loss compensation rejected in Mertens. A category need have been typically available in equity, not exclusive to equity, and direct possession should not control every restorative theory. Varity supports giving the redress provision practical effect within its limits, while Harris Trust recognizes that appropriate restoration can reach beyond fiduciaries. The government's trust-beneficiary analogy supports that characterization without establishing new property facts. The dissent would remove the categorical remedy bar and return plan interpretation and the actual remedial predicates for adjudication. It would award no reimbursement amount and would not adjudicate absent recipients' property.

#### Source Notes

29 U.S.C. §1132(a)(3); Great-West Life & Annuity Insurance Co. v. Knudson, No. 99-1786, 534 U.S. 204, for settlement allocation, claim and procedural record.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The dismissal of the reviewed federal reimbursement claim stands. An independently available claim against an appropriate holder remains subject to ordinary procedural rules; this judgment orders neither amendment nor joinder of the trust and decides no absent recipient's property rights.

**End of entry: Great-West Life & Annuity Insurance Co. v. Knudson, Merits decision, January 8, 2002.**

### Searles v. Van Bebber

Merits decision, January 8, 2002

Review of the Tenth Circuit judgment reported at 251 F.3d 869. The questions concern original 42 U.S.C. §1997e(e)'s restriction on mental or emotional compensation absent physical injury, the availability of nominal and punitive damages for the proved religious-diet violation, the consequence of a punitive instruction tied to invalid compensation; and the preserved court-access and due-process objection to the restriction at this remedial scope. The jury found liability, no physical injury and a separate culpable state of mind supporting punitive eligibility; it awarded $3,650 compensation and $42,500 punishment and returned verdicts for the other officials.

All nine Justices participated at argument or submission and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 9–0, at the reviewed remedial scope.**

Constitutional liability, the no-physical-injury finding, the punitive-culpability finding and the judgments for the other officials remain intact.

#### Opinion Topology

SCALIA, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STEVENS, O'CONNOR, KENNEDY, SOUTER, THOMAS, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined, and in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., joined except as to the court-access and due-process holding. The Chief Justice agreed in the disposition of that component.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Scalia | Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer throughout; Stone-Zsela except the constitutional-objection holding | All nine support every judgment component | All nine join statutory compensation, nominal and punitive holdings and remedies; eight Associates join court-access and due-process rationale; Stone-Zsela agrees only in that component's disposition |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The named injury restriction bars the compensatory award, not every remedy

**Holding and operative rule:** Original 42 U.S.C. §1997e(e) bars an action by a prisoner confined at filing for mental or emotional injury suffered in custody without the required prior showing of physical injury. That restriction bars the actual distress compensation awarded here even though the underlying wrong is constitutional; it does not erase the violation or compensation-independent remedies.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join this statutory holding in the Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress restricted recovery for a named injury category. Harris v. Garner establishes the confinement-at-filing trigger and the mental-or-emotional-injury limit; it does not convert that limit into immunity from every remedy. The jury found no physical injury, and the $3,650 award compensated mental or emotional harm. The religious source of the right does not exempt that recovery. Carey and Stachura also prevent compensation based merely on an abstract valuation of the constitutional right. The all-damages reading goes too far because it ignores the distinct functions of nominal recognition and punishment, while the contrary compensation argument cannot save a jury award for the expressly barred injury.

**Precedent treatment:** Harris is applied at its actual injury-and-filing scope; Carey and Stachura retain the distinction between a violation and compensable loss.

##### Nominal damages recognize the established violation

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1997e(e) does not bar nominal damages recognizing an established constitutional violation without compensable injury. On the preserved violation and lack of compensable injury here, the plaintiff is entitled to one dollar.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join this holding in the Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** Nominal relief does not compensate the mental or emotional injury named in the statute. Carey permits recognition of an established constitutional wrong without inventing injury or valuing the right itself as compensatory loss. The preserved liability verdict therefore requires the one-dollar award even though the distress compensation cannot stand.

**Precedent treatment:** Carey v. Piphus supplies nominal recognition; Stachura's prohibition on abstract-right compensation remains.

##### Punitive eligibility survives, but the amount requires a lawful redetermination

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1997e(e) does not categorically bar punitive damages for conduct satisfying Smith v. Wade's evil-motive-or-intent or reckless-or-callous-indifference standard. Punishment remains discretionary even when that culpability is established; an amount expressly tied to invalid compensation must be redetermined independently, preserving separate liability and culpability findings.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join this holding and remedy in the Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** Punitive damages punish culpable conduct and deter its repetition; they do not compensate distress. Smith supplies a distinct remedial predicate rather than a rule that punitive fault must invariably exceed every substantive violation standard. The jury separately made the required culpability finding, which survives the compensation defect. But the instruction expressly relied on the invalid compensatory award in setting the punitive amount. That linkage prevents preserving $42,500 as an independently sound calculation. The corrected proceeding concerns amount, without retrying established liability or punitive culpability. A positive punitive award is not compelled, punishment may not reproduce barred compensation, and no automatic punitive-to-nominal ratio applies. Fees must then be reconsidered in light of the lawful recovery.

**Precedent treatment:** Smith v. Wade's culpability requirement and discretionary punishment are applied; the distinct remedial functions recognized in Carey and Stachura prevent relabeling barred compensation.

##### The preserved constitutional objection fails at this remedial scope

**Holding and operative rule:** Applying the physical-injury restriction to this distress award does not deny access to court or due process where the established right remains enforceable through nominal relief and punitive relief on its distinct predicates. This ruling resolves the preserved challenge in these circumstances, without validating every possible application of the statute.

**Authority:** Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer join this portion of the Opinion of the Court. Stone-Zsela agrees in its disposition only.

**Controlling explanation:** The statute restricts one injury-based recovery rather than withdrawing adjudication of the underlying right. Searles has established liability, receives nominal relief and retains punitive relief subject to its lawful standard. At this actual scope, the constitutional objection therefore supplies no reason to restore barred compensation or upset the preserved remedial distinctions.

**Precedent treatment:** Harris's bounded restriction and Carey and Smith's distinct remedies operate together; no remedy-wide immunity follows.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Harris v. Garner: confinement at filing and the restriction to mental or emotional injury remain controlling; the nominal and punitive questions it left open are resolved here.
- Carey v. Piphus, 435 U.S. 247: nominal damages recognize a proved wrong without compensable injury.
- Memphis Community School District v. Stachura, 477 U.S. 299: abstract value of a constitutional right cannot substitute for actual compensatory injury.
- Smith v. Wade, 461 U.S. 30: evil motive or intent, or reckless or callous indifference, supplies punitive culpability; a qualifying award remains discretionary.

#### Law After Decision

Original section 1997e(e) bars the named distress compensation absent physical injury while preserving nominal recognition and punitive relief under their own predicates. Established liability and punitive culpability survive a separate damages-calculation defect. The lawful punitive amount and consequential fee decision remain ordinary remedial work under those settled rules.

#### Source Notes

Original 42 U.S.C. §1997e(e); Searles v. Van Bebber, 251 F.3d 869 (10th Cir. 2001), for the verdict, instructions and appellate disposition.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The lower court must enter one dollar nominal damages, redetermine punitive amount under instructions independent of invalid compensation, and reconsider fees. The constitutional-liability, no-physical-injury and punitive-culpability findings and verdicts for the other officials remain intact.

### Toyota Motor Manufacturing, Kentucky, Inc. v. Williams, No. 00-1089

Merits decision, January 8, 2002

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 7, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Sixth Circuit. The question is whether Williams's limitations established substantial limitation of performing manual tasks under the original ADA, warranting partial summary judgment in her favor. She experienced carpal-tunnel and related limitations in repetitive, gripping and overhead automobile-production tasks; the record also contains evidence of household and self-care function. Review concerns her favorable partial judgment. Toyota's separate request to reinstate its own summary judgment is outside the accepted question.

All nine Justices participated at argument or submission and decision.

#### Judgment

**Williams's partial summary judgment is set aside and the case remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

All nine require reconsideration of the same reviewed judgment. The lower courts apply the Court's manual-task standard, the controlling mitigation rule and ordinary summary-judgment principles. Toyota's separate summary judgment is not reinstated because that request lies outside the accepted question.

#### Opinion Topology

GINSBURG, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STEVENS, O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, SOUTER, THOMAS, and BREYER, JJ., joined, and in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., joined as to the occupational-shortcut and mitigation holdings. STONE-ZSELA, C. J., filed an opinion concurring in part and in the judgment.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Breyer throughout; Stone-Zsela only as to the occupational-shortcut and mitigation holdings | All nine support setting aside the reviewed partial judgment and remand | Eight join the severe-restriction, central-daily-task and permanent-or-long-term standard and its application; all nine join the independent occupational-shortcut and mitigation propositions |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Supports the same set-aside and remand | Joins occupational-shortcut and mitigation holdings; rejects the categorical threshold and proposes individualized important-manual-function standard |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Manual-task disability requires a substantial limitation of general daily-life function

**Holding and operative rule:** Under original 42 U.S.C. §12102(2)(A), an impairment substantially limits performing manual tasks when it prevents or severely restricts the individual from performing tasks central to most people's daily lives, with permanent or long-term impact. This is an individualized standard requiring considerable functional limitation, not complete incapacity or loss of virtually every manual ability; diagnosis alone is insufficient, and no numerical duration threshold applies.

**Authority:** The Opinion of the Court's manual-task-standard and application portions, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Eight direct joins establish the rule; Stone-Zsela does not join these portions.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that substantial limitation concerns a considerable restriction in the major activity asserted. The ADA's common definition is not confined to employment, so specialized assembly requirements cannot alone define manual-task disability. The original text and contemporaneous guidance concerning nature, severity, expected duration and enduring impact support the stated threshold.

Bragdon requires individual proof and rejects complete inability as an added condition. It does not erase substantiality or prohibit this activity-specific duration requirement. A permanent or long-term functional impact need not mean an incurable diagnosis; the Court sets no numerical minimum. Likewise, identifying central daily-life manual tasks does not restrict every major life activity to something performed each day or displace Bragdon's reproduction holding.

Williams is correct that sustained gripping, reaching and repetition may show important general capacities even if some household tasks remain possible. The standard admits that evidence; it rejects treating a class of demanding occupational tasks as sufficient by category alone. Household and self-care evidence also matters in its actual extent, without creating an automatic defense from the ability to perform some tasks. Ordinary summary-judgment principles govern competing evidence. The Court consults contemporaneous guidance without deciding general binding agency authority.

**Precedent treatment:** Bragdon's individual-proof and no-complete-incapacity principles remain; Sutton and Murphy's distinct occupational-breadth inquiry is not transferred to every activity; Kirkingburg supplies no additional disability method.

##### Occupational classification is not a substitute for the claimed activity

**Holding and operative rule:** Inability to perform a class of occupational tasks does not by itself establish substantial limitation in the separate major life activity of performing manual tasks. Workplace evidence remains relevant to the extent it proves limitation of the general manual functions at issue; ordinary household and self-care evidence must likewise be assessed without categorical conclusions.

**Authority:** All nine Justices directly join this severable proposition in the Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** Sutton and Murphy's class-of-jobs inquiry addresses working. A court cannot use that inquiry as a shortcut to establish a different activity. The Sixth Circuit's occupational category therefore does not establish Williams's manual-task disability as a matter of law. The relevant functional evidence must be assessed under the rule governing the particular activity, rather than excluded because it arises at work or treated as dispositive because it concerns a class of job tasks.

**Precedent treatment:** Sutton and Murphy's working rules remain confined to their actual activity; individual functional proof remains necessary.

##### Beneficial mitigation does not eliminate the underlying limitation

**Holding and operative rule:** The manual-task inquiry assesses the individual's underlying substantial limitation without treating beneficial correction or medication as eliminating it. Individual proof remains required, and coverage remains distinct from qualification, accommodation and safety; the claimant need not stop treatment.

**Authority:** All nine Justices directly join this severable application of Sutton and Murphy in the Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** Sutton and Murphy settle the mitigation baseline under the original ADA. Their rule identifies the functional condition assessed; it does not automatically establish its substantiality. The manual-task threshold applies to that legally defined underlying condition. Successful management does not remove protection by itself, while evidence still must establish this individual's substantial limitation. The Court therefore rejects neither the governing mitigation rule nor the independent need to prove disability.

**Precedent treatment:** Sutton and Murphy are applied unchanged; their underlying-impairment rule controls the remand.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Sutton v. United Air Lines, Inc., No. 97-1943, and Murphy v. United Parcel Service, Inc., No. 97-1992: the underlying-impairment rule is applied unchanged; beneficial mitigation does not defeat coverage, and the working-specific class inquiry does not define manual tasks.
- Bragdon v. Abbott, 524 U.S. 624: individualized substantiality and the distinction from complete inability remain; its reproduction and direct-threat holdings are intact.
- Albertson's, Inc. v. Kirkingburg, No. 98-591: its distinct safety-qualification rule remains; it supplies no manual-task or mitigation methodology.

#### Law After Decision

The original ADA now requires the stated severe restriction in central daily-life manual tasks and permanent or long-term impact, assessed individually under the existing underlying-impairment mitigation rule. Occupational categories alone do not establish disability; workplace and nonwork functional evidence remain relevant. The lower courts apply those settled rules to the reviewed partial judgment, with other claims retaining their existing procedural status.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela concurs in the occupational-shortcut and mitigation propositions and in setting aside the partial judgment, but rejects a categorical severe-and-permanent threshold. He would assess individualized substantial limitation of important manual functioning through breadth, frequency, duration and practical effect. These are relevant considerations, not separate mandatory elements. Important work-related functions cannot be excluded because they occur at work, and retained household abilities do not automatically defeat coverage. Expected duration informs substantiality without making permanence indispensable. Sutton and Murphy preserve the underlying-impairment inquiry; the difference between working and manual tasks requires correction of the appellate shortcut, not a restriction to only the most pervasive daily-life limitations. His proposed standard still requires proof rather than diagnosis alone, preserves distinct qualification and accommodation issues, and would not grant Toyota automatic judgment. That broader standard is noncontrolling.

#### Source Notes

Original 42 U.S.C. §12102(2)(A); contemporaneous 29 C.F.R. §1630.2(j); Toyota Motor Manufacturing, Kentucky, Inc. v. Williams, No. 00-1089, 534 U.S. 184, for the functional record and scope of review.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns through the Sixth Circuit for reconsideration of Williams's partial judgment under the controlling standard and ordinary procedural rules. The limited grant does not reopen an independently unreviewed judgment or forfeited coverage branch. Qualification, accommodation, safety and other preserved matters retain their separate elements and existing procedural status.

**End of entry: Toyota Motor Manufacturing, Kentucky, Inc. v. Williams, Merits decision, January 8, 2002.**

##### Other-agency displacement requires actual exercise over the relevant working conditions

**Authority:** The unanimous Opinion of the Court; all nine Justices directly join the rule, application and limits.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress made exercise of power, rather than its mere possession, decisive. The Coast Guard had not prescribed pertinent standards or comprehensively regulated the evacuation and emergency-response working conditions OSHA cited on this uninspected inland barge. Its general maritime responsibilities and investigation cannot supply that missing regulatory exercise.

Mallard Bay's coordination concern has force where actual regulatory regimes overlap. It does not justify leaving working conditions outside OSHA solely because another agency could have regulated them. The statute already accommodates genuine comprehensive regulation; the Court need not demand identical wording in the two agencies' rules. Conversely, regulation of unrelated vessel matters does not establish comprehensive occupational-safety control.

The result does not depend on choosing between hazard-based and area-based descriptions of working conditions: neither pertinent nor comprehensive actual regulation is established under either description here. Uninspected status is not itself proof of no Coast Guard regulation, and the holding does not allocate every maritime setting to OSHA. Statutory text resolves the dispute without a new deference holding or an inference of power from the accident's gravity.

**Precedent treatment:** The statutory-boundary principle is consistent with Mead's distinction between possession of authority and legally relevant exercise; Mead supplies no displacement merely from agency expertise.

##### Employment on this Louisiana barge is within the Act's workplace coverage

**Holding and operative rule:** Employment aboard this barge within Louisiana is employment in a workplace in a State under §4(a). Anchoring in navigable waters does not remove that workplace from the Act's geographic coverage; the independent other-agency exclusion remains governed by §4(b)(1)'s actual-exercise requirement.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join this independent holding in the Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** The Act's geographic and employment terms reach this Louisiana workplace. No general maritime exception appears in §4(a). Treating the Coast Guard's potential authority as a geographic exclusion would merge two distinct provisions and bypass §4(b)(1)'s condition. The Court therefore rejects the alternative workplace objection as well as the displacement theory. The receiving court must implement those holdings in the actual administrative posture, with the unchallenged citation merits retaining their status.

**Precedent treatment:** The decision construes the two statutory provisions independently; it creates no new maritime exclusivity or deference rule.

#### Precedent Treatment

- United States v. Mead Corp.: the distinction between statutory authority and its legally relevant exercise remains; agency expertise alone supplies no displacement. The present holdings rest on §§4(a) and 4(b)(1), not a new rule of interpretive deference.

#### Law After Decision

OSHA retains authority over these working conditions because Coast Guard potential authority was not exercised through pertinent standards or comprehensive regulation. Work on a barge in Louisiana falls within §4(a), subject independently to any displacement actually established under §4(b)(1). Actual overlap, rather than vessel status alone, governs other settings.
