## Event

United States v. Knights, No. 00-1260. Merits decision, December 10, 2001. Argued November 6, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, which affirmed suppression because the residence search pursued ordinary criminal investigation. The question is whether a known probation search condition and individualized reasonable suspicion justify the search notwithstanding that purpose. The written condition permitted searches by probation or law-enforcement officers, and the detective had reasonable suspicion connecting the residence to criminal activity.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument or submission and decision.

## Public Action

Reverse and remand, 9–0.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.** Supporting: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. The categorical investigative-purpose ground for suppression is rejected. The lower courts apply the condition-and-reasonable-suspicion rule; evidence is admissible if no independently preserved defect remains. Any genuinely open scope or factual issue must be resolved on its own record.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Scalia | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports the complete judgment | Entire opinion |

## Holdings

### The known search condition and individualized suspicion justify this probation search

**Holding and operative rule:** A warrantless search of a probationer's residence within a known, lawful search condition is reasonable when supported by particularized reasonable suspicion of criminal activity in the circumstances presented. Ordinary criminal-investigative purpose does not independently invalidate that combination; neither the condition alone nor reasonable suspicion alone supplies this holding.

**Authority:** The unanimous Opinion of the Court; all nine Justices directly adopt this rule, its justification, and its limits.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the probation relationship and notice of the express condition reduce the privacy interest implicated by this residential search, while individualized suspicion supplies an objective justification for this intrusion. Griffin establishes that lawful probation supervision changes the constitutional setting; it does not make its special-needs rationale the exclusive ground for every probation search. Knights's proposed purpose restriction would invalidate a search supported by this combined justification merely because the officer investigates crime. The Fourth Amendment does not require that result here.

Whren supports examining the objective justification after authority for the intrusion has been established. Its ordinary probable-cause traffic rule does not itself confer residential search power or decide motive doctrine for every search based on reasonable suspicion. Edmond and Ferguson retain their purpose inquiries in their suspicionless and special-needs settings. Scott governs the consequence of an unconstitutional supervisory search, not the antecedent validity of this condition-and-suspicion search; its exclusion rule remains intact. Any ultimate suspicion question properly remaining is independently reviewed under Ornelas, with its protection for historical findings and due weight to grounded contextual inferences.

The holding rests on reasonableness, not complete contractual consent or forfeiture of Fourth Amendment protection. Suspicionless residential searches and blanket consent are outside the question necessarily resolved because individualized suspicion supplies the sufficient ground here.

**Precedent treatment:** Griffin's supervisory-relationship principle is applied without making its rationale exclusive; Whren's objective method is applied within its source-specific limits; Edmond and Ferguson are distinguished; Scott's exclusion rule and Ornelas's review allocation remain operative.

## Precedent Treatment

- Griffin v. Wisconsin, 483 U.S. 868: the lawful supervisory relationship informs diminished privacy; special needs is not the exclusive possible justification.
- Whren v. United States, 517 U.S. 806: objective assessment follows a demonstrated source of search authority; its traffic holding is not generally extended to every reasonable-suspicion search.
- City of Indianapolis v. Edmond, 531 U.S. 32, and Ferguson v. City of Charleston, 532 U.S. 67: purpose remains material in their distinct suspicionless and special-needs settings.
- Pennsylvania Board of Probation & Parole v. Scott, 524 U.S. 357: exclusion for the institution's unconstitutional supervisory search remains unchanged; this decision finds the bounded search reasonable.
- Ornelas v. United States, 517 U.S. 690: the division between factual and ultimate legal review is applied unchanged.

## Law After Decision

The Fourth Amendment permits the defined probation-condition and individualized-suspicion combination despite ordinary investigative purpose. Existing scope, execution and exclusion rules retain their separate operation; the decision creates no general suspicionless-search or consent-waiver rule.

# EEOC v. Waffle House, Inc. — 2002-01-15

[Authoritative Record](../records/eeoc-v-waffle-house-inc_merits_2002-01-15.md)

## Event

EEOC v. Waffle House, Inc., No. 99-1823; 534 U.S. 279. Merits decision, January 15, 2002; October Term 2001, chunk 2. Argued October 10, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Fourth Circuit. The question is whether Baker's employment arbitration agreement prevents the EEOC from seeking individual relief in its own ADA action. Baker experienced a seizure, was discharged and filed a charge; disability discrimination remains alleged. The Fourth Circuit allowed prospective public relief but barred victim-specific remedies. The agency seeks reversal of that restriction.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

## Public Action

The restriction on victim-specific relief is reversed, and the case is remanded.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Restriction on victim-specific relief reversed and remanded, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Scalia and Thomas, who would vacate the FAA-based restriction and remand for consideration of any properly available independent state-law contract ground.

All nine reject FAA coverage and agree that actual recovery remains subject to statutory predicates and prevention of duplicate satisfaction. The Commission's public enforcement action continues; the majority permits pursuit of individual remedies without the dissenters' preliminary contract-enforceability inquiry.

## Holdings

### Part I. The FAA supplies no enforcement ground for this employment agreement

**Holding and operative rule:** Circuit City's section 1 employment-contract exclusion covers Baker's employment arbitration agreement, so the FAA supplies no enforcement command or federal arbitration-policy ground for restricting this EEOC action or its remedies. The exclusion does not itself determine an independent state-law basis for enforcement between Baker and Waffle House.

**Authority:** Part I of Justice Stevens's opinion is joined by all eight other Justices; nine direct joins control this ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the governing employment exclusion resolves the antecedent FAA question. Circuit City excludes employment contracts within the commerce reach asserted for the Act, including nontransportation employment. Baker's agreement belongs to that category. A general federal preference for arbitration cannot replace statutory coverage. The Fourth Circuit's FAA-based restriction consequently cannot stand. Gilmer concerns a securities-registration agreement and does not supply a contrary answer about this employment contract. Whether another source binds the employee remains distinct from the FAA holding.

### Part II. The Commission's independent action retains its individual-remedy authority

**Holding and operative rule:** An employee's private agreement selecting arbitration does not bind the nonparty EEOC or categorically eliminate victim-specific remedies Congress authorizes in the Commission's own ADA enforcement action. Even if an independent state-law ground makes the promise enforceable against the employee, that fact alone neither makes the Commission a contracting party nor removes those federal remedy categories; this is an independently sufficient ground for rejecting the relief bar.

**Authority:** Part II of Justice Stevens's opinion is joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, seven direct votes. Scalia and Thomas withhold assent to this ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the agency enforces its own statutory cause, rather than an assignment of Baker's claim. Section 12117(a) gives the Commission the enumerated Title VII enforcement powers, procedures and remedies. Section 706(f)(1) authorizes its action after the statutory process; section 706(g) authorizes appropriate individual equitable relief; and section 1981a expressly includes the Commission among plaintiffs who may seek the specified damages. Compensation and reinstatement can advance that public enforcement function.

First Options supplies the actual-assent principle, but the statutory enforcement structure completes the answer. General Telephone recognizes the independent public action including individual relief, and Gilmer preserves an agency enforcement role alongside an individual's arbitration obligation. Baker could commit himself to a forum; he did not undertake a promise for the Commission, which never agreed to arbitrate.

The dissent's strongest objection concerns the beneficiary's valid commitments, not direct compulsion of the agency. It would condition individual recovery on a state-law-contract determination. But the statutory permission for appropriate relief does not create a categorical exception whenever an employee chose an arbitral forum. Enforceability against Baker alone does not diminish the agency's enacted remedies. This conclusion decides the live private-contract argument without resolving the contract's state-law enforceability or a hypothetical FAA-covered agreement. An actual adjudication, settlement, release or satisfaction differs from the forum promise presented here and requires its own record.

### Part III. Statutory conditions govern any actual award

**Holding and operative rule:** The EEOC's authority to seek relief does not establish liability or an entitlement to an award: the applicable disability, qualification, unlawful-discrimination, injury, causation and remedy predicates must be proved, and the court must apply each remedy's conditions below. The court must prevent duplicate satisfaction of the same injury when actual overlapping recovery is established; no completed arbitration, settlement, release or prior satisfaction is established here.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join Part III's ordinary statutory and nonduplication requirements. Scalia and Thomas separately advocate the additional contractual limitation rejected by Part II; their Part III join does not adopt Part II.

**Statutory conditions incorporated in this holding:**

Back pay may not accrue earlier than two years before the charge and is reduced by interim earnings or amounts earnable with reasonable diligence. |
| Section 706(g)(2)(A) | Orders requiring union admission or reinstatement, employee hiring, reinstatement or promotion, or back pay are unavailable when the challenged refusal, suspension, expulsion or discharge rested on a reason other than the prohibited discrimination or retaliation. This is the distinct other-reason limitation incorporated with the enforcement provisions. |

That separate trigger does not establish a new ADA mixed-motive or causation rule here. |

For an accommodation claim, damages may not be awarded when the employer proves good-faith efforts, in consultation with the disabled person who informed it of the need, to identify and make an equally effective reasonable accommodation without undue hardship. |

The applicable size must be established for each of twenty or more calendar weeks in the current or preceding calendar year. |

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the enacted remedial menu remains conditional. Toyota supplies the operative disability rules; a seizure by itself establishes none of their required findings. Section 706(g)'s discretion concerns justified relief on proved facts, and section 1981a separately supplies damages on its own terms. Great-West interprets a different ERISA provision and does not delete this express damages authorization. The distinct limitations in section 706(g)(2)(A), section 706(g)(2)(B) and section 1981a must remain distinct rather than being combined into a new general exception. The employee's conduct can matter to mitigation, actual satisfaction or a properly established judgment or settlement. Without such a record, possible future duplication cannot justify removing every victim-specific remedy at the outset. Employer size and the other damages predicates remain matters for ordinary adjudication.

### Part IV. The categorical restriction is reversed without a contract-enforceability prerequisite

**Holding and operative rule:** The Fourth Circuit's categorical prohibition on victim-specific relief is reversed, and the action is remanded for adjudication of liability and appropriate statutory relief. The Commission may pursue that relief without first establishing that Baker's private forum promise is unenforceable under state law.

**Authority:** The same seven Justices who join Part II join Part IV; Scalia and Thomas instead support vacatur and a bounded inquiry into any properly available independent contract ground.

**Controlling explanation:** Both the absence of FAA coverage and the Commission's independent statutory authority defeat the restriction as entered. Because the second ground resolves the private forum agreement's asserted effect on the Commission's remedy categories, an antecedent state-law enforcement proceeding is unnecessary. The remand returns the ordinary statutory merits and remedy work under Parts II and III.

- **First Options of Chicago, Inc. v. Kaplan:** Applied for actual assent; it supports but does not replace the independent statutory-remedy analysis.
- **General Telephone Co. of the Northwest v. EEOC, 446 U.S. 318:** Applied for the Commission's independent public enforcement action, including individual relief.

## Law After Decision

The FAA supplies no bar for this excluded employment agreement. Independently, an employee's private forum promise does not remove Congress's individual-remedy categories from the EEOC's own enforcement action, even if the promise binds the employee under an independent source. Liability and every actual award remain governed by their statutory predicates, mitigation rules and nonduplication limits. Concrete adjudication, release, settlement and satisfaction questions await a record establishing them.

## Separate Writings

**Justice Thomas, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by Justice Scalia.** They join Part I because Circuit City's governing employment exclusion removes the FAA as a source of enforcement, despite their continuing disagreement with that precedent. They also join Part III's ordinary liability, remedy and nonduplication conditions. The Commission may bring its independent public action and cannot itself be compelled to arbitrate.

They reject Parts II and IV because nonparty assent does not answer whether a court should grant relief for Baker's individual injury in disregard of his own enforceable commitment. Section 706(g)'s requirement of appropriate relief and the distinction between permission to sue and entitlement to a particular remedy, in their view, require respect for a valid beneficiary promise even though broader public relief remains available. First Options addresses actual assent to arbitration, not that distinct remedial question; Great-West similarly preserves attention to the statute authorizing the particular remedy.

The FAA cannot establish enforceability here. They would therefore vacate its categorical restriction and remand to consider any properly available independent state-law ground, the promise's actual scope, and its consequence for individual relief under their view. They presume neither a valid independent ground nor a completed arbitration, and ordinary preservation limits remain. Their position does not compel arbitration of the agency or affirm the existing restriction on a displaced FAA premise.

# United States v. Arvizu — 2002-01-15

[Authoritative Record](../records/united-states-v-arvizu_merits_2002-01-15.md)

## Event

United States v. Arvizu, No. 00-1519; 534 U.S. 266. Merits decision, January 15, 2002; October Term 2001, chunk 2. Argued November 27, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 232 F.3d 1241, as amended. The district court rejected the stop and voluntary-consent challenges; Arvizu's conditional plea preserved suppression review. The Ninth Circuit held the stop unlawful and treated the ensuing search as tainted. The questions are the proper totality method and whether the supported pre-stop combination establishes reasonable suspicion. The United States seeks restoration of suppression denial; Arvizu seeks exclusion.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

## Public Action

The Ninth Circuit judgment resting on an unlawful stop is reversed, and the case is remanded for the preserved independent consent issues.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The stop was supported by reasonable suspicion. The Ninth Circuit must address the remaining preserved consent-voluntariness and scope issues under their applicable review. The unlawful-stop taint ground falls, but the conviction and complete suppression denial are not finally restored without that review.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | O'Connor | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Reverses unlawful-stop ruling and returns preserved independent consent issues | All four holdings; Scalia joins the governing Ornelas application while separately retaining his review objection |
| Concurrence | Scalia | No other Justice | Joins opinion and judgment in full | Criticism of inferential deference within independent review; same result under traditional independent review |

## Holdings

### I. Supported observations must be considered in their actual relationship

**Holding and operative rule:** Reasonable suspicion for an investigative stop depends on an objective, particularized inference from the total circumstances known before the seizure; an observation cannot be excluded merely because it has an innocent explanation or would be insufficient alone. Courts must still test each proposed inference's factual basis and actual weight, so neither the number of observations nor an officer's unexplained intuition supplies the required justification.

**Authority:** Justice O'Connor's opinion is joined throughout by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine directly adopt all four holdings and their limits; Scalia separately qualifies the review discussion without withholding his application join.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that context may make several lawful observations informative together. Terry requires articulable suspicion of wrongdoing; Cortez requires the whole picture and an inference particular to the person or vehicle stopped. Wardlow likewise permits contextual consideration without making location or ambiguous behavior automatically sufficient. Innocence of each isolated act does not compel a conclusion about the connected pattern.

The amended Ninth Circuit opinion acknowledged totality and softened some earlier formulations. It nevertheless categorically discounted the children's posture and waving and separated observations before assessing their relationship. That method removed potentially probative context too early. Arvizu's profiling objection remains substantial: every response cannot become suspicious merely because an officer says experience makes it so. The court must identify supported connections and may assign little or no weight to an asserted factor. The totality rule is not a command that every fact count positively, and the later discovery of marijuana cannot justify the earlier seizure.

### II. Ultimate sufficiency receives independent appellate judgment

**Holding and operative rule:** Under Ornelas, appellate courts independently determine ultimate reasonable suspicion while reviewing historical facts, credibility and genuinely factual subsidiary inferences for clear error, and giving genuine due weight to grounded local and experiential inferences. That contextual weight does not create deference or a presumption of correctness for the officer's ultimate constitutional conclusion.

**Authority:** All nine join this application of the governing Ornelas allocation. Scalia's concurrence preserves his objection to that allocation's inferential formulation without withholding a present join.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that review must preserve both the trial court's factual role and the appellate court's responsibility for constitutional sufficiency. Ornelas supplies that division. Local knowledge and experience can explain the significance of an observed route or pattern; they cannot manufacture a fact or insulate the conclusion that the seizure was lawful. Describing what the children did and deciding what constitutional significance their conduct has are not the same task.

Scalia objects that deference to an inferential conclusion can compromise genuinely independent review. The controlling formulation protects genuinely factual subsidiary judgments and gives contextual understanding actual weight while reserving the ultimate sufficiency decision to the reviewing court. It adopts no intermediate standard or numerical formula. The supported pre-stop findings here permit that legal application now; another remand simply to redo the stop comparison is unnecessary.

### III. This connected pre-stop pattern supplied reasonable suspicion

**Holding and operative rule:** The sensor-linked checkpoint-bypass route, final bypass turn, timing context and particular occupant and apparent-cargo observations, assessed together with their ordinary explanations and the agent's actual experience, supplied a particularized basis for this brief investigative vehicle stop. The holding does not require a traffic offense, direct contraband observation or proof that criminal activity was more likely than an innocent explanation, and does not treat any one greeting, vehicle type or neighborhood as independently sufficient.

**Authority:** All nine join this record-specific sufficiency holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the connection among the observations distinguishes this case from a general profile. Sensors tracked travel along a route capable of avoiding an operating checkpoint, and the final turn took the last bypass road. The approaching staffing transition supplied relevant local context. The driver's slowing and stiffness, combined with the children's persistent forward-facing waving and raised knees, supported a limited inference of concealed cargo and an attempted appearance of normality. Neither a greeting nor an acknowledgment of an officer is legally required; the behavior matters only within that route-and-load pattern.

The opposing account has force. The road serves homes, recreation and tourists; a minivan is ordinary family transport; children may wave or rest their feet on luggage; and the shift change was still about forty-five minutes away. The agent had local patrol and alien-stop experience, not a personal history of drug stops. These qualifications reduce the weight but do not eliminate the supported relationship. A similar minivan's drug stop one month earlier does not taint all minivans. Registration to a relative in a broadly described smuggling neighborhood contributes little without a particular connection and is unnecessary to the sufficient combination. The Court presumes neither knowledge of the staffing schedule nor erratic or unlawful driving.

Wardlow's actual insufficiency holding involved a materially thinner departure-and-location record. Here the sensor-tracked bypass and passenger/cargo observations provide the additional individualized connection. The conclusion authorizes the brief stop, not a search of the vehicle or its bags, and rests exclusively on information preceding the seizure.

### IV. The lawful stop removes taint but does not resolve independent consent issues

**Holding and operative rule:** The Ninth Circuit's judgment resting on the stop's illegality is reversed, and the case is remanded for proper appellate treatment of the preserved independent consent-voluntariness and scope issues. A lawful stop removes that taint ground but neither establishes voluntary consent nor determines its extent; the claimed exterior-only permission and disputed gun-hand interaction remain subject to their applicable review and preservation limits.

**Authority:** All nine adopt this complete disposition and bounded remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that stop justification and search authorization remain distinct. The district court rejected both the stop and voluntary-consent challenges, and the conditional plea preserved suppression review. The Ninth Circuit's unlawful-stop analysis treated the ensuing search as tainted without deciding the independent consent grounds. Reversing the stop ruling therefore does not finally restore the conviction or conclusively sustain the whole suppression denial. The receiving court must give the trial court's findings their applicable review and address the consent questions actually preserved.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1:** Applied for the objective, particularized reasonable-suspicion threshold and limited investigative-stop authority.
- **United States v. Cortez, 449 U.S. 411:** Applied for assessment of the whole picture and rational, individualized connections among observations.
- **Ornelas v. United States, 517 U.S. 690:** Applied for independent ultimate review, clear-error protection of facts and genuine contextual weight without a police-correctness presumption; no new review standard is adopted.
- **Illinois v. Wardlow, January 12, 2000:** Its contextual method is applied; its actual insufficiency holding is distinguished by the additional sensor-linked bypass and vehicle-specific observations here. Its rejection of categorical location or flight permission remains intact.
- **Florida v. J. L., March 28, 2000:** Its requirement of sufficient grounds before intrusion and rejection of retrospective contraband justification remain effective.
- **United States v. Knights, December 10, 2001:** Distinguished because the probation status and search condition there are absent; its confined holding supplies no ordinary-driver exception.

## Law After Decision

Supported observations capable of innocent explanations retain their actual contextual force but gain no automatic positive weight by accumulation. Independent judicial review determines whether their relationships supply individualized suspicion. This pre-stop combination meets that standard without neighborhood stigma, proof of an observed offense or post-stop contraband. The lawful-stop conclusion leaves the independent authority for the ensuing search to the preserved consent questions.

## Separate Writings

**Justice Scalia, concurring, joined by no other Justice.** He joins the Court's full application of governing law and its reversal and bounded remand. He retains his Ornelas dissent's preference for deferential review and his objection that giving weight to an inferential conclusion can be conceptually inconsistent with independently deciding the ultimate issue. A factual description of the children's waving can receive factual protection; whether it is suspicious cannot be sheltered from constitutional judgment simply by calling it an officer inference. Reversal follows here even under traditional independent review. His join applies Ornelas as governing law without endorsing its original choice of standard, dispensing with innocent explanations or authorizing the subsequent search.

## Procedure After Action

The Ninth Circuit must give the trial court's findings their applicable review and resolve the preserved consent-voluntariness and scope questions. The reasonable-suspicion issue is settled in favor of the stop. The lawful-stop conclusion does not itself finally restore the conviction or resolve the remaining request for suppression.

## Source Notes

United States v. Arvizu, 232 F.3d 1241, amended from 217 F.3d 1224; Terry, 392 U.S. 1; Cortez, 449 U.S. 411; Ornelas, 517 U.S. 690. The record distinguishes pre-stop observations from the later search and contraband discovery, and preserves disputed consent voluntariness and scope. Local patrol and alien-stop experience do not establish prior personal drug-stop experience. Registration neighborhood alone supplies no finding that the relative or address was tied to crime.

# Lee v. Kemna — 2002-01-22

[Authoritative Record](../records/lee-v-kemna_merits_2002-01-22.md)

## Event

Lee v. Kemna; No. 00-6933. Merits decision, January 22, 2002; argued October 29, 2001. Review of the Eighth Circuit's affirmance of denial of federal habeas relief after Missouri courts rejected the continuance claim. The question is whether the state's written-motion and absent-witness requirements supply an adequate procedural ground when subpoenaed alibi witnesses unexpectedly disappear during trial and counsel orally requests a short delay.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The Court vacates the judgment resting on this procedural-default ground and remands for the remaining habeas inquiry.

## Judgment & Remedy

Vacated and remanded, 6-3. **Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. **Opposing:** Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

The federal courts must consider the due-process continuance claim under the remaining applicable habeas rules, with this inadequate state-ground barrier removed. The conviction remains effective pending lawful further proceedings. The separate cause and innocence gateway rulings are not affirmatively reversed by this judgment.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Souter | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg, Breyer | Vacates and remands | Entire exceptional-inadequacy rule and bounded habeas remand |
| Dissent | Kennedy | Scalia, Thomas | Would affirm the default judgment | Rule 24.10's continuing substantive information function |

## Holdings

### I. This exceptional application of the continuance rules is inadequate to bar federal review

**Holding and operative rule.** A normally valid state procedural rule does not bar federal review when its application is exorbitant in the particular circumstances. Here the combined unexpected disappearance of subpoenaed witnesses who had attended trial, substantial prior presentation of the alibi and anticipated return, brief requested delay, and the trial judge's stated scheduling impediments make the additional written and repeated showings inadequate as a federal-review bar; the holding creates no general oral-motion or emergency exemption.

**Authority.** Souter's opinion speaks for Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, six Justices, on the complete rule and application.

**The distinct state requirements.** Missouri Rule 24.09 requires a written motion and affidavit unless the adverse party consents to an oral application. Rule 24.10 separately requires materiality and diligence; the witness's identity and residence if known, or diligence to learn them; reasonable grounds to expect attendance or testimony within a reasonable time; the particular expected facts and absence of another available person who could prove them as fully; and absence not procured, consented to or contrived by the applicant, with good faith rather than vexation or delay. If an affidavit is considered insufficient, the court must permit amendment. The oral-application exception does not erase these information requirements, and opponent consent is not inferred from silence.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that adequacy depends on the rule's operation as well as its ordinary legitimacy. Coleman preserves genuinely independent and adequate state grounds. Osborne distinguishes useful presentation from pointless repetition after a matter has been adequately brought before the judge. Ford protects fair notice and regular application, but neither regularity nor the absence of an identical earlier case conclusively answers this exceptional application. The Court respects Missouri's construction of its rules and decides the separate federal question.

The State has legitimate interests in current information about diligence, materiality and likely availability, as the dissent stresses. Here subpoenas, earlier attendance, prior alibi descriptions and counsel's explanation of the expected local presence supplied the substantial practical basis for the short request. The emergency made a contemporaneous sworn explanation of the unexplained disappearance unavailable. The trial judge's stated scheduling impediments would not have been cured by a perfectly written motion. Those facts work together: earlier testimony descriptions alone, a scheduling remark alone or an unexpected absence alone would not establish the same result. The holding neither declares literal compliance nor requires trial judges to announce every procedural defect. It holds that the additional formal demand, in this particular setting, does not adequately justify withholding federal consideration.

### II. Removal of the default barrier returns the distinct constitutional claim

**Holding and operative rule.** With this state ground inadequate, Lee need not additionally establish cause and prejudice to overcome that ground; the federal court must adjudicate his continuance claim under due process and any independently applicable habeas restriction. The state appellate court's procedural ruling does not become an adjudication of the federal claim's merits under 28 U.S.C. §2254(d) merely because it resolved the appeal.

**Authority.** The same six Justices join the bounded remand and its separation of adequacy from entitlement to relief.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that access to federal consideration and a right to relief are different inquiries. Coleman concerns excuses for an adequate default; it does not require such an excuse after the ground has been found inadequate. Trest confirms that default is a defense with actual predicates rather than a subject-matter-jurisdiction defect. The underlying due-process inquiry asks whether denial of the delay arbitrarily deprived Lee of a meaningful opportunity to present his defense in the actual trial circumstances. It requires examination of the missing testimony's disputed significance under the applicable habeas law. The relatives' account that an officer directed them to leave remains an allegation, and their anticipated alibi is not a finding. The separate gateway rulings are not reversed by eliminating this inadequate ground. The constitutional merits and entitlement to relief remain for adjudication because the federal courts did not reach them.

## Precedent Treatment

- Coleman v. Thompson, 501 U.S. 722: applies its requirement of an independent and adequate state ground and preserves exceptions for genuinely adequate defaults; the present inadequacy ruling precedes those excuses.
- Osborne v. Ohio, 495 U.S. 103: applies its distinction between useful presentation and purposeless repetition in the exceptional circumstances here; it supplies no general waiver of continuance requirements.
- Ford v. Georgia, 498 U.S. 411: preserves fair notice and regular application as adequacy safeguards without making them conclusive in every application.
- Trest v. Cain (December 9, 1997): applies its distinction between procedural default and jurisdiction; it supplies neither an automatic hearing entitlement nor the underlying due-process violation.
- O'Sullivan v. Boerckel (June 7, 1999): preserves ordinary exhaustion through a state's normal review opportunities; the present adequacy holding does not dispense with otherwise applicable habeas requirements.

## Law After Decision

The adequate-state-ground doctrine now expressly reaches this exceptional application of Missouri's continuance requirements. Useful, regularly applied procedural requirements remain enforceable, but their ordinary legitimacy does not make an exorbitant application adequate. Removal of this particular default barrier leaves the due-process merits, remaining lawful habeas restrictions and entitlement to relief for their own adjudication.

## Separate Writings

Kennedy, joined by Scalia and Thomas, would affirm because Rule 24.10 retained a substantive function apart from the written-motion form. Earlier alibi descriptions and earlier attendance did not necessarily establish what the witnesses would then say, whether they could be obtained within a reasonable time, or the current diligence and other facts needed to evaluate the delay. A focused contemporaneous presentation also creates a reviewable record. A known procedural ground may support appellate affirmance even when the trial judge relied on scheduling; an uncited requirement is not thereby useless. Osborne involved repetition of a legal objection already foreclosed, not the initial support for a factual request. The dissent objects that searching earlier portions of the record for substantial compliance can displace the State's actual presentation rule. It does not hold that every formally regular rule is adequate in every application, and relies on no finding about why the witnesses left or whether their alibi was true.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns through the Eighth Circuit for adjudication of the continuance claim and remaining applicable habeas questions with this default ground removed. The conviction remains in force pending lawful further proceedings.

Mo. Rules Crim. Proc. 24.09 and 24.10; Lee v. Kemna, 213 F.3d 1037; 28 U.S.C. §2254. The reason for the witnesses' departure, the truth of the proposed alibi and the constitutional effect of refusing the delay remain disputed rather than established by the adequacy judgment.

### The exception removes one disallowance without guaranteeing a deduction

**Holding and operative rule:** Compliance with the four-of-seven exception in the governing section 264(c)(1) removes the relevant section 264(a)(3) restriction on systematic premium-financing borrowing; it does not affirmatively confer a section 163 deduction or immunize the borrowing from the substantive-sham inquiry. Winn-Dixie's strongest argument is that Congress deliberately regulated insurance borrowing in detail and authorized the four-of-seven pattern. The exception has real work: qualifying timing avoids that particular statutory bar. But avoiding the bar is not an affirmative grant covering every transaction. This reading therefore does not permit general revenue policy to override a legislatively conferred benefit.

Here compliance with the exception and both adverse economic findings are accepted. Tax motivation alone is insufficient, and the Court does not require every intentionally tax-favored investment to be profitable without its incentive. Because both relevant substance findings are negative here, no universal conjunctive or disjunctive test is chosen for arrangements with a substantial nontax purpose or meaningful pretax returns. That different factual question is outside this review.

- Gitlitz v. Commissioner, January 9, 2001: its protection of enacted tax consequences is preserved alongside the independent requirements of the actual deduction; it supplies no immunity for this arrangement.

The four-of-seven exception is not a statutory safe harbor from substantive-sham scrutiny. This arrangement's accepted lack of both nontax business purpose and pretax profit potential supports the disallowance. Transactions with meaningful economic substance, mixed findings or an affirmative statutory incentive remain governed by their own legal and factual predicates.

# Guest v. Leis — 2002-02-27

## Holdings

### I. Limited account entrustment does not categorically extinguish subscriber privacy

**Holding and operative rule:** A user's limited entrustment of nonpublic subscriber information to a bulletin-board operator for account administration does not by itself extinguish a reasonable expectation of privacy. Confidential credentials and identifying records of a restricted account remain protected when the user actually expects confidentiality and the account's terms, functions and practices make that expectation reasonable; each claimant must establish a personal protected interest and an actual governmental intrusion.

**Authority:** Part I of Breyer's opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg, giving five direct votes for the complete rule. O'Connor also joins its application to confidential access credentials, giving that proposition six direct votes, but does not join its broader identifying-record scope. No aggregation under Marks is necessary.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that access for a limited operational purpose is not automatically surrender to unrestricted governmental examination. Katz protects a reasonable personal expectation of confidentiality. Carter's retained shared-privacy principle and Ferguson's distinction between a limited information relationship and a different governmental use support examining what the account actually protects. A password's function is to restrict access, not invite public inspection.

Miller and Smith remain binding for bank business records and dialed telephone numbers. Both involved information conveyed to another, and confidentiality alone therefore cannot distinguish them. Here the question concerns the user's own restricted account information, its exclusion and identification functions, the terms on which it is maintained, and the intrusion actually proved. The mere fact of an operator's possession cannot replace that inquiry. Nor does an operator's notice concerning access to messages conclusively establish that account credentials or identifying records were made public.

This rule does not immunize records from a lawful evidence search. Zurcher rejects a requirement that every person whose records are searched be a criminal suspect. The equipment could be seized for lawful off-site examination, but that authorization does not by itself resolve whether examination of particular subscriber information fell within the constitutionally sufficient, offense-limited warrant. The lower court used its categorical privacy conclusion to avoid that subscriber-specific inquiry. The affected institutional claims return for that application and their independent defendant, attribution and causation requirements. Publicly exposed information does not obtain protection through this holding.

### II. The personal defendants retain conduct-date qualified immunity

**Holding and operative rule:** An individual official is protected from personal constitutional damages when the unlawfulness of the challenged conduct was not sufficiently clear under the law existing when the official acted, assessed on the supported facts material to that conduct. The 1995 subscriber-information searches at issue fall within that protection: the later account-privacy rule does not supply prior notice, and the officials need not prove actual reliance on authority expressly permitting each search.

**Authority:** Part II of Breyer's opinion is joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg. Those eight direct joins sustain the personal judgment; Stone-Zsela dissents from this portion.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the constitutional question and the personal defense have different temporal inquiries. Harlow and Anderson require an objective assessment of conduct-date legal clarity, with the right stated at the specificity the actual conduct requires. Wilson and Elder preserve consideration of all relevant law rather than an identical-case requirement. Saucier left that defense in place, and Denius distinguished later recognition of privacy from sufficient earlier notice.

The challenged searches occurred in June and August 1995. The later Carter, Ferguson, Kyllo and Denius decisions cannot establish notice at those earlier dates. Miller and Smith supplied substantial support for treating voluntarily conveyed operational information as outside the subscriber's protected sphere; no then-operative rule made the newly recognized account-specific distinction sufficiently clear to defeat the personal defense on the reviewed claim.

The proposed actual-reliance requirement would replace the settled defense, not apply it. Harlow protects objectively reasonable official action amid legal uncertainty without requiring an inquiry into the official's actual legal sources or subjective consultation. Section 1983's remedial purpose does not by itself remove that established protection. The ordinary merits rule's allowances for reasonable factual perceptions do not eliminate the distinct problem of uncertainty about legal obligations. The Court therefore retains the objective defense. This immunity protects the personal defendants, not a governmental entity, and does not decide whether the disputed searches satisfied the substantive constitutional rule.

## Precedent Treatment

Katz v. United States supplies the personal reasonable-confidentiality inquiry, now applied to the specified restricted subscriber records.

United States v. Miller and Smith v. Maryland retain their holdings for conveyed bank business records and dialed numbers. Their third-party rationale is limited against an automatic extension to all confidential credentials and identifying records of a restricted bulletin-board account; confidentiality alone remains insufficient.

Minnesota v. Carter and Ferguson v. City of Charleston are applied at their retained-sharing and purpose-specific information boundaries, without creating a general rule for every third-party relationship.

Zurcher v. Stanford Daily is applied to preserve lawful evidence searches without requiring suspicion of wrongdoing by every affected record holder.

Harlow v. Fitzgerald and Anderson v. Creighton retain their objective conduct-date personal defense; the proposed authoritative-reliance replacement is rejected.

Wilson v. Layne, Elder v. Holloway, Saucier v. Katz and Denius v. Dunlap are applied to separate violation, legal notice and personal relief. Later privacy recognition supplies no retrospective notice for these 1995 searches.

## Law After Decision

From February 27, 2002, restricted bulletin-board subscriber information receives the stated personal, account-specific inquiry; entrustment alone is not a categorical forfeiture. Six Justices support that rule for confidential credentials and five support its stated broader identifying-record scope. The eight-Justice objective immunity holding preserves the existing personal defense and its conduct-date inquiry. A newly recognized constitutional application does not itself defeat that defense, and personal immunity supplies no institutional defense.

## Separate Writings

**O'Connor, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** Confidential access credentials have an exclusion function materially different from ordinary provider records. She would vacate the categorical dismissal only at that scope and require a claimant-specific showing on remand. Names, addresses and other routine identifying information deliberately conveyed for administration ordinarily fall within Miller and Smith; expected confidentiality alone does not distinguish them. Ferguson and Denius require attention to what was entrusted and the actual use, but do not establish the broader account rule adopted by five Justices. She joins objective personal immunity and rejects a requirement of actual authoritative reliance.

**Scalia, joined by Kennedy and Thomas, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** The three would affirm the subscriber-information judgment on the merits as well as on personal immunity. Miller and Smith do not depend on public dissemination: they address information voluntarily conveyed to a provider, often with an expectation of limited use. Calling the records digital or credential-related does not establish a constitutional distinction from that settled rule. Kyllo's protection of concealed home information does not control operational information held by a different person. Each claimant still must establish a personal interest and an intrusion; the existing equipment and private-message dispositions are distinct. The three join Part II's independent objective immunity ground.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** The Chief Justice joins the full privacy rule and both institutional remands but would also vacate personal immunity. After an actionable violation is established under the complete substantive rule, including its reasonable-factual-mistake allowances, he would require the official to identify binding precedent or enacted authority directly authorizing the challenged conduct, prove genuine reliance and show reasonable grounds for treating the permission as valid and applicable. An instruction accurately transmitting the authority may prove reliance; silence, unsettled law, departmental practice, general investigative authority or an ungrounded supervisor or prosecutor assurance would not. Knowing material falsification, concealed disqualifying facts or manufactured authority would defeat reasonable reliance. So would disregard of the authority's apparent limits, apparent unlawfulness under controlling constitutional principles, or a factual mistake already rejected under the substantive merits rule. The protection would include an unexpected change in controlling law that the official could not reasonably anticipate.

He would overrule Harlow and Anderson only insofar as insufficiently clear law independently excuses a proven violation, disapprove Wilson to that extent and retain Elder's consideration of relevant law for the new inquiry. Section 1983's remedial commitment, in his view, warrants that change while concrete reliance protects legitimate official decisiveness. He acknowledges an additional factual inquiry and litigation cost; ordinary pleading and summary-judgment procedures would remain. A valid warrant may defeat the constitutional claim, but its existence alone does not prove actual reliance, and later litigation citations cannot establish the official's 1995 knowledge. A prior judgment granting personal immunity would not itself constitute authority permitting the underlying conduct. He would allow the officials to make the newly required showing on remand. His proposal would not reopen final judgments, change the plaintiff's other elements, create an institutional remedy, displace an independent statutory defense or decide a separate absolute immunity.

## Holdings

### The three-year tax lookback excludes a prior legally disabling stay

**Holding and operative rule:** The three-year income-tax priority lookback in the applicable 11 U.S.C. section 507(a)(8)(A)(i), carried into dischargeability by section 523(a)(1)(A), is suspended for the interval during which a prior bankruptcy stay legally prevented collection of that same liability. This traditional suspension requires no bad-faith finding and does not extend the period for ordinary collection difficulty, every successive petition or every deadline in the Bankruptcy Code.

**Authority:** Breyer's Opinion of the Court is joined in full by all eight other Justices. Their direct unanimous agreement controls the rule, its statutory limits and the affirmance.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Code's temporary prohibition cannot consume the collection interval that its priority and discharge rules protect. The lookback is backward-looking, but its finite protection performs a limitations function; other remedies surviving the interval do not eliminate that function. Irwin supplies a rebuttable traditional tolling background, not a power to disregard statutory terms. The present suspension removes only time during which the Code legally disabled collection.

The taxpayers emphasize the absence of an express tolling clause and Congress's extensions elsewhere. Those provisions do different work. Section 523(b) addresses debts actually excepted from an earlier discharge, not this dismissed Chapter 13 proceeding. Section 108(c) addresses covered proceedings outside bankruptcy; its distinct setting does not forbid this suspension. The different 240-day rule's express treatment of offers in compromise does not make a collection stay usable time under the three-year rule. A broader construction of section 108(c)(1)'s suspension language is unnecessary to resolve this interaction.

Brockamp's detailed tax-refund filing and amount structure rebutted the incapacity extension sought there; it does not make every tax-related period untollable. Noland forbids changing statutory priority categories by general fairness, but this rule preserves the legislated interval and category. Raleigh keeps the substantive tax burden distinct from federal dischargeability. TRW's refusal to add a general discovery extension to the FCRA does not govern a later statutory prohibition on collection. No fraud, concealment or abuse premise supplies the rule.

The return was due October 15, 1993. The May 1, 1996 Chapter 13 filing imposed the stay that continued when the Chapter 7 petition followed on March 12, 1997. Excluding that interval places this debt within the three-year lookback. Because the legal disability operated regardless of motive, affirmance requires no finding that the taxpayers abused bankruptcy.

## Holdings

**The actual entireties rights are federal rights to property.** After demand and nonpayment, §6321 reaches the taxpayer's existing Michigan entireties rights considered together—present use, exclusion, enjoyment of income, survivorship and participation in joint disposition—even though the whole estate cannot be conveyed or partitioned unilaterally and is shielded from ordinary separate creditors. **Controlling explanation:** Drye assigns definition of the rights to state law and their federal lien classification to federal law. Michigan's actual beneficial incidents remain valuable legal advantages despite consensual transfer restrictions. The contrary argument correctly begins with property belonging to the taxpayer, but incorrectly makes unilateral withdrawal or monetization indispensable without statutory text. National Bank of Commerce rejects the proposition that local creditor limitations alone settle federal reach. Bess separates the particular reachable economic right from other interests in the arrangement. No single incident, bare future expectancy or unified state label controls every case. Rodgers concerns enforcement and nondebtor protections, not automatic collection of a half share; Romani preserves specific priority rules. Attachment precedes the conveyance to the wife, while the escrow agreement's half share supplies no adjudicated valuation.

- **Drye v. United States:** Its state-definition/federal-classification method governs the actual present rights; its unilateral inheritance power is an application, not an exclusive condition silently added to §6321.
- **United States v. National Bank of Commerce, 472 U.S. 713:** Applied to distinguish state creditor limitations from federal lien classification.
- **United States v. Bess, 357 U.S. 51:** Applied to identify the particular reachable economic interest without transferring other interests in the arrangement.
- **United States v. Rodgers, 461 U.S. 677:** Enforcement and nondebtor protections retain their separate statutory requirements; attachment supplies neither an automatic sale nor a half-value allocation.
- **United States v. Estate of Romani:** Its specific priority protections remain applicable on their own predicates after attachment.

## Law After Decision

Michigan entireties restrictions do not defeat federal attachment to the taxpayer's existing beneficial rights considered together. The lien follows those actual rights, while their valuation, competing priorities, enforcement authority and the nondebtor spouse's independent interests retain separate legal work. The rule does not turn a bare future expectancy into property or assume identical marital-property law in every State.

## Separate Writings

**Thomas, dissenting, joined by Stevens and Scalia.** Section 6321 reaches property belonging to the taxpayer; it cannot create a distinct individual estate from inseparable marital ownership. Michigan gives the husband neither unilateral withdrawal nor a power to sell, partition or redirect the estate. Drye and National Bank of Commerce involved those actual powers over value, and therefore do not settle this case. Joint enjoyment and a veto on conveyance are meaningful benefits, but remain incidents of the marital entity's ownership rather than separately owned assets. The longstanding collection treatment supports this statutory caution; it does not replace the inquiry. The dissent does not make a creditor-exemption label conclusive or declare all restricted, nontransferable interests unreachable. It would affirm the attachment ruling without deciding a different tenancy, completed severance or separate income right.

**Scalia, dissenting, joined by Thomas.** Entireties ownership also gives institutional security to the nondebtor spouse, including a spouse whose domestic contribution yields no separate income. Removing that protection through a new federal property classification counsels particular statutory caution. This additional ground neither finds that Mrs. Craft held that domestic role nor creates a general marital-assets exemption. Stevens joins the common statutory dissent, but not this additional writing.

Further proceedings determine valuation and lawful treatment of the escrow under the rights-specific attachment rule. The excluded transfer theory and the unrelated cross-appeal remain outside this review; the Court does not adopt either former lower-court remedial calculation as the attachment value.

## Holdings

### I. The statutory collateral standard and ordinary prejudice requirement

**Controlling proposition:** The July 1, 1997 federal application is governed by amended §2254. Clearly established constitutional law is measured at the relevant state merits adjudication; a state application must be objectively unreasonable, not merely incorrect, and ordinary Strickland prejudice requires a reasonable probability of a different result without an additional fundamental-unfairness or unreliability requirement for lawful mitigation.

**Authority:** Part I of Breyer's opinion is joined by all nine Justices at this framework level. Stevens does not join the Court's Cronic application or its final rejection of the ordinary tactical claim.

**Controlling explanation:** Lindh makes the application date decisive for the amendment's applicability. Strickland and Cronic existed when the State adjudicated the claim; Williams supplies the Court's present construction of the statutory review standard. Its contrary-to and unreasonable-application inquiries remain distinct, and its standard is not satisfied or defeated merely by pointing to judicial disagreement. The district court's added Fretwell requirement cannot govern lawful mitigating evidence. That error does not itself establish entitlement to the writ. A separately sufficient, correctly assessed performance ground can defeat the confined claim without deciding actual prejudice. Later opinions cannot create an April 1982 professional duty or constitutional law unavailable at the state merits date.

**Precedent treatment:** Lindh v. Murphy, June 23, 1997, supplies the application-date distinction. Williams v. Taylor (Terry Williams), 529 U.S. 362 (2000), supplies objective statutory review and rejection of an extra Fretwell hurdle for lawful mitigation. Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668 (1984), retains its separate performance and ordinary-prejudice requirements.

### II. These omissions within actual advocacy do not trigger Cronic's presumption

**Controlling proposition:** Counsel's failure to call additional mitigation witnesses and waiver of closing do not amount to a complete failure of adversarial testing in this sentencing proceeding, where counsel substantively presented mitigation already in evidence, sought mercy, obtained favorable cross-examination and resisted damaging prosecution proof. The challenged choices require Strickland analysis; the conclusion neither excuses abandonment nor eliminates other recognized circumstances in which Cronic presumes prejudice.

**Authority:** Parts II–IV of Breyer's opinion are joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, including the author in that eight-Justice coalition. Stevens dissents from these applications and the judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** Cronic addresses a complete loss of the relevant adversarial function, not every failure within representation. Counsel's opening directed the same jury to the guilt-stage experts and mitigating circumstances, invoked military service and remorse, and requested mercy. Cross-examination elicited the Bronze Star and honorable discharge, and objections excluded damaging prosecution material. These acts supplied adversarial sentencing assistance rather than physical presence alone. Cone argues that guilt-stage insanity evidence did not become a sufficient individualized case for life after conviction, and that the isolated acts left the decisive prosecution case unanswered. Those objections concern the quality and adequacy of the assistance actually given. They do not establish the complete failure on which this claimed presumption depends. No numerical tally of acts or universal rule that any activity defeats every Cronic category follows.

**Precedent treatment:** United States v. Cronic, 466 U.S. 648 (1984), is applied to the claimed complete-failure category; its other recognized grounds for presumed prejudice remain. Strickland governs the particular alleged errors within this represented proceeding.

### III. The confined tactical claim fails on a sufficient performance ground

**Controlling proposition:** On the reviewed available-mitigation and closing-argument record, the state court's rejection of deficient performance was not an objectively unreasonable application of Strickland. That sufficient ground defeats this confined claim without an actual-prejudice determination; it does not decide distinct allegations of failure to investigate or develop mitigation, their default treatment, or every remaining sentencing ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The ordinary claim was addressed below and is fairly included in review of the state rejection. Concrete reasons support its disposition: credited reliance on experts whose testimony remained before the jury, counsel's assessment of his mother's ineffective appearance, interviews of other potential witnesses, the reviewed record's lack of a showing that omitted testimony would help, and avoiding the more effective prosecutor's unanswered rebuttal. Neither a strategy label nor the district court's statement that strategy was unreviewable supplies the rule.

Cozzolino limited punishment proof to statutory aggravation or mitigation actually raised; rebuttal must explain or controvert a proposition advanced. Its troubled-childhood evidence did not make present criminal proclivities relevant. Later defense proof can make premature admission harmless only by supplying actual rebuttal relevance. Thus a general fear of all bad-acts questioning would be mistaken, but no rule immunized every possible character statement. The Court makes no new witness-specific admissibility finding.

Pritchett provided pretrial support for resentencing when one of two aggravators failed and the remaining weighing could not be assumed. It guaranteed no new hearing whenever any aggravator failed. Cone's 1984 direct appeal later distinguished the surviving aggravators and applied harmless error; that later ruling is not counsel's April 1982 knowledge. Counsel's testimony supplied multiple reasons for waiving closing, including existing mitigation and avoiding the particular rebuttal. The Court endorses no general practice of deliberately creating appellate error.

This independent state-performance assessment does not depend on the district court's erroneous additional prejudice requirement. The restriction of the reviewed record to available evidence makes preservation of separate development and default questions essential; broad praise of counsel's preparation does not decide them.

**Precedent treatment:** Williams and Strickland are applied to an independently sufficient, context-specific performance ground. Cozzolino v. State, 584 S.W.2d 765 (Tenn. 1979), and State v. Pritchett, 621 S.W.2d 127 (Tenn. 1981), retain their actual relevance and aggravator limits; Cone v. State, 665 S.W.2d 87 (Tenn. 1984), remains separately dated.

### IV. The remaining sentence matters require their own adjudication

**Controlling proposition:** The Cronic-based sentence writ is reversed and the confined ordinary tactical claim rejected; the Court of Appeals must adjudicate the remaining properly pending sentence claims and distinct investigation/default issues under their own legal requirements. The convictions remain undisturbed, and no blanket reinstatement of the district court's entire sentencing judgment follows.

**Controlling explanation:** The Sixth Circuit expressly left other sentencing challenges unaddressed. The separate investigation allegations raise disputed questions about the adequacy and independence of the second-petition bar and asserted cause from unavailable investigative funding. Cone's remand argument presents those issues but establishes neither cause nor invalidity of the state ground. Lee concerns a different state procedural application and does not resolve Tennessee's. Ordinary further adjudication determines any necessary proceedings; the mandate does not itself direct an evidentiary hearing or funding.

**Precedent treatment:** Lee v. Kemna, January 22, 2002, retains its exceptional state-ground holding and is not extended into a determination of these separate default issues.

Cronic's complete-failure doctrine remains distinct from Strickland's particular-error inquiry. Compaq Computer Corp. v. Commissioner. Merits decision, May 28, 2002. ## Holdings

#### I. Correct tax characterization requires consistent profit measurement

**Holding and operative rule:** A court independently reviews a transaction's legal characterization for federal tax purposes while reviewing its subsidiary historical findings for clear error. Where the taxpayer owns the dividend entitlement and bears the foreign tax liability, the pretax calculation includes the gross dividend, including withholding paid on the taxpayer's behalf; a worldwide after-tax calculation must also account for the corresponding legally available United States foreign tax credit.

**Authority:** Part I of Stevens's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies nine votes for this rule and its application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the tax paid on Compaq's behalf remains part of its gross dividend. Old Colony Trust v. Commissioner treats discharge of the taxpayer's obligation by another as income to the taxpayer; Diedrich v. Commissioner applies the same principle when a recipient pays the transferor's tax. Treasury Regulation §1.901-2(f)(1) identifies the foreign-tax payer by legal liability even when a withholding agent remits payment, and §1.901-2(f)(2)(i) preserves that treatment when another party assumes the liability. Compaq held the dividend entitlement on the record date and bore the Netherlands tax obligation. The withholding therefore did not turn the gross dividend into fictional income.

The Tax Court's calculation subtracted the foreign tax but excluded the corresponding United States credit, yielding an apparent loss of approximately $1.5 million. That calculation measures neither pretax nor worldwide after-tax return. Correctly including the gross dividend and actual transaction charges produces approximately $1.894 million before tax; accounting for the credit and approximately $644,000 in net worldwide tax produces approximately $1.25 million after tax. The Court rejects the Commissioner's inconsistent measure, not every inquiry into a transaction's actual economic operation.

Frank Lyon v. United States separates reviewable legal tax characterization from subsidiary historical facts. The findings about the one-hour meeting, limited investigation, promoter coordination and brief holding period remain intact. Correcting the legal computation does not find that Compaq conducted extensive diligence, and the profit figures do not establish a universal minimum-profit test.

**Precedent treatment:** Old Colony Trust and Diedrich are applied to withholding that discharges the dividend owner's tax liability; Frank Lyon's distinction between historical facts and legal tax characterization is applied without change.

#### II. This actual market transaction has economic substance and sufficient business purpose

**Holding and operative rule:** The established purchase of Royal Dutch American Depository Receipts, acquisition of the dividend entitlement and resale must be recognized under the applicable income, capital-loss and foreign-tax-credit provisions: its actual return, acquired economic rights and market exposure establish economic substance and sufficient business purpose despite its tax objective. Substantive-sham scrutiny remains available, but tax motivation, rapid resale and the reduction of avoidable risk do not by themselves defeat recognition of this demonstrated arrangement.

**Authority:** Part II of Stevens's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies nine votes for the complete bounded holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that these actual economic interests distinguish the arrangement from a tax form without qualifying substance. Gregory v. Helvering requires statutory substance rather than labels; Knetsch v. United States rejects purported borrowing without genuine economic function. Frank Lyon requires recognition of genuine multiparty rights and duties supported by substantive business considerations even when favorable tax treatment helps explain the transaction.

Compaq acquired and disposed of securities through 46 transactions on the New York Stock Exchange, held the dividend entitlement on the record date, and obtained an actual pretax return after transaction charges. Prices could and did move; execution and dividend risks remained. The promoter selected the counterparty, trade sizes and prices, and management undertook only limited investigation. Those facts support the Commissioner's contention that tax consequences drove the arrangement. They do not erase its established return and economic position or transform the trades into a closed market whose prices Compaq controlled. Neither nominal title nor reporting income alone supplies the conclusion.

Winn-Dixie Stores v. Commissioner preserves substantive qualification even when a taxpayer avoids a particular statutory prohibition. Its accepted absence of both nontax business purpose and pretax profit potential differs from this record. The credit scheme does not displace that scrutiny, but scrutiny must give effect to the actual economic rights and legally correct computation. The established facts resolve substance and purpose here; a new purpose trial would identify no remaining material fact necessary to this disposition.

The Court adopts no safe harbor for nominal profits, fabricated trades, circular controlled pricing, nonexistent foreign tax liability or failure of an independent credit limitation. The case presents both substance and sufficient purpose, so it does not require a universal choice among conjunctive, disjunctive or factor-based formulations for records lacking one of those features. The 1997 §901(k) holding-period provision does not govern the 1992 transaction or establish automatic validity for all earlier transactions.

**Precedent treatment:** Gregory and Knetsch retain substantive-sham scrutiny; Frank Lyon is applied to the real economic rights acquired here; Winn-Dixie's distinction between avoiding a particular restriction and substantively qualifying for a tax benefit remains intact.

#### III. Recognition removes the reviewed adjustment and its dependent penalty

**Holding and operative rule:** Within the Commissioner's stipulation that the transaction's reporting is otherwise unchallenged, recognition removes the deficiency and §6662 negligence penalty insofar as they depend on the rejected substantive-sham adjustment, leaving only computation under the applicable statutory requirements. The contingent request to deduct transaction expenses if the transaction is disregarded has no operative work once the transaction is recognized.

**Authority:** Part III of Stevens's opinion of the Court has nine votes for the deficiency, penalty and bounded computation treatment: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Its concluding treatment of the contingent expense-deduction request has eight opinion votes: Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; Stone-Zsela agrees with that disposition only.

**Controlling explanation:** Recognition restores the challenged income, loss and foreign-credit treatment within the reporting dispute the Commissioner has preserved. The sham adjustment therefore supplies no resulting underpayment on which to sustain the dependent negligence penalty. That conclusion does not treat limited investigation as invariably reasonable care. The alternative expense request was conditioned on disregard; recognition removes that condition and makes the alternative unnecessary. Further proceedings implement the applicable statutory computation within the stipulation, rather than reopen the case to search for an unasserted disallowance theory. Independent ownership, reporting and credit-limit objections excluded by the stipulation remain outside the question reviewed, and their statutory requirements remain in force.

## Holdings

#### I. Related timely acts do not revive completed discrete claims

**Holding and operative rule:** A known discrete discriminatory or retaliatory act must be charged within its own §706(e)(1) period, subject to independently established equitable grounds; a related later act or the present effect of the earlier act does not restart that period. The ordinary period is 180 days, extended where qualifying state or local proceedings were initially instituted to 300 days or thirty days after notice of their termination, whichever is earlier; the distinct statutory seniority-system triggers remain intact.

**Authority:** Part I of Thomas's opinion is joined by all eight other Justices. Parts I and III command nine votes; Part II commands six, as specified below.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that each completed discharge, promotion refusal, discipline or other discrete decision is its own employment practice for this filing question. Evans distinguishes an old act's continuing effects from a current violation. Ricks locates the relevant discriminatory decision rather than its later consequences. A common supervisor, motive or connection to timely conduct does not change when a completed practice occurred.

Morgan's broader related-act theory would make the filing command ineffective whenever an employee alleges another similar decision. The statute instead supplies particular periods and particular qualifications. Section 706(e)(2) separately treats a seniority system adopted for an intentionally discriminatory purpose: the practice occurs upon adoption, when an individual becomes subject to it, or when application injures the aggrieved person. That amendment supersedes Lorance's former seniority timing rule at its stated scope; it does not create a general related-act exception. Section 706(g) bars backpay liability beginning more than two years before the charge was filed with the Commission; that ceiling limits relief on a timely practice and revives no stale discrete claim.

The charge here was filed February 27, 1995, giving the agreed May 3, 1994 start of the 300-day window; no dispute invokes the earlier thirty-day alternative. The March 8 amendment concerns the March termination. Known discrete acts outside that period cannot support independent liability merely through relationship to later acts. This record does not require deciding whether occurrence or reasonable discovery governs a concealed or undiscovered discrete act. TRW's different statutory text and exception do not decide that distinct Title VII issue, and no discovery exception is adopted here.

**Precedent treatment:** Evans and Ricks govern completed acts and present effects; Lorance is subject to the enacted seniority amendment; TRW remains confined to its own statutory construction.

#### II. One continuing hostile environment may include its earlier components

**Holding and operative rule:** A hostile-environment charge encompasses the whole connected unlawful practice when at least one act contributing to that same environment occurs within the applicable filing period, including earlier constituent conduct relevant to liability. Earlier actionability or knowledge alone does not sever that practice, but a later unrelated incident, a different environment or an intervening change ending the earlier practice does not revive it; discrete decisions retain their separate clocks.

**Authority:** Part II of Thomas's opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplying six votes. O'Connor, Scalia and Kennedy dissent from this component.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the statutory unit is the hostile environment, not every utterance viewed alone. Harris supplies the totality inquiry into objectively and subjectively abusive conditions without requiring psychological injury. Oncale requires conduct because of protected status and assessment of severity or pervasiveness in context. An individual constituent incident need not alone establish that collective wrong. When conduct contributing to the same environment occurs within the window, the charged practice occurs there even if it began and became actionable earlier.

Amtrak and the partial dissent emphasize that an employee could have sued after recognizing an already actionable environment. That opportunity does not end a practice that continues. The statute measures occurrence of the charged wrong; it does not require the employee to split the same conditions at the window's first day. Rotella's rejection of delayed pattern discovery for a complete old injury addresses a different question. This rule identifies the continuing practice rather than adding a discovery extension.

The connection must be established, not assumed from the allegation list. The kind and persistence of conduct, participants and a material interruption inform whether one environment continued; these are contextual considerations rather than a mandatory exclusive checklist. An empty interval is not automatically dispositive, but a change ending the environment prevents a later incident from reviving it. A discrete personnel decision does not become timely merely because it also supplies evidence of hostility.

The claim remains subject to proof of the substantive environment, protected-status causation and employer attribution under the governing actual-aid rules. The two-year backpay ceiling and traditional equitable defenses remain distinct. On remand the courts must determine continuity and the evidentiary consequences under this rule; the present record does not establish that every alleged event belonged to one practice.

**Precedent treatment:** Harris and Oncale supply the collective substantive practice; Rotella is distinguished; the existing Andrade, Faragher and Harrison attribution rules remain separate and unchanged.

#### III. Equity, background evidence and trial review retain independent work

**Holding and operative rule:** Waiver, estoppel and equitable tolling require their established grounds, and a prejudicial-delay defense requires lack of diligence and resulting prejudice; related acts or an internal complaint alone establish neither. Older conduct may be admissible background evidence without creating independent liability for a stale discrete claim, and the appellate court must address the limitations ruling's actual evidentiary and instructional consequences and preserved independent objections before determining the proper further proceedings.

**Authority:** Part III of Thomas's opinion is joined by all eight other Justices. Their common agreement requires further appellate work; the three Part II dissenters would also reject the connected-practice liability rule.

**Controlling explanation:** Zipes treats timely charging as subject to waiver, estoppel and equitable tolling rather than as an inflexible jurisdictional barrier. Robbins & Myers does not let a collateral grievance automatically extend the filing period. Edelman permits verification of an otherwise sufficient timely submission; it does not make untimely initiation timely. Young's tolling for a legal disability supports no universal extension here. Each actual equitable ground must be proved.

Amtrak may assert a traditional delay defense upon proof of deficient diligence and prejudice. The earlier internal complaint does not establish either element by itself. The precise prejudice, remedy for proved delay, and application of that defense against the EEOC are not decided in this private action. Nor does the filing rule create a general employer defense based on preventive policies and employee nonuse, an issue the existing attribution decisions leave open.

The district court's partial summary judgment preceded a November 19, 1998 defense verdict. The Ninth Circuit vacated both judgments and ordered a new trial without resolving separate evidentiary objections. That verdict cannot automatically establish harmlessness because Morgan challenges the limitation of environment evidence and related instructions. Conversely, rejecting discrete revival proves no independent evidentiary error. The judgment is affirmed as to the connected-environment route, reversed as to stale discrete revival, and remanded to apply both rules and resolve preserved trial objections. Neither automatic restoration of the defense verdict nor an unconditional new trial for stale discrete claims follows.

**Precedent treatment:** Zipes and Robbins & Myers retain their distinct equitable and grievance rules; Edelman's timely-initiation condition and Young's actual-disability rationale remain intact.

## Holdings

#### I. Section 853(e) does not authorize restraint merely because property may later qualify as a substitute

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 853(e), as incorporated by §982, authorizes preservation of the property identified by its reference to subsection (a); it does not authorize restraint of untainted potential substitute property merely because §853(p) may later require its forfeiture. A continuing restraint based solely on that enlargement must be vacated even when the request for release arises after conviction.

**Authority:** Part I of O'Connor's opinion of the Court is supported by all nine Justices: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. That common coalition controls both holdings and the complete statutory remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that eventual forfeiture and present restraint require their respective statutory authority. Section 853(e) expressly identifies subsection-(a) property. Section 853(p) separately reaches other property of the defendant, up to the qualifying value, when the defendant's act or omission makes covered property unavailable because it cannot be found despite due diligence, was transferred or sold to or deposited with a third party, was placed beyond the court's jurisdiction, substantially diminished in value, or was commingled so that division cannot be accomplished without difficulty. Potential satisfaction of those conditions does not convert substitute property into subsection-(a) property.

The government invokes mandatory substitution, dissipation risk, §853(o)'s liberal-construction direction and the lower court's analogy to Billman's RICO restraint rule. Those arguments explain the preservation objective but do not change the property Congress designated. Liberal construction implements the enacted grants; it does not erase the distinction between the property presently restrainable and property reachable through a later substitution order. The possibility of dissipation cannot supply an omitted power.

Libretti requires statutory authority independently of agreement and distinguishes the actual forfeiture categories. Monsanto sustains restraint of tainted forfeitable property; Caplin & Drysdale rejects a counsel-fee exemption for such property. Neither establishes authority over this untainted IRA merely because it may become a substitute.

The continuing order invokes §853(e). The $1.2 million verdict and notice of possible substitution are not an order forfeiting the IRA, and the government has not sought the required amendment. Because the statutory source fails, the present denial of release cannot stand. The postconviction timing of the request does not repair that defect. The broader appellate counsel-of-choice question is unnecessary to resolve the challenged restraint and is not reached.

**Precedent treatment:** Libretti's statutory-boundary principle is applied; Monsanto and Caplin & Drysdale retain their tainted-property holdings and are distinguished from the missing substitute-restraint authority.

#### II. Release from this restraint leaves distinct lawful postconviction powers under their own conditions

**Holding and operative rule:** The Fourth Circuit's asset-restraint ruling is reversed, and the release motion is remanded with instructions to vacate the IRA restraint insofar as it rests solely on §853(e) and potential substitute status; the IRA must be released from that restraint unless the government obtains a distinct legally authorized postconviction order satisfying its actual predicates. Georgia garnishment protection cannot defeat authorized federal forfeiture, but federal supremacy does not supply missing federal restraint authority.

**Authority:** Part II of the same unanimous opinion controls the confined remedy and the federal-state distinction.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a new statutory label cannot preserve the invalid order. Section 853(g) operates after entry of an order declaring property forfeited and protects the property actually ordered forfeited. The existing money judgment is not the absent order against the IRA. Section 853(p) remains available under its own unavailability and value conditions; this decision neither supplies those findings nor relieves the government of obtaining the necessary order.

Georgia's garnishment provisions protect the account before payment and ordinarily protect paid benefits except for the garnishable quarter, subject to any greater exemption. Those State rules cannot defeat a valid federal forfeiture. But supremacy resolves conflict with authorized federal action; it does not authorize federal action Congress withheld.

Section 982(b)(2) presents a separate, unpreserved issue. Its intermediary restriction concerns a defendant who handled but did not retain the laundered property, with an exception when the offense conduct involved three or more separate transactions totaling at least $100,000 in any twelve-month period. Gormley did not raise that claim in the district court. Its factual predicates and any lawful consideration in later substitute proceedings are not decided here; the reservation creates no independent entitlement to reopen an issue.

The court must remove the unlawful basis now, rather than continue it on the possibility of eventual forfeiture. Any distinct postconviction order must stand on its own authority and actual conditions. The result grants relief from this restraint, not a constitutional exemption or an adjudication that precisely $80,000 must be paid to appellate counsel. The convictions and all other judgment components outside the granted asset question remain intact.

**Precedent treatment:** Libretti's separation of statutory authority from procedural or consensual sufficiency is preserved in the remedy; Monsanto and Caplin & Drysdale remain available within their actual tainted-property scope.

## Event

United States v. Farmer. Merits decision, June 10, 2002, on full merits submission. The question is whether a defendant who substantially shows that seized assets may be legitimate and necessary for trial counsel is entitled to prompt adversary review of taint and need.

Civil seizures began July 16, 1998. Farmer filed a claim and a $5,000 cost bond; no civil forfeiture action preceded the May 2, 2000 indictment. ## Holdings

#### I. This supported trial-counsel claim requires prompt adversary review

**Holding and operative rule:** A criminal defendant who makes a substantial, specific showing that assets held in continuing civil seizure may be untainted and are needed to retain trial counsel is entitled to a prompt, focused adversary hearing on the disputed taint and actual financial need. The denial is immediately reviewable under the collateral-order rule when postponement until the end of trial would irretrievably lose the asserted counsel opportunity; Farmer's sworn submissions and the identified legitimate-property claim satisfy the hearing threshold.

**Authority:** Part I of Kennedy's opinion of the Court has nine direct joins: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Their agreement covers reviewability, the threshold, its application and entitlement to the hearing; their explanatory and burden differences are stated separately below.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the preliminary seizure determination cannot conclusively settle this later, supported request. Civil seizures began in July 1998. Farmer filed a claim and a $5,000 cost bond, yet no civil forfeiture action preceded the May 2000 indictment. He then sought a hearing before trial. His affidavit and sworn answers supported the magistrate's effective-indigency determination. Counsel's affidavit identified agents' reported recognition of legitimate merchandise. That account supplies a particular supported assertion, not a final determination of title or lack of taint.

The government disputes Farmer's other resources and relies on the warrants. At appellate argument it accepted a hearing if he sufficiently established inability to retain counsel. The sworn record satisfies that initial requirement. The government remains free to contest the actual resources and assets at the hearing. Requiring conclusive proof before permitting the procedure needed to test those matters would confuse entitlement to process with entitlement to release.

Monsanto expressly reserved the adversary-hearing question; its approval of probable-cause restraint of tainted property does not answer this one. Caplin & Drysdale supplies no right to spend proved forfeitable proceeds, but a disputed asset does not become proved proceeds merely because it was seized. A focused post-seizure hearing preserves that distinction without trying the criminal charges. Immediate review protects the time-sensitive counsel opportunity that ordinary final review could no longer restore.

**Precedent treatment:** Monsanto's express process reservation is resolved for this supported post-seizure counsel claim; its substantive tainted-property ruling and Caplin & Drysdale's fee-exemption ruling remain intact.

#### II. The required process reflects the actual interest, error risk and bounded government burden

**Holding and operative rule:** Under Mathews, the substantial and time-sensitive interest in retaining trial counsel with legitimate funds, the concrete risk of error in continued custody and the value of focused adversary testing require the hearing on this record, while the government's forfeiture interest permits a substantial initial showing and a hearing confined to the disputed property and need. Neither every seizure nor an unsupported request triggers an asset hearing.

**Authority:** Part II of Kennedy's opinion of the Court has eight direct joins: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Scalia joins the operative hearing rule on his distinct post-seizure ground, not this Mathews explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the requested hearing has practical value proportionate to its confined burden. Mathews directs attention to the private interest, the risk of erroneous deprivation and probable value of additional procedure, and the government's interests and burdens. The ability to use legitimate assets for a defense can be lost before a later forfeiture judgment supplies an answer. Here the concrete property assertion, sworn need evidence and prolonged custody without a civil forfeiture action establish more than a speculative desire for additional process.

Adversary testing can identify other resources and examine whether the particular needed assets are supported by probable cause of taint. Warrants supported the seizures but did not resolve those disputed matters conclusively. Good preserves due process as an independent protection even when a seizure satisfies the Fourth Amendment. Its ordinary pre-seizure protection for civil real property does not automatically govern movable property or establish a new pre-seizure rule here; its independence principle nevertheless defeats treating the warrant as a complete procedural answer.

The government has a legitimate interest in preserving forfeitable property and avoiding a premature trial of the indictment. A substantial initial showing screens unsupported demands. Limiting the hearing to the particular assets and actual need protects that interest while providing usable review. This conclusion determines why a hearing is required; it does not itself determine how the separate ultimate release burden must be allocated.

**Precedent treatment:** Mathews is applied to this disputed post-seizure setting; Good's independent-process principle is applied without extending its real-property pre-seizure rule to all assets.

#### III. Farmer bears the confined release burden at the hearing

**Holding and operative rule:** At the limited hearing Farmer must establish by a preponderance of the evidence that the particular assets needed for counsel are untainted and were seized without probable cause supporting continued forfeiture custody, and that he actually needs those same funds to retain counsel; the government may contest his other resources and present probable cause of taint. If he establishes that showing, the court must permit access to the legitimate funds required for counsel unless an independently established lawful basis prevents release; if he does not, this counsel-access claim supplies no release.

**Authority:** Part III of Kennedy's opinion of the Court has eight direct joins: Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Stone-Zsela opposes the allocation of the taint burden to Farmer and does not join this part. His agreement that need must be proved by a preponderance does not become a join in the complete allocation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court adopts the Fourth Circuit's confined standard for the relief Farmer requests. The substantial initial showing opens the hearing; it is not the final release showing. Farmer seeks access to particular assets for a particular defense need and must establish both their legitimacy and that need by the stated standard. The government may rebut his financial showing and offer probable cause connecting the assets to forfeiture. The warrant is evidence of the initial basis, not a conclusive answer at the adversary hearing.

The competing allocation would require the government to justify continued restraint once taint is materially contested, leaving only need to Farmer. The Court instead retains the movant's burden for this limited counsel-access claim. A substantial threshold and a usable hearing prevent the owner from having to prove the entire case before obtaining process; they do not require shifting the ultimate burden for this requested release. The inquiry remains about the disputed assets and resources, not proof of criminal guilt or final adjudication of the entire forfeiture.

Success requires access to the legitimate amount actually needed unless a separate lawful basis for custody is established. Failure defeats release on this counsel claim. The ultimate burden in any forfeiture proceeding is unchanged. Living expenses, interest on seized funds and final forfeiture merits are outside the reviewed counsel question. Those limits do not diminish the definite hearing entitlement or the conditional relief the governing standard requires.

**Precedent treatment:** Monsanto and Caplin & Drysdale remain controlling for genuinely tainted property; neither had decided this hearing allocation. The Fourth Circuit's release standard is adopted here, not described as an already established Supreme Court test.
