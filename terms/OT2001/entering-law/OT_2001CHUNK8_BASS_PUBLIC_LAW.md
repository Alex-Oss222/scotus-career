## Event

United States v. Bass, No. 01-1471. Merits decision, June 28, 2002; October Term 2001, chunk 8. On review of the Sixth Circuit's judgment, 266 F.3d 532, sustaining the District Court's discovery order. The United States seeks reversal and removal of the resulting death-notice restraint. Bass seeks dismissal of the notice or discovery concerning alleged racial selection in federal capital charging. The questions concern the source and preliminary evidentiary basis for that discovery and the consequence of refusing the current order.

A federal grand jury in the Eastern District of Michigan charged Bass, who is Black, with offenses including the intentional firearm killings of two people. The Government filed notice of intent to seek death. The District Court ordered selection discovery and excluded the death notice after the Government declined to comply; a divided appellate panel sustained the discovery order. Bass relied on the Justice Department's 2000 national survey of capital-eligible charges and plea outcomes. The aggregate figures did not identify the relevantly comparable eligible population or defendants passed over at the pertinent decision. Bass was offered a plea bargain and declined it. Under the protocol in place since 1995, local United States Attorneys decide capital-eligible charges and submit those charged cases for the Attorney General's separate death-authorization decision.

### Rule 16 setting

Rule 16(a)(1)(C) concerns books, papers, documents, photographs, tangible objects, buildings or places within the Government's possession, custody or control. On a defendant's request, inspection and copying or photographing depend on one of three alternatives: materiality to preparation of the defense, intended use by the Government in its evidence in chief, or acquisition from or ownership by the defendant. Those triggers remain distinct.

Paragraph (a)(2) excludes internal attorney or agent reports, memoranda and other Government documents connected with investigation or prosecution, except the disclosures provided by (a)(1)(A), (B), (D) and (E) concerning defendant statements, prior criminal records, examinations and tests, and expert testimony. Government witness statements are separately governed by 18 U.S.C. §3500; no witness-statement production question is resolved here. Paragraph (a)(3) preserves the separate grand-jury disclosure provisions of Rules 6, 12(i), 26.2 and (a)(1)(A). No grand-jury exception is invoked as the source here, and none is decided. These provisions do not establish that every requested aggregate, criterion or deliberative document belongs to the same category.

Rule 16(d)(1) permits protective relief on sufficient showing, including an ex parte written showing whose full text must be sealed and preserved if relief is granted. Paragraph (d)(2) permits discovery, a continuance, exclusion and other just responses to noncompliance. These powers regulate an existing obligation; their existence is not a finding that a duty, exception, privilege or sanction predicate is established here.

## Holdings

### I. The aggregate showing does not support the discovery order as entered

**Holding and operative rule:** Bass's national charging totals and completed-plea figures do not distinguish relevantly comparable eligible defendants of another race, the local capital-eligible charging and central death-authorization stages, or plea offers from accepted pleas. On that showing the discovery order the District Court entered and the Sixth Circuit sustained cannot stand: the judgment sustaining it is reversed and the order is set aside, without any finding that comparators do not exist or that any selection decision was race-neutral.

**Authority:** Part I of the Court's per curiam opinion has nine direct joins: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the order rested on a showing without the comparison that any selection inquiry requires. Wayte's equal-protection standard asks whether similarly situated persons of another race were treated differently and whether the decision was made because of race; it imposes no proportionality requirement. The national charging totals describe selected defendants without connecting their racial distribution to a relevantly comparable eligible population or to persons passed over. An appropriate denominator or matched-person evidence could perform that work, but neither technique is mandatory. Official provenance establishes the source of the counts, not comparability of the unmeasured groups. McCleskey requires connection to the challenged decision without categorically excluding statistics; Yick Wo preserves inference from a sufficiently supported discriminatory pattern; Klinger ties comparison to the challenged treatment without requiring perfect identity.

Local capital-eligible charging and the Attorney General's later death authorization are distinct decisions. The national authorizer makes nationwide evidence potentially relevant, but does not turn charging totals into a comparison of authorization decisions. The Court imposes no local-only rule. Likewise, completed bargains combine offers and acceptance. Bass was offered a bargain and declined it. The reported acceptance disparity therefore does not establish that he was denied an offer, without proving equal terms or equality throughout the system.

The Sixth Circuit treated the national disparities as enough to justify inquiry and sustained the order on that basis. They cannot carry that weight. The Court makes no independent purpose-deficiency holding, no finding that comparators do not exist and no ultimate determination of discrimination or neutrality. Brown's rule governing deliberate racial classification in police investigation supplies neither a disclosure source nor the missing comparison.

### II. The dependent death-notice exclusion falls with the order

**Holding and operative rule:** The exclusion of the death notice, imposed because the Government declined to comply with the order set aside in Part I, loses its predicate and is set aside. Restoring the Government's ability to proceed with its notice under ordinary law decides no guilt, capital eligibility, punishment or execution.

**Authority:** Part II has the same nine direct joins as Part I.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a sanction enforces an existing obligation rather than creating one. Once the order fails, the Government's refusal to comply with it cannot support exclusion of its death notice. Armstrong set aside an unsupported order together with the dismissal that depended on it, and it requires that a sanction for refusing a valid order be fitted to the violation rather than imposed automatically. Carlisle preserves supervision within governing legal limits. Failure of the present order does not establish neutral prosecution or foreclose constitutional scrutiny. The particular sanction appropriate for refusing a valid order and the remedy for proved discrimination are different questions outside this disposition.

### III. Armstrong's first-stage threshold inquiry governs further proceedings

**Holding and operative rule:** Under Armstrong's staged framework, the comparative limits of Bass's proffer do not defeat a confined first-stage threshold inquiry aimed at the missing comparisons, and the District Court conducts that inquiry on remand. It determines whether specific, reliable evidence raises a substantial question of discriminatory effect and purpose at an identified selection stage, whether the comparisons the survey lacks are reasonably unavailable to Bass, and which existing records would supply them; only on a supported determination may it consider a focused order under Rule 16(a)(1)(C) for existing, material and nonprotected records.

**Authority:** Part III has five direct joins: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas dissent from Part III.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the failure of the order does not end the inquiry Armstrong prescribes. Armstrong reads Rule 16(a)(1)(C)'s phrase "material to the preparation of the defendant's defense" to include a properly presented constitutional defense capable of defeating the prosecution, as a capital selective-prosecution defense is, and it proceeds in three stages: a threshold inquiry, at which full comparator evidence is not indispensable when its reasonable unavailability is shown; focused disclosure of existing records; and the merits under Wayte. Wade's substantial threshold for discovery and Kimberlin's preservation of circumstantial proof within meaningful procedural limits inform the first stage.

Bass's proffer is not a generalized accusation. It rests on the Justice Department's own national survey, and under the protocol in place since 1995 every charged capital-eligible case is submitted for the Attorney General's national authorization decision. Statistics alone must connect to the selection mechanism; the survey concerns that mechanism but does not distinguish its stages, and the comparisons it lacks concern cases submitted within the Government's own process. In Armstrong a defined sample, a declarant's firsthand state and federal comparison and unanswered gaps in the Government's explanations justified a limited inquiry. Bass offers no comparable firsthand evidence about eligible defendants passed over at either stage, and neither court below determined whether the missing comparisons are reasonably unavailable to him. A confined first stage, not reinstatement of the order or an end to the inquiry, is therefore the course Armstrong prescribes.

The first stage is a court-run determination, not production. The District Court must keep local charging, central authorization, plea offers and their acceptance distinct and examine the legitimate distinctions Armstrong identifies. A focused order may follow only if it explains materiality, why less intrusive evidence is inadequate, and how confidentiality will be protected. Rule 16's protections and privileges remain, protective-order provisions supply no disclosure authority, and no nonexistent statistics may be compelled. If the needed records do not exist, the court reassesses. If the Government declines to comply with a valid order, any sanction must be fitted to the violation; exclusion of the death notice is not automatic.

## Precedent Treatment

- United States v. Armstrong, May 13, 1996: its Rule 16(a)(1)(C) construction, three-stage framework, limits on disclosure orders and rule that sanctions be fitted to the violation are applied; Part III directs its first stage on this record.
- Wayte v. United States, 470 U.S. 598: its effect-and-purpose standard governs the merits and frames the comparison; the discovery stages do not alter it.
- Wade v. United States, May 18, 1992: its Part B substantial threshold for discovery informs the first stage; it imposes no completed-merits burden on this selection inquiry.
- Kimberlin v. Quinlan, June 12, 1995: grounded statistical and circumstantial proof remains available within meaningful procedural limits, without a categorical direct-evidence demand.
- Klinger v. Department of Corrections, December 4, 1995: relevant comparison follows the challenged treatment without requiring perfect identity.
- McCleskey v. Kemp, 481 U.S. 279: a statistical inference must connect to the challenged decision; discovery is distinguished from proof, and statistics are not categorically excluded.
- Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356: inference from a supported discriminatory-administration pattern remains available; the present aggregates lack the necessary comparison.
- Brown v. City of Oneonta, November 13, 2001: deliberate racial classification in police investigation is distinct from the selection inference and discovery source here.
- Carlisle v. United States, April 29, 1996: supervisory authority remains bounded by governing law; Rule 16, not supervision or protective-order provisions, supplies the disclosure source.
- United States v. Williams, May 4, 1992: its grand-jury duty and dismissal rules are not transferred to this discovery order or its sanction.

## Law After Decision

Armstrong's staged framework governs selection discovery. An order compelling production cannot rest on aggregate figures that do not distinguish relevantly comparable eligible defendants, the distinct charging and authorization stages, or plea offers from accepted pleas, and a sanction for refusing such an order falls with it. Those limits do not defeat a confined first-stage threshold inquiry: official national statistics can be relevant to a national authorization decision, and whether missing comparisons are reasonably unavailable to the defendant is decided at the first stage. Any later focused order rests on Rule 16(a)(1)(C), reaches only existing, material and nonprotected records with explained materiality, less intrusive alternatives and confidentiality, and compels no new statistics. Offer and acceptance remain distinct. Nothing in the decision finds discrimination or neutral administration. The rule concerns indirect proof and does not resolve whether direct discriminatory admissions dispense with a comparator showing.

## Separate Writings

**Justice O'Connor, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by Justice Scalia, Justice Kennedy and Justice Thomas.** They join Parts I and II and dissent from Part III. The national figures do not compare relevantly similar eligible persons at either the charging or the authorization stage, and completed bargains do not show that Bass was denied the offer he received; the order and the exclusion that enforced it therefore cannot stand. Adhering to the views stated in her Armstrong opinion, she would not read Rule 16(a)(1)(C) to reach selection discovery, and she would return the prosecution to ordinary pretrial proceedings in which a later request supported by the required showing remains possible.

Even on the Court's framework, Armstrong treats full comparator evidence as dispensable only when its reasonable unavailability is shown. Bass asserted the Government's control of selection data but showed no such unavailability; he relied on aggregate counts. A court-directed first stage aimed at obtaining the comparisons the defendant did not supply shifts the preliminary burden to the Government and opens charging and authorization judgments to inquiry before the substantial threshold Wade requires has been met. Klinger permits relevant comparison without perfect identity; it does not make the absence of a comparison a reason for inquiry. Prosecutorial discretion is not immune from equal-protection review, and the dissent finds neither neutrality nor an absence of comparators. It would preserve later properly supported requests with appropriate protective conditions and decides no capital-sentencing question.

## Procedure After Action

The judgment sustaining the discovery order is reversed, the order and the dependent death-notice exclusion are set aside, and the case is remanded for the first-stage threshold inquiry under Armstrong. The original order is not reinstated, and this Court orders no production. Any later focused order requires a supported first-stage determination and Rule 16(a)(1)(C)'s limits as Armstrong construes them; if the Government declines to comply with a valid order, any sanction must be fitted to the violation. The prosecution otherwise continues through ordinary pretrial procedures, and the Court prescribes no further Court event. The remedy for proved selective prosecution remains outside this judgment.
