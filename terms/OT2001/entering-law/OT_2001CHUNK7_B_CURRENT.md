### US Airways, Inc. v. Barnett — 2002-04-29

[Record](../records/us_airways_v_barnett_merits_2002-04-29.md)

**Common authority:** Justice Breyer's Opinion of the Court is joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor and Kennedy. Those five direct joins control every holding below. The separate writings qualify or oppose the rule only to the extent expressly stated; no combination of dissenting grounds supplies additional authority.

### Reasonable accommodation and undue hardship perform separate statutory work

**Holding and operative rule:** An employee ordinarily satisfies the initial accommodation showing by identifying an accommodation reasonable on its face or in the run of cases, not merely one effective for that employee; once reasonableness is shown, the employer must demonstrate significant difficulty or expense in its particular operations under section 12111(10). A requested exception to a disability-neutral policy is not automatically outside the accommodation duty, and the expectation that another worker may obtain an open position under this noncontractual policy does not by itself make the position unavailable for the statutory inquiry.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that reasonable accommodation may require different treatment to provide an employee a workplace opportunity. Section 12111(9) expressly identifies possible reassignment to a vacant position and adjustment of policies. Its permissive examples do not make every requested preference reasonable, but they foreclose treating uniform enforcement alone as an answer. Nor does this policy's bidding expectation establish an incumbent who must be displaced: Barnett occupies the position, the employer declares it open for bidding, and the policy creates no contractual entitlement.

Reasonable is an independent limit. An effective change may affect fellow employees in ways not captured by the employer's own operational expense. The ordinary first showing and the employer's particular hardship defense therefore do different work. This allocation does not require the employee to disprove every possible business burden. Garrett preserves the enacted distinction between accommodation and the employer's demonstrated significant-difficulty-or-expense defense. The latter considers the accommodation's nature and cost; facility resources, staffing, expenses and operational impact; the entity's overall resources, size and facilities; and workforce functions and the facilities' geographic, administrative and fiscal relationships.

The contrary disability-created-obstacle theory would deny a seniority exception because the allocation applies to all workers. But the present accommodation responds to Barnett's inability to perform his former work, and reassignment and policy adjustment remain statutory possibilities. Reasonableness limits those possibilities without excluding the requested exception at the outset. This conclusion also rejects effectiveness alone as a complete test; the employer's actual seniority system must receive the independent consideration stated next.

**Precedent treatment:** Garrett's separation of statutory conditions is preserved. Sutton, Murphy and Toyota retain their distinct coverage rules; none converts coverage into a right to a particular accommodation.

### An established seniority system ordinarily defeats a conflicting request, subject to special circumstances

**Holding and operative rule:** An employer's showing that a requested reassignment conflicts with an established seniority system ordinarily establishes that the accommodation is unreasonable and warrants summary judgment on that issue, including when management created the system. The employee may defeat that ground by presenting evidence of special circumstances making an exception reasonable in the particular case; the employee bears that showing, and the employer retains the separate undue-hardship defense if reasonableness is established.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that established seniority carries weight beyond its possible financial cost. Objective ordering gives employees expectations of fair treatment, job security and predictable advancement. Requiring the employer in every ordinary case to justify whether those expectations should yield would substitute discretionary accommodation decisions for the consistency on which the system depends. Those benefits can exist under a management-created policy as well as a collective agreement. Hardison supplies an analogy concerning the importance of seniority, not its different statute's hardship threshold or an automatic ADA exemption.

The absence of an express ADA seniority exemption does not eliminate the word reasonable. Conversely, the independent force of reasonable does not make seniority absolute. The employee can show that the actual system weakens the expectation on which the ordinary inference rests. Examples include an employer's frequent exercise of reserved amendment authority, sufficiently reducing expectations of adherence, or existing exceptions whose nature makes one further exception unlikely to matter. The examples are nonexclusive, and no numerical frequency threshold is prescribed. Bare reserved amendment authority does not automatically establish such circumstances; the employee must explain why an exception is reasonable in the actual setting.

The objection that this approach merges reasonableness with hardship overlooks their different subjects. The seniority inquiry concerns the character of the requested preference and employees' expectations; the employer's defense concerns significant difficulty or expense in its operations. The special-circumstances opportunity answers the reason for the ordinary seniority inference rather than requiring the employee to negate every operational burden. It remains a genuine evidentiary opportunity under ordinary summary-judgment principles, even for a bona fide system, not merely a chance to prove that the policy is a sham. Scalia and Thomas's contrary exclusion would foreclose that opportunity; Souter and Ginsburg's ordinary-inquiry approach would give seniority no comparable initial effect. The statute supports the qualified rule adopted here.

**Precedent treatment:** Hardison is used for the bounded seniority analogy; its Title VII setting and hardship threshold remain distinct. No prior ADA holding is overruled: this decision resolves the previously open seniority question under Title I.

### Remand applies the announced standard to the reviewed seniority claim

**Holding and operative rule:** The seniority-accommodation judgment is vacated and remanded so that the parties may seek summary judgment under the Court's standard, with Barnett able to establish special circumstances and US Airways able to assert its separate statutory hardship defense. Independent unreviewed accommodation grounds and the affirmed retaliation judgment retain their existing procedural status.

**Controlling explanation:** The lower courts applied different formulations: the District Court treated the system as establishing undue hardship, while the Court of Appeals placed seniority within a case-specific hardship inquiry. Neither applied the ordinary seniority inference together with the employee's opportunity to show special circumstances. The appropriate remedy is further consideration under the correct rule. Actual amendment practices, the character of exceptions and effects on employee expectations are application matters under that rule. The Court's limited seniority review supplies no basis to reopen the separate cargo, interactive-process or retaliation dispositions. A conflict with independently enforceable collective-bargaining obligations is outside the question presented by this noncontractual policy.

**Precedent treatment:** Toyota and Waffle House preserve the distinction between a statutory rule and proof of the remaining claim and remedy conditions; their separate coverage and enforcement holdings are unchanged.

- **Board of Trustees of the University of Alabama v. Garrett, February 21, 2001:** Preserves the distinct qualification, accommodation and demonstrated-hardship conditions; its state-employer enforcement holding supplies no seniority exemption.
- **Trans World Airlines, Inc. v. Hardison, 432 U.S. 63:** Supplies a limited analogy about seniority expectations. Its Title VII protection of seniority and more-than-de-minimis hardship threshold do not become the ADA's rule.
- **Sutton v. United Air Lines, Inc. and Murphy v. United Parcel Service, Inc., June 22, 1999; Toyota Motor Manufacturing, Kentucky, Inc. v. Williams, January 8, 2002:** Their actual-disability rules remain separate from qualification and accommodation; the seniority decision neither changes those rules nor adjudicates the disputed coverage-preservation matter.
- **EEOC v. Waffle House, Inc., January 15, 2002:** Its independent public-enforcement and remedy rules remain intact; permission to pursue statutory relief is distinct from establishing actual entitlement.
- **Olmstead v. L.C., June 22, 1999, and PGA TOUR, Inc. v. Martin, May 29, 2001:** Their respective Title II resource and Title III fundamental-alteration rules retain their own predicates. Neither replaces Title I's reasonable-accommodation and undue-hardship inquiries.

Under original ADA Title I, established seniority ordinarily makes a conflicting reassignment unreasonable, even under an employer-created system. The employee can establish special circumstances making an exception reasonable; that opportunity is substantive and nonexclusive. If reasonableness is established, the employer still bears the distinct significant-difficulty-or-expense defense. Neutrality alone does not immunize an employment policy, effectiveness alone does not establish reasonableness, and seniority is neither merely a hardship factor nor an absolute exemption. The rule takes effect April 29, 2002. Actual application and the remaining claim conditions proceed under these settled rules.

### Correctional Services Corp. v. Malesko — 2001-11-27

**O'Connor, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, dissenting.** The dissent would reverse and dismiss the corporate constitutional claim. Meyer gives independent force to the distinction between an established officer action and judicial creation of a new entity remedy. A federal custodial duty, useful compensation and the absence of sovereign immunity do not themselves supply that cause. The private defendant weakens the public-fisc concern, but does not eliminate the separate question of who may create a corporate constitutional remedy. Richardson addresses immunity and expressly does not resolve corporate liability or cause creation. Individual actions, state tort remedies, prospective relief and contractual oversight retain their actual scope and reinforce remedial restraint; the dissent does not need to assume that tort relief exactly duplicates every constitutional injury. Limiting the proposed action to corporate policy narrows liability but still adds the disputed defendant. The dissent's objection concerns creation of that cause, not a finding that the company complied with the Constitution or a grant of sovereign status to every contractor.

**Scalia, joined by Thomas, dissenting separately.** They join O'Connor's dissent and add that the Court should not extend implied constitutional damages beyond the recognized Bivens, Davis and Carlson settings even when a proposed extension can be analogized to their reasoning. That distinct objection reaches the Court's claim of a sufficiently close custodial analogy: accepting the analogy does not, in their view, authorize another judicially created damages action. Existing recognized causes remain operative. They do not rely on treating Sandoval as a controlling general abandonment of statutory implication, because its actual statutory holding supplies no such premise. Their separate ground neither decides private employee liability nor overrules the recognized constitutional actions.

### Kelly v. South Carolina — 2002-01-09

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by no other Justice.** He joins Part I in full. The escape planning and proposed violence while confined invited the inference that the omitted parole information answers. Under Chapman, the complete presentation leaves reasonable doubt whether the misleading sentencing premise contributed to death. Strong aggravation does not resolve that separate inquiry. He would therefore reverse the death-sustaining judgment and require a new lawful penalty proceeding now if the State continues to seek death, preserving guilt. This is a record-specific harmfulness determination, not structural error or an automatic new-hearing rule in every capital case. Simmons and Shafer supply the governing protection and the separate error-effect standard; his disagreement concerns its application and the resulting immediate remedy.

**Justice Scalia, dissenting, joined by no other Justice.** He would affirm against this federal claim because the Constitution does not supply this further parole-information obligation. Gardner's unavailable adverse material and Skipper's excluded rebuttal evidence do not establish a general duty to explain parole law. He accepts Shafer's account of the statutory alternatives without joining the new constitutional application. Treating evidence capable of forecasting danger as sufficient risks erasing Simmons's conditional limit. He resolves neither informational adequacy nor harmlessness and does not join the other dissents.

**Justice Kennedy, dissenting, joined by no other Justice.** He accepts Simmons and Shafer's contextual rule but finds the trigger absent here. In his view the State actually used the material for retribution, character and inability to adapt safely to prison, rather than meaningfully invoking danger from ordinary future release. Parole ineligibility does not answer danger to guards or an escape risk. Evidence capable of supporting a prediction is not necessarily a State invocation of the particular danger the rule addresses. He requires no verbal formula and does not deny that circumstantial evidence can establish the trigger on a different record. He would affirm without separately deciding adequacy or error effect.

**Justice Thomas, dissenting, joined by no other Justice.** Assuming the sentencing alternatives and future-danger predicate, he finds the actual information adequate. Defense argument conveyed permanent imprisonment, the judge directed the ordinary meaning of life, and the jury received neither a direction barring parole consideration nor a response to a parole inquiry that displaced the explanation. The good-time evidence also belongs in the full presentation. As in his Shafer dissent, substance can satisfy Simmons without the requested statutory formula. He therefore would affirm for compliance, not absence of danger or harmlessness. His constitutional disagreement with Simmons remains a reservation, not an independent ground, and he makes no finding about the jurors' actual beliefs.

All four separate positions are noncontrolling at the stated scope. The case returns to South Carolina for the Chapman determination; the federal merits event is complete, while state sentencing proceedings continue under the mandate. No later state result or external consequence is admitted, and no roster or institutional change occurs.

### Zahrey v. Coffey — 2002-03-19

[Record](../records/zahrey-v-coffey_merits_2002-03-19.md)

Justice Scalia dissents, joined by Justice Thomas. They would reverse the reinstatement of the reviewed personal damages claim because the asserted due-process boundary was not sufficiently clear in 1996. They do not decide that deliberate fabrication can never violate the Constitution or that a properly presented Fourth Amendment claim necessarily fails.

Mooney, Pyle and Napue concern deliberate falsehood in the adjudication of guilt. Franks and Malley identify a specific seizure-based protection against material deception. Those authorities forcefully condemn the alleged conduct, but the present theory combines investigative fabrication, pretrial rather than postconviction custody, and the same official's later absolutely protected advocacy. Buckley separated immunity from an actionable constitutional injury and expressly reserved the latter. Albright produced distinct approaches to the constitutional source of a prosecution-related injury, not a settled general due-process rule.

The adverse decision on remand in Buckley, 20 F.3d 789, and Rhodes, 939 F.Supp. 1256, demonstrate substantive uncertainty about a closely related theory. The objection is therefore more than the absence of a factual twin. Anderson requires particularized legal clarity; neither the moral wrongfulness of lying nor a later explanation of why the claim should succeed supplies that clarity at the conduct date. The dissenters would not make every circuit disagreement sufficient for immunity, but find this combination of source, injury and capacity uncertainties material. Later Fletcher and Clanton cannot settle the question retrospectively.

Both Justices accept that title and subsequent advocacy do not transform a conceded investigative act into protected advocacy. They also preserve the distinction between lack of absolute immunity and constitutional or damages liability. Their proposed judgment rests on qualified immunity for this pleaded theory, leaves the ultimate scope of a properly presented constitutional claim open, and supplies neither approval of the alleged misconduct nor an extension of judgment to other defendants or excluded claims.


### Michigan v. Environmental Protection Agency — 2002-04-23

[Record](../records/michigan_v_environmental_protection_agency_merits_2002-04-23.md)

**Scalia, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by Thomas.** The enacted criterion concerns amounts of pollution contributing to another State's nonattainment or interfering with maintenance. Cheapness of removal measures expense, not that pollution relationship. Congress's express economic techniques permit ways of meeting an authorized result; they do not authorize EPA to redefine the result through cost. MCI and the limiting-criteria analysis in Iowa Utilities Board require those enacted words to constrain agency choice independently; Chevron supplies no power to replace them. Walton's acceptance of reasonable formal implementation does not help when the disputed choice falls outside the statutory criterion. The two would remand the affected cost determination for agency reconsideration under the pollution standard, without imposing a unique significance number or requiring elimination of every molecule of interstate emissions. They would not preserve the same amount merely by renaming its stage.

They join Part II because Train and the actual alternatives distinguish a lawful statewide result from compelled source selection. That join does not approve the challenged cost-derived amounts. Having resolved the rule on statutory grounds, they do not reach the constitutional sufficiency question. Their existing agreement that agency self-restraint cannot cure missing congressional guidance remains unaffected. The dissent preserves the separate geographic and notice corrections and chooses no judicial replacement budget.


### US Airways, Inc. v. Barnett — 2002-04-29

[Record](../records/us_airways_v_barnett_merits_2002-04-29.md)

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, concurring.** He joins the Court's opinion in full and emphasizes that the special-circumstances showing must be a real and practical opportunity. Evidence that the employer retains power to change the system, that exceptions are already frequent, or that one more exception is unlikely to matter can carry the employee's showing in the particular case. Those avenues must receive substantive consideration rather than being treated as a formality. His full join preserves the Court's requirement to explain the actual effect on seniority expectations; retained power alone is not an automatic entitlement, and the employer's separate hardship defense remains.

**Justice Stevens, concurring.** He joins the full seniority presumption and remand. Seniority bears substantially on reasonableness, not only on hardship, but the employer's categorical position still fails. The existing interactive-process ground remains outside this seniority decision. Material application questions include why the mailroom position became open for bidding, whether the request is best understood as reassignment or continuation of the existing placement, and its actual effect on other employees. Barnett must answer the pertinent questions to establish special circumstances; the concurrence treats them as record-dependent inquiries, not a new mandatory checklist or findings in his favor.

**Justice O'Connor, concurring.** She joins the Court's opinion in full while explaining her preferred legal-entitlement rule. A position is unavailable when another employee occupies it or has an enforceable entitlement to it; an expectation under a noncontractual seniority policy alone supplies no legal possessor. On that approach, this policy does not defeat Barnett's initial reasonable-accommodation showing, and US Airways would have to establish actual hardship. She objects that a particularized special-circumstances inquiry can blur the distinction between general reasonableness and the employer's particular hardship burden. She nevertheless adopts the Court's statutory rule because a common rule is necessary and the practical exception will often produce the same result as her preferred approach. Disclaimers, reserved authority and actual exceptions can weaken expectations; her concurrence does not find that every noncontractual system does so. Her full join includes the employee's meaningful opportunity and the separate employer defense.

**Justice Scalia, dissenting, joined by Justice Thomas.** They would reverse the seniority ruling and return for a disposition rejecting the demanded priority. Reading sections 12112(a) and (b)(5)(A) together, they require reasonable removal of workplace obstacles that disability prevents an employee from overcoming. A standing requirement or inaccessible workstation can be such an obstacle even though expressed neutrally. A bona fide seniority allocation unrelated to disability is different: an exception grants priority over coworkers rather than removing the disability-created barrier. Reassignment still requires the employer to seek and offer a suitable vacant job within the lawful allocation instead of simply dismissing a worker unable to perform the former job. Their Martin and Olmstead positions support that distinction at their respective statutory scopes, without supplying a substitute Title I rule. They reject the Court's special-circumstances exception as both beyond the statutory duty and uncertain even for established systems. Their ground is not immunity for every neutral policy, and their proposed disposition preserves independent unreviewed claims.

**Justice Souter, dissenting, joined by Justice Ginsburg.** They would affirm the rejection of employer summary judgment because Barnett has met the initial reasonableness showing, leaving US Airways to prove actual hardship. The ADA omits the express seniority protections found in Title VII and the ADEA; the committee reports likewise treat seniority as a consideration rather than a special exemption. Hardison's different statute and protected collective agreement do not justify a presumption for this employer's unilateral policy. They give reasonableness independent work through feasibility, suitability and the actual change requested. Barnett seeks to retain a job he performs, displaces no incumbent, and confronts a policy the employer disclaims as contractual and reserves power to change. Those circumstances satisfy the initial showing without establishing ultimate liability or eliminating evidence of operational disruption. They preserve the distinct federal force of enforceable labor contracts recognized in Lincoln Mills and Charles Dowd Box; no such agreement governs this request. The employer's proof of actual significant difficulty or expense, including concrete noncash effects, remains necessary under their view.


### Henderson v. Norris — 2002-05-13

[Record](../records/henderson_v_norris_merits_2002-05-13.md)

**Kennedy, concurring in Part I and dissenting from Parts II–III, joined by O'Connor throughout the separate writing and by Thomas in the assumed-framework application only.** The dissent would restore denial of the sentencing claim. The tiny quantity, first conviction and lack of ordinary parole make the sentence severe, but the completed distribution offense and supported record do not meet the extraordinarily demanding threshold under Harmelin. Legislative judgments can account for distribution's preventive and social harms without measuring sentence length arithmetically by weight. The comparison evidence does not convert the exceptional severity into a constitutional prohibition. The dissent acknowledges the real difference from Harmelin's quantity and treats clemency as discretionary grace; it assumes neither a large enterprise nor prior convictions. Thomas's agreement assumes narrow proportionality for this disposition and does not adopt its constitutional foundation. Merits denial makes a separately unestablished new-rule bar unnecessary to these positions.

**Scalia, concurring in Part I and dissenting from Parts II–III.** Harmelin's distinct textual and historical account rejects prison-duration proportionality as an independent requirement of the Cruel and Unusual Punishments Clause. The actual sentence and comparisons may present a sentencing-policy objection, but they do not establish the asserted constitutional right on his ground. His acceptance of the Excessive Fines inquiry in Bajakajian supplies no prison-duration rule. He accepts the controlling temporal law and does not rely on amended section 2254(d) or a fictitious criminal history. His rationale is distinct from the other dissenters' conclusion that this sentence passes a narrow proportionality test.


### South Camden Citizens in Action v. New Jersey Department of Environmental Protection; review of 274 F.3d 771 (3d Cir. 2001), lower-court Nos. 01-2224 and 01-2296. No Supreme Court docket supplied. — 2002-05-28

Justice Stevens, joined by Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, concurs in the direct-route remand and dissents from Part I. He would reconsider Powell's §1983 holding to the extent it rejects this identified entitlement. The statutory beneficiaries, nondiscrimination protection, express implementation authority and a valid, sufficiently definite implementing requirement can, in his view, define the individual protection that §1983 enforces. Blessing's particular-right inquiry and Wright's and Wilder's recognition of authorized elaboration support that conclusion. He does not treat every regulation or administrative command as a right or establish a general entitlement to equal environmental burdens. Sandoval independently requires consideration of the direct claim, so his §1983 disagreement does not undermine remand, actual causation, proper-defendant or equitable requirements.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela concurs in the §1983 rejection and direct prospective adjudication but dissents from vacating the appellate judgment in full. He would affirm the rejected §1983 basis and vacate only the foreclosure of the retained Title VI theory. The two channels answer different questions, and Sandoval's direct-action rule leaves Powell's §1983 rule intact. He would require prompt determination of the covered recipient, actual regulatory duty, causal effect, proper prospective defendant and interim-relief requirements. Protection requires a supported lawful order; his disposition would not automatically restore the injunction or decide the permit's validity.

### Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Echazabal — 2002-06-10

Stone-Zsela concurs in Parts II and III and in the judgment. He reads §12113(a)'s general qualification defense, its accommodation condition and subsection (b)'s nonexclusive example as independently authorizing the bounded own-health defense. The EEOC has actual rulemaking authority, and its significant-risk, objective-evidence and accommodation safeguards receive substantial persuasive weight because they implement the statutory limits. ### Devlin v. Scardelletti — 2002-06-10

Thomas, joined by Scalia and Kennedy, dissents. A mandatory class may bind its members through a representative without making each member an independent litigant. Felzen distinguishes participation in settlement supervision from authority to continue litigation, and Marino and Rule 24 supply the ordinary route to personal party status. Devlin's own pension interest and inability to opt out establish the force of the judgment, but in their view do not eliminate that procedural requirement. The right to challenge an actual intervention denial supplies a separate avenue; it does not undo the denial already affirmed here. They would affirm the status dismissal, without deciding settlement fairness, adequate representation or the pension merits, and without adopting a universal prohibition on every nonparty appeal.

### Barnes v. Gorman — 2002-06-17

Stevens concurs in the judgment, joined by Ginsburg and Breyer. The commissioners are sued in official capacities, so the punitive award falls on the municipal entity. Newport's established municipal-punishment limitation excludes that award absent clear congressional displacement, which the incorporated disability remedies do not supply. Compensation for the plaintiff's loss remains distinct from punishment borne by the public. This ground neither grants immunity to every individual wrongdoer nor reopens the separate ruling that the Police Board is not an arm of the State.

The three expressly acknowledge that Newport was neither raised nor decided below. They nevertheless reach this existing defendant-specific legal limit within the presented punitive-remedy question because official-capacity status is established and no new evidentiary finding or retrial is needed, avoiding the broader question of private-recipient remedies. Newport's own treatment of a defaulted question arose in different circumstances, including lower-court consideration of its merits, and does not automatically excuse this nonpreservation. Stevens's earlier procedural dissent in Newport remains a caution about default, not a reason to misdescribe the ground as preserved. This confined choice creates no general exception to preservation.

They withhold the Court's general statutory exclusion. Franklin's appropriate-remedy principle, the distinct recipient responsibility discussed in Davis, and their objections to added remedy restrictions in Kolstad and Great-West counsel against using a funding analogy to decide the entire range of private-recipient relief. They need not adopt a general affirmative punitive-availability rule to resolve this municipal award. They therefore support removal of this punitive component, preserve compensation and seek no punitive-culpability or amount-review remand.
