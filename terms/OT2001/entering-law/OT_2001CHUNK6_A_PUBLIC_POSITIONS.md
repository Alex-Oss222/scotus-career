#### Wharf (Holdings) Ltd. v. United International Holdings, Inc., 532 U.S. 588

**Docket or dockets:** No. 00-347.
**Decided:** May 21, 2001.

##### Whether an oral option sold with a concealed contemporaneous intent not to honor it constitutes securities deception

**Holding:** Selling an actual option for value while concealing an existing intention never to honor it constitutes deception connected to the sale of that security under section 10(b) and Rule 10b-5. Oral form alone does not exclude the proved option; the private plaintiff must still establish materiality, the required culpability, legally sufficient reliance, causation and recoverable loss.

**Proposition-level authority:** Thomas's unanimous opinion of the Court.

**Material application:** The option acquired for services is the purchased security, even though the contemplated later share transfer never occurred. The option finding and contemporaneous fraudulent intent distinguish the verdict from mere subsequent breach.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The lower court's state-law enforceability determination is not reopened. Treatment of an agreement unenforceable under state law remains undecided, and state enforceability is not made an invariably separate federal element. Negligence, unfairness and nonperformance alone are insufficient. Foreign-transaction limits and the independent state punitive award lie outside review.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The reviewed federal securities judgment is affirmed; the Court orders no share transfer, new trial or damages recalculation and preserves unreviewed state grounds.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Merrill Lynch, Pierce, Fenner & Smith, Inc. v. Kravitz supplies the knowingly false undertaking/breach distinction, without defining options or compelling every transaction. United States v. O'Hagan supplies transaction-connected deception, leaving its source-deception dispute untouched. Blue Chip Stamps v. Manor Drug Stores is satisfied by the option purchase and adds no writing requirement. Santa Fe Industries, Inc. v. Green preserves deception beyond commercial unfairness. Central Bank of Denver, N.A. v. First Interstate Bank of Denver, N.A. retains its distinct affirmative-assistance action, unnecessary to this primary seller fraud.

#### Cardinal Chemical Co. v. Morton International, Inc., 508 U.S. 83 (1993)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 92-114.
**Decided:** May 17, 1993.

##### An intermediate noninfringement ruling does not terminate counterclaim jurisdiction

**Holding:** The Federal Circuit’s affirmance of noninfringement does not deprive it of jurisdiction over a live, independent invalidity counterclaim merely because its own ruling would resolve the infringement claim. That intermediate ruling remains subject to this Court’s review and does not justify treating validity review as automatically advisory.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stevens’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct majority agreement at this level of generality; no Marks inference.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Altvater v. Freeman, 319 U.S. 359: applies independent-counterclaim distinction.
- Electrical Fittings Corp. v. Thomas & Betts Co., 307 U.S. 241: distinguished; unnecessary affirmative-defense adjudication is different.

##### An established invalidity controversy does not disappear without a change

**Independent alternative holding:** Once an independent invalidity counterclaim presents an actual controversy, the party asserting later mootness must demonstrate a change that eliminates it. Affirmance of noninfringement alone does not establish that change or authorize automatic vacatur of the invalidity judgment.

**Proposition-level authority for alternative holding:** Stevens’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct majority agreement at this level of generality; no Marks inference.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Altvater v. Freeman, 319 U.S. 359: applies independent-counterclaim distinction.
- Aetna Life Insurance Co. v. Haworth, 300 U.S. 227, and United States v. W.T. Grant Co., 345 U.S. 629: distinguishes initial controversy burden from later mootness burden.
- United States v. Munsingwear, Inc., 340 U.S. 36: actual intervening mootness rule preserved; automatic application rejected.
- INS v. Chadha, 462 U.S. 919: agreement on one legal issue does not eliminate underlying adversity.

##### The automatic practice cannot be saved as discretion on this record

**Holding:** The Federal Circuit may not routinely vacate a live invalidity judgment merely because it affirms noninfringement, nor save that same automatic practice by calling it discretionary. On this record the validity appeal should be considered; unusual case-specific grounds for declining review or actual mootness remain available, but no universal duty to decide validity in every patent appeal is imposed.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stevens’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas (7 Justices). Direct majority agreement at this level of generality; no Marks inference.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Actual later mootness and lawful unusual-case discretion remain available; no universal command to decide validity, technical validity ruling or fee ruling follows. The first two jurisdictional grounds independently defeat automatic mootness; the seven-Justice discretionary holding is distinct.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Vacate only the vacatur of the invalidity judgment and remand for consideration of the live validity appeal on this record; the noninfringement affirmance remains intact.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Blonder-Tongue Laboratories, Inc. v. University of Illinois Foundation, 402 U.S. 313: applies distinct public and preclusion significance of invalidity.
- Altvater, 319 U.S. 359: independent judgment cannot be discarded solely because infringement fails.

#### Thomas Jefferson University v. Shalala, 512 U.S. 504

**Docket or dockets:** No. 93-120.

**Decided:** 1994-06-24.

##### The redistribution provision reasonably limits newly shifted educational costs

**Holding:** Under the governing rule giving an administering agency's interpretation of its own regulation controlling weight unless plainly erroneous or inconsistent with that regulation, the Secretary reasonably construes 42 C.F.R. §413.85(c) to bar increased reimbursement resulting from transferring previously medical-college-borne educational costs to the hospital's patient-care account, even when the underlying clinical education is generally eligible. That construction sustains the challenged denial for the reporting year ending June 30, 1985; it does not establish a universal historic-dollar ceiling, make every newly measured expense ineligible, or decide the separate community-support ground.

**Proposition-level authority:** Justice Kennedy's Opinion of the Court, Part I, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Scalia and Souter. Five participating Justices directly adopt this regulatory construction, its application and its limits.

**Material application:** Section 413.85(a)(1) provides that allowable cost may include the net cost of approved educational activities under paragraph (g), apportioned for Medicare utilization under §413.50. Permission to include those costs is not an unconditional payment command. Paragraph (g) deducts tuition revenue and includes trainee stipends, teacher compensation and other direct and indirect educational-activity costs under Medicare cost-finding principles. The recodified paragraph (a)(2) directs payment for approved residency programs in medicine, osteopathy, dentistry and podiatry to §413.86 for cost reporting periods beginning on or after July 1, 1985. That later-period exception does not itself disallow the reporting year ending June 30, 1985, and the Court fixes no later-period payment or base-year amount.

Section 413.17(a), subject to the separate exception in paragraph (d), includes the cost of services, facilities and supplies furnished by organizations related through common ownership or control at the related organization's cost, capped at the price of comparable items that could be purchased elsewhere. It does not independently make an otherwise excluded educational cost allowable. No party's entitlement under paragraph (d) is decided, and its separate exception is not a premise of this holding. Section 1395x(v)(1)(A)'s reasonable-cost system takes direct and indirect costs into account within authorized methods and included-item rules, excludes unnecessary costs in efficient delivery, and bars shifting Medicare costs to non-Medicare patients or the reverse. It does not guarantee payment of every asserted actual expense.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Bowles v. Seminole Rock & Sand Co., 325 U.S. 410 (1945): applies controlling weight to a permissible agency interpretation of its own regulation; the plainly-erroneous-or-inconsistent limit remains operative.
- Udall v. Tallman, 380 U.S. 1 (1965): applies the same own-regulation standard; an alternative reasonable reading does not by itself displace the Secretary's permissible construction.
- Gardebring v. Jenkins, 485 U.S. 415 (1988): preserves the limit where an alternative reading is compelled by the regulation's plain language or other indications of the Secretary's intent at promulgation; no such compulsion is established here.
- Martin v. Occupational Safety and Health Review Commission, 499 U.S. 144 (1991): applies its account of reasonable own-regulation interpretation and administrative expertise; its allocation of interpretive authority under the Occupational Safety and Health Act remains specific to that statutory structure.
- Good Samaritan Hospital v. Shalala, June 7, 1993: preserves reimbursement within lawful cost methods and limitations, including authorized exceptions and independent challenges to unlawful methods; actual expenditure alone does not establish entitlement here.
- MCI Telecommunications Corp. v. American Telephone & Telegraph Co., June 17, 1994: preserves the requirement to respect operative statutory limits; its mandatory-tariff holding does not invalidate this textually supported regulatory restriction or replace the own-regulation standard.
- Director, Office of Workers' Compensation Programs v. Greenwich Collieries, June 20, 1994: preserves the distinction between delegated authority and its actual exercise; its treatment of a general reasonable-doubt policy does not render every statement of regulatory intent nonoperative or create a Medicare proof burden.
- Ohio State University v. Secretary, Department of Health and Human Services, 996 F.2d 122 (6th Cir. 1993): rejects its contrary construction insofar as it excludes this transfer of otherwise eligible clinical-education costs from the redistribution provision; its separate community-support analysis is not resolved.

##### The actual agency ground survives the asserted inconsistency

**Holding:** The Administrator's expressed redistribution ground and the established history of college-borne costs support this denial; a memorandum's silence about redistribution and an intermediary's prior allowance do not, on this record, establish an authoritative contrary interpretation that defeats it. A considered, otherwise permissible interpretive change is not automatically invalid, but contrary text, genuine inconsistency and the required agency explanation must be examined, and a reviewing court may not substitute a new ground or missing finding.

**Proposition-level authority:** Justice Kennedy's Opinion of the Court, Part II, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Scalia and Souter. The same five Justices adopt this answer to the consistency and actual-ground objections; it supports application of the redistribution holding rather than furnishing an alternative reimbursement bar.

**Material application:** Letter 78-7 requires that faculty-service costs would be allowable if incurred directly, be allocated consistently among recipient components, comply with related-organization and comparable-price limits, and satisfy the applicable patient-care relation, documentation, reasonable-allocation and reasonable-amount requirements. Its identified additional nonfaculty costs are limited to direct and space costs. It does not permit duplicative additions where the hospital maintains its own corresponding library, administrative structure, office or clerical support. Those conditions survive; the Court does not read silence about redistribution as an unconditional promise of all overhead. The record does not explain the failure to claim reimbursement before 1974, and the Court does not turn that silence into a finding of community support or an estoppel rule.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court affirms on redistribution alone. It neither endorses nor rejects the separate construction treating all non-Medicare funding as community support; decides no merits of the Administrator's additional university general-and-administrative-cost ruling under §§413.9, 413.85 and Letter 78-7; and orders no reimbursement, penalty, recoupment, reopening of the settled faculty-compensation amounts, or relief on the unadjudicated alternative enforcement claim. The original $2,861,247 disputed difference is not adjudicated as an amount remaining after settlement. Materially different retrospective applications, later §413.86 calculations and entitlement under §413.17(d) remain unresolved. No independently sufficient alternative holding is adopted, and no fractured-rationale synthesis is needed.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Good Samaritan Hospital v. Shalala, June 7, 1993: applies its rule that considered interpretive change is not automatically disqualifying while preserving scrutiny of text, delegation and reasons; unsupported litigation positions receive no automatic controlling weight.
- National Railroad Passenger Corp. v. Boston & Maine Corp., March 25, 1992: applies the requirement of a discernible actual agency rationale; the express redistribution ground satisfies it without a new judicial explanation.
- SEC v. Chenery Corp., 318 U.S. 80 (1943): preserves review on the agency's own ground rather than a substitute invented on review.
- Motor Vehicle Manufacturers Assn. v. State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Co., 463 U.S. 29 (1983): preserves required reasoned agency decisionmaking and the prohibition on appellate rationalizations supplying missing findings.
- Bowen v. Georgetown University Hospital, 488 U.S. 204 (1988): preserves the rejection of unsupported litigation rationalizations; this decision rests on the Administrator's stated rationale and decides no new retroactive-rulemaking question.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The challenged reimbursement denial is affirmed, 5–4, on redistribution alone. No additional reimbursement or remand is ordered. The separate community-support construction and general-and-administrative-cost ruling are neither independently sustained nor rejected; settled faculty amounts and the unadjudicated alternative enforcement claim remain untouched.

#### Ohio Adult Parole Authority v. Woodard

**Docket or dockets:** No. 96-1769.

**Decided:** 1998-03-25.

##### Required clemency procedures do not create an entitlement to mercy or triallike review

**Holding:** Ohio's required investigation, hearing and advisory recommendation do not create a substantive entitlement to clemency because they do not constrain the Governor to grant it on specified facts. The analogy to a first appeal as of right does not itself require appellate representation, triallike evidence procedure or a new determination of guilt in this discretionary executive process.

**Proposition-level authority:** O’Connor’s opinion: Part I-A has all eight Associates, with Stone-Zsela also joining the no-right-to-mercy conclusion. Part I-B has O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; Stone-Zsela joins only the mercy-versus-criminal-trial distinction. Stevens does not join the rejection of the established-process analogy.

**Limits and questions not reached:** A further hearing or revised recommendation upon new information is permitted, not mandatory. No right to favorable mercy follows from the required administrative steps.

##### Capital-clemency procedures retain a constitutional minimum, satisfied on these pleadings

**Holding:** An established capital-clemency process must afford minimal safeguards against fundamentally arbitrary procedure, including arbitrary denial of any access or substitution of an entirely arbitrary device such as a coin flip for consideration. The notice and offered interview here, considered with the pleaded restrictions, satisfy that minimum; they establish no entitlement to clemency, trial protections, a favorable recommendation or judicial review of mercy's merits.

**Proposition-level authority:** O’Connor’s opinion: Part II-A's minimum safeguard has six direct joins: O'Connor, Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Part II-B's sufficient-process application has five: O'Connor, Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Stevens disputes the application; Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas agree with rejection of the claim on their distinct entitlement/adjudication ground. Both controlling propositions rest on direct majority joins, without combining separate rationales under Marks.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The application accepts the alleged three-day interview notice, ten-day hearing notice, counsel restrictions, preparation difficulty and inability to testify or submit documents at the hearing. It assumes no additional document channel. The rule fixes no universal procedural checklist and leaves independent constitutional wrongs distinct.

##### The optional interview does not establish compelled self-incrimination

**Holding:** An optional clemency interview does not compel self-incrimination merely because the prisoner must choose between providing potentially harmful information and remaining silent; absence of counsel, confidentiality or immunity does not itself convert this invitation into compulsion.

**Proposition-level authority:** O’Connor’s Part III-A, joined by all eight other Justices.

**Independent alternative holding:** The conclusion also holds if an ordinary civil adverse inference from silence is assumed, but it does not authorize an automatic privilege penalty, compelled waiver or criminal use of compelled answers.

**Proposition-level authority for alternative holding:** O’Connor’s Part III-B, joined by all seven other Associates; Stone-Zsela does not join this additional ground.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Ohio’s actual use of silence or answers is not found. A genuine threat of an automatic privilege penalty, compulsory waiver or criminal use of compelled answers requires its own analysis.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The due-process remand is reversed 8–1, with Stevens opposing. The self-incrimination remand is reversed unanimously. The independent no-entitlement ruling remains undisturbed.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- **Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat:** Applied to discretionary mercy and the distinction between seeking relief and entitlement to its grant; not extended to extinguish independent constitutional interests.
- **Sandin v. Conner:** Applied at its substantive-constraint rule. Mandatory procedure alone is insufficient, and directly retained constitutional interests remain separate.
- **Doe v. District of Columbia:** Applied to distinguish investigative duties from a substantive entitlement.
- **Johnson v. Rodriguez:** Applied to the same procedure/entitlement boundary and preservation of independent constitutional wrongs; its ordinary initial-parole rule does not displace the retained-life floor.
- **Evitts v. Lucey:** Distinguished at the adjudicatory first appeal as of right; it does not itself require the requested capital-clemency procedures.
- **Ford v. Wainwright:** Its distinction between a protected interest and appropriate flexible procedure informs the minimum-process rule; its separate incompetency protection remains unchanged.
- **Herrera v. Collins:** Preserved as a record-specific innocence disposition; no exclusive-clemency rule is attributed to it.
- **Minnesota v. Murphy:** Applied to distinguish voluntary choices from supported threats of a privilege penalty.
- **Baxter v. Palmigiano:** Applied by the eight Associates to ordinary civil adverse inferences, distinct from automatic punishment or compelled criminal testimony.
- **LaChance v. Erickson / McManus:** Preserved for separately established compulsion and waiver; it supplies no general prohibition on lawful civil inferences.

### Controlling weight for a responsible agency's fair regulatory interpretation

**Governing question:** Judicial treatment of a responsible agency's interpretation of its own regulation, including a construction presented in an amicus brief.

**Current rule:** The responsible agency's fair and considered interpretation controls unless plainly erroneous or inconsistent with the regulation. Courts must assess the reading against the operative text, lawful allocation of interpretive responsibility and supported agency treatment. An amicus presentation neither disqualifies the construction nor establishes its correctness. Interpretive authority does not permit amendment of contrary text, unsupported litigation rationalization, automatic authority for an agency lawyer or deference to unproved employer-specific factual assertions.

**Authority by component:**

- Auer v. Robbins — The Secretary's fair and considered regulatory interpretation controls within its legal bounds, 1997-02-19; Kennedy Part III, joined by all seven other Associates (eight); Stone-Zsela's replacement proposal is noncontrolling

**Present operation effective:** Auer, 1997-02-19, for the amicus and factual-assertion boundaries.

**Related current rules:** Authoritative interpretive Guidelines commentary preserves its separate statutory and constitutional limits. Medicare educational costs shifted from a related institution remains the distinct substantive reimbursement rule.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### An intermediate noninfringement ruling does not terminate counterclaim jurisdiction

**Controlling proposition:** The Federal Circuit’s affirmance of noninfringement does not deprive it of jurisdiction over a live, independent invalidity counterclaim merely because its own ruling would resolve the infringement claim. That intermediate ruling remains subject to this Court’s review and does not justify treating validity review as automatically advisory.

**Authority:** Stevens’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). This proposition commands a majority of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an intermediate appellate court cannot treat its own noninfringement ruling as the final, unreviewable end of the litigation. Its judgment remains subject to this Court’s review. A reversal of noninfringement could restore the immediate importance of the independently adjudicated validity issue, so the appellate court’s own ruling cannot by itself remove authority to review that issue. Altvater confirms that a counterclaim seeking a declaration of invalidity presents more than a defense to the particular infringement claim. Electrical Fittings does not support the automatic rule: that case involved an unnecessary validity adjudication in rejecting infringement, not an independent counterclaim judgment requiring appellate treatment. Judicial caution about unnecessary questions cannot convert the Federal Circuit’s merits decision into a jurisdictional event beyond further review. This is an independently sufficient reason to reject the automatic mootness premise. The Court does not disturb the noninfringement affirmance here, decide technical validity or foreclose actual mootness arising from a different event.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Altvater v. Freeman, 319 U.S. 359: applies independent-counterclaim distinction.
- Electrical Fittings Corp. v. Thomas & Betts Co., 307 U.S. 241: distinguished; unnecessary affirmative-defense adjudication is different.

##### An established invalidity controversy does not disappear without a change

**Controlling proposition:** Once an independent invalidity counterclaim presents an actual controversy, the party asserting later mootness must demonstrate a change that eliminates it. Affirmance of noninfringement alone does not establish that change or authorize automatic vacatur of the invalidity judgment.

**Authority:** Stevens’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). This proposition commands a majority of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the distinct validity controversy continues unless something actually ends it. Aetna places the initial burden of establishing a real controversy on the party seeking declaratory relief. That threshold was met here. W. T. Grant assigns the burden differently when a party contends that an established controversy has later become moot. The automatic practice identifies no new fact ending the dispute about the patents’ validity. Altvater explains why an invalidity counterclaim remains distinct from a defense to infringement: success on the particular infringement allegation need not resolve the parties’ continuing patent controversy. Munsingwear addresses vacatur after genuine intervening mootness; it does not supply the missing intervening event here. Both parties ask for validity review but disagree about the result, so their agreement on the need for review does not eliminate adversity, as Chadha’s treatment of agreement on a legal issue confirms. The Court leaves genuine later mootness available and does not decide validity itself.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Altvater v. Freeman, 319 U.S. 359: applies independent-counterclaim distinction.
- Aetna Life Insurance Co. v. Haworth, 300 U.S. 227, and United States v. W. T. Grant Co., 345 U.S. 629: distinguishes initial controversy burden from later mootness burden.
- United States v. Munsingwear, Inc., 340 U.S. 36: actual intervening mootness rule preserved; automatic application rejected.
- INS v. Chadha, 462 U.S. 919: agreement on one legal issue does not eliminate underlying adversity.

##### The automatic practice cannot be saved as discretion on this record

**Controlling proposition:** The Federal Circuit may not routinely vacate a live invalidity judgment merely because it affirms noninfringement, nor save that same automatic practice by calling it discretionary. On this record the validity appeal should be considered; unusual case-specific grounds for declining review or actual mootness remain available, but no universal duty to decide validity in every patent appeal is imposed.

**Authority:** Stevens’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas (7 Justices). This proposition commands a majority of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that jurisdiction cannot be acknowledged while the same automatic refusal is preserved under another name. The validity counterclaim has consequences beyond whether the particular accused products infringe. Blonder-Tongue explains why an adjudication of invalidity can protect others from repeated enforcement of a patent that should not restrict the public. Routine vacatur also prevents review of the district court’s work and can force the prevailing accused infringer to confront the same validity controversy again. The patent holder has sought review as well, so this record presents no reason to preserve a categorical refusal. Judicial restraint is important, but it does not justify a standing practice that disregards a live independent judgment whenever a separate ground resolves infringement. The Federal Circuit should consider the validity appeal on remand. The Court does not eliminate lawful case-specific discretion, dictate the technical validity result or require unnecessary constitutional adjudication. An unusual case presenting a genuine reason to withhold review must be assessed on its own circumstances.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Blonder-Tongue Laboratories, Inc. v. University of Illinois Foundation, 402 U.S. 313: applies distinct public and preclusion significance of invalidity.
- Altvater, 319 U.S. 359: independent judgment cannot be discarded solely because infringement fails.

#### Current-Law Effect

Before: independent invalidity counterclaims had distinct legal consequences. After: the automatic mootness-and-vacatur practice is rejected on two nine-Justice jurisdictional grounds; seven Justices also require consideration on this record rather than automatic avoidance relabeled discretion. Genuine later mootness and lawful unusual-case discretion remain. No patent validity is decided.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia, joined by Souter, joins Parts I–III and the judgment. He agrees that the independent validity counterclaim is not automatically moot and that the jurisdictional premise of the Federal Circuit’s practice fails. He does not join Part IV. Because both parties oppose the automatic practice, the scope of appellate discretion has not received the same adversary development. He would remand for the Federal Circuit to exercise genuine discretion in the first instance, while rejecting a conclusion that jurisdiction itself forces every validity question to be decided. The seven-Justice Part IV nevertheless governs this record, with its express preservation of unusual case-specific grounds.

### Thomas Jefferson University v. Shalala, No. 93-120

Merits decision, June 24, 1994

#### Chronology and Posture

512 U.S. 504. Argued April 18, 1994; decided June 24, 1994. On certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit. The questions concern the Secretary's construction of Medicare's educational-cost redistribution provision and the separate community-support ground for denying reimbursement.

The university's teaching hospital operates an approved clinical graduate-medical-education program through affiliated medical-college faculty. A later study allocated to the hospital nonsalary categories previously borne by the college. ##### The redistribution provision reasonably limits newly shifted educational costs

**Controlling proposition:** Under the governing rule giving an administering agency's interpretation of its own regulation controlling weight unless plainly erroneous or inconsistent with that regulation, the Secretary reasonably construes 42 C.F.R. §413.85(c) to bar increased reimbursement resulting from transferring previously medical-college-borne educational costs to the hospital's patient-care account, even when the underlying clinical education is generally eligible. That construction sustains the challenged denial for the reporting year ending June 30, 1985; it does not establish a universal historic-dollar ceiling, make every newly measured expense ineligible, or decide the separate community-support ground.

**Authority:** Justice Kennedy's Opinion of the Court, Part I, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Scalia and Souter. Five participating Justices directly adopt this regulatory construction, its application and its limits.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that eligibility of an educational activity does not remove every limit on financing it. Seminole Rock and Tallman supply the governing weight for a permissible interpretation of the administering agency's own regulation; Gardebring preserves contrary text and evidence of intent at promulgation, and Martin preserves review for consistency and reasonableness. Section 413.85(c)'s final sentence addresses increased costs from redistribution between educational and patient-care units. Its reference to costs reasonably reaches a financial transfer even when the clinical instruction remains unchanged. The hospital's contextual reading is substantial: the sentence also discusses activities customarily undertaken by providers. But that first clause need not consume the second clause's separate financial restriction. The permissive allowance in subsection (a), the net-cost calculation in subsection (g), and the related-organization cost rule can operate subject to that restriction. The college previously carried the disputed categories; the new study allocated them to the hospital. The Secretary reasonably treated that allocation as redistribution. Permission to include those costs is not an unconditional payment command. The recodified paragraph (a)(2) directs payment for approved residency programs in medicine, osteopathy, dentistry and podiatry to §413.86 for cost reporting periods beginning on or after July 1, 1985. Section 413.17(a), subject to the separate exception in paragraph (d), includes the cost of services, facilities and supplies furnished by organizations related through common ownership or control at the related organization's cost, capped at the price of comparable items that could be purchased elsewhere. It does not independently make an otherwise excluded educational cost allowable. No party's entitlement under paragraph (d) is decided, and its separate exception is not a premise of this holding. #### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Substantive state constraints and meaningful prison liberty

**Controlling proposition:** A State creates protected prison liberty when substantive law meaningfully restricts officials’ authority to impose a significant deprivation. Mandatory wording alone is insufficient: courts must examine the genuine constraint in the whole disciplinary scheme and the meaningful restraint, without making state codification necessary for directly protected constitutional liberty.

**Authority:** Breyer’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg; five Justices adopt this sufficient, nonexclusive state-entitlement rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that legal substance matters more than a regulation’s drafting conventions. Hewitt supplies the recognition that substantive state constraints may create protected liberty; Thompson distinguishes genuine predicates from procedures that do not require a particular substantive outcome. The Court narrows reliance on mandatory words alone without discarding real constraints simply because they operate inside prison. The complete scheme must be considered: defined misconduct, an impartial adjudicative process, valid evidence and the relationship between guilt and a significant sanction can confine official authority even when officials retain discretion to select a commensurate penalty afterward. Wolff distinguishes substantial protected liberty from routine management and supplies the separate inquiry into appropriate process. Mere mandatory administration, routine transfers or brief inconveniences do not automatically create entitlements. Nor is this an exclusive requirement of state codification for intrinsic constitutional liberty, an unexpected restraint or a sentence-duration interest. The Court adopts no new multifactor hardship test and leaves the ultimate application to the district court under the stated rule.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Hewitt v. Helms: narrows mechanical reliance on mandatory wording while retaining genuine substantive-predicate liberty protection; no wholesale methodology replacement controls.
- Kentucky Department of Corrections v. Thompson: applies distinction between substantive constraints and procedural directions alone.
- Wolff v. McDonnell: preserves significant prison liberty and context-sensitive procedural protection.
- Meachum v. Fano: distinguishes ordinary sentence-authorized management from a genuine protected constraint.
- Vitek v. Jones; Washington v. Harper: preserve distinct directly protected, qualitatively different restraints; this state-entitlement rule is nonexclusive.
- Farmer v. Brennan; Helling v. McKinney: distinguish Eighth Amendment conditions liability from this procedural-liberty inquiry.

**Controlling proposition:** The summary judgment rejecting the reviewed liberty-and-process claim cannot stand on a mechanical reading of Hawaii’s regulatory words or dismissal of genuine punitive constraints. **Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the dispute requires application of the substantive rule to the complete disciplinary scheme and the actual deprivation. #### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. Deceptive use of entrusted information in securities trading can be primary §10(b) fraud

**Holding and operative rule:** A person who, with scienter, owes an information source a duty of loyalty and confidentiality, feigns fidelity, and secretly uses material nonpublic information entrusted in that relationship for securities trading commits deception in connection with the transaction when the securities use consummates the concealed disloyalty. The source need not be the trading counterparty; a qualifying duty, deception and transaction connection are indispensable, and full disclosure of the trading plan to every person owed the relevant duty removes this theory's deception.

**Authority:** Part I of Stevens's Court opinion is joined by O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: six direct votes. Stone-Zsela supplies a seventh judgment vote on his narrower source-participation ground, which is not a limitation adopted by the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** Chiarella makes a duty essential when silence is asserted to be deceptive; possession of superior information alone creates no general duty to the market. Carpenter recognizes that confidential information can be property and its secret conversion can defraud its source. The additional §10(b) connection exists here because trading is the use through which the entrusted information is secretly converted into personal gain. The source deception and securities use are thus linked, even though the source is not the buyer or seller opposite O'Hagan. The contrary view separates a completed source fraud from a later trade. That account misses the alleged feigned fidelity and concealed trading use that consummate this breach. O'Hagan obtained the bidder's confidential tender-offer information through its law firm and used it to acquire the affected target's stock and options. This is his alleged primary deception, not liability based on status, innocent receipt, equal access or assistance imputed from another person's fraud. Central Bank's confined private affirmative-assistance action supplies neither the primary offense nor a criminal mental-state shortcut. Disclosure to one principal does not necessarily discharge a duty to another; disclosure sufficient to remove this securities deception does not authorize conduct forbidden by other law. The Court decides the legal theory, not every possible confidential relationship or whether all preserved instruction, sufficiency and harmless-error objections fail. The Eighth Circuit's categorical rejection is reversed on that bounded basis.

##### II. Section 14(e) authorizes the bounded preventive rule in this tender-offer setting

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 14(e)'s express authority to prescribe means reasonably designed to prevent tender-offer fraud permits Rule 14e-3(a), in the source-misappropriation setting presented, to prohibit the covered trading without proof of a fiduciary breach as an additional element. The rule's actual commencement, information, source, knowledge, instrument and public-disclosure conditions remain necessary; the delegation does not authorize treating every informational disparity as fraud or regulate outside the tender-offer and antifraud domain.

**Authority:** Part II of the same Court opinion is joined by O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, Stone-Zsela and Scalia: eight direct votes. Thomas dissents from this application while accepting that the statute permits some preventive rules.

**Operative conditions and distinct alternatives:** A person must have taken a substantial step or steps to commence, or commenced, a tender offer. The trader must be someone other than the offering person and must possess material information relating to the offer, knowing or having reason to know both that it is nonpublic and that it was acquired, directly or indirectly, from the offering person, the issuer, or an officer, director, partner, employee or other person acting on behalf of either. The prohibition covers purchasing, selling or causing purchase or sale of the securities sought or to be sought in the offer, securities convertible into or exchangeable for them, and options or rights to obtain or dispose of those securities. Its disclosure alternative requires the information **and its source** to be publicly disclosed a reasonable time before trading. Private disclosure to the source, which can eliminate §10(b) deception, does not itself satisfy that separate public-disclosure alternative.

**Controlling explanation:** The enacted authority concerns prevention as well as completed fraud. Secret tender-offer trading presents timing and proof problems: material information may pass from transaction participants before public announcement, and proof of the precise source breach may be difficult. The rule responds with a bounded prohibition tied to the offer, identified sources, material nonpublic information and the trader's actual or reason-to-know knowledge. Requiring proof of every completed fiduciary fraud as an additional element would deprive the preventive delegation of its intended work. MCI requires an agency to remain within the authority the statute grants; it does not erase this express prevention authority or replace Chevron generally. The strongest objection is that a source condition is not itself proof of a breach and that an agency may not simply redefine fraud. The Court agrees with both propositions but finds this rule reasonably designed to prevent fraud in the present setting; the source and knowledge conditions delimit the preventive measure rather than purporting to prove a completed breach. The ruling does not resolve principal-authorized warehousing or every application of Rule 14e-3(a). Preserved pre-offer scope and due-process arguments, trial objections and other unaddressed challenges remain for the Eighth Circuit; an unpreserved contention is not decided in the first instance.

##### III. The criminal-punishment qualification and unresolved trial questions remain operative

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 78ff(a) requires a willful violation for criminal punishment and bars imprisonment for that rule violation when the defendant proves lack of knowledge of the rule. That lack-of-knowledge protection does not erase the underlying rule, create general immunity from fines or require the government to prove knowledge of the rule as an element of every violation.

**Authority:** This qualification is part of Part II and has the same eight-Justice coalition. It states the limit under which the criminal Rule 14e-3(a) ruling is sustained, not a factual adjudication of O'Hagan's knowledge.

**Controlling explanation:** Authorization of a preventive rule does not establish every prerequisite to criminal punishment. The knowledge or reason-to-know conditions concerning the information and its source remain distinct from willfulness and from the defendant's statutory protection against imprisonment for an unknown rule. Neither the trader's possession of information nor recognition of the rule's validity conclusively resolves those questions. Central Bank's severe-recklessness standard concerns a confined private accessory action and cannot be transplanted into this criminal prosecution. Princeton's separate copyright willfulness rule likewise supplies no universal criminal standard. The Eighth Circuit must consider preserved questions left unresolved by its categorical legal rulings; the Court orders no new jury finding, automatic imprisonment or unconditional reinstatement.

##### IV. The mail-fraud reversal cannot rest on the rejected securities premise

**Holding and operative rule:** The Eighth Circuit's reversal of mail-fraud counts 1–20, insofar as it rested on the securities theories' legal invalidity, must be reversed now that the Court rejects that premise. The case is remanded for consideration of preserved alternate grounds not reached below; money-laundering counts 55–57 were not presented for review and remain undisturbed.

**Authority:** Part III of the Court opinion is joined by O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer and Stone-Zsela: seven direct votes. Scalia and Thomas join the mail-fraud reversal and remand, making that judgment unanimous, but not this entire rationale; Thomas's independent Carpenter ground is separate.

**Controlling explanation:** The lower court's asserted dependence of mail fraud on the rejected securities theories does not support its judgment once those categorical securities rulings fall. That is sufficient to require renewed consideration without deciding every alternate trial objection. The Court does not infer a jury finding on an unaddressed issue, declare every instruction valid or make an ultimate sufficiency determination. Recognition of a lawful legal theory supports reinstatement only to the extent the convictions survive the remaining properly preserved challenges. The petition's limits independently preserve the money-laundering disposition; a reversal of related mail-fraud counts cannot enlarge the mandate to unreviewed counts.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Chiarella supplies the need for a duty making nondisclosure deceptive; the Court rejects a general parity-of-information duty and applies the duty inquiry to the source relationship.
- Carpenter supplies the property and source-deception principles; it did not itself resolve every securities-connection question. Thomas separately relies on its independent mail-fraud ground.
- Central Bank retains the confined private action for culpable affirmative substantial assistance, with its own reliance, causation, loss and mental-state predicates. It supplies neither this primary criminal offense nor a universal securities scienter rule.
- MCI supplies the enacted-delegation boundary and is distinguished because §14(e) expressly authorizes fraud-prevention rules. Its rejection of statutory replacement does not forbid consequential action within an actual delegation.
- Chevron remains within its lawful statutory domain; no new categorical exception or general replacement is adopted.
- Princeton's distinct copyright willfulness rule is not imported into securities criminal punishment.

#### Law After Decision

Section 10(b) reaches deceptive trading against an information source within the six-Justice rule, without requiring that the source be the counterparty or participate in a securities transaction as Stone-Zsela separately proposes. A real duty, scienter, concealed disloyal use and the required securities connection remain essential. Section 14(e) independently sustains the bounded preventive rule in the present tender-offer setting, subject to all operative conditions and §78ff(a)'s distinct punishment qualification. Neither ruling imposes market-wide informational equality, decides all confidentiality relationships, narrows Central Bank's private accessory action or eliminates preserved trial challenges. The reviewed mail-fraud disposition is reversed; the unreviewed laundering disposition remains in force.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stone-Zsela concurs in Parts II and III and in the §10(b) judgment. His source-bound rule requires information entrusted by a source participating in an actual or contemplated securities transaction, a defined duty of fidelity or confidentiality, scienter, concealed planned use, and trading in securities materially affected by that source's transaction. Trading must consummate or monetize the deception. O'Hagan's firm represented the contemplated bidder, and the target's securities were affected by that very tender offer, so the required connection exists. The additional condition gives independent work to the securities nexus and avoids a general federal law of confidential information. He reserves family, friendship, government-service, ordinary-employment and commercial relationships in which the source is not participating in the transaction. Full disclosure to the source removes the theory's deception; innocent receipt and equal access do not establish it. He does not attribute his limit to Central Bank or narrow that decision's separate private-assistance rule.

Scalia concurs in the Rule 14e-3(a) holding and the mail-fraud result but dissents on §10(b). Criminal lenity prevents punishment under an insufficiently clear extension of source deception beyond deception of a party to the securities transaction. That objection does not defeat §14(e)'s express prevention authority, which supports the bounded tender-offer rule. Nor does it establish immunity from mail fraud. He joins the mail-fraud reversal and remand without adopting Thomas's entire independent analysis or the Court's securities-dependent explanation. He does not adopt every part of Thomas's agency-theory objection.

Thomas concurs in the mail-fraud result and dissents from both securities rulings. For §10(b), he regards the source fraud as complete independently of any eventual trade; the government's proposed distinctions do not supply a coherent statutory securities connection, and a court cannot replace the agency's actual theory with an improved one. For §14(e), he accepts power to adopt some preventive rules but rejects this justification and application even if misappropriation could otherwise violate §10(b). Establishing that information came from the bidder does not demonstrate why proving the relevant breach is so difficult that the rule should omit it; knowledge of source is not knowledge of breach. He does not invalidate every preventive rule, classic target-insider application or differently justified protection. On mail fraud, Carpenter independently permits prosecution of the fraudulent taking and use of confidential information as property, regardless of whether the same conduct satisfies the securities statutes. He therefore supports reversal and remand for preserved alternate objections without automatic reinstatement or a new factual finding.

#### Source Notes

The lower judgment and count groups are reported in United States v. O'Hagan, 92 F.3d 612. Exchange Act §§10(b), 14(e) and 32(a), 15 U.S.C. §§78j(b), 78n(e) and 78ff(a), and Rules 10b-5 and 14e-3(a) supply the operative provisions. [The public record recited at 521 U.S. 642](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/96-842.ZS.html) supports the facts, chronology and the petition's limits. The disposition resolves legal theories while leaving preserved trial challenges open. No new factual conclusion about knowledge, instructions, sufficiency or an unreviewed count is made.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Eighth Circuit must reconsider the reviewed securities and mail-fraud counts under the legal rules stated here and address preserved instruction, sufficiency, harmless-error, scope, due-process and other alternate grounds it did not reach. Reinstatement is appropriate only insofar as the judgments survive those legally available objections; no conviction is restored free of them by this mandate. The money-laundering reversal remains untouched. No other trading relationship or tender-offer application is adjudicated merely by upholding the theories presented.

### Ohio Adult Parole Authority v. Woodard, No. 96-1769

Merits decision, March 25, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 10, 1997; decided March 25, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the Sixth Circuit, reviewing 107 F.3d 1178. The questions concern due process in Ohio's capital-clemency procedure and whether the offered interview compels self-incrimination.

Woodard brought this §1983 action after receiving notice of a voluntary interview and a clemency hearing. He did not request the interview. The District Court granted judgment on the pleadings. The Sixth Circuit rejected an independent entitlement to clemency, but ordered further proceedings on an established-process theory of due process and on a possible unconstitutional condition on silence.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Due-process remand | Reversed, 8–1 | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Stevens | Reject the present process claim; no entitlement to clemency or new hearing follows |
| Self-incrimination remand | Reversed, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | — | Reject the claim based on the offered voluntary interview |

The Sixth Circuit's rejection of an independent entitlement to clemency is undisturbed. The case returns for proceedings consistent with these rulings. The Court neither grants nor denies mercy, sets an execution date, adjudicates a stay, nor orders immunity or a new clemency hearing.

#### Opinion Topology

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. Required clemency procedures do not create an entitlement to mercy or triallike review

**Holding and operative rule:** Ohio's required investigation, hearing and advisory recommendation do not create a substantive entitlement to clemency because they do not constrain the Governor to grant it on specified facts. The analogy to a first appeal as of right does not itself require appellate representation, triallike evidence procedure or a new determination of guilt in this discretionary executive process.

**Authority:** Part I-A has all eight Associates, with Stone-Zsela also joining the no-right-to-mercy conclusion. Part I-B has O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; Stone-Zsela joins only the mercy-versus-criminal-trial distinction. Stevens does not join the rejection of the established-process analogy.

**Controlling explanation:** Dumschat distinguishes seeking clemency from entitlement to its grant. Sandin requires a genuine substantive constraint on a meaningful deprivation; Doe and Johnson likewise distinguish required administration from an entitlement to a favorable discretionary decision. Ohio must investigate, hold the required hearing and recommend action even after an execution stay, but its recommendation is advisory and the Governor retains complete discretion. A later hearing or revised recommendation on new information is permitted, not required. These provisions do not compel clemency.

Evitts protects the first appeal as of right within adjudication of guilt. Clemency performs a different executive function. Its availability after conviction does not itself import appellate or trial procedure. The Court rejects the Sixth Circuit's general integral-stage theory as a basis for the requested remand, while preserving independently retained constitutional rights. Herrera's record-specific innocence ruling establishes no rule that executive mercy is the exclusive constitutionally sufficient substitute for every claim of innocence.

##### II. Capital-clemency procedures retain a constitutional minimum, satisfied on these pleadings

**Holding and operative rule:** An established capital-clemency process must afford minimal safeguards against fundamentally arbitrary procedure, including arbitrary denial of any access or substitution of an entirely arbitrary device such as a coin flip for consideration. The notice and offered interview here, considered with the pleaded restrictions, satisfy that minimum; they establish no entitlement to clemency, trial protections, a favorable recommendation or judicial review of mercy's merits.

**Authority:** Part II-A's minimum safeguard has six direct joins: O'Connor, Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Part II-B's sufficient-process application has five: O'Connor, Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Stevens disputes the application; Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas agree with rejection of the claim on their distinct entitlement/adjudication ground. Both controlling propositions rest on direct majority joins, without combining separate rationales under Marks.

**Controlling explanation:** A valid death sentence reduces the process owed before discretionary mercy is denied, but the prisoner remains alive. That retained interest supplies a minimum protection independent of a state-created entitlement to release. Ford illustrates the distinction between the interest protected and the flexible procedure appropriate to it; this case does not import Ford's separate prohibition on executing an incompetent prisoner or its particular hearing requirements.

Woodard alleges three days' interview notice, ten days' hearing notice, difficulty preparing while postconviction proceedings continued, exclusion of counsel from the interview, chair-controlled counsel participation at the hearing, and inability to testify or submit documents at the hearing. Accepting that pleaded account, the notice and offered personal interview provided access, and those restrictions do not establish fundamentally arbitrary procedure requiring further adjudication. The Court does not find an unmentioned alternative document channel or reject the allegations as untrue. It establishes neither a fixed list of required safeguards nor an accuracy code for every disputed item in a clemency file. Johnson's rejection of a parole-file process entitlement does not extinguish independent constitutional protection.

##### III. The optional interview does not establish compelled self-incrimination

**Holding and operative rule:** An optional clemency interview does not compel self-incrimination merely because the prisoner must choose between providing potentially harmful information and remaining silent; absence of counsel, confidentiality or immunity does not itself convert this invitation into compulsion. The conclusion also holds if an ordinary civil adverse inference from silence is assumed, but it does not authorize an automatic privilege penalty, compelled waiver or criminal use of compelled answers.

**Authority:** Part III-A's present voluntary-interview holding has all nine Justices. Part III-B's additional ordinary-inference ground and its limits have all eight Associates; Stone-Zsela does not join that additional ground.

**Controlling explanation:** Murphy distinguishes a difficult evidentiary choice from a command backed by a genuine threatened penalty for asserting the privilege. Woodard was invited, not required, to attend or answer. The State's refusal to promise confidentiality or immunity does not establish that it compelled answers. No automatic loss of clemency eligibility for silence, compelled waiver or criminal use of compelled testimony is established.

The eight Associates additionally apply Baxter's distinction between ordinary adverse inferences in civil proceedings and automatic sanctions attached to silence. Even assuming such an inference in this civil clemency evaluation, the voluntary choice does not become compelled testimony. The record does not establish Ohio's actual use of silence or answers; the assumption supplies an alternative legal ground, not a factual finding. LaChance preserves separately supported compulsion and waiver claims, and Johnson confirms that absence of entitlement to a benefit does not excuse an independent unconstitutional penalty. A materially supported coercive condition would therefore require its own analysis.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat:** Applied to discretionary mercy and the distinction between seeking relief and entitlement to its grant; not extended to extinguish independent constitutional interests.
- **Sandin v. Conner:** Applied at its substantive-constraint rule. Mandatory procedure alone is insufficient, and directly retained constitutional interests remain separate.
- **Doe v. District of Columbia:** Applied to distinguish investigative duties from a substantive entitlement.
- **Johnson v. Rodriguez:** Applied to the same procedure/entitlement boundary and preservation of independent constitutional wrongs; its ordinary initial-parole rule does not displace the retained-life floor.
- **Evitts v. Lucey:** Distinguished at the adjudicatory first appeal as of right; it does not itself require the requested capital-clemency procedures.
- **Ford v. Wainwright:** Its distinction between a protected interest and appropriate flexible procedure informs the minimum-process rule; its separate incompetency protection remains unchanged.
- **Herrera v. Collins:** Preserved as a record-specific innocence disposition; no exclusive-clemency rule is attributed to it.
- **Minnesota v. Murphy:** Applied to distinguish voluntary choices from supported threats of a privilege penalty.
- **Baxter v. Palmigiano:** Applied by the eight Associates to ordinary civil adverse inferences, distinct from automatic punishment or compelled criminal testimony.
- **LaChance v. Erickson / McManus:** Preserved for separately established compulsion and waiver; it supplies no general prohibition on lawful civil inferences.

#### Law After Decision

Capital clemency remains discretionary executive mercy, separate from judicial collateral review. Required administrative steps create no right to a favorable outcome. A directly controlling minimum protects remaining life against fundamentally arbitrary clemency procedure, but the pleaded restrictions here do not breach it. The optional interview establishes no compelled self-incrimination, including on the eight-Justice alternative assumption of an ordinary civil adverse inference. No general absence of constitutional protection is declared.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Kennedy, joined by Scalia and Thomas, concurring in part and in the judgment.** Ohio's procedure does not constrain the ultimate mercy decision, and the appellate analogy supplies no right to the process requested. Dumschat and the substantive distinction applied in Sandin and Johnson resolve these pleaded process claims. These Justices do not join the affirmative retained-life framework or its application; their judgment agreement adds no join to Part II. They preserve independently grounded constitutional prohibitions and fully join the self-incrimination analysis, including its actual-compulsion and automatic-penalty limits.

**Stevens, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** A prisoner remains alive, and an established procedure integral to the State's final decision to take life must afford basic fairness and meaningful participation. His Dumschat position and Evitts's established-process principle support bounded inquiry into the process as a whole, even though clemency need not replicate a trial and no right to mercy exists. He joins the minimum safeguard but would preserve the due-process remand to consider the relevant materials rather than hold these pleadings insufficient. He finds no violation already established and prescribes none of the requested safeguards automatically. He joins both self-incrimination grounds, distinguishing this voluntary invitation from a command backed by a serious threatened sanction.

The state procedure, judgment-on-the-pleadings posture, Sixth Circuit rulings and asserted deficiencies appear in [523 U.S. 276–279 and 289–290](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-523/pdf/USREPORTS-523-272.pdf). The same source at 286 identifies the absence of a record showing actual use of answers or silence and the Authority's refusal to promise confidentiality or immunity. The pleaded limitations are evaluated as allegations, not resolved against Woodard through new factual findings. No equal-protection claim is presented.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Sixth Circuit must conform its disposition to rejection of the presented due-process and self-incrimination claims. Its no-entitlement ruling stands. The decision neither retries guilt nor prevents lawful executive clemency review under Ohio's existing duties; it orders no particular recommendation, hearing, release, stay or execution.

### California Public Employees' Retirement System and Florida State Board of Administration v. Paul Felzen et al., No. 97-1732

Merits decision, January 20, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 11, 1999. On writ of certiorari to the Seventh Circuit, Felzen v. Andreas, 134 F.3d 873 (January 21, 1998). The question is whether shareholders who respond to Rule 23.1 notice, appear and object to approval of a derivative settlement must intervene to appeal. Both institutions were heard in opposition to the settlement but chose not to seek intervention. The District Court approved the settlement on July 7, 1997; their July 14 notice of appeal identified the settlement judgment and fee/expense order. The Court of Appeals dismissed on the appellate-status ground without reaching the settlement's merits.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

**Opposing:** Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

#### Opinion of the Court

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela delivered the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. Those five Justices adopt both holdings, their complete reasoning, qualifications and confined remedy. Justice Stevens filed a dissent, joined by Justices Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, proposing a limited representative-objector basis for appeal and reversal and remand without deciding the settlement or fee merits.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. Rule 23.1 objection does not itself confer the party status needed for this appeal

**Holding and operative rule:** A shareholder who is otherwise not a party does not acquire appellate party status merely by appearing under Rule 23.1 notice and presenting objections that the district court hears and rejects when approving a derivative settlement. To appeal that settlement judgment, the shareholder must properly become a party through intervention; shareholder interest, notice and the recognized opportunity to object do not themselves supply the necessary status.

**Authority:** The five-Justice opinion of the Court identified above controls both holdings and their stated qualifications.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an opportunity to assist the settlement court by objecting is distinct from authority to continue the corporate litigation on appeal. Marino v. Ortiz supplies both the general party-status rule and its application to persons actually heard in opposition to a settlement who had not intervened. Rule 23.1 recognizes shareholder participation in the district court's supervision of the corporate claim; that function does not itself confer the separate appellate role. Tryforos v. Icarian Development Co. supplies the strongest contrary rule. Its distinction between objecting to disposition and assuming prosecution expressly included an appeal by a shareholder who appeared to oppose a proposed settlement. The Court rejects that settlement-appeal proposition insofar as it permits this appeal without intervention: limiting an objector's appellate objectives does not remove the status prerequisite Marino applies. Izumi Seimitsu Kogyo Kabushiki Kaisha v. U.S. Philips Corp. reinforces the distinction between interest and status, although its undisturbed intervention denial and separately unpresented intervention question differ from this case's direct status challenge. Johnson v. Manhattan Railway Co. does not establish an independent route here. Its discussion of invitation, appearance and appeal at page 495 is followed at page 496 by an explanation why the applicable equity rule would not obstruct intervention, in a distinct receivership setting. Neither passage decides modern derivative-objector status by itself. These institutions were heard but sought no intervention. Their reliance on earlier circuit practice does not constitute admission as parties, and no denial of an application is before the Court.

**Limits:** This is a rule for the presented derivative-settlement appeal, not a categorical prohibition of every nonparty appeal under every procedural regime. Section 1291 supplies the final-decision framework, Appellate Rule 3(c) concerns identification of appellants, Rule 23.1 governs the recognized objection role, and Rule 24 supplies intervention; neither §1291 nor Rule 3(c) is treated as expressly deciding every party-status category. The decision rests on actual participation and the absence of intervention, not on a finding that the institutions violated a notice or service condition.

##### 2. Intervention review and nonparty due-process protection remain distinct

**Holding and operative rule:** Requiring intervention for this settlement appeal does not foreclose review by an actual party or an appropriate appeal from an actual denial of intervention. Nor does it impose a general duty to intervene in another person's suit to preserve an otherwise independent claim: constitutional limits on binding genuinely unrepresented persons remain, without establishing an appellate entitlement or a collateral remedy in this case.

**Authority:** The same five-Justice opinion of the Court controls this boundary and the confined affirmance.

**Controlling explanation:** Izumi preserves a lawful route for reviewing an intervention denial while distinguishing it from review of the underlying judgment by a party. No such denial exists here, so the Court decides neither entitlement to intervention nor the timing or result of an application never made. Richards v. Jefferson County protects a person's own rights against preclusion without constitutionally sufficient consent, control, genuine privity, adequate representative litigation or another recognized basis consistent with due process. Its rule does not make every affected person an appellate party. Matsushita Electric Industrial Co. v. Epstein preserves the independent requirement of adequate representation; notice, opportunities to object or opt out do not cure its constitutional absence. Those protections remain effective, but neither adequate nor inadequate representation is established by the derivative label or by these institutions' appearance. The claim settled below belongs to the corporation, while Richards involved direct personal claims. Keeping that distinction does not decide whether any particular independent shareholder claim exists or may proceed. The status ruling accordingly leaves the settlement's fairness, compensation terms and any properly presented intervention or due-process question undecided.

**Questions not reached and remedial limits:** The Court orders no reopening, tolling, late intervention, new hearing, discovery or review of a fictional denied motion. It does not independently decide the petition's requested pure-prospectivity question or adopt the Court of Appeals' broader assertion that every possible operation of Chevron Oil was eliminated. Harper's rule against selective prospectivity after application of a federal rule to the Court's litigants remains at its established scope; it creates no unsupported transition entitlement here. No settlement-fairness, fee-merits, collusion, representation-adequacy, personal-claim or collateral-preclusion judgment follows.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Marino v. Ortiz, 484 U.S. 301:** Applied to the distinction between being heard as a settlement objector and properly obtaining party status for appeal. Its separate equally divided collateral-action disposition supplies no general preclusion rule here.
- **Tryforos v. Icarian Development Co., 518 F.2d 1258, Part I and footnote 22:** Its express route for an appearing Rule 23.1 shareholder to appeal a proposed settlement without intervention is rejected to the extent inconsistent with today's rule. Its separate service, notice-of-appeal and fee rulings are not adjudicated or displaced merely by that conclusion.
- **Izumi Seimitsu Kogyo Kabushiki Kaisha v. U.S. Philips Corp. (November 30, 1993):** Its party-status boundary and route to review an actual intervention denial are applied; its different §1254(1), denied-intervention and question-presentation posture is preserved. It is not treated as having already decided the specific Rule 23.1 route presented here.
- **Johnson v. Manhattan Railway Co., 289 U.S. 479, 495–496:** Its invitation/appearance discussion and adjacent intervention analysis are read together in their receivership context. It establishes neither an unqualified modern nonparty-appeal right nor, by itself, a universal formal-motion requirement.
- **Richards v. Jefferson County (June 10, 1996):** Its protection of independent rights against unsupported nonparty preclusion remains intact. It supplies no automatic appellate status and no general duty to intervene to preserve an independent claim.
- **Matsushita Electric Industrial Co. v. Epstein (February 27, 1996):** Its adequate-representation component remains distinct from notice and objection opportunities. No broader collateral-review question is resolved.
- **Harper v. Virginia Department of Taxation (June 18, 1993):** Its applied-rule prohibition on selective prospectivity retains its actual scope. The Court does not adopt a blanket disposition of all prospective rulings or every possible Chevron Oil question.

#### Law After Decision

Rule 23.1 continues to protect the opportunity to object to disposition of a derivative action, but an otherwise nonparty shareholder must use the intervention route to obtain the party status required for this settlement appeal. The contrary settlement-appeal proposition in Tryforos no longer governs this setting. Actual-party review and review of a genuine intervention denial remain distinct lawful routes. The decision neither converts notice into constitutionally adequate representation nor weakens the protection of independent rights against unsupported nonparty preclusion. It leaves the settlement undisturbed without deciding its merits or creating a new intervention or collateral proceeding.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Justice Stevens, joined by Justices Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, dissenting.** The dissent would recognize a confined appellate role for a shareholder who responds to the Rule 23.1 settlement notice, appears, presents objections, and has those objections heard and rejected in the disposition of the corporate claim. The procedural role, rather than mere ownership, financial exposure, receipt of notice or potential binding effect, supplies status to seek review of that disposition. It does not make the objector a plaintiff controlling prosecution or give a nonappearing shareholder a right to appeal.

Tryforos, which Justice Stevens authored as a circuit judge, supplies the direct distinction between objecting to disposition and undertaking prosecution. Its footnote expressly includes proposed settlements, although the actual objection there concerned dismissal with prejudice and the corporation's lack of service. Marino presents a serious contrary example because its objectors were heard too. The dissent distinguishes their adverse personal interests in another action from the Rule 23.1 shareholder's defined role concerning disposition of the represented corporate claim. Izumi involved an undisturbed intervention denial and a different presented question; it does not foreclose deciding that this recognized role supplies limited status. Johnson offers an equity analogy linking invitation, appearance, objection and appeal, but its adjacent intervention discussion and receivership setting prevent treating it as a universal nonintervention rule. Richards protects independent nonparty rights without itself creating appellate status.

The dissent would reverse only the status dismissal and remand for further appellate proceedings subject to ordinary preservation and appeal requirements. It would not invalidate the settlement, find collusion or inadequate representation, permit new claims, promise success on the objections or establish reviewability or entitlement on every fee contention. It proposes no independent prospective-only ruling, reopening, tolling or automatic late intervention. Its limited representative-objector rule does not control the judgment.

#### Source Notes

[The Seventh Circuit opinion](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/134/134.F3d.873.97-2829.html) supplies the status dismissal and the objectors' decision not to intervene. The original petition caption and Rule 23.1 notice, preliminary order, hearing and appeal excerpts identify both institutional petitioners, the invitation to object, their actual appearance and the dated orders. The notice's settlement descriptions and the District Court's fairness findings are not adopted here as Supreme Court findings. [Tryforos's primary court transcription](https://static.case.law/f2d/518/cases/1258-01.json), the [official Marino report](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-484/pdf/USREPORTS-484-301.pdf), and the [official Johnson report](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-289/pdf/USREPORTS-289-479.pdf) support the distinct procedural routes and limits discussed. No determination rests on disputed exact waiver wording, an unreported ruling in the hearing's omitted middle, or an unestablished intervention application.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Seventh Circuit's dismissal stands because this attempted nonparty appeal cannot proceed. No intervention application, denial, reopening, tolling or late-intervention entitlement is adjudicated. ### Mitchell v. United States, No. 97-7541

Merits decision, April 5, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 9, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the Third Circuit, 122 F.3d 185 (1997). The questions concern whether a guilty plea waives the privilege at sentencing and whether silence may support the disputed drug quantity.

Mitchell pleaded guilty without an agreement to conspiracy and three distribution counts, expressly reserving attributable quantity. The colloquy described the right to silence at trial. At sentencing, the court credited cooperating witnesses and used her failure to contradict them as support for finding at least five kilograms, producing a ten-year minimum. Counsel disputed the testimony's reliability. The Third Circuit affirmed on waiver; a separate concurrence considered any error harmless because other evidence supported quantity.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed in the sentencing respect; sentence vacated and remanded, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas, who would affirm against the inference challenge while rejecting automatic plea waiver.

The District Court must determine quantity from affirmative evidence admissible under the governing sentencing standards without treating silence as evidence against Mitchell. No replacement term, automatic minimum, plea withdrawal, acquittal or jury hearing is ordered. Consequential adjustments to other sentence components must follow governing law; no independent ruling automatically eliminates supervised release or the assessment.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Exact scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | Breyer | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | Retained privilege and the limited effect of the plea and colloquy |
| Opinion of the Court, Parts II–III | Breyer | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg | No adverse inference concerning crime facts; prejudice and bounded sentencing relief |
| Concurrence in Part I and dissent from Parts II–III and the judgment | Scalia | O'Connor, Thomas | Would permit this inference and affirm without adopting waiver |
| Separate dissent | Thomas | No other Justice | Additional willingness to reconsider Griffin and Carter in an appropriate case; no present overruling |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. The plea did not waive privilege concerning reserved quantity

**Holding and operative rule:** A guilty plea does not itself waive the privilege against compelled self-incrimination concerning disputed sentencing facts while testimony creates a genuine risk of additional criminal punishment. This accepted plea, its limited admissions and its trial-silence warning did not waive protection concerning expressly reserved quantity.

**Authority:** Part I of Breyer's Opinion of the Court, joined by all eight other Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Estelle preserves protection against sentencing incrimination. Brown prevents a voluntary witness from disclosing a subject selectively while refusing relevant cross-examination; it does not make a plea's limited factual inquiry consent to compelled testimony on unresolved quantity. Rule 11 permits inquiry into a plea's factual basis, and refusal to supply necessary information may leave that basis inadequate. Here the court accepted the plea and reserved quantity. Neither awareness of possible punishment nor admission of the offense establishes the missing waiver. The plea and actual voluntary admissions remain usable. When sentence is fixed, conviction final and further testimony presents no remaining adverse criminal consequence, the privilege has no continuing incriminating risk to protect; finality alone does not extinguish another genuine criminal exposure. Immunity-backed testimony is not decided.

##### II. Protected silence cannot prove the crime's circumstances at sentencing

**Holding and operative rule:** A sentencing court may not draw an adverse factual inference from a defendant's protected silence in determining the circumstances and details of the crime. The Government must establish disputed quantity through lawful affirmative evidence; permission for judicial sentencing findings does not authorize this source of proof.

**Authority:** Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg: six Justices including Breyer.

**Controlling explanation:** Griffin prevents using protected silence to supply criminal proof, and Estelle preserves the privilege when punishment remains unresolved. Their protection cannot depend on whether admitted guilt precedes determination of the crime's remaining punitive facts. Baxter's civil inference and Woodard's discretionary-clemency setting differ from this initial criminal sentencing determination. Witte and Watts permit certain sentencing information within lawful authority but retain independent constitutional limits; Dunnigan permits consequences for established willful material perjury, not an inference from giving no testimony. This holding concerns the source of proof, not whether every sentencing fact is an element or must go to a jury. It does not bar voluntary statements. Remorse and actual acceptance-of-responsibility claims remain undecided. The ruling does not prevent denial of an affirmative acceptance reduction for failure to carry a lawful burden; it creates no new burden or entitlement and decides no particular reduction ruling.

##### III. Other potentially sufficient evidence does not establish harmlessness here

**Holding and operative rule:** The Government must establish beyond a reasonable doubt that this constitutional error did not contribute to the affected sentencing determination. Because the judge expressly relied on silence in crediting disputed quantity testimony, the finding cannot be preserved merely because the remaining evidence could support it; the sentence must be vacated and quantity reconsidered without that inference.

**Authority:** Part III, joined by the same six Justices as Part II.

**Controlling explanation:** Chapman asks whether error contributed, not whether an appellate court could independently assemble sufficient evidence. The judge used silence to support the disputed testimony and indicated that a contrary privilege ruling could warrant closer examination. No independent conclusive quantity determination unaffected by that use is established. Wright independently requires a lawful sentencing basis established in the sentencing findings rather than a substituted appellate ground; its Guidelines holding is not converted into a universal constitutional rule. The District Court must reassess quantity from affirmative evidence admissible under the governing sentencing standards. The Court neither declares the witnesses unreliable nor guarantees a lower sentence. The pleas and admitted offenses remain undisturbed, and any lawful consequential sentencing adjustments remain for remand.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Estelle supplies continuing protection against incrimination at sentencing. Brown supplies the scope-based rule for a voluntary witness's waiver; neither makes every plea statement inadmissible nor establishes a permanent privilege.
- Griffin's no-inference protection now applies to proof of crime facts at criminal sentencing; its established trial operation remains. Estelle supplies the continuing privilege that this application protects.
- Baxter's civil-inference rule and Woodard's discretionary-clemency setting are distinguished; neither authorizes this initial criminal-sentencing inference.
- Witte and Watts preserve lawful sentencing findings within lawful authority. Dunnigan preserves consequences for established willful material perjury. None removes independent constitutional safeguards or permits an adverse inference from protected silence here.
- Chapman supplies the contribution-based constitutional harmlessness standard. Wright separately requires a lawful sentencing basis in the sentencing findings; its Guidelines holding is not converted into a universal constitutional rule.
- Jones and Monge supply no quantity classification or universal mandatory-minimum rule here.

No precedent is overruled.

#### Law After Decision

The privilege survives this plea concerning reserved punitive facts, and protected silence cannot supply adverse proof of the crime's circumstances at sentencing. Express reliance requires relief unless the Government establishes constitutional harmlessness. Lawful sentencing allocation, proof requirements and voluntary-statement use otherwise remain; no universal jury rule, mandatory-minimum classification or right to an acceptance reduction is adopted.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Scalia, joined by O'Connor and Thomas.** The three join Part I because the plea did not waive privilege on reserved quantity, but distinguish the right to refuse compelled testimony from an ordinary evidentiary inference drawn alongside credited evidence. They accept Griffin and Carter at their established guilt-phase scope and decline the sentencing extension. Baxter and Woodard illustrate the distinction between inference, compelled answers and an automatic penalty for invocation; sentencing's different evidentiary rules, recognized in McMillan, remain relevant. They dispute the majority's separation of offense circumstances from other sentencing information. Their position permits neither compulsory testimony, treating the plea as an admission of reserved quantity, an automatic privilege penalty nor proof by silence alone. Scalia retains his broader Jones penalty-range position without deciding a new quantity-classification claim here. They find no error and therefore do not reach harmlessness as a ground for affirmance. Conditional acceptance of lawful relief under the Court's contrary rule adds no vote for reversal.

**Thomas.** Thomas joins Scalia's opinion but separately questions the constitutional foundations of Griffin and Carter: an ordinary inference does not itself compel testimony, and a statutory no-comment policy does not necessarily establish a constitutional prohibition. He would consider reconsidering those decisions in an appropriate case. He casts no vote to overrule them here, and no other Justice joins this prospective recommendation. Scalia instead preserves their established operation.

The chronology, reserved quantity, sentencing reliance and separate lower-court harmlessness ground are recited in [526 U.S. 314](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/526bv.pdf#page=425); the judgment below is reported at 122 F.3d 185. The published recitation establishes neither a new credibility finding nor an independent quantity determination free from the challenged reliance.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns through the Third Circuit for resentencing consistent with the Court's ruling. Quantity must be determined from affirmative evidence admissible under the governing sentencing standards, with no adverse inference from protected silence. The guilty pleas remain intact; the Court fixes no new sentence.

### Board of Trustees of the University of Alabama v. Garrett, No. 99-1240

Merits decision, February 21, 2001

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 11, 2000. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit, 193 F.3d 1214. The court of appeals reversed the district court's immunity disposition in the consolidated employment actions. The question is whether Congress validly authorized private federal damages actions against state employers under Title I of the Americans with Disabilities Act. Garrett alleges disability-related adverse treatment at a state hospital; Ash alleges employment discrimination and denied accommodation associated with asthma and sleep apnea. Those allegations establish no statutory violation. This review concerns Title I only.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer.

**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stone-Zsela | Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Affirmance, 5–4 | Entire opinion; statutory authorization and independent Article I and Section 5 grounds |
| Dissent | O'Connor | Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Would reverse the Title I immunity rejection | Structural State-suit objection and insufficient Section 5 fit; statutory clarity accepted |
| Supplementary dissent | Kennedy | O'Connor | Same proposed reversal | Official prejudice may arise from indifference; opposition rests on the particular remedy's fit, not presumed State compliance |

#### Holdings

##### I. Article I sustains this expressly authorized employment action

**Holding and operative rule:** Congress may subject a State to the expressly authorized private Title I federal action when the covered employment obligation and its enforcement measure fall within the Commerce and Necessary and Proper Clauses. Those conditions are satisfied here, so the State defendants have no categorical immunity from these actions; authorization and amenability establish neither liability nor entitlement to a particular award.

**Authority:** The five-Justice opinion of the Court identified above directly adopts this independently sufficient ground and the Section 5 ground below.

**Controlling explanation:** Florida Prepaid permits expressly authorized private enforcement of a valid Article I duty against a State; Kimel applies that principle to employment. Title I regulates hiring, retention and other terms of covered employment, an economic relationship participating in actual labor markets. That connection satisfies Lopez before aggregate effects are considered; generalized productivity or social costs do not supply the constitutional basis. Garcia and EEOC v. Wyoming sustain direct federal regulation of covered state employment. An ordinary action enforcing the valid duty is an appropriate enforcement measure, not compelled State administration of a federal program.

Section 12202 expressly removes State immunity for violations of the chapter and makes legal and equitable remedies available on the same terms as against other public or private entities. Section 12117 supplies the employment-enforcement channels. These provisions resolve authorization without establishing consent. The States' distinction between valid employment regulation and private suit seeks reconsideration of Florida Prepaid and Kimel; the Court adheres to their enforcement rule. Requiring an additional individual constitutional violation would remove the statutory protection that Congress validly enacted. The merits and award-specific limits remain for ordinary adjudication.

##### II. Section 5 independently sustains the presented Title I enforcement

**Holding and operative rule:** Title I's protection of qualified workers against disability discrimination, including reasonable accommodation subject to its enacted qualifications and defenses, is congruent and proportional preventive and remedial enforcement of equal protection against state employers. Section 5 therefore independently sustains the expressly authorized actions without requiring each employee to prove a separate constitutional violation.

**Authority:** The same five-Justice opinion directly adopts this alternative sufficient holding; no combination of separate opinions is needed.

**Controlling explanation:** Boerne permits prevention extending beyond conduct that a court would independently hold unconstitutional, while requiring a fitted relationship to the constitutional injury. Cleburne forbids irrational disability prejudice. Heller and Romer require a rational relationship to the actual disadvantage while preserving relevant generalizations and conceivable legitimate explanations; they create neither heightened disability scrutiny nor a constitutional entitlement to every accommodation.

Congress received accounts of categorical exclusions, stereotyped assumptions, harassment and rejection in State departments and universities, alongside evidence concerning local employers, private conduct and other public services. The Court does not treat every reported incident as an adjudicated constitutional wrong or the approximately 600 state-actor submissions as a count of State-employment violations. The differentiated employment evidence and history nevertheless support the preventive inference that official exclusion can conceal or perpetuate irrational prejudice. Statutes prohibiting discrimination do not by their existence establish effective protection or compliance.

The States correctly observe that accommodation and screening duties can exceed constitutional rationality. That difference is permitted prevention, not sufficient proof of invalidity. Qualification, essential-function requirements, known-limitations rules, job-related screening and the separate hardship and accommodation defenses connect the means to the identified risk. Florida Prepaid confirms that fitted preventive enforcement need not demand an individual constitutional tort from every plaintiff. No incident quota, formal-findings requirement, geographic limit or sunset is imposed. The Court sustains the bounded employment scheme; it does not redefine every statutory breach as unconstitutional.

**Statutory limits and work remaining:** Covered-employer status requires an industry affecting commerce and ordinarily at least 15 employees for each working day in at least 20 calendar weeks of the current or preceding year; the original two-year transition used 25 employees. Agents are included; the United States, wholly owned federal corporations, Indian tribes and bona fide private membership clubs other than labor organizations that are exempt from taxation under 26 U.S.C. section 501(c) are excluded from this employer definition. Actual coverage remains to be proved.

Disability retains impairment substantially limiting a major life activity, record-of-impairment and regarded-as branches. A qualified worker must perform essential functions with or without reasonable accommodation. Employer judgment receives consideration; a job description prepared before advertising or interviews is evidence, not a conclusive veto. Accommodation examples are permissive and include reassignment to a vacant position. Section 12112(b)(5)(A) concerns known limitations and allows the employer to demonstrate undue hardship: significant difficulty or expense assessed by cost, facility and entity resources, workforce, operational and organizational factors. Section 12112(b)(6) separately addresses screening criteria shown job-related for the position and consistent with business necessity; section 12113(a) separately requires that performance cannot be accomplished by reasonable accommodation. Direct threat concerns a significant risk to the health or safety of others that reasonable accommodation cannot eliminate. No general reasonableness exception or Title II fundamental-alteration defense replaces those provisions.

No medical-inquiry, foreign-workplace, religious-entity, food-handling or drug/alcohol defense is established or adjudicated here. The Court creates no exception or holding about those distinct provisions. Title II, Rehabilitation Act waiver, individual liability and award-specific limitations lie outside this Title I abrogation question; no Title II dismissal or abandonment is ordered. Qualification, discrimination, causation and relief are ordinary remaining merits issues, not unresolved aspects of the constitutional enforcement holdings.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Florida Prepaid, June 23, 1999: applies both its express Article I enforcement rule and its independent congruent-and-proportional prevention principle; neither requires consent or an individual constitutional tort.
- Kimel, January 11, 2000: applies its Article I employment-enforcement holding; its reservation of the ADEA's Section 5 validity remains untouched.
- Lopez: applies the identifiable commerce connection before aggregation; no general social-cost theory or automatic jurisdictional-word test is adopted.
- Garcia and EEOC v. Wyoming: apply their support for valid federal regulation of state employment.
- Boerne: applies congruence and proportionality, preserving preventive authority and the prohibition on constitutional redefinition.
- Cleburne, Heller and Romer: apply actual rationality review without heightened disability scrutiny or a constitutional guarantee of accommodation.
- Olmstead: distinguishes its statutory integration holding and express nondecision of immunity and Section 5; its Title II defense is not imported.
- Union Gas and Seminole Tribe: preserve the constitutional conclusion extended in Florida Prepaid and the distinction between authorization and power; no categorical Article I prohibition displaces current law.
- College Savings Bank: preserves the rejection of consent inferred from ordinary State activity; these actions rest on legislative authority.

#### Law After Decision

Effective February 21, 2001, private federal Title I actions against covered state employers may proceed under the express enforcement provisions on either of two independent grounds: valid Article I employment enforcement and congruent and proportional Section 5 prevention. The existing general frameworks remain; the decision adds their controlling Title I application. Courts must apply the statutory coverage, qualification, defenses, proof and remedy conditions in each case. The ADA's broader duties do not become the constitutional minimum, and ordinary adjudication does not require proof of an individual constitutional tort as an added entry condition.

#### Separate Writings

**Justice O'Connor, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, dissenting.** The dissent accepts section 12202's express authorization and Congress's authority to regulate employment, but distinguishes that authority from power to compel a nonconsenting State to defend a private damages action. It would reconsider Florida Prepaid and Kimel's Article I enforcement rule and the retained Union Gas conclusion to the necessary extent. Independently, it finds Title I's accommodation, screening, justification and private-remedy reach insufficiently connected to the identified unconstitutional State-employment conduct under Boerne. It recognizes preventive authority and irrational official prejudice; it requires no numerical incident threshold, formal findings, violation in every State, sunset or constitutional tort in every preventive action. The statutory safeguards narrow the gap but do not, in its view, establish the required fit. It would reverse only the presented Title I immunity rejection.

**Justice Kennedy, joined by O'Connor, additionally dissenting.** Disability exclusion can result from official indifference and persistent stereotyping as well as express hostility. The existence of state antidiscrimination laws does not prove compliance, and lack of reported constitutional litigation does not settle Congress's enforcement authority. The disagreement concerns whether the employment evidence and safeguards adequately connect this private damages regime to the constitutional injury. The writing preserves ordinary rationality review and lawful prevention while joining the proposed scoped reversal.

### Wharf (Holdings) Ltd. v. United International Holdings, Inc., No. 00-347

Merits decision after plenary review, May 21, 2001

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 21, 2001; decided May 21, 2001. Review of the Tenth Circuit's judgment sustaining the civil jury judgment. The question is whether selling an oral option while secretly intending never to honor it constitutes deception in connection with a securities sale under section 10(b) and Rule 10b-5.

United supplied services for Wharf's cable-system bid. The jury found an actual option to acquire a ten-percent interest and a contemporaneous intention never to honor it, supported by internal memoranda. The option, rather than an unrealized later share purchase, is the security acquired. The lower court's state-law enforceability determination is an existing review premise.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed on the reviewed securities issue, 9-0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The Tenth Circuit's judgment is affirmed on the reviewed federal securities issue. The existing judgment is preserved within that scope. #### Opinion Topology

Thomas delivered the opinion of the Court, joined in full by all eight other Justices.

#### Holdings

##### Deception at the sale of the option

The Court holds that a seller who obtains value by selling an actual option while concealing a contemporaneous intention never to honor it commits deception in connection with that security's sale. Oral form alone does not exclude the proved option; materiality, required culpability, legally sufficient reliance, causation and recoverable loss remain necessary to the private action.

**Authority:** The opinion of Thomas, joined in full by all eight other participating Justices, controls the rule and all qualifications below.

The jury's option finding and contemporaneous memoranda distinguish this case from subsequent nonperformance. Kravitz supplies the distinction between a knowingly false securities undertaking and breach alone. O'Hagan requires an actual connection between deception and the transaction; direct deception of the option purchaser supplies it without revisiting source deception. Blue Chip Stamps requires a purchaser or seller, which the option acquisition supplies. Its evidentiary concerns do not authorize adding a writing requirement to the statute's coverage. Santa Fe excludes unfairness without deception, not the deliberate sham proved here. Central Bank's separate assistance theory is unnecessary because Wharf's own sale is the primary conduct.

The Tenth Circuit's conclusion that this agreement was enforceable under the then-applicable Colorado statute is not reopened. The Court does not decide treatment of an agreement unenforceable under state law, which is outside the accepted federal question, and does not make state enforceability an invariable additional element of section 10(b). A later breach, negligence or commercial unfairness alone proves neither initial deception nor the remaining elements. Unpresented foreign-transaction limits and the independent state-law punitive award are outside review.

#### Precedent Treatment

- United States v. O'Hagan: applied to require transaction-connected deception; the direct counterparty fraud here leaves its distinct source-deception dispute untouched.
- Blue Chip Stamps v. Manor Drug Stores: purchaser-seller standing is satisfied by the acquired option; no categorical writing requirement is added.
- Santa Fe Industries, Inc. v. Green: its requirement of deception beyond unfairness is preserved and satisfied by the verdict findings.
- Central Bank of Denver, N.A. v. First Interstate Bank of Denver, N.A.: its confined affirmative-assistance action remains in force; no assistance theory is necessary for this seller's primary fraud.

#### Law After Decision

Section 10(b) and Rule 10b-5 reach this knowing sham option sale, whether the established option is oral or written. The ordinary private-action elements continue to separate securities fraud from contract nonperformance. Coverage of state-law-unenforceable agreements remains undecided.

### United States v. Mead Corp., No. 99-1434

Merits decision, June 18, 2001

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 8, 2000. On writ of certiorari to the Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit. The questions concern the judicial weight of Customs tariff-classification ruling letters and whether the bound-diary provision includes these ring-bound day planners. The Federal Circuit reversed judgment for the United States, rejected both controlling and persuasive weight and held the planners outside the challenged dutiable classification. The January 1993 headquarters letter changed earlier duty-free treatment and offered dictionary and commercial reasons.

All nine Justices participate.

#### Judgment

**Vacated and remanded, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Stone-Zsela and Scalia.

The remand requires independent tariff construction with persuasive consideration of Customs' explanation. It directs no particular classification or monetary amount. The two dissenters would instead reverse and sustain the classification, on distinct grounds.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Parts I and II | Kennedy | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supplies controlling interpretive rules | No controlling deference for this mechanism; independent judicial construction with Skidmore consideration |
| Opinion of the Court, Part III | Kennedy | Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Vacates and remands | Corrected classification inquiry without a predetermined tariff answer |
| Concurrence in Parts I and II and dissent from the judgment | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Would reverse and sustain the classification | Independent reading of bound diary; remand only for implementation |
| Dissent | Scalia | No other Justice | Would reverse and sustain the classification | Controlling reasonable-choice treatment for the official agency interpretation |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. This ruling mechanism does not exercise authority to bind the reviewing court

**Holding and operative rule:** Controlling Chevron treatment requires congressional conferral, expressly or through the statutory scheme, of authority to make interpretations carrying the force of law and an interpretation issued in exercise of that authority. This transaction-specific, revisable Customs classification letter does not satisfy that requirement; general administrative responsibility, expertise and official institutional endorsement do not themselves confer controlling interpretive force on the mechanism.

**Authority for Holdings I and II:** Parts I and II of Kennedy's opinion have the eight direct supporters identified in the opinion table, including its author. Scalia does not join either proposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the power to administer and classify does not make every administrative interpretation controlling law for a reviewing court. Haggar requires actual delegated authority and statutory fit before acceptance of a reasonable choice within genuine ambiguity. Its formally promulgated Customs regulation does not establish the same authority for the different mechanism here. Christensen distinguishes controlling authority from an advisory interpretation's useful reasoning without deciding that all informal actions have the same status.

Customs letters guide particular transactions, may be modified, issue from numerous offices and do not bind unrelated importers as a generally applicable regulation would. Their place in the protest and judicial-review scheme confirms that administrative operation is distinct from delegated interpretive lawmaking. Formal rulemaking or adjudication can be important evidence of delegation, but neither notice and comment nor a document label is an exclusive prerequisite. Independent judicial factfinding likewise does not itself defeat lawful deference; Haggar remains intact.

The Solicitor General's brief, cosigned by Treasury's General Counsel, represents this as Customs' official position. The Court accepts that representation. It identifies whose interpretation is defended, but does not establish that Congress gave this ruling mechanism controlling legal effect. Headquarters' developed reasons and the responsible institution's endorsement strengthen the claim to consideration, not the statutory delegation. The 1993 action remains governed by its own legal regime; later procedures for changing rulings do not supply retrospective authority. Other statutory mechanisms and interpretation of an agency's own regulations are outside this grant.

##### II. Lack of controlling force does not eliminate persuasive consideration

**Holding and operative rule:** A court independently construing the tariff must consider this ruling letter's reasons according to their actual thoroughness, expertise, logic, consistency, commercial understanding and other persuasive force under Skidmore. No fixed weight or result follows; the Federal Circuit erred by excluding that consideration merely because the letter lacked controlling force.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an agency can offer useful reasons without possessing authority to bind the court's answer. Skidmore provides that separate inquiry, and Christensen preserves it when controlling statutory deference is unavailable. Customs addressed dictionary meaning, commercial usage and whether a ring mechanism binds pages. Those reasons bear on the actual tariff language and cannot be discarded by attaching a nonregulatory label to the letter.

Mead correctly points to the change from earlier duty-free treatment. That change may weaken a consistency claim; it does not eliminate every reason the agency offers. Expertise, national responsibility and official endorsement likewise do not automatically make the position persuasive. The court evaluates the explanation on its merits within the enacted tariff boundaries. Neither the fact that Customs changed course nor the fact that it administers a complex scheme decides the tariff question in advance. The distinction preserves meaningful independent judicial interpretation rather than converting persuasion into a lesser form of mandatory deference.

##### III. The judgment returns classification under the corrected rules

**Holding and operative rule:** The Federal Circuit judgment is vacated and the case remanded for independent classification of the imported planners with the persuasive consideration required by Holding II. The remand establishes neither the bound-diary classification nor the residual duty-free classification, and it directs no particular assessment, refund amount, new agency proceeding or rulemaking.

**Authority:** Part III has the seven direct supporters identified in the opinion table, including Kennedy as author. Stone-Zsela and Scalia oppose the open classification remand and would sustain the challenged classification on different grounds.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the appellate judgment cannot stand on an inquiry that wholly excluded the permissible persuasive contribution of Customs' interpretation. The goods are described as ring-bound planners containing calendar and memorandum pages; no missing product fact supplies the reason for return. Nor is there a general entitlement to have a lower court decide every interpretive question first. The complete tariff question is presented, but neither proposed definitive construction commands a majority: independent construction sustaining the category and controlling acceptance of Customs' reasonable construction rest on distinct noncontrolling grounds. The seven-Justice disposition instead requires the corrected judicial inquiry without predetermining its answer.

The arguments remain concrete. Mead contends that removable pages and prospective scheduling are outside bound diaries. Customs answers that rings bind pages and that diaries include dated prospective entries. The reviewing court must decide the statutory meaning, consider the actual agency reasons and implement the resulting classification through ordinary tariff procedures. The disputed tariff meaning is genuinely undecided here for want of a majority construction; assessment calculations following the eventual lawful classification are ordinary implementation, not a second unresolved deference rule.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.: retained for reasonable choices within actual congressionally delegated interpretive authority; its threshold is applied to this Customs mechanism.
- United States v. Haggar Apparel Co.: its actual-delegation and statutory-boundary requirements are applied; its reserved informal-letter issue is resolved for this mechanism, while its regulation and independent-factfinding holdings remain intact.
- Christensen v. Harris County: its distinction between controlling authority and persuasive reasoning is extended to this classification letter without excluding every informal agency interpretation.
- Skidmore v. Swift & Co.: applied as an independent persuasive inquiry, with no predetermined weight or mandatory classification outcome.
- Appalachian Power Co. v. EPA: its operational-legal-consequence reviewability holding and prohibition on interpretive amendment remain distinct; reviewability does not itself establish authority to bind a court's statutory interpretation.

#### Law After Decision

Materially equivalent Customs ruling letters do not command controlling Chevron treatment through this mechanism. Courts must independently interpret the tariff while giving the agency's actual reasons the persuasive consideration they earn. Other genuine delegations, including valid regulations governed by Haggar, retain their own authority. Official endorsement does not collapse the delegation and persuasion questions. Neither ultimate classification of these planners commands a majority; the governing judicial inquiry is settled, and the tariff answer returns for decision under it.

#### Separate Writings

Stone-Zsela joins Parts I and II but dissents from the judgment. He would independently classify the goods under the tariff's ordinary words. A diary may organize prospective appointments and daily affairs, rather than only retrospective narrative. Rings fasten pages into a bound volume despite removability; neither sewing, glue nor permanence is required. Calendar, note and appointment pages satisfy that description, and auxiliary telephone pages do not defeat it. Loose unattached sheets remain different from the assembled imported articles. Customs' dictionary and commercial reasons persuade but do not control. The narrower appellate reading adds restrictions absent from the text. With both the question and undisputed product features before the Court, he would sustain the bound-diary classification and remand only for entry-specific judgment, assessment or refund implementation, without fixing an amount.

Scalia dissents on a different ground. Congress's classification assignment, genuine tariff ambiguity and Customs' publicly confirmed official interpretation warrant controlling reasonable-choice treatment in his view. The Solicitor General's brief cosigned by Treasury's General Counsel establishes the official-position condition; the statutory scheme supplies delegation, not those signatures alone. Ring binding and dated calendar and note pages make the classification reasonable despite the residual category. He objects to the Court's less determinate delegation threshold and its substitution of open persuasive review, while declining to treat every employee opinion as authoritative. He would sustain Customs' classification and leave only ordinary assessment implementation. His controlling-deference ground does not join Stone-Zsela's independent textual construction or either Court deference proposition.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Federal Circuit must reconsider the tariff classification through independent statutory construction that evaluates the actual persuasive force of Customs' explanation. Ordinary further proceedings follow within the applicable review structure; no new agency hearing, missing-signature search or future rulemaking is a condition of return. Any resulting assessment or refund depends on the lawful classification and the relevant entries and calculations. No monetary amount is awarded here.
