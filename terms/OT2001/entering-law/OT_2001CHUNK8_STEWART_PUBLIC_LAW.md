## Event

Stewart v. Smith, No. 01-339. Merits decision, June 28, 2002; October Term 2001, chunk 8. On review of the Ninth Circuit's judgment, 241 F.3d 1191, reversing the federal District Court's procedural-default ruling. The state director seeks reversal; Smith seeks federal consideration of his sentencing ineffective-assistance claim. The question is whether Arizona's waiver ruling is independent of federal law when the necessary form of waiver depends on the kind of constitutional right asserted.

Smith was convicted in Arizona in 1982 of murder, kidnapping and sexual assault. He received death on the murder count and consecutive prison terms on the others. His 1995 third Rule 32 petition raised a sentencing ineffective-assistance claim omitted from two earlier petitions. The state court applied Rule 32.2(a)(3) and rejected the explanation that successive counsel's affiliation with the same public defender's office prevented presentation. The federal District Court treated the state ruling as a bar. The Ninth Circuit accepted adequacy but held the ruling nonindependent. The Arizona Supreme Court's subsequent authoritative clarification addresses what the rule required for the 1995 petition: the kind of right, not adjudication of its violation, determines whether personal, knowing, voluntary and intelligent waiver is required.

## Holdings

### I. The clarified waiver rule classifies rights without deciding their violation

**Holding and operative rule:** Under Arizona's authoritative construction of Rule 32.2(a)(3) for the 1995 petition, whether a claim requires personal, knowing, voluntary and intelligent waiver depends on the kind of right implicated; rights within that heightened-waiver category are not forfeited by mere omission. Identifying the category does not itself adjudicate the alleged federal violation, although a state procedural determination that actually depends on a federal constitutional ruling is not independent.

**Authority:** Part I of the Court's per curiam opinion; Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer directly join the complete rule and its stated limits.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that identifying a right differs from deciding whether it was violated. The Arizona Supreme Court's answer determines what the state rule required at the time of Smith's third petition. It makes the requisite form of waiver depend on the right at the claim's core, not on whether the particular ineffective-assistance allegation succeeds. The Court accepts that state-law construction without making Arizona's categories federal constitutional merits law or declaring every constitutional claim waivable by omission.

Ake supplies the rule that a state procedural question dependent on a federal constitutional ruling is not an independent ground for direct review. Even assuming the same independence standard governs this habeas question, the clarified classification satisfies it. Coleman preserves the distinct adequate-and-independent-ground requirements. No universal identity between direct-review jurisdiction and habeas default must be decided to resolve this case. Trest and Lambrix confirm that procedural default is nonjurisdictional.

The opposing argument correctly warns that a state label cannot insulate a judgment actually resting on federal merits. That warning requires examination of the particular order, not rejection of every rule that refers to a constitutional category. Delaware v. Prouse likewise distinguishes identification of a right from reliance on a federal ruling. The actual state judgment receives that separate examination in Part II.

### II. The actual state order rests on omission and consideration of an excuse

**Holding and operative rule:** The state order applying Rule 32.2(a)(3) rests on Smith's omission of the sentencing ineffective-assistance claim from his earlier petitions and its rejection of the asserted office-affiliation excuse, rather than on adjudication of trial counsel's constitutional performance. Its colorability reference, read in that context, does not make this procedural ruling dependent on federal merits.

**Authority:** Part II of the unanimous per curiam opinion; all nine Justices directly join the actual-ground determination.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the order's function controls. The state court found the claim waived because Smith had not presented it in the earlier proceedings. It then considered his explanation that appellate and prior postconviction counsel were unwilling to challenge another lawyer from their public defender's office. Its reference to a colorable claim explained its rejection of that asserted institutional obstacle. The court did not first decide whether trial counsel had rendered ineffective assistance at sentencing and use that answer as the predicate of waiver.

Smith's concern about the particular order therefore fails on this record, even though it would be legally material in a genuinely merits-dependent judgment. Carey reinforces examination of an order's actual meaning rather than treating a merits reference as conclusive. This ruling neither finds loyal representation nor decides that every office-affiliation conflict is harmless. Mickens involved a distinct undisclosed victim-counsel appointment and does not make common office membership an automatic conflict or default excuse. Whether a legally available cause theory is otherwise established remains a separate question.

### III. Independence is settled, while genuinely remaining requirements retain their own force

**Holding and operative rule:** The Ninth Circuit's nonindependence ruling is reversed, and the case is expressly remanded for proceedings consistent with the independent character of this state ground. Adequacy was accepted below and is not affirmatively reopened; any properly preserved, legally available cause-and-prejudice or miscarriage-of-justice question retains its own requirements, and a valid, applicable, preserved and unexcused procedural bar must be enforced.

**Authority:** Part III of the unanimous per curiam opinion; all nine Justices join reversal and the bounded express remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that correction of the independence error does not decide every condition for habeas relief. Coleman preserves the separate grounds and exceptions. Lee's exceptional adequacy decision concerns a different application of a state rule and supplies no new challenge to adequacy already accepted here. Trest and Lambrix preserve the actual predicates of default and reject its treatment as a subject-matter-jurisdiction defect.

The Court expresses no opinion whether Smith has established valid cause. Rejecting the asserted significance of office affiliation for independence does not decide every properly presented excuse. Bell preserves genuinely pending investigation and default questions, and Horn confirms that resolution of one habeas condition does not dispose of every other condition needed for relief. Further proceedings must address only matters legally remaining, without an automatic evidentiary hearing or reopening of settled issues. The underlying sentencing ineffective-assistance claim receives no merits determination from this judgment.

## Precedent Treatment

- Stewart v. Smith, 202 Ariz. 446, 46 P.3d 1067 (2002): its authoritative state-law construction governs what Rule 32.2(a)(3) required for the 1995 petition, without deciding the federal violation or every application of waiver.
- Ake v. Oklahoma, 470 U.S. 68: its federal-merits-dependence standard is satisfied even assuming it also governs this habeas independence question; no universal direct-review/habeas identity is adopted.
- Coleman v. Thompson, 501 U.S. 722: its independent and adequate state-ground requirements and cause/prejudice or miscarriage-of-justice exceptions retain separate force.
- Delaware v. Prouse, 440 U.S. 648: its distinction between identifying a right and resting on a federal ruling informs the classification inquiry.
- Trest v. Cain, December 9, 1997: procedural default remains nonjurisdictional and depends on its actual predicates; the decision supplies no automatic hearing or cause finding.
- Lambrix v. Singletary, May 12, 1997: separate collateral-review gates and lawful sequencing are preserved; deciding independence establishes no underlying constitutional entitlement.
- Lee v. Kemna, January 22, 2002: its exceptional adequacy application is distinct and does not reopen an otherwise unavailable adequacy challenge here.
- Carey v. Saffold, June 17, 2002: examination of the actual state order is preserved; a merits reference is not conclusive of the order's procedural meaning.
- Mickens v. Taylor, March 27, 2002: its specific victim-counsel conflict setting supplies no automatic office-affiliation conflict or default excuse in this case.
- Bell v. Cone, May 28, 2002: genuinely pending investigation and default matters retain their distinct requirements; no automatic inquiry follows.
- Horn v. Banks, June 17, 2002: satisfaction of one habeas condition does not eliminate other preserved, applicable conditions for granting relief.

## Law After Decision

Arizona's classification by the type of right does not itself make its waiver ruling dependent on federal merits. Federal courts must still examine the particular state order; this order rests on omission and consideration of an excuse. Independence is resolved without changing the heightened-waiver qualification, default's nonjurisdictional character, the accepted adequacy determination or the independent requirements of any legally remaining excuse and constitutional claim.
