## Standards and Tests selection: Discovery for an indirect racial selective-prosecution claim

### Discovery for an indirect racial selective-prosecution claim

**Governing question:** Discovery of federal charging selection and the relevant Rule 16 scope.

**Current rule:** Rule 16(a)(1)(C)’s material-preparation-of-the-defense branch does not authorize discovery to prepare a selective-prosecution challenge to the decision to prosecute. Before obtaining discovery on an indirect racial-selection theory through any properly established authority, the defendant must offer some credible evidence tending to show discriminatory effect and purpose, including a reliable inference that persons of another race relevantly similarly situated for federal prosecution could have been prosecuted but were not. This is less than a prima facie merits case. Relevant similarity is not identity: grounded statistics, circumstantial comparison and a specifically justified alternative when comparator names cannot reasonably be obtained may support the inference. A selected numerator alone, demographic generalization or hope that discovery creates the missing link does not. A protective order or reduced request scope cannot supply the threshold or original authority.

**Authority by component:**

- Confined Rule 16 nonauthorization: I. Rule 16 does not authorize discovery to prepare a selective-prosecution claim, 1996-05-13; O’Connor Part I with Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg (six).
- Preliminary effect/purpose showing and comparator flexibility: II. The existing showing does not support discovery of discriminatory federal selection, same date; O’Connor Part II with Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer (eight).

**Present operation effective:** Armstrong, 1996-05-13.

**Exceptions and limits:** The rule neither decides every guilt-unrelated affirmative defense nor supplies an alternative discovery source. Rule 16’s intended-use and defendant-origin triggers remain separate; aggregate data, criteria and internal deliberations are not automatically identical categories. No universal naming requirement, fixed comparator checklist or transfer to police-stop discovery follows. Whether direct admissions of discriminatory purpose dispense with comparator proof remains open.

**Holdings navigation:** United States v. Armstrong, 517 U.S. 456.

## Standards and Tests selection: Sequencing nonretroactivity and procedural default on habeas review

### Sequencing nonretroactivity and procedural default on habeas review

**Governing question:** The order of two nonjurisdictional habeas defenses.

**Current rule:** Teague nonretroactivity and state procedural default are distinct nonjurisdictional limits. No inflexible priority requires either always to precede the other; an accepted Teague question may be resolved while disputed default predicates remain reserved. This does not excuse default, permit bypass of an established jurisdictional defect or decide constitutional merits. An appellate court has no mandatory duty to raise an omitted state procedural-default defense; the defense is not a subject-matter-jurisdiction defect.

**Authority by component:**

- Lambrix v. Singletary — I. The Court may decide the accepted nonretroactivity issue before the unresolved default defense, 1997-05-12; Stevens with Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; seven

- An appellate court has no mandatory duty to raise an omitted state procedural-default defense: Trest v. Cain, 1997-12-09; Stevens for all nine Justices.

**Present operation effective:** Trest, 1997-12-09, for the no-duty rule; Lambrix, 1997-05-12, retains the sequencing rule.

**Exceptions and limits:** The specific old-rule conclusion for the influential capital recommendation at November 24, 1986 finality is an application, not a universal exception to Teague. Preserved adequate, independent and unexcused state grounds remain enforceable.

**Holdings navigation:** Lambrix v. Singletary (1997-05-12) — The Court may decide the accepted nonretroactivity issue before the unresolved default defense. Trest v. Cain — Habeas procedural default and appellate initiative.

**Open question:** Trest leaves judicial authority to raise an omitted default, the proper exercise of any discretion, intentional waiver or forfeiture, actual default and applicable exceptions unresolved. Any lawful later consideration must address the actual ground with an appropriate opportunity to respond; no universal extra-briefing requirement is imposed.

##### Rule 16 authority for selective-prosecution discovery

**Holding:** Rule 16(a)(1)(C)'s defense-preparation materiality provision does not authorize discovery to prepare this selective-prosecution challenge to the decision to prosecute. The holding concerns that claim alone, rather than all affirmative defenses unrelated to guilt or every possible source of constitutionally warranted inquiry.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (O'Connor), Part I, joined by Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg; six. Stevens's separate agreement that the Rule supplies no entitlement is not a join in the Court's reasoning.

**Material application:** The other disjunctive triggers—Government intent to use the material in its evidence in chief and acquisition from or ownership by the defendant—are not established. Protective and sanction powers regulate an existing duty rather than create it. No established entitlement under Rule 17(c), *Brady*, or compulsory process supplies the missing source on this showing. The Court makes no waiver finding and does not attribute its construction to the Government or lower courts.

##### Preliminary showing of discriminatory selection

**Holding:** Discovery on this indirect racial-selection theory requires some credible evidence tending to show discriminatory effect and purpose, including a reliable basis to infer that relevantly similarly situated persons of another race eligible for federal prosecution could have been prosecuted but were not. This is less than a prima facie merits case. **Proposition-level authority:** Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; eight. The shared rule assumes discovery may be available on an appropriate showing without establishing another source of authority.

Government proffers identify gaps without establishing neutrality or disproving the sample's bounded count. Insufficiency does not prove comparators nonexistent. Access difficulty is real; discovery may complete a grounded inference but cannot create its missing connection solely from hope.

**Holding:** The unsupported discovery order and dismissal for refusing it cannot stand. # Brown v. City of Oneonta; 221 F.3d 329 (2d Cir. 2000); no Supreme Court docket supplied — 2001-11-13

[Authoritative Record](../records/brown-v-city-of-oneonta_merits_2001-11-13.md)

## Event

Brown v. City of Oneonta. Decided November 13, 2001. On review of the Second Circuit's amended judgment, 221 F.3d 329. The question is whether deliberate investigative selection by race escapes racial-classification review when the racial feature comes from a victim's description and additional hostility is not shown, and what constitutional standard governs that selection.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the affected equal-protection dismissal and remands.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed in part and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The reversal reaches the rejection of the equal-protection claim insofar as it rests on a categorical victim-description exemption. The affected claim is restored for application of the governing racial-classification standard to the actual selection, scope and burdens. Independently resolved claims, existing Fourth Amendment dispositions and the earlier qualified-immunity judgment protecting university officials for release of the list remain undisturbed.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | O'Connor | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports the judgment | Entire opinion |

## Holdings

**Deliberate racial selection requires justification.** When officials intentionally select people for investigation by race, they use a racial classification even if a victim supplied that feature and no hostility is shown. The government must establish an important lawful investigative objective and a substantial, evidence-supported relationship between that objective and the actual selection, its coverage and burdens, including a supported response to concrete practicable alternatives offering comparable investigative value with materially less racial disadvantage.

**Authority:** O'Connor's opinion for all nine Justices controls the classification rule, its standard and the bounded remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the source of identifying information can justify an official racial choice; it does not erase that choice. Adarand Constructors, Inc. v. Peña supplies the common justification standard; Engineering Contractors and City of Dallas require examination of the evidence connecting the objective to the actual measure and burden. Solving the reported violent attack is an important lawful objective. A concrete description linking race to age, sex, an injured hand, location and timing may support appropriately limited measures. The Court therefore rejects the contention that classification review requires police to ignore useful descriptions. It also rejects a categorical permission to investigate a racial community without examining the description's other features and the practical scope of the operation. The wider sweep and alleged encounter with a woman require that examination. Testimony, investigative records and reasonable inferences can establish fit; no commissioned study or universal least-restrictive-means requirement follows. Consensual questioning and a Fourth Amendment seizure are distinct, but consent alone does not remove an intentionally racial burden from equal-protection review. Membership in a described group does not establish an individual encounter, injury or defendant's responsibility.

**Precedent treatment:** Peña's common standard is extended to purposeful investigative selection; its evidentiary and remedial limits remain.

**Remand rule.** The lower courts must examine the actual selection criteria, scope and burdens under that standard, and address personal responsibility, coercion where relevant, immunity and relief through their distinct governing rules. Immunity remains measured against the law at the September 1992 conduct; this decision does not retrospectively supply clearly established notice.

**Authority and explanation:** The same unanimous opinion controls. Lesage separates unequal-process injury from consequential loss and appropriate relief. The categorical exemption prevented the necessary examination; removing it restores the affected claim without reopening independently sustained dispositions.

## Precedent Treatment

- Adarand Constructors, Inc. v. Peña (June 12, 1995): extended to deliberate investigative selection; the common substantial-justification standard governs rather than strict scrutiny.
- Engineering Contractors (1998) and City of Dallas (1999): applied for evidence-supported fit, actual burdens and concrete practicable alternatives.
- Eisenberg (May 30, 2000): its acknowledgment of the binding common standard is preserved; its distinct educational component is not extended to criminal investigation.
- Lesage (November 29, 1999): applied to keep the classification inquiry separate from individual causation, immunity and consequential relief.

## Law After Decision

Purposeful investigative selection by race is subject to the common substantial-justification standard. Victim-supplied identifying information may justify fitted measures, but its source and the absence of hostility create no categorical exemption. The rule governs the classification and its justification; individual liability and remedies remain subject to their existing requirements.

## Separate Writings

No separate opinion.

## Procedure After Action

The affected equal-protection claim returns for proceedings under the stated rule. Unchallenged and independently sustained dispositions retain their force.

## Source Notes

U.S. Const., Amdt. 14; Brown v. City of Oneonta, 221 F.3d 329 (2d Cir. 2000).

# Lee v. Kemna; No. 00-6933 — 2002-01-22

[Authoritative Record](../records/lee-v-kemna_merits_2002-01-22.md)

## Event

Lee v. Kemna; No. 00-6933. Merits decision, January 22, 2002; argued October 29, 2001. Review of the Eighth Circuit's affirmance of denial of federal habeas relief after Missouri courts rejected the continuance claim. The question is whether the state's written-motion and absent-witness requirements supply an adequate procedural ground when subpoenaed alibi witnesses unexpectedly disappear during trial and counsel orally requests a short delay.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The Court vacates the judgment resting on this procedural-default ground and remands for the remaining habeas inquiry.

## Judgment & Remedy

Vacated and remanded, 6-3. **Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. **Opposing:** Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

The federal courts must consider the due-process continuance claim under the remaining applicable habeas rules, with this inadequate state-ground barrier removed. The conviction remains effective pending lawful further proceedings. The separate cause and innocence gateway rulings are not affirmatively reversed by this judgment.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Souter | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg, Breyer | Vacates and remands | Entire exceptional-inadequacy rule and bounded habeas remand |
| Dissent | Kennedy | Scalia, Thomas | Would affirm the default judgment | Rule 24.10's continuing substantive information function |

## Holdings

### I. This exceptional application of the continuance rules is inadequate to bar federal review

**Holding and operative rule.** A normally valid state procedural rule does not bar federal review when its application is exorbitant in the particular circumstances. Here the combined unexpected disappearance of subpoenaed witnesses who had attended trial, substantial prior presentation of the alibi and anticipated return, brief requested delay, and the trial judge's stated scheduling impediments make the additional written and repeated showings inadequate as a federal-review bar; the holding creates no general oral-motion or emergency exemption.

**Authority.** Souter's opinion speaks for Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, six Justices, on the complete rule and application.

**The distinct state requirements.** Missouri Rule 24.09 requires a written motion and affidavit unless the adverse party consents to an oral application. Rule 24.10 separately requires materiality and diligence; the witness's identity and residence if known, or diligence to learn them; reasonable grounds to expect attendance or testimony within a reasonable time; the particular expected facts and absence of another available person who could prove them as fully; and absence not procured, consented to or contrived by the applicant, with good faith rather than vexation or delay. If an affidavit is considered insufficient, the court must permit amendment. The oral-application exception does not erase these information requirements, and opponent consent is not inferred from silence.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that adequacy depends on the rule's operation as well as its ordinary legitimacy. Coleman preserves genuinely independent and adequate state grounds. Osborne distinguishes useful presentation from pointless repetition after a matter has been adequately brought before the judge. Ford protects fair notice and regular application, but neither regularity nor the absence of an identical earlier case conclusively answers this exceptional application. The Court respects Missouri's construction of its rules and decides the separate federal question.

The State has legitimate interests in current information about diligence, materiality and likely availability, as the dissent stresses. Here subpoenas, earlier attendance, prior alibi descriptions and counsel's explanation of the expected local presence supplied the substantial practical basis for the short request. The emergency made a contemporaneous sworn explanation of the unexplained disappearance unavailable. The trial judge's stated scheduling impediments would not have been cured by a perfectly written motion. Those facts work together: earlier testimony descriptions alone, a scheduling remark alone or an unexpected absence alone would not establish the same result. The holding neither declares literal compliance nor requires trial judges to announce every procedural defect. It holds that the additional formal demand, in this particular setting, does not adequately justify withholding federal consideration.

### II. Removal of the default barrier returns the distinct constitutional claim

**Holding and operative rule.** With this state ground inadequate, Lee need not additionally establish cause and prejudice to overcome that ground; the federal court must adjudicate his continuance claim under due process and any independently applicable habeas restriction. The state appellate court's procedural ruling does not become an adjudication of the federal claim's merits under 28 U.S.C. §2254(d) merely because it resolved the appeal.

**Authority.** The same six Justices join the bounded remand and its separation of adequacy from entitlement to relief.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that access to federal consideration and a right to relief are different inquiries. Coleman concerns excuses for an adequate default; it does not require such an excuse after the ground has been found inadequate. Trest confirms that default is a defense with actual predicates rather than a subject-matter-jurisdiction defect. The underlying due-process inquiry asks whether denial of the delay arbitrarily deprived Lee of a meaningful opportunity to present his defense in the actual trial circumstances. It requires examination of the missing testimony's disputed significance under the applicable habeas law. The relatives' account that an officer directed them to leave remains an allegation, and their anticipated alibi is not a finding. The separate gateway rulings are not reversed by eliminating this inadequate ground. The constitutional merits and entitlement to relief remain for adjudication because the federal courts did not reach them.

## Precedent Treatment

- Coleman v. Thompson, 501 U.S. 722: applies its requirement of an independent and adequate state ground and preserves exceptions for genuinely adequate defaults; the present inadequacy ruling precedes those excuses.
- Osborne v. Ohio, 495 U.S. 103: applies its distinction between useful presentation and purposeless repetition in the exceptional circumstances here; it supplies no general waiver of continuance requirements.
- Ford v. Georgia, 498 U.S. 411: preserves fair notice and regular application as adequacy safeguards without making them conclusive in every application.
- Trest v. Cain (December 9, 1997): applies its distinction between procedural default and jurisdiction; it supplies neither an automatic hearing entitlement nor the underlying due-process violation.
- O'Sullivan v. Boerckel (June 7, 1999): preserves ordinary exhaustion through a state's normal review opportunities; the present adequacy holding does not dispense with otherwise applicable habeas requirements.

## Law After Decision

The adequate-state-ground doctrine now expressly reaches this exceptional application of Missouri's continuance requirements. Useful, regularly applied procedural requirements remain enforceable, but their ordinary legitimacy does not make an exorbitant application adequate. Removal of this particular default barrier leaves the due-process merits, remaining lawful habeas restrictions and entitlement to relief for their own adjudication.

## Separate Writings

Kennedy, joined by Scalia and Thomas, would affirm because Rule 24.10 retained a substantive function apart from the written-motion form. Earlier alibi descriptions and earlier attendance did not necessarily establish what the witnesses would then say, whether they could be obtained within a reasonable time, or the current diligence and other facts needed to evaluate the delay. A focused contemporaneous presentation also creates a reviewable record. A known procedural ground may support appellate affirmance even when the trial judge relied on scheduling; an uncited requirement is not thereby useless. Osborne involved repetition of a legal objection already foreclosed, not the initial support for a factual request. The dissent objects that searching earlier portions of the record for substantial compliance can displace the State's actual presentation rule. It does not hold that every formally regular rule is adequate in every application, and relies on no finding about why the witnesses left or whether their alibi was true.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns through the Eighth Circuit for adjudication of the continuance claim and remaining applicable habeas questions with this default ground removed. The conviction remains in force pending lawful further proceedings.

## Source Notes

Mo. Rules Crim. Proc. 24.09 and 24.10; Lee v. Kemna, 213 F.3d 1037; 28 U.S.C. §2254. The reason for the witnesses' departure, the truth of the proposed alibi and the constitutional effect of refusing the delay remain disputed rather than established by the adequacy judgment.

# Mickens v. Taylor; No. 00-9285 — 2002-03-27

[Authoritative Record](../records/mickens_v_taylor_merits_2002-03-27.md)

## Event

Mickens v. Taylor, No. 00-9285. Argued November 5, 2001; decided March 27, 2002. On writ of certiorari to the en banc Fourth Circuit, reviewing denial of federal habeas relief. The appointed lawyer had recently represented the murder victim, and the appointing judge knew the relationship but did not inquire or disclose it. The question is whether that omitted inquiry dispenses with a showing that the conflict adversely affected representation. A federal evidentiary hearing resulted in a finding against adverse effect; the separate factual challenge to that finding is outside the granted question.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The denial of habeas relief on the reviewed conflict claim is affirmed.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

**Opposing:** Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, who would reverse and obtain a new trial on the separate grounds stated below.

The conviction and sentence remain undisturbed on this claim. The Court orders no new trial or additional conflict hearing solely because the judge failed to inquire.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Kennedy | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas | Adverse-effect requirement, grant limits and affirmance in full |
| Dissent | Stevens | None | Disclosure, informed acceptance and exceptional protective-duty ground; new trial |
| Dissent | Souter | None | Prospective judicial-duty ground; new trial; no actual-conflict-only Wood remand |
| Dissent | Breyer | Ginsburg | Narrow State-created structural ground; new trial; general inquiry framework reserved |

## Holdings

### An omitted inquiry does not itself establish the adverse effect necessary here

**Holding and operative rule:** Outside the established circumstances requiring automatic relief, a trial court's failure to inquire into a particular potential conflict it knew or reasonably should have known about does not itself establish a Sixth Amendment violation. A defendant invoking the conflict-based relaxation of ordinary outcome prejudice must at least show that competing obligations adversely affected counsel's performance; that showing is different from proving a reasonable probability of a different verdict under Strickland.

**Authority:** Justice Kennedy's Opinion of the Court is joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas. Those five directly adopt the rule, the confined application and affirmance. The four dissenters support a new trial on distinct grounds and do not establish a common alternative holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the right protects loyal, effective representation rather than imposing automatic reversal as a sanction for a judge's omitted procedure. Holloway requires automatic relief when counsel makes an adequate timely conflict objection to joint representation and the court compels continuation without the required inquiry or protection. That distinct objection-and-compulsion predicate is absent here. Sullivan requires an actual conflict adversely affecting performance before ordinary outcome prejudice is dispensed with. Wood's actual-conflict inquiry does not create a separate rule awarding relief for divided obligations without an effect on representation in this setting. Cronic's exceptional denial-of-assistance principle does not convert this omitted inquiry into a complete loss of counsel.

The contrary positions emphasize that the State selected the murder victim's recent lawyer, the judge knew the particular risk and Mickens lacked the information needed to object. The Court accepts the seriousness of that combination and the duty to inquire. Swidler preserves confidentiality after death, so death does not eliminate the potential conflict. But neither continuing duties nor judicial silence establishes that counsel withheld a defense, altered a choice or otherwise performed differently. The appointment's gravity alone does not establish the structural denial or representation injury needed for the proposed categorical remedy.

The federal court conducted an evidentiary hearing and found no adverse effect. The granted question asks whether that requirement can be dispensed with; it does not reopen the separate factual challenge to the finding. Even assuming Sullivan's relaxed-prejudice route applies to this successive representation, the existing determination defeats this claim. The Court does not decide its application to every category of successive representation, which lies beyond the reviewed question. Holloway retains its established rule, and no ordinary outcome-prejudice burden is added after the predicates of an applicable conflict exception are satisfied.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Holloway v. Arkansas, 435 U.S. 475:** Preserved for compelled joint representation over an adequate timely objection; its automatic remedy is not extended solely because the judge knew a potential conflict.
- **Cuyler v. Sullivan, 446 U.S. 335:** Applied on the assumption favorable to Mickens that its relaxed-prejudice route is available; an actual conflict adversely affecting performance remains necessary here, without additional ordinary outcome prejudice.
- **Wood v. Georgia, 450 U.S. 261:** Its inquiry obligation and actual-conflict requirement do not establish the proposed separate exemption from adverse effect in this setting.
- **Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668:** Ordinary outcome prejudice remains distinct from the adverse-effect requirement under an applicable conflict exception.
- **United States v. Cronic, 466 U.S. 648:** Its exceptional presumed-prejudice circumstances are not established merely by this unperformed inquiry.
- **Swidler & Berlin v. United States:** Post-death confidentiality remains; its survival proves neither a particular confidence nor impairment of this defense.
- **Both Williams v. Taylor decisions:** Constitutional entitlement, concealed-fact cause, factual development and state-merits review remain distinct; the federal hearing does not supply a state merits adjudication.

## Law After Decision

A known potential conflict and the judge's neglected inquiry do not themselves establish the adverse effect required for this conflict-based exception to ordinary prejudice. Holloway retains its distinct automatic-relief rule. The Court assumes Sullivan's route is available here without deciding its application to every successive representation. The existing no-adverse-effect determination defeats this claim within the granted question.

## Separate Writings

**Stevens, dissenting.** Stevens would reverse the habeas denial and secure a new trial because the State appointed the murder victim's recent lawyer in a capital case without disclosure or the accused's informed acceptance, despite the judge's knowledge. The right to a meaningful lawyer-client relationship, counsel's disclosure obligation and the court's protective duty operate together. This exceptional State-created arrangement warrants presumed impairment under Cronic; Holloway, Sullivan and Wood supply the conflict safeguards, while Swidler preserves continuing confidentiality without proving any particular confidence. His rule concerns this undisclosed capital victim-counsel appointment, not every former-client relationship or ethical breach. He does not require another actual-conflict hearing before recognizing the constitutional entitlement.

**Souter, dissenting.** Souter would reverse and require a new trial under the court's prospective duty to address a sufficiently identified risk before representation unfolds. When the judge already has adequate notice, Holloway's preventive protection should not depend solely on an objection from a potentially conflicted lawyer. The judge must inquire, secure an intelligent waiver or arrange replacement when warranted. Souter distinguishes that prospective duty from later discovery: he reads Wood's retrospective inquiry as requiring actual conflict and adverse effect, rather than supporting an actual-conflict-only remand here. His discussion of possible adverse effects supplies no decision of the factual issue outside the grant and no direction for another hearing.

**Breyer, dissenting, joined by Ginsburg.** These Justices would reverse and secure a new trial on a narrower structural ground. The State supplied the recent criminal lawyer of the murder victim to defend the accused in a capital case; the immediate incompatibility, the capital proceeding's use of the victim's character and the State's creation of the arrangement produce a breakdown in the trial framework. Cronic and Fulminante support relief without reconstructing individual performance effects at that conjunction of facts. Young v. United States ex rel. Vuitton reinforces the concern for institutional fairness. They expressly leave the general relationship among Holloway, Sullivan and Wood unresolved and do not join Souter's general known-risk rule or an actual-conflict-only remand.

Each dissent seeks the new-trial remedy through ordinary conditional habeas relief, permitting the State to retry with constitutionally adequate representation. None orders immediate unconditional release, acquittal or a new sentence alone. Their constitutional positions do not adjudicate an independently presented collateral defense on predicates absent from the question decided. Counsel maintained the narrower actual-conflict theory; the dissenters give their own legal answers to the granted conflict question and the requested new-trial remedy.

# Bell v. Cone; No. 01-400; 535 U.S. 685. — 2002-05-28

[Authoritative Record](../records/Bell_v_Cone_Merits_2002-05-28.md)

## Event

Bell v. Cone, No. 01-400, 535 U.S. 685. Merits decision, May 28, 2002. Argued March 25, 2002. On writ of certiorari to the Sixth Circuit.

The questions concern presumed prejudice from counsel's failure to present new mitigation witnesses and waiver of closing at capital sentencing, and the availability of federal habeas relief from the state ineffective-assistance adjudication. The Sixth Circuit affirmed denial of conviction relief but granted a sentence-only conditional writ requiring a new penalty proceeding within 180 days after remand. It left other sentencing grounds unaddressed. The federal application was filed July 1, 1997; the challenged state merits adjudication was entered September 19, 1986, followed by appellate affirmance on November 4, 1987 and denial of permission to appeal by the Tennessee Supreme Court on March 14, 1988.

## Participation

## Public Action

## Judgment & Remedy

The Sixth Circuit's Cronic-based grant of sentence relief is reversed and the case remanded, 8–1. Supporting: Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Opposing: Stevens.

The remaining, independently pending sentencing grounds return for ordinary adjudication, including the distinct investigation/development default questions to the extent legally available. The convictions are unaffected. Reversal removes the conditional sentence writ on this ground; it does not restore the district court's entire sentencing disposition or resolve the remaining appeal.

## Holdings

### I. The statutory collateral standard and ordinary prejudice requirement

**Controlling proposition:** The July 1, 1997 federal application is governed by amended §2254. Clearly established constitutional law is measured at the relevant state merits adjudication; a state application must be objectively unreasonable, not merely incorrect, and ordinary Strickland prejudice requires a reasonable probability of a different result without an additional fundamental-unfairness or unreliability requirement for lawful mitigation.

**Authority:** Part I of Breyer's opinion is joined by all nine Justices at this framework level. Stevens does not join the Court's Cronic application or its final rejection of the ordinary tactical claim.

**Controlling explanation:** Lindh makes the application date decisive for the amendment's applicability. Strickland and Cronic existed when the State adjudicated the claim; Williams supplies the Court's present construction of the statutory review standard. Its contrary-to and unreasonable-application inquiries remain distinct, and its standard is not satisfied or defeated merely by pointing to judicial disagreement. The district court's added Fretwell requirement cannot govern lawful mitigating evidence. That error does not itself establish entitlement to the writ. A separately sufficient, correctly assessed performance ground can defeat the confined claim without deciding actual prejudice. Later opinions cannot create an April 1982 professional duty or constitutional law unavailable at the state merits date.

**Precedent treatment:** Lindh v. Murphy, June 23, 1997, supplies the application-date distinction. Williams v. Taylor (Terry Williams), 529 U.S. 362 (2000), supplies objective statutory review and rejection of an extra Fretwell hurdle for lawful mitigation. Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668 (1984), retains its separate performance and ordinary-prejudice requirements.

### II. These omissions within actual advocacy do not trigger Cronic's presumption

**Controlling proposition:** Counsel's failure to call additional mitigation witnesses and waiver of closing do not amount to a complete failure of adversarial testing in this sentencing proceeding, where counsel substantively presented mitigation already in evidence, sought mercy, obtained favorable cross-examination and resisted damaging prosecution proof. The challenged choices require Strickland analysis; the conclusion neither excuses abandonment nor eliminates other recognized circumstances in which Cronic presumes prejudice.

**Authority:** Parts II–IV of Breyer's opinion are joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, including the author in that eight-Justice coalition. Stevens dissents from these applications and the judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** Cronic addresses a complete loss of the relevant adversarial function, not every failure within representation. Counsel's opening directed the same jury to the guilt-stage experts and mitigating circumstances, invoked military service and remorse, and requested mercy. Cross-examination elicited the Bronze Star and honorable discharge, and objections excluded damaging prosecution material. These acts supplied adversarial sentencing assistance rather than physical presence alone. Cone argues that guilt-stage insanity evidence did not become a sufficient individualized case for life after conviction, and that the isolated acts left the decisive prosecution case unanswered. Those objections concern the quality and adequacy of the assistance actually given. They do not establish the complete failure on which this claimed presumption depends. No numerical tally of acts or universal rule that any activity defeats every Cronic category follows.

**Precedent treatment:** United States v. Cronic, 466 U.S. 648 (1984), is applied to the claimed complete-failure category; its other recognized grounds for presumed prejudice remain. Strickland governs the particular alleged errors within this represented proceeding.

### III. The confined tactical claim fails on a sufficient performance ground

**Controlling proposition:** On the reviewed available-mitigation and closing-argument record, the state court's rejection of deficient performance was not an objectively unreasonable application of Strickland. That sufficient ground defeats this confined claim without an actual-prejudice determination; it does not decide distinct allegations of failure to investigate or develop mitigation, their default treatment, or every remaining sentencing ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The ordinary claim was addressed below and is fairly included in review of the state rejection. Concrete reasons support its disposition: credited reliance on experts whose testimony remained before the jury, counsel's assessment of his mother's ineffective appearance, interviews of other potential witnesses, the reviewed record's lack of a showing that omitted testimony would help, and avoiding the more effective prosecutor's unanswered rebuttal. Neither a strategy label nor the district court's statement that strategy was unreviewable supplies the rule.

Cozzolino limited punishment proof to statutory aggravation or mitigation actually raised; rebuttal must explain or controvert a proposition advanced. Its troubled-childhood evidence did not make present criminal proclivities relevant. Later defense proof can make premature admission harmless only by supplying actual rebuttal relevance. Thus a general fear of all bad-acts questioning would be mistaken, but no rule immunized every possible character statement. The Court makes no new witness-specific admissibility finding.

Pritchett provided pretrial support for resentencing when one of two aggravators failed and the remaining weighing could not be assumed. It guaranteed no new hearing whenever any aggravator failed. Cone's 1984 direct appeal later distinguished the surviving aggravators and applied harmless error; that later ruling is not counsel's April 1982 knowledge. Counsel's testimony supplied multiple reasons for waiving closing, including existing mitigation and avoiding the particular rebuttal. The Court endorses no general practice of deliberately creating appellate error.

This independent state-performance assessment does not depend on the district court's erroneous additional prejudice requirement. The restriction of the reviewed record to available evidence makes preservation of separate development and default questions essential; broad praise of counsel's preparation does not decide them.

**Precedent treatment:** Williams and Strickland are applied to an independently sufficient, context-specific performance ground. Cozzolino v. State, 584 S.W.2d 765 (Tenn. 1979), and State v. Pritchett, 621 S.W.2d 127 (Tenn. 1981), retain their actual relevance and aggravator limits; Cone v. State, 665 S.W.2d 87 (Tenn. 1984), remains separately dated.

### IV. The remaining sentence matters require their own adjudication

**Controlling proposition:** The Cronic-based sentence writ is reversed and the confined ordinary tactical claim rejected; the Court of Appeals must adjudicate the remaining properly pending sentence claims and distinct investigation/default issues under their own legal requirements. The convictions remain undisturbed, and no blanket reinstatement of the district court's entire sentencing judgment follows.

**Controlling explanation:** The Sixth Circuit expressly left other sentencing challenges unaddressed. The separate investigation allegations raise disputed questions about the adequacy and independence of the second-petition bar and asserted cause from unavailable investigative funding. Cone's remand argument presents those issues but establishes neither cause nor invalidity of the state ground. Lee concerns a different state procedural application and does not resolve Tennessee's. Ordinary further adjudication determines any necessary proceedings; the mandate does not itself direct an evidentiary hearing or funding.

**Precedent treatment:** Lee v. Kemna, January 22, 2002, retains its exceptional state-ground holding and is not extended into a determination of these separate default issues.

Cronic's complete-failure doctrine remains distinct from Strickland's particular-error inquiry. Williams's objective unreasonable-application rule and ordinary lawful-mitigation prejudice rule govern; the district court's additional Fretwell burden is rejected. Lindh determines which statutory review regime applies. Cozzolino and Pritchett retain their specific Tennessee limits, with the later Cone ruling kept separate from counsel's trial-date knowledge. ## Holdings

### Timely ordinary state review includes its intervals

**Holding and operative rule:** Under 28 U.S.C. §2244(d)(2), a properly filed state collateral application remains pending during timely continuation through the State's ordinary review process, including the interval before a timely filing at the next available level. California's successive-level original petitions qualify when they perform that review function; an abandoned or completed process, an unreasonable gap or an unrelated later attack does not receive interval exclusion merely because another collateral petition is possible.

**Authority:** Ginsburg's opinion of the Court, joined by the other five members of the six-Justice coalition identified in Opinion Topology; all six adopt both holdings.

**Controlling explanation:** Pending describes a review process awaiting completion, not only a paper physically awaiting one judge's order. A contrary construction would consume the federal period during steps ordinarily needed to complete state review and encourage premature federal filings. California's original-petition terminology does not change that practical function. The dissent correctly insists that a properly filed application, not a merely possible future claim, is required. The Court's rule retains that requirement and confines interval protection to timely continuation of the ordinary course; extraordinary or unrelated filings do not qualify automatically. Artuz distinguishes filing conditions from claim-relief bars and leaves pendency separate. Duncan requires state rather than federal review. Boerckel explains the ordinary state-review round, without making every extraordinary remedy part of it.

**Precedent treatment:** Artuz and Duncan are applied without alteration; Boerckel supplies ordinary-review context. The previously open interval question is resolved at this functional, timely-continuation scope.

### State timeliness and the actual federal calculation remain necessary

**Holding and operative rule:** An alternative merits discussion does not establish timely state filing or override a valid independent state determination that the relevant interval was unreasonable. The Ninth Circuit must determine the California order's actual meaning, the applicable state timing standard, the supported reasons for this interval and the exact elapsed time, then recalculate the federal limitation without excluding any interval that fails the governing requirements.

**Authority:** The same six-Justice Court opinion adopts this rule and bounded remand; Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas also reject a conclusive merits-language inference but do not join the Court's functional-pendency rule or resulting mandate.

**Controlling explanation:** The California Supreme Court referred both to the merits and lack of diligence. Its merits phrase therefore cannot alone resolve whether the four-and-one-half-month interval complied with California law. A clear state determination of untimeliness defeats interval exclusion even if merits were also considered. Where the order's meaning is uncertain, the federal court must identify the actual state ground and relevant timing circumstances; certification or another lawful clarification may be used if needed, but is not automatically required. Neither a fixed nationwide deadline nor an assumption that this interval is necessarily reasonable or necessarily late supplies the answer. Proper filing, pendency and reasonable state timing remain distinct. Artuz does not turn an actual filing deadline into a relief bar. Duncan forecloses counting federal habeas pendency as state review and preserves independently available commencement or equitable grounds only on their own requirements. Unpresented equitable tolling and the conviction's constitutional merits are outside this decision.

**Precedent treatment:** Artuz's filing-deadline-exception reservation remains; Duncan's independent timing rules remain. Lee's adequate-state-ground inquiry and Young's bankruptcy tolling do not determine statutory pendency here.

## Holdings

### A preserved nonretroactivity defense must be resolved before granting relief

**Holding and operative rule:** A federal habeas court may not grant a writ while leaving unresolved a properly preserved, applicable Teague defense capable of defeating relief. Section 2254(d) adds a condition to relief; satisfaction of that condition does not remove nonretroactivity review merely because the state court did not discuss it, and the requirement to justify a grant does not impose an inflexible sequence before every denial.

**Authority:** The Court's per curiam opinion, joined by all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Caspari requires adjudication of the preserved defense. Williams gives §2254(d) its independent inquiry into the state merits decision; Teague instead compares the precise rule sought with law dictated when the conviction became final. A later ruling may articulate an old rule, but that determination must actually be made. Pennsylvania raised the defense in both federal courts. The Third Circuit therefore could not treat its favorable §2254(d) conclusion as a complete basis for sentence relief.

On remand the court must fix finality, identify the rule claimed and determine whether prefinality precedent dictated it. A later decision's date, different facts or judicial disagreement alone does not establish newness. If the rule is new, the court must consider any applicable exception: rules withdrawing conduct or a class of persons or punishments from the State's substantive power, and watershed procedures essential to accuracy and fundamental fairness. Both exceptions remain. The Court decides neither Mills's newness nor either exception's application because the reviewed error is the omission of that analysis.

Schiro preserves the nonjurisdictional character and ordinary preservation limits of the defense; no forfeited defense is raised here. Lambrix permits denial on another independently sufficient ground without an inflexible priority between nonretroactivity and default. Tyler's distinct successive-application requirement is not imported into this case. The instructional merits and other genuinely remaining habeas requirements must be addressed only as necessary after the preserved defense receives its proper legal effect.

**Precedent treatment:** Caspari and Williams are applied to distinct conditions for relief; Schiro and Lambrix retain their preservation and sequencing limits; Tyler's separate statutory gate is distinguished.
