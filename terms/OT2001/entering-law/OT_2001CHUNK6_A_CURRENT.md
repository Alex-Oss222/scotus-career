## Event

Toyota Motor Manufacturing, Kentucky, Inc. v. Williams, No. 00-1089. Merits decision, January 8, 2002. Argued November 7, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Sixth Circuit. The question is whether Williams's limitations established substantial limitation of performing manual tasks under the original ADA, warranting partial summary judgment in her favor. She experienced carpal-tunnel and related limitations in repetitive, gripping and overhead automobile-production tasks; the record also contains evidence of household and self-care function. Review concerns her favorable partial judgment. Toyota's separate request to reinstate its own summary judgment is outside the accepted question.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument or submission and decision.

## Public Action

Set aside Williams's partial summary judgment and remand, 9–0.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Williams's partial summary judgment is set aside and the case remanded, 9–0.** Supporting: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine require reconsideration of the same reviewed judgment. The lower courts apply the Court's manual-task standard, the controlling mitigation rule and ordinary summary-judgment principles. Toyota's separate summary judgment is not reinstated because that request lies outside the accepted question.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Breyer throughout; Stone-Zsela only as to the occupational-shortcut and mitigation holdings | All nine support setting aside the reviewed partial judgment and remand | Eight join the severe-restriction, central-daily-task and permanent-or-long-term standard and its application; all nine join the independent occupational-shortcut and mitigation propositions |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Supports the same set-aside and remand | Joins occupational-shortcut and mitigation holdings; rejects the categorical threshold and proposes individualized important-manual-function standard |

## Holdings

### Manual-task disability requires a substantial limitation of general daily-life function

**Holding and operative rule:** Under original 42 U.S.C. §12102(2)(A), an impairment substantially limits performing manual tasks when it prevents or severely restricts the individual from performing tasks central to most people's daily lives, with permanent or long-term impact. This is an individualized standard requiring considerable functional limitation, not complete incapacity or loss of virtually every manual ability; diagnosis alone is insufficient, and no numerical duration threshold applies.

**Authority:** The Opinion of the Court's manual-task-standard and application portions, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Eight direct joins establish the rule; Stone-Zsela does not join these portions.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that substantial limitation concerns a considerable restriction in the major activity asserted. The ADA's common definition is not confined to employment, so specialized assembly requirements cannot alone define manual-task disability. The original text and contemporaneous guidance concerning nature, severity, expected duration and enduring impact support the stated threshold.

Bragdon requires individual proof and rejects complete inability as an added condition. It does not erase substantiality or prohibit this activity-specific duration requirement. A permanent or long-term functional impact need not mean an incurable diagnosis; the Court sets no numerical minimum. Likewise, identifying central daily-life manual tasks does not restrict every major life activity to something performed each day or displace Bragdon's reproduction holding.

Williams is correct that sustained gripping, reaching and repetition may show important general capacities even if some household tasks remain possible. The standard admits that evidence; it rejects treating a class of demanding occupational tasks as sufficient by category alone. Household and self-care evidence also matters in its actual extent, without creating an automatic defense from the ability to perform some tasks. Ordinary summary-judgment principles govern competing evidence. The Court consults contemporaneous guidance without deciding general binding agency authority.

**Precedent treatment:** Bragdon's individual-proof and no-complete-incapacity principles remain; Sutton and Murphy's distinct occupational-breadth inquiry is not transferred to every activity; Kirkingburg supplies no additional disability method.

### Occupational classification is not a substitute for the claimed activity

**Holding and operative rule:** Inability to perform a class of occupational tasks does not by itself establish substantial limitation in the separate major life activity of performing manual tasks. Workplace evidence remains relevant to the extent it proves limitation of the general manual functions at issue; ordinary household and self-care evidence must likewise be assessed without categorical conclusions.

**Authority:** All nine Justices directly join this severable proposition in the Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** Sutton and Murphy's class-of-jobs inquiry addresses working. A court cannot use that inquiry as a shortcut to establish a different activity. The Sixth Circuit's occupational category therefore does not establish Williams's manual-task disability as a matter of law. The relevant functional evidence must be assessed under the rule governing the particular activity, rather than excluded because it arises at work or treated as dispositive because it concerns a class of job tasks.

**Precedent treatment:** Sutton and Murphy's working rules remain confined to their actual activity; individual functional proof remains necessary.

### Beneficial mitigation does not eliminate the underlying limitation

**Holding and operative rule:** The manual-task inquiry assesses the individual's underlying substantial limitation without treating beneficial correction or medication as eliminating it. Individual proof remains required, and coverage remains distinct from qualification, accommodation and safety; the claimant need not stop treatment.

**Authority:** All nine Justices directly join this severable application of Sutton and Murphy in the Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** Sutton and Murphy settle the mitigation baseline under the original ADA. Their rule identifies the functional condition assessed; it does not automatically establish its substantiality. The manual-task threshold applies to that legally defined underlying condition. Successful management does not remove protection by itself, while evidence still must establish this individual's substantial limitation. The Court therefore rejects neither the governing mitigation rule nor the independent need to prove disability.

**Precedent treatment:** Sutton and Murphy are applied unchanged; their underlying-impairment rule controls the remand.

## Precedent Treatment

- Sutton v. United Air Lines, Inc., No. 97-1943, and Murphy v. United Parcel Service, Inc., No. 97-1992: the underlying-impairment rule is applied unchanged; beneficial mitigation does not defeat coverage, and the working-specific class inquiry does not define manual tasks.
- Bragdon v. Abbott, 524 U.S. 624: individualized substantiality and the distinction from complete inability remain; its reproduction and direct-threat holdings are intact.
- Albertson's, Inc. v. Kirkingburg, No. 98-591: its distinct safety-qualification rule remains; it supplies no manual-task or mitigation methodology.

## Law After Decision

The original ADA now requires the stated severe restriction in central daily-life manual tasks and permanent or long-term impact, assessed individually under the existing underlying-impairment mitigation rule. Occupational categories alone do not establish disability; workplace and nonwork functional evidence remain relevant. The lower courts apply those settled rules to the reviewed partial judgment, with other claims retaining their existing procedural status.

## Separate Writings

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela concurs in the occupational-shortcut and mitigation propositions and in setting aside the partial judgment, but rejects a categorical severe-and-permanent threshold. He would assess individualized substantial limitation of important manual functioning through breadth, frequency, duration and practical effect. These are relevant considerations, not separate mandatory elements. Important work-related functions cannot be excluded because they occur at work, and retained household abilities do not automatically defeat coverage. Expected duration informs substantiality without making permanence indispensable. Sutton and Murphy preserve the underlying-impairment inquiry; the difference between working and manual tasks requires correction of the appellate shortcut, not a restriction to only the most pervasive daily-life limitations. His proposed standard still requires proof rather than diagnosis alone, preserves distinct qualification and accommodation issues, and would not grant Toyota automatic judgment. That broader standard is noncontrolling.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns through the Sixth Circuit for reconsideration of Williams's partial judgment under the controlling standard and ordinary procedural rules. The limited grant does not reopen an independently unreviewed judgment or forfeited coverage branch. Qualification, accommodation, safety and other preserved matters retain their separate elements and existing procedural status.

## Source Notes

Original 42 U.S.C. §12102(2)(A); contemporaneous 29 C.F.R. §1630.2(j); Toyota Motor Manufacturing, Kentucky, Inc. v. Williams, No. 00-1089, 534 U.S. 184, for the functional record and scope of review.

# Riley v. St. Luke's Episcopal Hospital — 2002-01-09

[Authoritative Record](../records/riley-v-st-lukes-episcopal-hospital_merits_2002-01-09.md)

## Event

Riley v. St. Luke's Episcopal Hospital, 252 F.3d 749 (5th Cir. 2001) (en banc). Merits decision, January 9, 2002; October Term 2001, chunk 2. On merits review of the Fifth Circuit's en banc judgment reversing dismissal of a False Claims Act action. The questions are whether a nonintervened relator prosecution violates the Take Care Clause and whether the relator must be appointed under the Appointments Clause. The government declined merits intervention and defended the statute on appeal. The health-care-fraud allegations remain allegations. Defendants seek constitutional dismissal; Riley seeks continuation of the action.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated at merits submission and decision: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

## Public Action

The en banc judgment rejecting the Take Care and Appointments Clause challenges is affirmed.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 7–2, on both the Take Care Clause and Appointments Clause components.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Scalia and Thomas.

The Fifth Circuit's reversal of dismissal and remand remain effective. The underlying action proceeds under the statute's actual allocation of party powers and ordinary statutory and merits requirements.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Breyer | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg | Affirms both constitutional rulings and preserves remand | Entire Take Care, Appointments and disposition holdings, including each statutory-control qualification |
| Dissent | Scalia | Thomas | Would reverse both constitutional rulings and bar the nonintervened prosecution | Retained sovereign-enforcement authority, inadequate executive direction, required appointment and bounded governmental-prosecution remedy |

## Holdings

### I. The bounded assigned-claim mechanism satisfies the Take Care Clause

**Holding and operative rule:** A relator may prosecute the False Claims Act's partially assigned federal pecuniary claim after initial governmental declination without violating the Take Care Clause, where the relator has the Act's case-specific claimant role and the government retains the actual intervention, dismissal, settlement, participation, discovery and alternate-remedy powers with the qualifications set out below. The holding rests on that combined structure, not a general authority to transfer executive functions to private persons, and initial declination does not constitute approval of every later litigating act.

**Authority:** Justice Breyer's opinion, joined throughout by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg, supplies seven direct votes for both constitutional holdings and their limits.

**Statutory conditions incorporated in this holding:**

| Provision | Governmental power and controlling qualification |
|---|---|
| Section 3730(b)(1) | Voluntary dismissal requires written consent and reasons from both the court and the Attorney General. |
| Sections 3730(b)(2)–(4) | The complaint and written disclosure of substantially all material evidence and information possessed by the relator must reach the government. The complaint is filed in camera, remains sealed for at least sixty days, and is served on the defendant only when the court orders. The government may elect intervention within sixty days after receiving both the complaint and the material evidence and information; good cause supports extensions of the seal period. Before that period or an extension expires, the government must proceed and conduct the action or notify the court of declination, allowing the relator to conduct it. |
| Section 3730(c)(1) | If the government proceeds, it has primary responsibility and is not bound by the relator's acts; the relator remains a party subject to subsection (c)(2)'s particular limits. |
| Section 3730(c)(2)(A) | The government may dismiss over the relator's objection after notifying the relator of its motion and providing an opportunity for a hearing. The settlement standard in subparagraph (B) is not the dismissal standard. |
| Section 3730(c)(2)(B) | Settlement over objection requires a hearing and the court's determination that the settlement is fair, adequate and reasonable under all the circumstances. Good cause permits an in-camera hearing. |
| Section 3730(c)(2)(C) | Upon the government's showing that unrestricted relator participation would interfere with or unduly delay its prosecution, or would be repetitious, irrelevant or for harassment, the court may in its discretion limit participation, including witness numbers, testimony length or cross-examination. |
| Section 3730(c)(2)(D) | On a defendant's distinct showing that unrestricted participation would be for harassment or cause undue burden or unnecessary expense, the court may limit the relator's participation. |
| Section 3730(c)(3) | After declination, the relator conducts the case. At the government's request it receives all pleadings and deposition transcripts, with transcripts at its expense. The court may allow later government intervention for good cause without limiting the relator's status and rights. |
| Section 3730(c)(4) | Whether or not the government proceeds, an in-camera showing that particular discovery would interfere with a criminal or civil investigation or prosecution arising from the same facts permits a stay of that discovery for no more than sixty days. An extension requires a further in-camera showing of reasonable diligence in the governmental investigation or proceedings and interference from the proposed discovery. This is not an indefinite stay of the whole case for any policy reason. |
| Section 3730(c)(5) | The government may pursue an available alternate remedy, including administrative civil penalties, while preserving the relator's same rights in that proceeding. Findings or conclusions bind parties to the section 3730 action when finally determined on appeal to the appropriate federal court, when the time for such appeal expires, or when judicial review is unavailable. |

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this defined financial assignment and the government's retained protections permit the action. Vermont Agency establishes the relator's assigned financial injury for Article III but expressly leaves Article II unanswered. Early qui tam practice informs the nature of that claimant's interest; neither that practice nor the civil label conclusively resolves executive responsibility.

The dissent correctly identifies a public interest beyond the relator's personal share: litigation may bind federal interests, and the Executive does not select, direct or remove the relator as an ordinary prosecutor. The statute nevertheless gives the government the first opportunity to take over and preserves meaningful later means of protecting the claim, investigation and enforcement interests. The table's conditions are part of those powers. A hearing requirement is not an unrestricted executive veto, and conditioned intervention is not at-will supervision. Those protections must be assessed with the relator's limited proprietary role rather than tested as though Congress had created a general prosecutor.

Morrison supplies a functional inquiry into interference with executive performance; its independent criminal office and supervisory arrangement are not identical to this assigned civil claim or a mandatory checklist for every claimant. The conditions also protect the assignee's existing procedural and financial interests and the court's adjudicative function. The arrangement does not disable the Executive's constitutional responsibility merely because it initially leaves litigation to the relator. No particular governmental dismissal, settlement, discovery or intervention request has been refused here. Whether a court may override a supported executive choice in such a later dispute requires its own record; that absent-facts question does not postpone the present decision about the statute's allocation.

### II. A case-specific relator is not a continuing federal officer

**Holding and operative rule:** A relator enforcing this partially assigned pecuniary claim under the False Claims Act is a private claimant with a defined litigation role, not the occupant of a continuing federal office requiring appointment under the Appointments Clause. The distinction turns on the position and actual functions: it is not established merely by the absence of salary, formal title, appointment or final adjudicative power, and it authorizes no private criminal prosecution, rulemaking or unrestricted sovereign coercive power.

**Authority:** The seven-Justice opinion identified above directly adopts this independent Appointments Clause holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that significant consequences for government interests do not turn every statutory civil claimant into a federal officeholder. Vermont Agency establishes a real financial assignment. The Act permits this claimant to seek a judicial remedy and a contingent personal share; it does not establish an enduring governmental position with continuing jurisdiction and general duties to adjudicate, regulate or prosecute public matters.

Freytag requires attention to significant statutory duties. Landry applies that principle to an actual continuing hearing office and rejects final-decision power as indispensable. Edmond and Hilario examine the combined hierarchy governing actual offices rather than making one factor universally decisive. Those cases do not eliminate the antecedent distinction between an office and a litigant. Morrison also shows why limited duration alone does not disprove officeholding: its independent counsel occupied a governmental office even though its jurisdiction and tenure were limited.

The dissent views litigation of the retained federal claim as governmental prosecution through a role continuing until judgment. That identifies the action's public consequence but does not establish a governmental position independent of the particular assigned claim. The relator's statutory status and party powers persist to enforce that claim, subject to the government controls described above. The office conclusion therefore rests on the entire function and structure, not on relabeling executive authority as private or on denying its importance. The two Article II inquiries are distinct; Article III standing resolves neither by itself.

### III. The rejection of the two constitutional dismissal grounds stands

**Holding and operative rule:** The Fifth Circuit's judgment rejecting the Take Care and Appointments Clause challenges is affirmed, preserving its reversal of dismissal and remand for ordinary adjudication of the false-claims action under the applicable statutory and merits requirements. The government remains a nonintervenor on the merits unless it lawfully exercises its intervention power, and Vermont Agency's sovereign-State defendant limitation remains applicable within its own scope.

**Authority:** The same seven Justices adopt the disposition and its statutory limits.

**Controlling explanation:** Both asserted constitutional grounds for dismissal fail under the assigned-claim structure presented. The allegations of health-care fraud remain allegations for ordinary litigation. The government's appellate defense of the statute does not become merits intervention, and rejection of Article II challenges does not establish statutory coverage of every defendant. Vermont Agency's distinct defendant-coverage rule survives. The appellate remand thus proceeds under the actual allocation of party powers rather than a judicially created governmental takeover.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Vermont Agency of Natural Resources v. United States ex rel. Stevens, 529 U.S. 765:** Reaffirmed for assigned-claim standing and its distinct sovereign-State defendant limit; its express Article II reservation is resolved here for the bounded FCA mechanism, not retroactively attributed to that earlier judgment.
- **Morrison v. Olson, 487 U.S. 654:** Applied for the executive-interference inquiry and the distinction between a limited-duration public office and this claimant; its independent-counsel arrangement is not made a universal control checklist.
- **Freytag v. Commissioner, 501 U.S. 868:** Applied for the actual statutory position and significant-duty inquiry; no salary or final-decision shortcut replaces it.
- **Edmond v. United States, May 19, 1997:** Its actual combined-hierarchy approach to existing offices is preserved; the present threshold inquiry distinguishes a claimant from such an office.
- **United States v. Hilario, March 19, 2001:** Its combined-control and actual-office inquiries remain in force; a judicially appointed United States Attorney is not equated with a private assignee.
- **Landry v. FDIC, June 15, 2001:** Its continuing-office and significant-hearing-duty rule is preserved, including the rejection of final-order authority as indispensable; this civil claimant does not occupy that kind of position.

## Law After Decision

The FCA's bounded assigned-claim mechanism may constitutionally continue after initial governmental declination. Its private claimant does not occupy a continuing federal office, and the actual retained government powers, with their distinct statutory conditions, are sufficient for the arrangement presented. These Article II conclusions are separate from assigned-injury standing and statutory defendant coverage. They authorize no general private executive office and do not decide an actual judicial override of a supported government request absent from this record.

## Separate Writings

**Justice Scalia, dissenting, joined by Justice Thomas.** They would reverse on both Article II grounds and bar this nonintervened prosecution while preserving lawful governmental enforcement. A partial financial assignment does not eliminate the sovereign's retained claim, penalties, settlement interests or exposure to a binding judgment. In their view, initiation and prosecution of that public component exercise executive power through a statutory role continuing until the suit ends. The Executive neither selected this prosecutor nor possesses the ordinary chain of direction that execution of the laws requires.

Scalia's Morrison dissent supplies his executive-control premise; Vermont Agency's assignment-based standing opinion expressly reserves this distinct Article II question. His and Thomas's published Laidlaw concern about private public-law enforcement likewise distinguishes an injured claimant from uncontrolled governmental enforcement. Edmond, Hilario and Landry require attention to actual authority, appointment and supervision rather than final judgment power alone. The relator's financial incentive and title do not remove the significant public function. Early qui tam practice and a court-bound assigned claim are substantial contrary considerations, but they do not cure prosecution of the retained public interest without lawful appointment and constitutionally adequate direction.

The statutory safeguards are real but conditional. Later intervention requires good cause, dismissal requires notice and a hearing opportunity, settlement has its separate judicial standard, and discovery protection depends on its stated showing. These measures do not supply the missing selection and direction. The dissent does not demand presidential at-will removal of every inferior officer or decide every private statutory action. It would return for the procedural consequence of disallowing Riley's nonintervened prosecution, including any lawful governmental election, without invalidating section 3730(a), dismissing government claims with prejudice, presuming intervention or appointment, or treating the entire Act as inseverable.

## Procedure After Action

The Fifth Circuit's reversal of dismissal and remand stand. The action returns for ordinary adjudication of the claims and applicable defenses, subject to the actual statutory powers of the government and the relator's statutory limits. The government's appellate constitutional participation does not alter its nonintervenor merits status.

# EEOC v. Waffle House, Inc. — 2002-01-15

[Authoritative Record](../records/eeoc-v-waffle-house-inc_merits_2002-01-15.md)

## Event

EEOC v. Waffle House, Inc., No. 99-1823; 534 U.S. 279. Merits decision, January 15, 2002; October Term 2001, chunk 2. Argued October 10, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Fourth Circuit. The question is whether Baker's employment arbitration agreement prevents the EEOC from seeking individual relief in its own ADA action. Baker experienced a seizure, was discharged and filed a charge; disability discrimination remains alleged. The Fourth Circuit allowed prospective public relief but barred victim-specific remedies. The agency seeks reversal of that restriction.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

## Public Action

The restriction on victim-specific relief is reversed, and the case is remanded.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Restriction on victim-specific relief reversed and remanded, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Scalia and Thomas, who would vacate the FAA-based restriction and remand for consideration of any properly available independent state-law contract ground.

All nine reject FAA coverage and agree that actual recovery remains subject to statutory predicates and prevention of duplicate satisfaction. The Commission's public enforcement action continues; the majority permits pursuit of individual remedies without the dissenters' preliminary contract-enforceability inquiry.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stevens | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer throughout; Scalia and Thomas as to Parts I and III only | Reverses the categorical relief restriction and remands | Part I: FAA exclusion; Part II: independent agency remedies; Part III: ordinary relief conditions; Part IV: reversal without contract prerequisite |
| Concurrence in part and dissent in part | Thomas | Scalia | Joins rejection of FAA premise and ordinary relief conditions; would vacate and return the additional contract issue | Parts I and III joined; Parts II and IV rejected on derivative-remedy ground |

## Holdings

### Part I. The FAA supplies no enforcement ground for this employment agreement

**Holding and operative rule:** Circuit City's section 1 employment-contract exclusion covers Baker's employment arbitration agreement, so the FAA supplies no enforcement command or federal arbitration-policy ground for restricting this EEOC action or its remedies. The exclusion does not itself determine an independent state-law basis for enforcement between Baker and Waffle House.

**Authority:** Part I of Justice Stevens's opinion is joined by all eight other Justices; nine direct joins control this ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the governing employment exclusion resolves the antecedent FAA question. Circuit City excludes employment contracts within the commerce reach asserted for the Act, including nontransportation employment. Baker's agreement belongs to that category. A general federal preference for arbitration cannot replace statutory coverage. The Fourth Circuit's FAA-based restriction consequently cannot stand. Gilmer concerns a securities-registration agreement and does not supply a contrary answer about this employment contract. Whether another source binds the employee remains distinct from the FAA holding.

### Part II. The Commission's independent action retains its individual-remedy authority

**Holding and operative rule:** An employee's private agreement selecting arbitration does not bind the nonparty EEOC or categorically eliminate victim-specific remedies Congress authorizes in the Commission's own ADA enforcement action. Even if an independent state-law ground makes the promise enforceable against the employee, that fact alone neither makes the Commission a contracting party nor removes those federal remedy categories; this is an independently sufficient ground for rejecting the relief bar.

**Authority:** Part II of Justice Stevens's opinion is joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, seven direct votes. Scalia and Thomas withhold assent to this ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the agency enforces its own statutory cause, rather than an assignment of Baker's claim. Section 12117(a) gives the Commission the enumerated Title VII enforcement powers, procedures and remedies. Section 706(f)(1) authorizes its action after the statutory process; section 706(g) authorizes appropriate individual equitable relief; and section 1981a expressly includes the Commission among plaintiffs who may seek the specified damages. Compensation and reinstatement can advance that public enforcement function.

First Options supplies the actual-assent principle, but the statutory enforcement structure completes the answer. General Telephone recognizes the independent public action including individual relief, and Gilmer preserves an agency enforcement role alongside an individual's arbitration obligation. Baker could commit himself to a forum; he did not undertake a promise for the Commission, which never agreed to arbitrate.

The dissent's strongest objection concerns the beneficiary's valid commitments, not direct compulsion of the agency. It would condition individual recovery on a state-law-contract determination. But the statutory permission for appropriate relief does not create a categorical exception whenever an employee chose an arbitral forum. Enforceability against Baker alone does not diminish the agency's enacted remedies. This conclusion decides the live private-contract argument without resolving the contract's state-law enforceability or a hypothetical FAA-covered agreement. An actual adjudication, settlement, release or satisfaction differs from the forum promise presented here and requires its own record.

### Part III. Statutory conditions govern any actual award

**Holding and operative rule:** The EEOC's authority to seek relief does not establish liability or an entitlement to an award: the applicable disability, qualification, unlawful-discrimination, injury, causation and remedy predicates must be proved, and the court must apply each remedy's conditions below. The court must prevent duplicate satisfaction of the same injury when actual overlapping recovery is established; no completed arbitration, settlement, release or prior satisfaction is established here.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join Part III's ordinary statutory and nonduplication requirements. Scalia and Thomas separately advocate the additional contractual limitation rejected by Part II; their Part III join does not adopt Part II.

**Statutory conditions incorporated in this holding:**

| Remedy provision | Operative conditions and limits |
|---|---|
| Section 706(g)(1) | On the prescribed finding of an intentional unlawful employment practice, the court may enjoin it and order appropriate affirmative action, including hiring, reinstatement, back pay and other equitable relief. Back pay may not accrue earlier than two years before the charge and is reduced by interim earnings or amounts earnable with reasonable diligence. |
| Section 706(g)(2)(A) | Orders requiring union admission or reinstatement, employee hiring, reinstatement or promotion, or back pay are unavailable when the challenged refusal, suspension, expulsion or discharge rested on a reason other than the prohibited discrimination or retaliation. This is the distinct other-reason limitation incorporated with the enforcement provisions. |
| Section 706(g)(2)(B) | For a proved section 703(m) violation where the respondent shows it would have taken the same action without the impermissible motivating factor, the court may grant declaratory relief, limited injunctive relief and fees and costs directly attributable to that claim, but may not award damages or order the admission, reinstatement, hiring, promotion or back pay described in subparagraph (A). That separate trigger does not establish a new ADA mixed-motive or causation rule here. |
| Section 1981a(a) | For this ADA action, compensatory and punitive recovery is available for unlawful intentional discrimination under section 102 or a violation of its section 102(b)(5) accommodation requirements, not disparate-impact damages. For an accommodation claim, damages may not be awarded when the employer proves good-faith efforts, in consultation with the disabled person who informed it of the need, to identify and make an equally effective reasonable accommodation without undue hardship. |
| Section 1981a(b)(1) | Punitive damages require malice or reckless indifference to federally protected rights; governmental respondents are excluded. |
| Section 1981a(b)(2) | Compensatory damages under this provision exclude back pay, its interest and other section 706(g) relief. |
| Section 1981a(b)(3) | The combined cap, for each complaining party, on compensatory damages for future pecuniary loss, emotional pain, suffering, inconvenience, mental anguish, loss of enjoyment of life and other nonpecuniary loss, together with punitive damages is $50,000 for more than 14 and fewer than 101 employees; $100,000 for more than 100 and fewer than 201; $200,000 for more than 200 and fewer than 501; and $300,000 for more than 500. The applicable size must be established for each of twenty or more calendar weeks in the current or preceding calendar year. |

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the enacted remedial menu remains conditional. Toyota supplies the operative disability rules; a seizure by itself establishes none of their required findings. Section 706(g)'s discretion concerns justified relief on proved facts, and section 1981a separately supplies damages on its own terms. Great-West interprets a different ERISA provision and does not delete this express damages authorization. The distinct limitations in section 706(g)(2)(A), section 706(g)(2)(B) and section 1981a must remain distinct rather than being combined into a new general exception. The employee's conduct can matter to mitigation, actual satisfaction or a properly established judgment or settlement. Without such a record, possible future duplication cannot justify removing every victim-specific remedy at the outset. Employer size and the other damages predicates remain matters for ordinary adjudication.

### Part IV. The categorical restriction is reversed without a contract-enforceability prerequisite

**Holding and operative rule:** The Fourth Circuit's categorical prohibition on victim-specific relief is reversed, and the action is remanded for adjudication of liability and appropriate statutory relief. The Commission may pursue that relief without first establishing that Baker's private forum promise is unenforceable under state law.

**Controlling explanation:** Both the absence of FAA coverage and the Commission's independent statutory authority defeat the restriction as entered. Because the second ground resolves the private forum agreement's asserted effect on the Commission's remedy categories, an antecedent state-law enforcement proceeding is unnecessary. The FAA supplies no bar for this excluded employment agreement. Independently, an employee's private forum promise does not remove Congress's individual-remedy categories from the EEOC's own enforcement action, even if the promise binds the employee under an independent source. ## Separate Writings

**Justice Thomas, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by Justice Scalia.** They join Part I because Circuit City's governing employment exclusion removes the FAA as a source of enforcement, despite their continuing disagreement with that precedent. # Barnhart v. Sigmon Coal Co. — 2002-02-19

[Authoritative Record](../records/barnhart-v-sigmon-coal-co_merits_2002-02-19.md)

## Event

Barnhart v. Sigmon Coal Co., No. 00-1307. Argued November 7, 2001; decided February 19, 2002. On writ of certiorari to the Fourth Circuit's judgment rejecting the challenged Coal Act assignments. The question is whether asset succession to an original signatory operator, without an independently qualifying statutory relationship, permits assignment under the Coal Act.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated.

## Public Action

The Court affirms the judgment against the challenged assignments on the asserted asset-successor theory.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 6–3; the 86 assignments cannot stand on the asserted asset-successor theory.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg.

**Opposing:** Stevens, O'Connor and Breyer.

The 86 assignments remain set aside to the extent they rest solely on the rejected direct asset-successor theory. Any independently established statutory relationship or enforceable contractual obligation retains its own force; administration of unassigned beneficiaries remains governed by the Coal Act's lawful mechanisms.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Thomas | Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg | Supports the judgment | Entire opinion; every controlling proposition and its stated limits |
| Dissent | Stevens | O'Connor, Breyer | Opposes the judgment | Entire statutory disagreement and contrary remedy |

## Holdings

### Asset succession alone does not create a new assignment category

**Holding and operative rule:** Sections 9701(c)(2) and 9706 do not authorize assigning a signatory operator's beneficiaries to an asset purchaser solely because it directly succeeded to that operator's business. A purchaser independently within an enumerated related-person class remains subject to the statute; the holding does not determine an independent contractual obligation or every contested meaning of successor.

**Authority:** Thomas's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg, controls the complete statutory rule, its limits and the affirmance.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that financial responsibility must arise from the relationships Congress enacted. Section 9701(c)(2)(A) identifies a member of the signatory's controlled group under section 52(a), including the signatory; a trade or business under common control under section 52(b); and a person with a partnership interest or joint venture with a signatory in a coal-industry business employing eligible beneficiaries, except an interest held solely as a limited partner. The successor sentence refers to those listed classes rather than adding all direct successors of an original signatory. Subsection (c)(2)(B) measures the relationship on July 20, 1992, or immediately before an earlier cessation of business. The assignment priorities in section 9706(a) do not enlarge these classes.

Section 9706(b)(2) separately permits a post-enactment assignment transfer to a successor under its conditions, retaining the transferor and related persons as guarantors and requiring notice to the trustees. That provision does not automatically reach this earlier asset purchase. Russello gives effect to Congress's differing language; Ron Pair requires enforcement of the resulting text. The strongest objection is that reaching a related person's successor while excluding the direct successor creates an anomalous financing gap. But an imperfect allocation of statutory liability is not an absurdity authorizing a new liable-person class. Jericol's continuation of the business and agreement assumption do not establish the claimed statutory relationship. The contextual reading required by Chickasaw and J.E.M. does not replace the specific classes with general successor policy.

**Precedent treatment:** Russello and Ron Pair are applied to the differentiated categories; Chickasaw and J.E.M. retain contextual construction without supplying an omitted category.

### Clear statutory limits resolve this assignment theory

**Holding and operative rule:** The definite assignment classes cannot be enlarged by an administrative preference for preserving program revenue. Because that statutory limit resolves these assignments, the Court does not reach the unnecessary constitutional objections or decide an unestablished independent contractual ground.

**Authority:** The same six-Justice Court opinion controls.

**Controlling explanation:** Mead distinguishes an agency's interpretive authority from the persuasive force of its reasoning; neither permits liability contrary to clear statutory categories. Eastern Enterprises invalidated particular tier-three assessments without a controlling affirmative constitutional rationale. It did not invalidate the Coal Act generally and does not supply a constitutional shortcut here. The Court's statutory judgment resolves the challenged theory while leaving any genuinely independent obligation to its own legal predicates.

**Precedent treatment:** Mead's statutory boundary is applied; Eastern Enterprises is preserved at the scope of its judgment and independently majority-supported property proposition.

## Precedent Treatment

- Russello v. United States, 464 U.S. 16: different language in related provisions supports the enacted distinction.
- United States v. Ron Pair Enterprises, Inc., 489 U.S. 235: the statutory text governs despite a claimed policy anomaly.
- Chickasaw Nation v. United States, November 27, 2001, and J. E. M. Ag Supply, Inc. v. Pioneer Hi-Bred International, Inc., December 10, 2001: contextual reading preserves each provision's actual function; neither adds this successor category.
- United States v. Mead Corp., June 18, 2001: interpretive authority and persuasion remain distinct, and neither authorizes departure from clear liability conditions.
- Eastern Enterprises v. Apfel, June 25, 1998: the limited assessment judgment and absence of a controlling affirmative constitutional rationale remain; the Coal Act is not invalidated as a whole.

## Law After Decision

Direct asset succession to an original signatory is insufficient by itself for the challenged statutory assignments. Independently qualifying related persons, the separate prospective assignment-transfer mechanism and enforceable contractual obligations remain governed by their own conditions. This is a statutory coverage rule, not a general immunity for asset purchasers or a constitutional invalidation of the program.

## Separate Writings

**Stevens, joined by O'Connor and Breyer, dissenting.** The dissent would reverse and remand for application of an inclusive succession rule. Congress allocated existing health-benefit obligations across continuing business relationships. Reading the successor sentence in that integrated structure avoids the anomaly of reaching a related person's successor while exempting the closer direct successor that continued the coal business and assumed its agreement. The contextual treatment of successors in 1 U.S.C. sections 1 and 5, together with the labor-law succession principles illustrated by Wiley, Golden State Bottling and Fall River Dyeing, supports that reading; those authorities do not establish liability for every solvent purchaser.

The dissent recognizes that a differentiated list and a separate prospective transfer provision could reflect compromise. Its answer is that the entire succession structure supplies the context for that list, and the Court's separation defeats the allocation Congress made. Legislative explanations corroborate that construction rather than replacing the text. The dissent does not treat a bargaining discount as a proved fact, impose unrestricted federal common-law liability, or decide the constitutional question. Preserved successor-status and other application issues remain for the proper tribunal on remand.

## Procedure After Action

The Fourth Circuit's judgment against these assignments stands. The statutory basis asserted in this review cannot support reassignment; any independent obligation must be established on its own terms.

# Winn-Dixie Stores, Inc. v. Commissioner — 2002-02-20

[Authoritative Record](../records/winn-dixie-stores-inc-v-commissioner_merits_2002-02-20.md)

## Event

Winn-Dixie Stores, Inc. v. Commissioner. Decided February 20, 2002. On merits review of the Eleventh Circuit's June 28, 2001 judgment, 254 F.3d 1313, affirming the Tax Court's 1993 deficiency determination. The question is whether satisfying the four-of-seven exception to section 264's interest limitation requires deductions for a borrowing arrangement accepted to lack both a nontax business purpose and any prospect of pretax profit.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated.

## Public Action

The Court affirms the judgment sustaining the reviewed 1993 deficiency and disallowance of the claimed interest and related fees.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 9–0; the reviewed 1993 interest and fee deductions remain disallowed.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The reviewed deficiency determination and interest-and-fee disallowance remain intact. The judgment reaches this borrowing arrangement and the accepted findings, leaving economically independent insurance expenditures outside its scope.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|

## Holdings

### The exception removes one disallowance without guaranteeing a deduction

**Holding and operative rule:** Compliance with the four-of-seven exception in the governing section 264(c)(1) removes the relevant section 264(a)(3) restriction on systematic premium-financing borrowing; it does not affirmatively confer a section 163 deduction or immunize the borrowing from the substantive-sham inquiry. Where accepted findings establish both no nontax business purpose and no prospect of pretax profit, that exception does not require the claimed interest and related fee deductions. The accepted four-of-seven compliance means that no portion of the annual premium was financed by policy borrowing in four of the first seven years.

**Authority:** Kennedy's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies nine direct votes for the statutory rule, narrow application and affirmance.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an exception to one restriction does not dispense with the substantive requirements of the claimed deduction. Knetsch examines whether the transaction supplies genuine substantive indebtedness notwithstanding the absence of a particular prohibition; Gregory likewise distinguishes statutory substance from a tax form that lacks it. Real policies, payments and contractual obligations do not alone establish the borrowing arrangement's qualifying substance.

Winn-Dixie's strongest argument is that Congress deliberately regulated insurance borrowing in detail and authorized the four-of-seven pattern. The exception has real work: qualifying timing avoids that particular statutory bar. But avoiding the bar is not an affirmative grant covering every transaction. Gitlitz preserves tax consequences Congress actually confers while retaining independent deduction requirements; it does not protect a transaction that fails those requirements. This reading therefore does not permit general revenue policy to override a legislatively conferred benefit.

Here compliance with the exception and both adverse economic findings are accepted. Interest and fees exceeded relevant benefits, contemporaneous memoranda and the accepted absence of a business use support the substantive-sham conclusion, and no factual retrial is called for. Tax motivation alone is insufficient, and the Court does not require every intentionally tax-favored investment to be profitable without its incentive. Because both relevant substance findings are negative here, no universal conjunctive or disjunctive test is chosen for arrangements with a substantial nontax purpose or meaningful pretax returns. That different factual question is outside this review.

**Precedent treatment:** Knetsch and Gregory are applied to the accepted transaction; Gitlitz's express statutory benefits and independent qualification requirements remain intact.

## Precedent Treatment

- Knetsch v. United States, 364 U.S. 361: applied to distinguish substantive indebtedness from formal borrowing that fails to supply the claimed deduction's economic substance; tax motive alone remains insufficient.
- Gregory v. Helvering, 293 U.S. 465: applied for statutory substance rather than a general prohibition on arranging affairs to reduce tax.
- Gitlitz v. Commissioner, January 9, 2001: its protection of enacted tax consequences is preserved alongside the independent requirements of the actual deduction; it supplies no immunity for this arrangement.

## Law After Decision

The four-of-seven exception is not a statutory safe harbor from substantive-sham scrutiny. This arrangement's accepted lack of both nontax business purpose and pretax profit potential supports the disallowance. Transactions with meaningful economic substance, mixed findings or an affirmative statutory incentive remain governed by their own legal and factual predicates.

# Barnhart v. Walton — 2002-03-27

[Authoritative Record](../records/barnhart_v_walton_merits_2002-03-27.md)

## Event

Barnhart v. Walton, No. 00-1937. Argued January 16, 2002; decided March 27, 2002. On writ of certiorari to the Fourth Circuit, 235 F.3d 184. The questions are whether the agency may require twelve months of disabling inability as well as lasting impairment, and whether actual substantial work within that period before a favorable disability determination defeats projected-duration and trial-work theories. The circuit affirmed the October 1995 substantial-gainful-activity finding but reversed the denial on both legal grounds.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The challenged Fourth Circuit legal rulings are reversed, and the case is remanded.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The lower court must implement denial on the reviewed duration and trial-work eligibility grounds. Its affirmed October 1995 substantial-gainful-activity finding remains undisturbed.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Parts I and III | Souter | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Formal delegated-regulation ground; duration and timing rules, qualifications and disposition |
| Opinion of the Court, Part II | Souter | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Additional contextual support from sustained interpretation and administrative consideration |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Scalia | None | Joins Parts I and III; withholds Part II |
| Concurrence | Stone-Zsela | None | Joins the Court; adds independent statutory agreement |

## Holdings

### I. The agency may require twelve months of disabling inability

**Holding and operative rule:** Under the twelve-month branch of the adult disability definitions in 42 U.S.C. §§423(d)(1)(A) and 1382c(a)(3)(A), the agency may require that the impairment cause inability to engage in substantial gainful activity lasting, or expected to last, continuously for at least twelve months. The expected-death branch remains distinct; the Court sustains a permissible delegated construction rather than declaring it the only statutory implementation Congress allows.

**Authority:** Justice Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part I, is joined by all eight other Justices. All nine directly adopt the formal delegated-regulation ground, the functional-duration rule and its temporal qualification. Part II's additional contextual support is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; Scalia does not join that additional ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the agency's construction fits the integrated definition. The relative clause concerning duration follows impairment, but the defined disability is inability to work because of that impairment. The wording does not unambiguously require benefits for a lasting diagnosis after the disabling inability ends. The agency may read the lasting condition as the condition that produces the described inability. The expected-death alternative concerns the impairment; it does not mean that inability or work itself causes death. Title II's ordinary five-month waiting condition does not compel an impairment-only interpretation, and that waiting rule is not imported into SSI.

Section 405(a), incorporated into SSI by §1383(d)(1), supplies rulemaking authority consistent with the benefit statutes. The agency exercised it in binding implementing regulations and the final trial-work provision, after a proposal and notice and comment. Mead requires actual delegation and its exercise, not expertise or form alone; those predicates are established here. Chevron permits the resulting reasonable choice. The July 2000 final rule became effective August 10, 2000. Smiley permits use of its authoritative interpretation of the preexisting statute for antecedent events without backdating a new legislative condition, where clear contrary prior official guidance is absent. The longstanding duration approach and 1995 proposal supply continuity here; no contrary assurance to Walton is established. The lower court's no-retroactivity statement therefore does not exclude the interpretive use at issue.

**Additional controlling explanation in Part II:** The sustained administrative interpretation and the agency's detailed attention to the connected eligibility provisions further support the reasonableness of its choice. These considerations operate within the established delegation and formal exercise of authority; they create neither power from longevity alone nor a holding that every earlier letter, manual or Social Security Ruling has controlling force. Scalia accepts the sufficient formal-regulation ground while withholding this additional contextual ground.

### II. Actual work before a favorable disability finding may defeat the initial duration claim

**Holding and operative rule:** When work demonstrating substantial gainful activity resumes within twelve months after onset of the disabling impairment and before notice of a determination or decision finding disability, the agency may use that known event to reject an initial claim based on an earlier expectation of twelve-month inability. Section 422(c)'s trial-work protection presupposes qualifying Title II entitlement and does not independently establish a missing initial duration condition.

**Authority:** Part III of Souter's opinion is joined by all eight other Justices. All nine directly adopt the permissible timing construction, the qualifications below, and the disposition of Walton's two legal theories.

**Operative qualifications:** A claimant returning to work more than twelve months after onset but before adjudication may qualify for trial-work protection if actual duration and the other entitlement conditions are satisfied; passage of twelve months alone does not cure disabling inability that ended earlier. A claimant returning within twelve months after onset, but after notice finding disability, may qualify even when that notice precedes the benefit-award notice. A disability finding, its notice, an award, payment and statutory entitlement are distinct; neither qualification requires prior payment. The separate exclusion for work during a required waiting period remains separate, and the pre-application exclusion concerns months before application, not permanent disqualification for every earlier work episode.

Trial-work services are distinct from substantial gainful activity. The operative nine service months occur within sixty consecutive months and need not themselves be consecutive. New medical or other evidence independent of protected trial work may establish cessation under the governing rules before nine months; a repeated trial-work period is not created within the same period of disability or entitlement. Ordinary Title II insured status, application, age and the five-month waiting condition, including the qualifying prior-entitlement or cessation route within the statutory sixty-month window, remain separate conditions. The Title II trial-work scheme is not transferred to SSI. Statutory blindness, the separate child definition, genuinely unsuccessful work attempts and distinct reopening or reliance questions concerning a claimant with an existing favorable determination are outside the presented adult eligibility dispute.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the statute does not fix an earlier moment at which an expectation becomes irrevocable before the initial favorable determination. Expected duration permits prompt awards before twelve months have elapsed; it does not require the agency to disregard actual work already known when initial eligibility is decided. The agency may distinguish establishing disability from deciding whether an entitled person's disability has ceased. A protection against using trial work to establish cessation cannot alone create the initial entitlement it presupposes.

Walton objects that entitlement need not await an award and that adjudication timing can produce unequal results. The first point is correct and is preserved by the two qualifications above. The agency acknowledged the second and reasonably accommodated two statutory purposes: prompt prospective awards and exclusion of demonstrated short-term disability. That explanation does not declare every administrative delay harmless; it answers the claimed requirement to treat an earlier expectation as conclusive here. Good Samaritan preserves reasonable administrative choice within the assigned program, while Smiley preserves qualified interpretive use for antecedent events. The actual statutory grant and rulemaking satisfy Mead; no new generalized deference test or resort to Auer is needed.

Walton's October 1995 substantial-gainful-activity finding stands. His later closed-period award was superseded on administrative review, and no favorable finding notice preceded his return. The circuit's impairment-only interpretation, accrued-expectation theory and independent unreasonableness conclusion therefore fail. Its challenged legal rulings are reversed, with remand to implement the denial on these eligibility grounds.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.:** Applied to a permissible choice within the agency's actual statutory assignment; the Court does not require its own sole preferred construction.
- **United States v. Mead Corp.:** Applied through identified statutory delegation and actual exercise in binding implementing regulations; complexity, expertise or notice and comment alone does not establish authority.
- **Smiley v. Citibank (South Dakota), N.A.:** Applied to qualified interpretive use for antecedent events without backdating a new legislative condition or disregarding clear contrary prior official guidance.
- **Good Samaritan Hospital v. Shalala:** Preserves reasoned administrative choice within the assigned statutory scheme.
- **Christensen v. Harris County:** Preserves the distinction between advice and delegated lawmaking; no blanket controlling status is conferred on every Social Security Ruling.
- **Auer v. Robbins:** No extension is required; statutory authority and the actual regulations supply the ground.
- **Wisconsin Department of Health and Family Services v. Blumer, Barnhart v. Sigmon Coal Co., Edelman v. Lynchburg College and Ragsdale v. Wolverine World Wide, Inc.:** Their distinct limits on agency authority and statutory consequences remain. Independent construction in another statute does not eliminate lawful delegated choice here.

## Law After Decision

The agency may administer the adult duration definitions through a twelve-month requirement for disabling inability and use actual substantial work before the relevant favorable finding notice in deciding initial eligibility. The holding sustains that reasonable delegated choice; it does not compel every future lawful implementation. The stated pre-award and post-twelve-month qualifications prevent converting entitlement into prior payment or a universal award prerequisite. Title II trial work retains its own scope and conditions.

## Separate Writings

**Scalia, concurring in part and in the judgment.** Scalia joins Parts I and III, including the formal delegated-regulation ground, permissible duration and timing constructions, qualifications and reversal. He does not join Part II's additional reliance on sustained administrative interpretation. The formal rule and genuine delegation suffice; duration of an agency practice alone should not confer special controlling weight, and the status of old informal documents cannot be assumed. His position does not demand the Court's independent preferred construction or a new multifactor exception to the governing delegation rules.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring.** The Chief joins the Court's permissible-construction holding and adds that the interpretation also survives independent reading. The lasting impairment must produce the inability that the definition describes; expected duration permits timely decisions, but does not require ignoring actual predecision substantial work. Trial-work protection does not itself create initial entitlement. The agency's longstanding, expert reasons deserve substantial persuasive weight because they fit those enacted distinctions, while its statutory rulemaking assignment and operative regulations separately establish actual delegated authority. This additional ground changes neither the Court's permission into a command nor the stated entitlement qualifications.

Further proceedings implement the denial on the reviewed eligibility grounds. The Court preserves the established substantial-gainful-activity finding and the distinct qualifications stated in Holdings.

# Festo Corp. v. Shoketsu Kinzoku Kogyo Kabushiki Co.; No. 00-1543; 535 U.S. 722. — 2002-05-28

[Authoritative Record](../records/Festo_Corp_v_Shoketsu_Kinzoku_Kogyo_Kabushiki_Co_Merits_2002-05-28.md)

## Event

Festo Corp. v. Shoketsu Kinzoku Kogyo Kabushiki Co., No. 00-1543, 535 U.S. 722. Merits decision, May 28, 2002. Argued January 8, 2002. On writ of certiorari to the Federal Circuit.

The questions concern actual claim narrowing, patentability purpose and the extent of prosecution-history estoppel—the restriction against recovering subject matter surrendered to obtain a patent. The en banc Federal Circuit reversed the infringement judgment under a complete bar to equivalents for amended limitations. The Stoll and Carroll patents concern magnetic rodless cylinder assemblies. The issued claim boundaries, the reasons and effects of the amendments, and the remaining infringement questions require distinct treatment.

## Participation

## Public Action

The Stoll ring restriction, Stoll magnetizable-sleeve restriction and Carroll ring restriction are qualifying patentability-related narrowings. ## Opinion Topology

GINSBURG, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, SOUTER, THOMAS and BREYER, JJ., joined.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer | Unanimous vacatur and corrected-scope remand | All controlling propositions, three established trigger applications, rebuttable scope inquiry and remaining procedural/infringement conditions |


## Holdings

### I. Actual narrowing and patentability purpose are separate requirements

**Controlling proposition:** An amendment that actually narrows a claim to obtain the patent by satisfying a patentability requirement can create prosecution-history estoppel, including narrowing for a disclosure requirement. Actual narrowing is determined from the claim's legally operative scope before and after amendment in its patent and prosecution context; a voluntary change receives the same substantive inquiry, while a cosmetic or merely explanatory change is not automatically a surrender.

**Authority:** Ginsburg's opinion of the Court is joined in full by the other eight participating Justices at the propositions and remand stated here. Existing Warner-Jenkinson propositions not reconsidered remain governed by that decision's own authority.

**Controlling explanation:** Warner-Jenkinson preserves nonliteral protection while enforcing actual claim limitations and prosecution surrender. Exhibit Supply protects what the applicant relinquished to obtain the patent. Neither confines patentability to novelty or prior-art avoidance. A substantive restriction addressing disclosure can therefore matter; describing an objection as formal does not immunize the ensuing narrowing. Conversely, a §112 rejection or changed words do not establish the purpose and legal scope of each resulting limitation.

Moving a restriction from a dependent claim into an independent claim can narrow that independent claim, notwithstanding the restriction's earlier presence elsewhere. The Court rejects the contrary categorical implication. A feature also need not be separately novel or demanded word-for-word by the examiner to contribute to a voluntary patentability narrowing. These conclusions give SMC's combination-purpose argument real force without treating the overall prosecution response as a conclusive answer for every limitation.

**Precedent treatment:** Warner-Jenkinson Co. v. Hilton Davis Chemical Co., March 3, 1997, is applied to actual limitation-specific surrender. Exhibit Supply Co. v. Ace Patents Corp., 315 U.S. 126 (1942), protects the actual concession without establishing an irrebuttable bar to all equivalents.

### II. The existing purpose-explanation burden remains distinct

**Controlling proposition:** When required narrowing lacks a disclosed legally sufficient reason, the patentee seeking an equivalent bears the explanatory burden. After fair consideration, an inadequate explanation permits the rebuttable presumption of a patentability-related reason and estoppel at the limitation; a supported nonpatentability explanation must be considered, including in the context of an older prosecution record.

**Controlling explanation:** Warner-Jenkinson already assigns this burden to the applicant, who chose the narrowed claim. It does not demand nonexistent contemporaneous formulations or turn historical silence into misconduct. Nor does it guarantee a hearing, reopening or another opportunity to discharge an existing burden. The Court adds no automatic purpose presumption for every voluntary textual change.

The written Stoll §112 response is established, but does not alone show that both the ring and sleeve terms had one purpose. Carroll's replacement combination was offered against the German reference, but the particular ring amendment still requires analysis. An attorney-error account cannot erase the issued sleeve restriction, although a supported nonpatentability explanation must receive its proper consideration. SMC's characterization of an effective concession and the district court's earlier description of the sleeve's reason as a mystery are not a stipulation that the burden has failed. The accepted record permits the three trigger determinations stated below; an admission is not their prerequisite.

**Precedent treatment:** Warner-Jenkinson's explanation burden and older-record qualification are applied unchanged; no subjective bad-faith condition or automatic second evidentiary opportunity is added.

### III. Qualifying narrowing presumptively surrenders the asserted equivalent, subject to concrete rebuttal

**Controlling proposition:** Once patentability-related narrowing is established, the patentee presumptively surrendered the asserted equivalent at the affected limitation. The patentee may rebut with a concrete, objectively supported basis, anchored in the prosecution, showing that the actual relinquishment did not encompass that variation—for example, that the equivalent was not reasonably foreseeable at the relevant prosecution stage, that the amendment's rationale related only tangentially to it, or that it could not reasonably have been described within the claim at that time.

**Controlling explanation:** A complete bar sacrifices all nonliteral protection beyond the actual concession. Unrestricted flexibility, however, would deny competitors the public boundary produced by amendment. The rebuttable presumption gives each interest legal effect. The accused infringer need not first prove that this very equivalent was surrendered; that would collapse the trigger and scope inquiries. The patentee must establish the concrete reason the variation remains outside the surrender, judged at the amendment rather than through an uncommunicated intention to retain broad rights. The stated grounds are not automatic safe harbors, a closed mechanical list or an unstructured fairness balance.

Objective grounding in the prosecution does not require a universal exclusion of reliable technical or explanatory evidence outside the contemporaneous file. Such evidence may illuminate the claim, prosecution and reason for amendment, particularly in an older record. It cannot contradict a clear public concession, delete a claim term or substitute the inventor's private wishes for the grant. Ordinary procedure governs a proffer's admissibility and any further evidentiary opportunity. Markman permits useful technical explanation while leaving public claim meaning to the court; Warner-Jenkinson requires fair treatment of supported explanations without automatic reopening.

Successful rebuttal removes only this estoppel barrier; technical equivalence and infringement still require proof. Failed rebuttal forecloses that equivalent at the amended limitation. This scope presumption is separate from the existing presumption about an unexplained amendment's purpose.

**Precedent treatment:** Warner-Jenkinson is developed through the explicit rebuttable scope presumption while its purpose burden remains intact. Markman v. Westview Instruments, Inc., April 23, 1996, preserves judicial claim construction and useful technical evidence without dictating every appellate standard or a special hearing. Graver Tank & Manufacturing Co. v. Linde Air Products Co., 339 U.S. 605 (1950), retains protection against insubstantial substitutions within the legal claim boundaries.

### IV-A. The Stoll ring amendment is qualifying patentability narrowing

**Controlling proposition:** Incorporating the pair-of-rings restriction into Stoll's replacement independent claim narrowed that claim and served a patentability purpose in the response to the §112 rejection. Festo's account that the old sealing means already distinguished the references does not defeat that trigger, although it may bear on whether the amendment's rationale encompassed the single-ring equivalent under the separate scope inquiry.

**Controlling explanation:** The original independent claim's general sealing means at each end and the replacement's specific ring structure are not interchangeable. The restriction's earlier presence in a dependent claim did not give the old independent claim the same limit. This comparison adopts no general means-plus-function construction and decides no technical equivalence.

The replacement sought allowance after the disclosure rejection, requested reconsideration under §112 and addressed impurity-prevention structure in discussing the references. Even accepting that the older claim already distinguished those references, the applicant chose a more specific description to answer the statutory objection. That is a patentability reason; the examiner need not dictate the exact words. It is unnecessary to decide SMC's additional contention that the rings were independently indispensable to avoiding the art. The asserted earlier distinction remains potentially relevant to the actual extent of surrender, but does not itself rebut the scope presumption.

**Precedent treatment:** Warner-Jenkinson's actual-narrowing and patentability rule is applied to this specific claim and response; its preserved equivalents inquiry supplies the separate work on remand.

### IV-B. The Stoll sleeve amendment is qualifying patentability narrowing

**Controlling proposition:** Adding magnetizable sleeve material to Stoll's replacement independent claim narrowed that claim in the documented patentability response. The earlier proffer of inventor nonintention, lack of independent necessity and attorney mistake does not establish a supported nonpatentability purpose for the attorney's actual submission or permit deletion of the issued restriction; the scope presumption applies to the nonmagnetizable-sleeve equivalent, subject to Festo's separate rebuttal burden.

**Controlling explanation:** The original independent claim lacked this material restriction. The applicant incorporated it from a dependent claim while recasting the rejected claim to obtain allowance. Neither a separate examiner demand nor independent necessity of magnetizability is a condition of the narrowing's legal effect. The sleeve remains distinct from the cylinder tube and the ring limitations.

The Court fairly considers the earlier account that the inventor neither intended nor authorized the restriction and that it was unnecessary. Even accepting the asserted absence of inventor intent, the proffer gives no supported nonpatentability reason for the prosecuting attorney's actual selection; it instead requests that the claim be read without the chosen words. A genuinely supported nonpatentability explanation can matter, including when counsel made the amendment. The asserted mistake here does not displace the objective submission or remove the literal term. No finding of false testimony or disobedient attorney intent follows.

This conclusion rests on the actual response and the legal insufficiency of the contrary grounds. It does not rest on a supposed concession, the district judge's description of the reason as a mystery, or historical silence treated as misconduct. It adds no presumption that every voluntary edit is patentability-related and demands no nonexistent contemporary formula. Rejecting literal deletion still does not conclusively extinguish every sleeve equivalent; that remains the separate scope inquiry.

**Precedent treatment:** Warner-Jenkinson's fair-consideration and public-claim rules are applied together. Its older-record qualification neither makes an explanation automatically sufficient nor guarantees another evidentiary opportunity.

### IV-C. The Carroll ring amendment is qualifying patentability narrowing

**Controlling proposition:** Replacing Carroll's former independent claim with claim 9's express pair-of-rings limitation narrowed the claim as part of the combination submitted to distinguish the German reference and preserve patentability. The feature's alleged presence in the reference and the emphasis on other features do not defeat that trigger, although those particulars may bear on the scope of surrender of the single-ring equivalent.

**Controlling explanation:** Disclosure of rings in the specification did not itself impose the new limitation on the former independent claim. The Court identifies this ring restriction rather than substituting another component's narrowing. The applicant included it in the chosen replacement combination offered against the reference. Independent novelty, indispensability and a word-for-word examiner demand are not prerequisites to the legal effect of that choice.

The verified emphasis on magnets, end members and cushions may show a tangential relationship between the amendment's rationale and the asserted substitute; Festo must establish the relevant scope showing. Its acknowledgment of some unrelated abandonment neither concedes surrender of this equivalent nor conclusively rebuts the presumption. The Court applies the rule to this accepted ring addition and documented response, without declaring that every element of every amended combination necessarily has one purpose. Stoll's disclosure rejection and sleeve explanation are not attributed to Carroll.

**Precedent treatment:** Warner-Jenkinson's limitation-specific surrender rule governs the trigger, while the separate scope presumption preserves the legal relevance of the particular amendment rationale to the accused substitute.

### V. Preservation and the all-elements inquiry retain their own work

**Controlling proposition:** A dispositive preservation ruling must identify the actual defense, procedural act and governing rule for the particular patent and stage. The separate requirement that every material claim element be satisfied remains operative; vacating the complete-bar judgment decides neither that objection nor the legally available infringement and remedy questions.

**Controlling explanation:** The Stoll trial exchanges include counsel's immediate disagreement with an abandonment characterization. They establish neither a general surrender of estoppel nor a Carroll-specific waiver. The separate report that Carroll estoppel was not raised at summary judgment retains its attributed procedural scope. The court of appeals must address a properly presented objection under the applicable rule, without assuming an unestablished procedural omission. It must also address the all-elements objection that the en banc court left unresolved. Legal boundaries come first; genuine remaining infringement facts retain ordinary factfinder treatment. Further district-court work follows only when necessary under the corrected rule and ordinary procedure.

**Precedent treatment:** Warner-Jenkinson's element-specific equivalents rule remains controlling; overall similarity cannot erase a material limitation. Its existing no-bad-intent rule is not reconsidered, and retains that decision's eight-Justice authority.

## Precedent Treatment

Warner-Jenkinson's limitation-specific protection, purpose-explanation burden and contextual older-record qualification remain. This decision adds the rebuttable scope-of-surrender presumption after a qualifying narrowing, without merging the two burdens. Its existing no-bad-intent holding remains at its original authority. Graver Tank preserves nonliteral protection for insubstantial differences within the claim boundaries, and Exhibit Supply protects actual prosecution surrender. Markman preserves judicial construction, including embedded technical-usage questions and useful explanatory evidence, while leaving external infringement facts and unaddressed appellate standards in their ordinary places. No precedent is overruled.

## Law After Decision

Narrowing for any patentability requirement, including disclosure, may trigger estoppel. The law now expressly presumes surrender of the asserted equivalent after that trigger, while permitting concrete objective rebuttal directed to what the amendment reasonably relinquished. Required-narrowing explanation and scope rebuttal are separate burdens. Every material limitation remains operative; neither rejection of a complete bar nor successful estoppel rebuttal establishes infringement. Older records receive fair treatment without automatic evidentiary reopening. The three disputed amendments meet the trigger; only particular-equivalent scope rebuttal and the surviving ordinary procedural and infringement applications return for decision.

## Separate Writings



## Procedure After Action

The case returns first to the Federal Circuit for Festo's particular-equivalent rebuttal at the three established narrowing triggers, properly presented preservation objections and remaining all-elements and infringement questions. The existing legal triggers are not reopened. Further district-court proceedings occur only as required by the corrected scope rule and ordinary procedure. No patent invalidation, infringement judgment, damages award, new hearing or evidentiary reopening is ordered.

# In re Prudential Insurance Co. of America Sales Practice Litigation; review of 261 F.3d 355 (3d Cir. 2001). No Supreme Court docket supplied. — 2002-05-28

[Authoritative Record](../records/In_re_Prudential_Insurance_Co_of_America_Sales_Practice_Litigation_Merits_2002-05-28.md)

## Event

In re Prudential Insurance Co. of America Sales Practice Litigation. Merits decision, May 28, 2002. On review of the Third Circuit's August 2, 2001 judgment, 261 F.3d 355, affirming the District of New Jersey's settlement-enforcement restriction. Submitted on full merits papers before May 28, 2002; no oral-argument or exact submission date is specified.

The question is whether the Anti-Injunction Act permits a settlement-enforcement court to bar evidence concerning released policies in a state action concerning policies expressly excluded from the settlement. The Lowes excluded two eligible policies and remained bound as to two others. Their Florida complaint seeks relief on the excluded policies and proposes facts about the released transactions as pattern evidence. The March 29, 2000 federal order restricts motion practice, discovery, evidence and other action involving those facts. The Florida court stayed the action on April 17 and placed it on an inactive docket on May 3, 2000, subject to appropriate reopening.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate at submission or argument and decision. Nine participate; the quorum is six and a majority is five.

## Public Action

The Court issues its signed merits decision.

## Judgment & Remedy

The Third Circuit's judgment is reversed insofar as it sustains the categorical restriction, and the case is remanded, 9–0. Supporting: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The restriction must be removed. The settlement and release remain operative. Any further injunction must identify the released claim or conclusively resolved issue, its lawful preclusive scope, the conduct requiring restraint and the applicable statutory exception. The Florida action remains subject to that court's ordinary reopening and evidentiary rules; the mandate does not direct admission of particular proof.

## Opinion Topology

SCALIA, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, KENNEDY, SOUTER, THOMAS, GINSBURG and BREYER, JJ., joined.

## Holdings

### I. Enforcement jurisdiction and the actual release

**Controlling proposition:** A federal court may enforce a settlement incorporated into its judgment or covered by an express retention of enforcement jurisdiction. That jurisdiction does not itself authorize an injunction against state proceedings or determine the scope of the release; the court must separately satisfy 28 U.S.C. §2283.

**Authority:** Scalia's opinion of the Court, joined in full by the other eight participating Justices, controls each holding here. The shared ground is the statute and this release, not a universal rule allocating every evidence question exclusively to the second forum.

**Controlling explanation:** Kokkonen permits enforcement where the judicial disposition incorporates the obligation or expressly retains jurisdiction, as this disposition does. Prudential therefore has a forum for enforcing the settlement. But Congress separately restricts injunctions against state proceedings to those expressly authorized by Act of Congress, necessary in aid of federal jurisdiction, or necessary to protect or effectuate federal judgments. No Act expressly authorizing this injunction is identified. The two remaining exceptions must each justify the restraint actually imposed; retained jurisdiction cannot do their work.

**Precedent treatment:** Kokkonen v. Guardian Life Insurance Co., 511 U.S. 375 (1994), is applied to the incorporated release and retained jurisdiction; it creates no independent Anti-Injunction Act exception.

### II. Judgment protection does not turn shared facts into released claims

**Controlling proposition:** A settlement court may enjoin actual prosecution of a released claim or adjudication inconsistent with a matter conclusively resolved, within the release's lawful scope and §2283's judgment-protection exception. It may not prohibit evidence offered for an expressly retained claim merely because the evidence concerns a released transaction; it must identify the protected claim or issue and connect the proposed use of proof to that adjudicative conflict.

**Controlling explanation:** Chick Kam Choo requires identification of the adjudication protected against relitigation. Matsushita gives lawful representative releases their real effect, including claims beyond those tried; it does not erase valid exclusions. Baker distinguishes a judgment's binding effect from the separate demand that another court give its evidence restrictions compulsory operation. The Lowes are bound for two policies, and a request for punitive or other relief may be an indirect attempt to recover on a released transaction. Their excluded-policy label therefore is not conclusive. Equally, the notice expressly allowed policy-specific exclusions, and use of common sales-practice facts does not by itself seek compensation for a released policy or reopen a decided issue. Prudential's concern about circumvention warrants inquiry into the claim and the use of proof, not a categorical prohibition based only on factual association. The present order exceeds that boundary.

**Precedent treatment:** Chick Kam Choo v. Exxon Corp., 486 U.S. 140 (1988), supplies the precise relitigation inquiry. Matsushita Electric Industrial Co. v. Epstein, 516 U.S. 367 (1996), preserves lawful release effect and valid exclusions. Baker v. General Motors Corp., 522 U.S. 222 (1998), informs the bounded distinction between adjudicative effect and evidentiary operation; its different binding-party setting remains material.

### III. Administration alone does not establish necessity in aid of jurisdiction

**Controlling proposition:** Section 2283's aid-of-jurisdiction exception requires necessity to prevent serious impairment of the federal court's ability to administer or dispose of its case. Retained jurisdiction, settlement complexity, shared facts and concern about future claims do not establish that necessity for the breadth of this order; the All Writs Act supplies no exemption from §2283.

**Controlling explanation:** Atlantic Coast Line requires more than federal jurisdiction or ordinary interference with a federal right. Ongoing administration of a nationwide settlement is relevant, but the stated grounds do not show that litigation over the expressly retained policies, or their overlapping proof, seriously impairs that administration. The All Writs Act permits appropriate assistance to existing jurisdiction; it does not enlarge the separate statutory exceptions. This independently defeats the asserted aid-of-jurisdiction justification for the categorical restriction. Any narrower protection must identify the actual conduct, necessity and statutory basis. The sufficient statutory grounds make the Lowes' constitutional notice and vagueness objections unnecessary to decide; the completed settlement approval is not reopened.

**Precedent treatment:** Atlantic Coast Line Railroad Co. v. Brotherhood of Locomotive Engineers, 398 U.S. 281 (1970), is applied to the required serious impairment and necessity, preserving its independent restraint on federal interference.

## Precedent Treatment

Kokkonen's settlement-enforcement jurisdiction remains distinct from permission to enjoin another court. Chick Kam Choo's precise relitigation inquiry and Atlantic Coast Line's necessity rule govern the two live exceptions separately. Matsushita's lawful-release and binding-representation limits remain, including the effect of valid exclusions. Baker's distinction between judgment recognition and evidentiary operation is applied at this release-specific scope; no general witness-access right follows. Amchem Products, Inc. v. Windsor and Ortiz v. Fibreboard Corp. retain their representative-structure requirements; settlement benefits do not replace them, and this decision does not reopen the completed certification or settlement approval. No precedent is overruled.

## Law After Decision

A settlement court with enforcement jurisdiction must separately establish an Anti-Injunction Act exception for the particular restraint. Policy-specific exclusions retain practical effect: shared facts alone do not convert a preserved claim into a released one. Supported protection against actual released relief or inconsistent adjudication remains available. Ordinary reopening, admissibility, privilege and claim-specific determinations remain with the appropriate court under their governing rules.

## Separate Writings



## Procedure After Action

The case returns through the Third Circuit for removal of the categorical restriction and any supported narrower enforcement request under the stated statutory standards. The Florida stay and inactive status require ordinary reopening; the retained claims were not dismissed. The release remains enforceable within its lawful scope.

### US Airways, Inc. v. Barnett — 2002-04-29

[Record](../records/us_airways_v_barnett_merits_2002-04-29.md)

**Common authority:** Justice Breyer's Opinion of the Court is joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor and Kennedy. Those five direct joins control every holding below. The separate writings qualify or oppose the rule only to the extent expressly stated; no combination of dissenting grounds supplies additional authority.

### Reasonable accommodation and undue hardship perform separate statutory work

**Holding and operative rule:** An employee ordinarily satisfies the initial accommodation showing by identifying an accommodation reasonable on its face or in the run of cases, not merely one effective for that employee; once reasonableness is shown, the employer must demonstrate significant difficulty or expense in its particular operations under section 12111(10). A requested exception to a disability-neutral policy is not automatically outside the accommodation duty, and the expectation that another worker may obtain an open position under this noncontractual policy does not by itself make the position unavailable for the statutory inquiry.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that reasonable accommodation may require different treatment to provide an employee a workplace opportunity. Section 12111(9) expressly identifies possible reassignment to a vacant position and adjustment of policies. Its permissive examples do not make every requested preference reasonable, but they foreclose treating uniform enforcement alone as an answer. Nor does this policy's bidding expectation establish an incumbent who must be displaced: Barnett occupies the position, the employer declares it open for bidding, and the policy creates no contractual entitlement.

Reasonable is an independent limit. An effective change may affect fellow employees in ways not captured by the employer's own operational expense. The ordinary first showing and the employer's particular hardship defense therefore do different work. This allocation does not require the employee to disprove every possible business burden. Garrett preserves the enacted distinction between accommodation and the employer's demonstrated significant-difficulty-or-expense defense. The latter considers the accommodation's nature and cost; facility resources, staffing, expenses and operational impact; the entity's overall resources, size and facilities; and workforce functions and the facilities' geographic, administrative and fiscal relationships.

The contrary disability-created-obstacle theory would deny a seniority exception because the allocation applies to all workers. But the present accommodation responds to Barnett's inability to perform his former work, and reassignment and policy adjustment remain statutory possibilities. Reasonableness limits those possibilities without excluding the requested exception at the outset. This conclusion also rejects effectiveness alone as a complete test; the employer's actual seniority system must receive the independent consideration stated next.

**Precedent treatment:** Garrett's separation of statutory conditions is preserved. Sutton, Murphy and Toyota retain their distinct coverage rules; none converts coverage into a right to a particular accommodation.

### An established seniority system ordinarily defeats a conflicting request, subject to special circumstances

**Holding and operative rule:** An employer's showing that a requested reassignment conflicts with an established seniority system ordinarily establishes that the accommodation is unreasonable and warrants summary judgment on that issue, including when management created the system. The employee may defeat that ground by presenting evidence of special circumstances making an exception reasonable in the particular case; the employee bears that showing, and the employer retains the separate undue-hardship defense if reasonableness is established.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that established seniority carries weight beyond its possible financial cost. Objective ordering gives employees expectations of fair treatment, job security and predictable advancement. Requiring the employer in every ordinary case to justify whether those expectations should yield would substitute discretionary accommodation decisions for the consistency on which the system depends. Those benefits can exist under a management-created policy as well as a collective agreement. Hardison supplies an analogy concerning the importance of seniority, not its different statute's hardship threshold or an automatic ADA exemption.

The absence of an express ADA seniority exemption does not eliminate the word reasonable. Conversely, the independent force of reasonable does not make seniority absolute. The employee can show that the actual system weakens the expectation on which the ordinary inference rests. Examples include an employer's frequent exercise of reserved amendment authority, sufficiently reducing expectations of adherence, or existing exceptions whose nature makes one further exception unlikely to matter. The examples are nonexclusive, and no numerical frequency threshold is prescribed. Bare reserved amendment authority does not automatically establish such circumstances; the employee must explain why an exception is reasonable in the actual setting.

The objection that this approach merges reasonableness with hardship overlooks their different subjects. The seniority inquiry concerns the character of the requested preference and employees' expectations; the employer's defense concerns significant difficulty or expense in its operations. The special-circumstances opportunity answers the reason for the ordinary seniority inference rather than requiring the employee to negate every operational burden. It remains a genuine evidentiary opportunity under ordinary summary-judgment principles, even for a bona fide system, not merely a chance to prove that the policy is a sham. Scalia and Thomas's contrary exclusion would foreclose that opportunity; Souter and Ginsburg's ordinary-inquiry approach would give seniority no comparable initial effect. The statute supports the qualified rule adopted here.

**Precedent treatment:** Hardison is used for the bounded seniority analogy; its Title VII setting and hardship threshold remain distinct. No prior ADA holding is overruled: this decision resolves the previously open seniority question under Title I.

### Remand applies the announced standard to the reviewed seniority claim

**Holding and operative rule:** The seniority-accommodation judgment is vacated and remanded so that the parties may seek summary judgment under the Court's standard, with Barnett able to establish special circumstances and US Airways able to assert its separate statutory hardship defense. Independent unreviewed accommodation grounds and the affirmed retaliation judgment retain their existing procedural status.

**Controlling explanation:** The lower courts applied different formulations: the District Court treated the system as establishing undue hardship, while the Court of Appeals placed seniority within a case-specific hardship inquiry. Neither applied the ordinary seniority inference together with the employee's opportunity to show special circumstances. The appropriate remedy is further consideration under the correct rule. Actual amendment practices, the character of exceptions and effects on employee expectations are application matters under that rule. The Court's limited seniority review supplies no basis to reopen the separate cargo, interactive-process or retaliation dispositions. A conflict with independently enforceable collective-bargaining obligations is outside the question presented by this noncontractual policy.

**Precedent treatment:** Toyota and Waffle House preserve the distinction between a statutory rule and proof of the remaining claim and remedy conditions; their separate coverage and enforcement holdings are unchanged.

- **Board of Trustees of the University of Alabama v. Garrett, February 21, 2001:** Preserves the distinct qualification, accommodation and demonstrated-hardship conditions; its state-employer enforcement holding supplies no seniority exemption.
- **Trans World Airlines, Inc. v. Hardison, 432 U.S. 63:** Supplies a limited analogy about seniority expectations. Its Title VII protection of seniority and more-than-de-minimis hardship threshold do not become the ADA's rule.
- **Sutton v. United Air Lines, Inc. and Murphy v. United Parcel Service, Inc., June 22, 1999; Toyota Motor Manufacturing, Kentucky, Inc. v. Williams, January 8, 2002:** Their actual-disability rules remain separate from qualification and accommodation; the seniority decision neither changes those rules nor adjudicates the disputed coverage-preservation matter.
- **EEOC v. Waffle House, Inc., January 15, 2002:** Its independent public-enforcement and remedy rules remain intact; permission to pursue statutory relief is distinct from establishing actual entitlement.
- **Olmstead v. L.C., June 22, 1999, and PGA TOUR, Inc. v. Martin, May 29, 2001:** Their respective Title II resource and Title III fundamental-alteration rules retain their own predicates. Neither replaces Title I's reasonable-accommodation and undue-hardship inquiries.

Under original ADA Title I, established seniority ordinarily makes a conflicting reassignment unreasonable, even under an employer-created system. The employee can establish special circumstances making an exception reasonable; that opportunity is substantive and nonexclusive. If reasonableness is established, the employer still bears the distinct significant-difficulty-or-expense defense. Neutrality alone does not immunize an employment policy, effectiveness alone does not establish reasonableness, and seniority is neither merely a hardship factor nor an absolute exemption. The rule takes effect April 29, 2002. Actual application and the remaining claim conditions proceed under these settled rules.
