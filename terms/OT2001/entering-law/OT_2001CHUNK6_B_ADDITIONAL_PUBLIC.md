### McIntyre, Executor of Estate of McIntyre, Deceased v. Ohio Elections Commission, No. 93-986

Merits decision, April 19, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 12, 1994; decided April 19, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the Supreme Court of Ohio, reviewing its September 22, 1993 judgment, 67 Ohio St.3d 391, sustaining an identification requirement as applied to Margaret McIntyre's leaflets opposing a school-tax levy.

Margaret distributed the leaflets on April 27 and 28, 1988; some identified her and some did not. Family assistance and placement on windshields do not establish an organization or campaign committee. The Commission imposed a $100 fine on March 30, 1990, after the March 30, 1989 complaint. The trial court held the statute unconstitutional as applied; the court of appeals reinstated the fine, and the Ohio Supreme Court affirmed. Margaret died during the litigation, and the executor of her estate continued this challenge. No fraud or falsity in her message was adjudicated. The questions are the scrutiny governing identification on this individual's political leaflets and whether Ohio's information and fraud-prevention interests justify this application.

Ohio Rev. Code Ann. §3599.09(A) (1988) provides:

> No person shall write, print, post, or distribute, or cause to be written, printed, posted, or distributed, a notice, placard, dodger, advertisement, sample ballot, or any other form of general publication which is designed to promote the nomination or election or defeat of a candidate, or to promote the adoption or defeat of any issue, or to influence the voters in any election, or make an expenditure for the purpose of financing political communications through newspapers, magazines, outdoor advertising facilities, direct mailings, or other similar types of general public political advertising, or through flyers, handbills, or other nonperiodical printed matter, unless there appears on such form of publication in a conspicuous place or is contained within said statement the name and residence or business address of the chairman, treasurer, or secretary of the organization issuing the same, or the person who issues, makes, or is responsible therefor. The disclaimer 'paid political advertisement' is not sufficient to meet the requirements of this division. When such publication is issued by the regularly constituted central or executive committee of a political party, organized as provided in Chapter 3517. of the Revised Code, it shall be sufficiently identified if it bears the name of the committee and its chairman or treasurer. No person, firm, or corporation shall print or reproduce any notice, placard, dodger, advertisement, sample ballot, or any other form of publication in violation of this section. This section does not apply to the transmittal of personal correspondence that is not reproduced by machine for general distribution.
>
> The secretary of state may, by rule, exempt, from the requirements of this division, printed matter and certain other kinds of printed communications such as campaign buttons, balloons, pencils, or like items, the size or nature of which makes it unreasonable to add an identification or disclaimer. The disclaimer or identification, when paid for by a campaign committee, shall be identified by the words 'paid for by' followed by the name and address of the campaign committee and the appropriate officer of the committee, identified by name and title.

The ordinary identification duty permits a residence or business address. The political-party committee alternative, personal-correspondence exception, discretionary small-item rulemaking permission, and campaign-committee paid-for-by requirement have distinct triggers. Personal correspondence is excepted only when not machine-reproduced for general distribution. The secretary may grant exemptions by rule; the listed objects are not automatically exempt and exemption is not mandatory. No particular implementing rule is established in this record. No established fact places these leaflets within a committee provision or exemption; that source limit is not a finding that no exemption rule existed. Subsection (B) is not adjudicated.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Supreme Court of Ohio's judgment is reversed, 8–1. The enforcement order and $100 fine imposed for these leaflets must be set aside.

The judgment is as applied to this individual's issue leaflets. It awards no damages and does not invalidate every subsection, candidate-finance report, committee disclaimer, organizational disclosure, or rule directed at fraudulent impersonation.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Compelled identification on these political leaflets requires exacting scrutiny

**Controlling proposition:** A content-defined requirement that an individual identify herself on ordinary political issue leaflets burdens core political expression, including the choice to speak anonymously. The State must satisfy exacting scrutiny by showing narrow tailoring to an overriding state interest; viewpoint neutrality or the election setting alone does not lower that standard.

**Authority:** Justice Stevens's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer: seven direct rationale votes. Justice Thomas concurs only in the judgment on his distinct anonymous-political-pamphlet ground; Justice Scalia dissents.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the author's choice to omit her identity is protected as part of political expression. Talley protects anonymous handbills and recognizes the place of anonymous publication in public discussion. Ohio's requirement selects publications by their electoral subject and conditions their circulation on identification. It is therefore a content-based burden on the individual's communication, not simply a neutral rule for administering a ballot. Burson preserves demanding scrutiny for campaign-specific restrictions even though its different polling-place judgment had no shared validity rationale. Ohio invokes the modest size of the identification obligation and its election-related purpose. Neither answers whether the State may impose the burden on this ordinary issue leaflet. The Court applies exacting scrutiny requiring narrow tailoring to an overriding interest. It does not decide that anonymity is always benign or require proof that this speaker faced retaliation. Candidate finance reports, organizational attribution, coordinated spending, and fraudulent impersonation present distinct questions left outside this holding.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Talley v. California, 362 U.S. 60: applies protection of anonymous handbills to this individual political leaflet; the Court does not establish immunity for fraudulent communications.
- Burson v. Freeman, May 26, 1992: applies the independently majority-supported scrutiny distinction; its fractured polling-place validity judgment does not settle this leaflet requirement.
- Anderson v. Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780: distinguishes the election-administration setting; it does not reduce scrutiny for every election-related speech restriction.

##### The asserted voter-information interest does not justify this identification demand

**Controlling proposition:** A general interest in helping voters evaluate a source does not justify requiring this ordinary individual to place her name and residence or business address on her independently produced, truthful political leaflet. The Court leaves candidate finance, campaign committees, organizational spending, and other materially distinct disclosure settings for separate analysis.

**Authority:** Justice Stevens's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer: seven direct rationale votes. Justice Thomas concurs only in the judgment on his distinct anonymous-political-pamphlet ground; Justice Scalia dissents.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that voters' interest in source information does not justify this direct condition on an ordinary citizen's political pamphlet. Talley protects the decision to publish without personal attribution; an author need not establish that identity has no possible informational value. Ohio relies on Buckley's campaign-finance disclosure and Bellotti's discussion of identifying corporate advertising. Those sources concern different mechanisms and interests. A report identifying financial participation in a campaign is not the same requirement as putting a private individual's name and address on every issue leaflet she distributes. Corporate spending likewise supplies no automatic rule for this record, which establishes neither a campaign organization nor coordinated financing. The Court does not hold that source information is useless or that all disclosure is forbidden. It holds that the general informational rationale is inadequate for the burden imposed here. Ohio's committee alternatives and personal-correspondence exception do not transform Margaret McIntyre's independently produced leaflets into those different statutory categories or justify this sanction.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Talley v. California, 362 U.S. 60: applies protection for the ordinary author's choice of anonymous publication.
- Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1: distinguishes campaign-finance reporting and its specific interests; those disclosure holdings remain undisturbed.
- First National Bank of Boston v. Bellotti, 435 U.S. 765: distinguishes corporate-advertising source information; its observation does not resolve this private individual's leaflet.

##### Fraud prevention does not justify this application to truthful independent advocacy

**Controlling proposition:** Although preventing electoral fraud is a weighty interest, Ohio may not impose this categorical identification requirement on truthful, independently produced individual issue leaflets without the necessary narrow fit to that interest. The enforcement order and $100 fine must be set aside; properly tailored fraud, impersonation, and materially different disclosure rules remain available.

**Authority:** Justice Stevens's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer: seven direct rationale votes. Justice Thomas concurs only in the judgment on his distinct anonymous-political-pamphlet ground; Justice Scalia dissents.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Ohio's fraud-prevention interest does not supply the missing fit. Talley protects anonymous expression without preventing laws directed at false or fraudulent communications. Ohio's identification requirement reaches ordinary truthful advocacy regardless of any finding of deception, false attribution, or corrupt financing. The record contains no adjudicated fraud in McIntyre's leaflet. The State argues that identification helps investigators find wrongdoers and deters unlawful claims before they affect an election. That practical benefit does not explain why every individual issue pamphlet must carry a name and address when existing laws address fraud and false attribution more directly. The personal-correspondence exception and permission to exempt impractical small items do not cure this application to printed leaflets; no implementing exemption rule resolves the fine presented. The Court neither assumes that anonymity guarantees honest speech nor invalidates all disclosure provisions. It sets aside this sanction, preserves properly tailored enforcement against actual abuse, and leaves organizational and financial-reporting requirements outside the judgment.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Talley v. California, 362 U.S. 60: applies anonymous-publication protection while distinguishing measures directed at fraud.
- Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1: leaves candidate-finance disclosure and corruption-related interests intact rather than treating them as dispositive of this ordinary leaflet.
- First National Bank of Boston v. Bellotti, 435 U.S. 765: leaves materially different corporate source-disclosure questions outside the individual sanction decided here.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Talley's anonymous-handbill protection applies to this individual's political issue leaflet. Burson's majority-supported scrutiny distinction remains separate from its fractured validity judgment concerning polling-place environs. Anderson's election-administration framework does not decide this direct speech burden. Buckley's campaign-finance disclosure holdings and Bellotti's corporate-advertising discussion concern materially different interests and mechanisms and remain undisturbed. No precedent is overruled.

Effective April 19, 1995, the State may not enforce this name-and-residence-or-business-address requirement against the truthful, independently produced individual political leaflets at issue. Seven Justices directly adopt exacting scrutiny and the inadequate information and fraud-prevention justifications; Thomas supplies a separate eighth judgment vote. No Marks synthesis is needed. The decision leaves candidate and committee disclosures, coordinated expenditures, large financial contributions, organizational spending, fraudulent impersonation, and appropriately tailored fraud regulation outside the adjudicated application. It does not create an automatic personal-correspondence or small-item exemption, decide an unproduced exemption rule, or adjudicate subsection (B).

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Thomas concurs in the judgment. Talley's protection and the historical expressive tradition it recognizes support an individual's anonymous political pamphlet. The asserted justification cannot sustain a penalty imposed solely for missing identification on this ordinary leaflet. He does not adopt the Court's modern value-balancing and content-based-scrutiny methodology as his own ground. His agreement in this judgment therefore adds no vote to the Court's complete scrutiny explanation, and his writing does not invalidate every financing or organizational disclosure rule.

Justice Scalia dissents. Election-specific attribution is a limited accountability requirement that helps voters identify a communication's source and assists enforcement against electoral wrongdoing. Buckley's disclosure principles and Bellotti's source-information discussion support that safeguard in his view. Talley's general prohibition covering all anonymous handbills is distinguishable from Ohio's election category. He recognizes the expressive burden but regards the State's justification as sufficient here; he would sustain the fine. He preserves appropriate protection where a speaker establishes threats or reprisals, without finding that McIntyre experienced either. He does not endorse a general power to forbid anonymous discussion outside the election setting.

#### Source Notes

The [official United States Reports, 514 U.S. 334](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep514/usrep514334/usrep514334.pdf), caption and page 340, supplies executor capacity; pages 338–339 note 3 reproduce the quoted 1988 subsection (A), including its qualifications. These locations support objective party and statutory facts. No executor first name, death date, substitution date, implementing exemption rule, personal threat, fraud finding, or founding-era source detail is inferred. The complete subsection (A) quotation is statutory text, not a quotation from this Court's reasoning; subsection (B) is not represented as supplied.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Supreme Court of Ohio's judgment is reversed. Further proceedings must set aside the enforcement order and $100 fine against Margaret McIntyre, whose estate's executor maintains the challenge. Ohio may enforce valid disclosure rules consistent with the holding. The Court orders no damages, replacement election procedure, blanket statutory repeal, or retained Supreme Court proceeding.

### Buckley v. American Constitutional Law Foundation, Inc., No. 97-930

Merits decision, January 12, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 14, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the Tenth Circuit, reviewing 120 F.3d 1092. The Court reviews the registered-voter qualification, the personal-name component of the circulation badge, and final and monthly reports identifying individual paid circulators and their compensation. Other petition-administration and reporting fields retain the limits stated below.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**Common authority.** The six-Justice Court coalition identified above controls Parts I, III-A and III-B. O'Connor and Breyer's additional full joins give Part II eight rationale votes. Thomas concurs in each invalidity judgment on separate grounds; those votes do not join the Court's methodology. Each component carries its own limits.

##### I. Registered-voter condition

**Holding and operative rule.** Colorado may not require otherwise qualified resident-adult initiative circulators to be registered voters on this record. Excluding otherwise qualified speakers because they are unregistered substantially burdens protected petition circulation, while the retained identification and enforcement mechanisms serve traceability more closely.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that a person need not register to vote to engage in the protected political conversation involved in circulating a petition. Meyer v. Grant protects that interaction and the available pool of speakers. Colorado's condition excludes at least 400,000 otherwise qualified but unregistered voters. Easy enrollment does not eliminate that reduction or show that registration adequately measures residence, availability for process or honesty.

The State's strongest argument treats registration as an administrable qualification in a legally operative election process. Anderson and the governing Burdick rule permit appropriately fitted administration, but require attention to the actual burden and interest. Here the sworn identifying and residential-address affidavit, signature review, subpoenas and targeted fraud sanctions address traceability without the registration exclusion. Timmons's different candidate-nomination setting does not make this speaker restriction minor. The Court invalidates the registration condition, not the age floor, circulation period or residence rule whose validity is not presented. An affidavit cannot preserve the invalid condition merely by requiring a registration recital.

##### II. Personal name displayed during solicitation

**Holding and operative rule.** Colorado may not compel every circulator to display a personal name on a badge during protected face-to-face petition advocacy when the retained post-circulation identification and enforcement measures serve the asserted interests with materially less speech burden. The personal-name component is invalid; standalone paid-or-volunteer and employer-information requirements are not adjudicated.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that compelled personal attribution at the expressive encounter requires a justification fitted to that burden. McIntyre protects individual political advocacy against inadequately justified identification demands; Meyer protects petition circulation, and Riley prevents payment from stripping a solicitor of speech protection. The trial record shows deterrence from the personal badge, particularly when the subject is unpopular. That is a burden on the actual political conversation.

Even assuming fraud prevention and detection are compelling interests, Colorado has not shown the necessary fit. The identifying affidavit, signature assurances, review, protests and fraud penalties allow officials to identify and investigate circulators without exposing their names to everyone approached. The later public affidavit differs in timing and accessibility from a worn badge; the Court creates no anonymity from lawful investigation. A witness's interest in reporting misconduct does not establish the needed additional benefit of this blanket personal-name display. This holding does not decide standalone paid-status, sponsor or employer attribution, nor invalidate lawful post-circulation verification.

##### III-A. Final individual-circulator reports

**Holding and operative rule.** The challenged final-report requirements publishing each paid circulator's identity, address and individual compensation fail the required substantial relationship to Colorado's asserted informational and enforcement interests on this record. Disclosure of initiative proponents and aggregate spending remains distinct; the decision creates no categorical exemption from initiative-finance disclosure.

**Controlling explanation.** Buckley v. Valeo permits campaign-finance disclosure substantially related to important governmental interests. That inquiry requires attention to what the information reveals. Proponent identity and aggregate expenditures reveal who finances an initiative and the extent of paid efforts. The individual messenger is not, for that reason, a financing source or a candidate contributor. Colorado has not demonstrated the added benefit necessary to justify publishing every paid circulator's identity and compensation, without a threshold separating consequential financing from any paid messenger.

The State argues that payee information permits tracing and checking spending beyond aggregate totals. That is a legitimate subject of inquiry, but the retained sponsor, aggregate, amount-per-signature and verification mechanisms make the asserted incremental need insufficient here. Final reporting is less immediate than a badge; it still requires its own disclosure justification. Kentucky Right to Life's payer-identification rule concerns a different financing function and does not answer this demand for every messenger's information. A narrower individual-reporting rule supported under the governing scrutiny remains open.

##### III-B. Monthly individual-circulator reports

**Holding and operative rule.** Colorado's challenged monthly requirements publishing each paid circulator's identity, residential and business addresses and individual amounts paid or owed are likewise invalid on this record. The Court decides that individual-information package, not the independent validity of every other monthly-report field.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court separately considers publication during the circulation campaign. Timely information can expose abuse and inform voters before qualification; that argument gives monthly reporting work that a final report cannot always perform. But timing does not cure the insufficient relationship between identifying each messenger and the State's demonstrated financing or enforcement need. It also exposes the individual while the political effort is underway. The retained sponsor and aggregate disclosures and enforcement mechanisms remain material alternatives. The decision does not hold that referendum disclosure has no informational value or that every individual challenge requires proof of reprisals.

The Tenth Circuit invalidated the monthly subsection as a whole. The Court affirms the reviewed invalidity of individual circulator disclosures and leaves the independent validity of its other prescriptions, including proponents and measure identity, undecided. It neither orders those fields conclusively reinstated nor independently declares them unconstitutional. Any ordinary remedial or severability implementation must respect the existing judgment and the scope actually decided.

**Common limits and remedy.** The reviewed registration, personal-name and individual-disclosure invalidations stand. Age, circulation timing, verification affidavits, fraud and signature-review mechanisms are outside the reviewed invalidations and remain governed by their own terms. This decision does not invalidate standalone paid-status or sponsor attribution, create a replacement initiative code, grant damages, destroy public records, authorize fraud or decide every future disclosure rule. Unreviewed requirements receive no automatic new constitutional endorsement.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Meyer v. Grant, 486 U.S. 414:** Applied to the protected political interaction and reduction of available speakers; ordinary properly fitted administration remains possible.
- **McIntyre, April 19, 1995:** Extended to the personal-name demand during live petition advocacy at the stated narrow-fit scope; candidate-finance, organizational and independent post-circulation settings retain their distinctions.
- **Riley v. National Federation of the Blind, 487 U.S. 781:** Applied to protection of paid solicitation and the burden of compelled speech during the encounter; its discussion supplies no adjudication of the reserved badge fields.
- **Anderson v. Celebrezze and Burdick, June 8, 1992:** Their burden-sensitive administrative inquiry remains; the Court applies the actual petition-speech burdens rather than treating every election regulation as equally severe.
- **Timmons, April 28, 1997:** Distinguished as a candidate-nomination setting; it supplies no automatic lesser review for these speaker restrictions.
- **Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1:** Its disclosure scrutiny and legitimate financial-information interests are applied to each report; no categorical initiative-disclosure immunity is adopted.
- **Kentucky Right to Life, September 14, 1998:** Its payer and source-identification distinctions remain; identification of the financer does not automatically justify identifying each messenger.

#### Law After Decision

Registration cannot be used to exclude otherwise qualified resident-adult circulators; personal names cannot be compelled on the challenged solicitation badge; and the reviewed final and monthly individual-disclosure packages cannot be enforced. Governing law preserves distinct authority for lawful administration, verification, fraud controls, sponsor and aggregate disclosures. The independent validity and remedial treatment of unreviewed badge and monthly fields remain outside this judgment. No fractured-opinion or narrowest-ground synthesis is needed: each Court holding has at least six direct joins, and the badge holding has eight.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Thomas, concurring in the judgment.** Thomas agrees that every reviewed restriction is invalid but applies traditional strict scrutiny to the direct speech and severe associational burdens. Registration reduces the Meyer-protected speaker pool without a narrowly fitted traceability justification. The personal-name badge directly intrudes on core political speech and fails narrow tailoring even assuming a compelling anti-fraud interest. The reports' effect on circulation is more attenuated; compelled disclosure independently burdens association and belief. Singling out paid circulators poorly fits general identification, and the need to expose each individual's identity and compensation has not been demonstrated. Monthly exposure is considered separately from final reporting. As an alternative, the challenged packages also fail Buckley's more forgiving substantial-relationship test. He does not displace Buckley's surviving disclosure holdings, adopt the Court's entire method, invalidate retained reports or decide reserved fields.

**O'Connor, joined by Breyer, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** They join Part II's complete personal-name-badge explanation: direct intrusion into political conversation requires narrow tailoring, and the retained affidavit and enforcement tools defeat this demand on this record. They would sustain registration and the challenged final and monthly reports. Under Anderson–Burdick, readily available enrollment provides an administrable connection to the formal petition process without the severe burden found by the Court; Meyer does not make every qualification equivalent to a ban on paid circulators. Reports are indirect financial-disclosure burdens rather than badges worn during persuasion. Identifying payees and individual disbursements can permit checking campaign activity beyond aggregate sums, and contemporaneous monthly information can expose abuse before the vote. They find the relationship sufficient even under more demanding disclosure review. They acknowledge the excluded unregistered speakers and possible exposure costs, but reach different applications. They propose no automatic admission rule, universal minimum scrutiny or independent disposition of reserved fields.

The personal-name, paid-status, employer, final-report and monthly-report elements are distinct. The reviewed reporting question concerns individual circulator identity and compensation; the independent validity of all other monthly fields remains undecided.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The reviewed invalidations and corresponding injunctive protection remain in force. The Court neither independently validates nor invalidates unreviewed badge and monthly-report fields. The actual lower judgment, the precise review scope and ordinary remedial law govern their remaining implementation. No replacement system or further Supreme Court proceeding is ordered.

### Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools, No. 90-918

Merits decision, February 26, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 11, 1991. On writ of certiorari to the Eleventh Circuit, 911 F.2d 617. The district court dismissed the complaint on the categorical ground that Title IX did not permit damages; the Eleventh Circuit affirmed.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

The questions are whether compensatory damages are available in the recognized Title IX private action for intentional discrimination, and whether the Spending Clause notice principle bars that relief.

Franklin seeks reversal and ordinary litigation of her damages claim. She alleges repeated sexual harassment and abuse by teacher/coach Andrew Hill while she attended a federally funded high school, official awareness and failure to act, discouragement of her complaint and closure of the investigation following Hill’s conditional resignation. These are allegations, not judicial findings. She no longer attends the school. The lower court relied on its Title VI precedent Drayden and a Spending Clause limitation; Judge Johnson regarded Drayden as controlling without reaching the additional constitutional characterization. Respondents and the United States defend the damages bar. Backpay and prospective student relief would not redress the pleaded completed injury.

#### Judgment

The Eleventh Circuit is reversed, 9–0, and the case is remanded for further proceedings on the damages claim.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Categorical bar to Title IX damages | Reversed and remanded; 9-0 | Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | No opposing vote | Compensatory relief may be sought for proved intentional discrimination; liability, attribution, causation and amount remain to be adjudicated. |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | White | Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter | Supports reversal and remand | Appropriate-remedies rule and its application to intentional Title IX discrimination, including the Spending Clause objection. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Compensatory relief in the established Title IX action

**Controlling proposition:** When a cognizable federal statutory cause of action exists, courts may award appropriate relief absent clear congressional direction limiting it; recognizing that remedy does not create a new cause of action. In Title IX’s established private action, compensatory damages are available for proved intentional discrimination, subject to proof of recipient responsibility, causation and compensable loss.

**Authority:** White’s opinion, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy and Souter; seven Justices control the remedial rule and application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Title IX does not confine an established private action to remedies incapable of redressing a completed personal injury. Cannon supplies the cause of action; Bell v. Hood and Davis v. Passman distinguish that authority to sue from the appropriate relief a court may grant. Borak confirms that statutory silence about remedy does not itself confine a recognized action to a declaration. Guardians and Darrone support compensatory relief for intentional violations of closely related discrimination statutes. Congress’s 1986 reference to remedies at law and in equity, followed by legislation preserving private enforcement, supplies no direction to bar damages. Recognizing compensation therefore implements the existing action rather than expanding judicial power to hear a new one. Franklin’s departure from school shows why backpay and prospective relief cannot compensate the pleaded past injury. The allegations still require adjudication; the Court awards nothing and decides neither a final recipient-attribution rule nor punitive or unintended-effects liability.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Cannon v. University of Chicago*, 441 U.S. 677 — applied as the established Title IX cause of action; not reopened.
- *Bell v. Hood*, 327 U.S. 678 — applied for appropriate remedies in an existing cognizable action, absent a congressional limit.
- *Davis v. Passman*, 442 U.S. 228 — applied to separate existence of an action from available relief.
- *J. I. Case Co. v. Borak*, 377 U.S. 426 — applied against limiting relief merely because the statute does not enumerate the remedy.
- *Guardians Association v. Civil Service Commission*, 463 U.S. 582 — its intentional-discrimination remedial support is applied without treating that decision as having a single majority rationale.
- *Consolidated Rail Corp. v. Darrone*, 465 U.S. 624 — analogous monetary relief supports the availability of compensation.

##### Notice does not bar damages for the pleaded intentional violation

**Controlling proposition:** The Spending Clause notice principle does not categorically bar compensatory relief for an intentional violation of Title IX’s express prohibition of sex discrimination within its established private action. Ambiguous funding conditions, unintentional effects and the separate requirements for attributing misconduct to a recipient are not resolved.

**Authority:** White’s opinion, joined by the same seven Justices, rejects the distinct funding-notice ground offered to sustain dismissal.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the asserted lack of notice cannot sustain this categorical damages bar. Pennhurst requires fair notice of conditions imposed on recipients of federal funds; it does not authorize intentional breach of a clear nondiscrimination condition while limiting the injured person to ineffective prospective relief. Title IX expressly forbids sex discrimination, and Cannon supplies the established enforcement action. The pleaded sexual harassment and abuse describe intentional discrimination of the kind addressed by Meritor Savings Bank’s explanation of sex-based harassment, without deciding here when a school is responsible for a teacher’s conduct. Darrone also defeats the premise that a spending-based statute necessarily excludes a monetary award for intentional violations. The objection cannot succeed merely by describing compensation as payment from a recipient’s treasury. This conclusion rests on the enacted condition and existing enforcement law, not a general power to redress every injury. Genuinely ambiguous conditions, proof of institutional responsibility and unintended discriminatory effects remain distinct questions.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman*, 451 U.S. 1 — its funding-condition notice rule is preserved; it does not bar relief for this alleged intentional violation.
- *Meritor Savings Bank, FSB v. Vinson*, 477 U.S. 57 — its identification of sex-based harassment informs the pleaded discrimination; no Title VII attribution formula is imported.
- *Consolidated Rail Corp. v. Darrone*, 465 U.S. 624 — applied against a categorical objection to monetary remedies under spending legislation.
- *Cannon v. University of Chicago*, 441 U.S. 677 — the established action supplies the enforcement premise for the notice analysis.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Effective February 26, appropriate relief in a cognizable statutory action is not barred by remedial silence alone, and Title IX permits compensation for intentional discrimination. Before this decision, the cause existed but the presented damages bar was unresolved; afterward, that bar and the categorical Spending Clause objection are rejected. No new implied action, punitive remedy, liability for unintended effects, automatic institutional responsibility, or finding against the school is established.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia, joined by Thomas, agrees that the damages claim may proceed because the 1986 enactment acknowledges legal as well as equitable remedies. They decline to presume every remedy merely from a cause of action that judges previously implied, and do not join the Court’s broader remedial reasoning.

Dates and procedural background: [503 U.S. 60 (1992), pp. 60–78](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-503/pdf/USREPORTS-503-60.pdf).

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court review ends with reversal and remand. The damages complaint returns for ordinary proceedings on liability, attribution, causation and compensable loss. No amount is awarded and no prospective school-supervision order is entered.

**End of entry: Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools, merits decision, February 26, 1992.**

### Davis v. Monroe County Board of Education, No. 97-843

Merits decision, May 24, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 12, 1999. On certiorari to the en banc Eleventh Circuit, 120 F.3d 1390, which affirmed dismissal of the district's Title IX damages claim. The question is whether the funding recipient may be responsible for its own deliberate response to serious student-on-student sexual harassment within its substantial control.

A fifth-grade student allegedly endured repeated touching and sexual propositions by a classmate over several months. The child and her mother reportedly informed teachers and the principal; officials allegedly failed to protect her while the conduct continued and interfered with education. The harasser's later guilty plea is a procedural recital, not proof of every civil element or earlier institutional notice. The case concerns pleading sufficiency, not findings of recipient fault or damages.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

Only the dismissal of the appealed Title IX damages claim against the district is reversed. The recipient claim may proceed under the conditions explained below. Knowledge, culpability, control, sex-based educational deprivation, causation, compensable injury and any supported defense remain to be proved or decided. No compulsory trial, damages award, particular punishment, individual-capacity recovery or reopening of unrelated claims is ordered.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | Ginsburg | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Breyer | Supports reversal | Sufficient actual-notice recipient-fault route for controlled peer harassment, including control, culpability, causation and limits. |
| Opinion of the Court, Part II | Ginsburg | Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Breyer | Supplies the governing educational-harm standard | Severe-or-pervasive, objectively abusive and actually experienced environment impairing educational access or benefit. |
| Opinion of the Court, Part III | Ginsburg | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Breyer | Supports ordinary reversal/remand | Sufficiency of these allegations and the bounded remedy; no final element or liability finding. |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Supports reversal; joins Parts I and III | Requires severe, pervasive and objectively offensive harassment; separately proposes willfully blind reporting-failure responsibility. |
| Dissent | Kennedy | Scalia, Thomas | Would affirm the appealed dismissal | Statutory attribution and funding-notice objection to nonagent peer responsibility. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### A school's own culpable response can support damages for controlled peer harassment

**Holding and operative rule:** A Title IX funding recipient may be liable for its own response to peer harassment because of sex when an official empowered to correct the harassment or its substantial danger has actual notice, the recipient substantially controls both the harasser and the relevant educational context, and deliberate and unreasonable disregard of that danger causally contributes to further harassment or vulnerability producing educational deprivation and compensable injury. A peer's attendance does not impute the peer's acts to the recipient; knowledge, culpability, control, sex-based discrimination and causal loss are independent requirements.

**Authority:** The six-Justice opinion in Ginsburg's Part I establishes this sufficient route.

**Controlling explanation:** Franklin permits compensation for proved intentional Title IX discrimination but leaves recipient responsibility to be established. Kinman's actual-notice route identifies a recipient's own deliberate disregard of a known educational danger. The Court extends that sufficient route to peer conduct where the school has substantial remedial control; it does not claim Kinman already decided peers. The school's knowing, culpable continuation of educational deprivation supplies the institutional wrong, answering the objection that a student cannot personally bind the funding recipient. Mere discipline authority or an ineffective result does not suffice. Actual notice must reach an official empowered to correct the danger; generic constructive notice, the harasser's own knowledge and technical policy noncompliance do not establish this route. A reasonable response is lawful even if harassment continues, and courts prescribe neither a selected punishment nor a victim's preferred remedy. Constitutional and statutory limits on discipline inform control and response. Gebser's school-delegated teacher authority does not transfer to classmates; its conjunctive teacher route and the other teacher holdings remain intact. The Court neither adopts ordinary negligence or general agency law nor rejects every distinct theory of direct official discrimination. Receipt of assistance remains separately necessary under NCAA v. Smith. Causal injury cannot be inferred solely from harassment predating legally sufficient recipient notice.

##### Educational deprivation follows the contextual severe-or-pervasive standard

**Holding and operative rule:** The harassment must be sufficiently severe or pervasive to create an objectively abusive educational environment actually experienced as abusive and impair educational access or benefit in its full context. Sexually charged conduct or offense alone does not establish discrimination because of sex, the required educational harm or the independent institutional elements.

**Authority:** The five-Justice opinion in Ginsburg's Part II controls. Stone-Zsela does not join this disjunctive threshold; no combination of separate rationales is needed for its authority.

**Controlling explanation:** Kinman adopted severe-or-pervasive, objectively abusive and actually experienced educational harassment without mandatory psychiatric injury, measurable grade loss or a rigid list of factors. Its reliance on Meritor and Harris preserves attention to welcome, objective and experienced abuse, and the actual setting. The peer relationship affects the contextual application and recipient-control inquiry; it does not silently replace the substantive standard with a requirement that conduct be both severe and pervasive. Age, relationship, educational setting and actual effect may matter without becoming a mandatory checklist. Neither physical exclusion nor total loss of schooling is invariably required, and no one proxy is sufficient by itself. Ordinary teasing or offense fails without the substantive and institutional requirements. No universal rule determines whether one incident always suffices or never can. These allegations involve repeated touching and propositions over months with educational interference, a materially different claimed environment. The Court decides no ultimate fact and does not treat every unwelcome interaction as actionable discrimination.

##### The pleaded recipient claim warrants ordinary further proceedings

**Holding and operative rule:** Allegations of repeated school-setting harassment, reports including to the principal, continued conduct, deliberately inadequate protection and resulting educational interference state a claim for examination under the sufficient recipient-fault route. The categorical dismissal must be reversed, but an absent element, reasonable response, inadequate control or notice, missing causation or a supported defense may defeat recovery on the developed record.

**Authority:** The six-Justice opinion in Part III controls this allegation-stage application and remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The alleged response concerns the district's own use of authority after reports of a known danger, rather than automatic responsibility for the classmate. The asserted repeated abuse and educational disruption permit the necessary elements to be tested. Allegations that reports reached the principal do not finally establish every official's corrective power, knowledge or opportunity; the criminal plea supplies no earlier notice or complete causal showing. The plaintiff must prove the institutional and substantive conditions, including postnotice causal injury. Existing compensation principles therefore require ordinary further adjudication, not immediate damages or judicial control of discipline. No compulsory trial follows if ordinary procedure establishes a missing element or valid defense. The decision reaches only the appealed district Title IX claim and leaves unrelated and individual claims outside the mandate.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools is applied to available compensation for proved intentional discrimination, with recipient responsibility, causation and loss still distinct.
- Kinman v. Omaha Public School District is extended at its sufficient actual-notice/culpable-disregard route to substantially controlled peer conduct. Its severe-or-pervasive contextual educational-harm standard remains; the peer extension is stated expressly.
- Gebser v. Lago Vista Independent School District retains the limited teacher-authority-plus-recipient-fault conjunction and its rejection of exclusivity in teacher cases. Neither delegated teacher power nor that complete route is imputed to peers.
- NCAA v. Smith preserves the distinct federal-assistance recipient requirement; economic connection or control alone does not establish receipt.
- Meritor Savings Bank v. Vinson informs the distinction between participation and welcome through Kinman without importing employment attribution wholesale.
- Harris v. Forklift Systems informs the contextual objective-and-experienced abuse inquiry through Kinman, without mandatory psychiatric injury or any indispensable factor.
- Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman retains its funding-notice principle; the controlling recipient-own-wrong route concerns intentional educational discrimination, not automatic liability for a third party.

#### Law After Decision

A funding recipient's own actually informed, deliberately unreasonable response to peer harassment within substantial control can support damages when all independent discrimination, educational-harm and causal-injury elements are established. The controlling environment threshold remains severe or pervasive, objectively abusive and actually experienced as abusive, with impaired educational access or benefit. Teacher-attribution rules remain separately bounded. Ordinary negligence, policy breach, unsuccessful reasonable correction and peer attendance alone do not establish liability. The Chief's separate reporting-failure extension does not become a controlling peer rule, and no complete alternative-attribution or defense code is decided.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and in the judgment:** The Chief joins the actual-notice recipient-fault route and the reversal on these allegations. The school is answerable for its own deliberately indifferent failure to use available authority after reliable notice, with meaningful control, clearly inadequate response, sex-based educational deprivation and causal injury independently proved. Kinman's sufficient notice route remains available, while Gebser's teacher authority cannot simply be attributed to a classmate. He would require severe, pervasive and objectively offensive peer harassment depriving the victim of equal educational access, and therefore withholds assent from the Court's severe-or-pervasive threshold. He additionally would recognize the recipient's willfully blind, deliberately indifferent reporting failure causing continuation as a sufficient institutional ground. That proposed reporting-failure extension is his separate position, not the Court's peer rule. Neither misconduct nor negligent supervision alone suffices; officials retain disciplinary discretion so long as the response is not deliberately indifferent. He requires no particular punishment and makes no liability finding.

**Kennedy, joined by Scalia and Thomas, dissenting:** They would affirm the appealed peer-harassment dismissal because Title IX's funding condition does not sufficiently establish this institutional attribution. Franklin makes compensation available after recipient discrimination is shown; it does not identify the recipient's wrong. Kinman concerned a teacher, and Gebser's additional route still requires materially enabling delegated teacher authority. A nonagent pupil does not have that link. These Justices address the Court's own-response theory directly: disciplinary power and knowing nonresponse to a student's discrimination do not, on their view, supply the necessary statutory duty or clear funding notice. Legal limits on school control and the breadth of the proposed obligation reinforce the objection. They neither find the allegations false nor approve the conduct. A supported claim that school officials themselves enforce rules differently because of sex remains distinct; they decide no such new claim. Existing teacher law and remedies for established recipient discrimination remain intact.

#### Source Notes

The reported pleading posture, argument and decision dates appear in the [official United States Reports, 526 U.S. 629–686](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/526bv.pdf), and the judgment below is 120 F.3d 1390. Exact warnings, each official's power, grievance efficacy and postnotice causal events remain allegation-stage matters; the allegations are not findings on a complete evidentiary record. Title IX's 20 U.S.C. §1681(a) prohibition, the existing recipient requirement and the stated limits govern the claim.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The appealed Title IX dismissal is reversed and the case returns for ordinary proceedings under the controlling recipient and educational-harm rules. The lower courts determine the actual facts, elements, supported defenses and any compensable injury. No damages, compulsory trial, disciplinary measure, reopening of unrelated claims or additional Supreme Court date is directed.

**End of entry: Davis v. Monroe County Board of Education, merits decision, May 24, 1999.**

### Kolstad v. American Dental Association, No. 98-208

Merits decision, June 22, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 1, 1999; decided June 22, 1999. On writ of certiorari to the D.C. Circuit, 139 F.3d 958 (en banc), concerning the circumstances permitting punitive damages under Title VII for unlawful intentional discrimination. Punitive employer attribution is part of that remedial inquiry.

A jury found intentional sex discrimination in the 1992 selection of Thomas Spangler over Kolstad for the legislative-director position and awarded $52,718 in backpay on October 26, 1995. The district court withheld punitive submission. The panel sustained liability but reversed punitive dismissal; the en banc court reinstated punitive withholding under an added egregiousness requirement. Liability and backpay are outside the component reopened here. William Allen made the final selection after consultation with Washington office head Leonard Wheat; neither precise executive title nor punitive managerial status is conclusively determined in this review.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Vacated and remanded on punitive withholding, 8–1.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Thomas.

The intentional-discrimination verdict and $52,718 backpay judgment remain intact. The common vacatur does not resolve the divided positions on whether to direct a punitive trial; neither more specific remand position has five votes.

O’Connor delivers one signed lead opinion. Agreement on vacatur is distinct from agreement on agency or a trial instruction; Thomas’s alternative agency agreement is distinct from his judgment vote.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Punitive eligibility turns on culpability toward federal rights, not an additional egregious act

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1981a(b)(1) permits punitive damages for intentional discrimination committed with malice or reckless indifference to the claimant’s federally protected rights. At minimum, the actor must discriminate in the face of a perceived risk that the conduct violates federal law; egregious conduct may prove that state of mind but is not a separate indispensable element.

**Authority:** Part I of O’Connor’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: eight direct joins. Thomas does not join this construction.

**Controlling explanation:** The statute separates intentional discrimination from culpability concerning federal rights. Negligence about legal duties does not suffice. Conversely, its words do not require an additional aggravated act after the specified mental state is shown. Smith v. Wade explains that reckless or callous indifference can support punitive consideration without a universally higher evidentiary threshold. Hazen Paper distinguishes an intentional employment decision from knowing or reckless illegality and rejects added outrageousness under the ADEA. That analogy respects the statutes’ different remedies; Title VII’s own punitive language controls here.

The additional showing remains meaningful where the relevant prohibition is unknown, the liability theory is novel or poorly recognized, or the actor reasonably believes a statutory exception permits the decision. Those are possible distinctions, not findings about the Association. Obviousness, concealment and knowledgeable implementation may prove the required state circumstantially. A direct admission, personal insult, pattern or wholly different category of evidence is unnecessary. The separate punitive tier therefore retains work without the en banc court’s extra severity requirement. Its judgment withholding punitive consideration under that requirement cannot stand.

##### Submission to the jury, a punitive award and its amount are separate decisions

**Holding and operative rule:** The court must assess whether the evidence and reasonable employee-favorable inferences permit the applicable punitive predicates. Neither a liability verdict nor rejection of the employer’s explanation automatically requires punitive submission; if submission is warranted, the jury retains discretion whether to award punitive damages.

**Authority:** The eight-Justice Part I directly adopts these distinctions. Thomas independently accepts the screening and discretionary-award principles in his separate opinion, while applying his different aggravated-culpability standard.

**Controlling explanation:** The same evidence may support liability and punitive culpability without making their legal elements identical. Hicks permits circumstantial discrimination proof without requiring a separate category of direct evidence; it does not make punitive culpability automatic. Smith preserves the jury’s discretion. Statutory caps and ordinary review of an amount do not replace eligibility, and no new clear-and-convincing requirement is imposed. The existing intentional-discrimination verdict and $52,718 backpay judgment cannot be retried through the punitive inquiry. No §1981a compensatory award, punitive sum, employee-count cap determination or constitutional amount ruling is entered.

##### Managerial punitive attribution depends on actual authority and scope

**Holding and operative rule:** Ordinary Title VII liability does not automatically establish punitive attribution. For the managerial route at issue, actual entrusted authority and discretion, together with action within the scope of employment, supply the relevant agency connection; a title alone or an invariant top-management requirement does not decide it.

**Authority:** Part II-A of O’Connor’s opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas: five direct supporters of this managerial rule. Thomas supports this rule as an alternative if his statutory objection is rejected, while opposing vacatur. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer do not join this new dispositive agency section.

**Controlling explanation:** Meritor requires agency principles adapted to Title VII, rather than automatic attribution of every employee’s act. The managerial-and-scope reference in Restatement (Second) of Agency §217C and Torts §909 distinguishes punitive responsibility from ordinary compensation. A delegated promotion decision does not necessarily fall outside employment scope because its discriminatory manner violates an employer prohibition. The relevant inquiry concerns actual managerial responsibility; it supplies no final finding about Allen’s or Wheat’s punitive category. Neither formal title nor the final signature alone resolves the part each actor played. The Court adopts no universal respondeat-superior rule and leaves unused authorization, hiring and ratification routes undecided rather than converting the managerial route into their exclusive substitute.

##### Actual good-faith compliance limits managerial vicarious punishment

**Holding and operative rule:** An employer is not vicariously liable for punitive damages through the managerial-and-scope route when the discriminatory decision is contrary to its meaningful, reasonably designed and actually implemented good-faith efforts to comply with Title VII. A paper policy, employee nonreporting or a good-faith label alone does not establish that condition; this limited rule neither removes ordinary liability and compensation nor excuses the employer’s own discriminatory policy or independently established own wrongdoing.

**Authority:** Part II-B, joined by the same five Justices—Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. These are direct substantive joins; Thomas’s vote to affirm is unchanged. No majority adopts a universal allocation or standard of proof for this limitation.

**Controlling explanation:** Punishment raises a distinct concern about a principal liable only through another person’s act. Meritor permits adaptation of common-law agency to Title VII’s statutory purpose, including encouraging real preventive compliance. The managerial reference rule supplies the starting point; the Court now adopts this limited punitive modification. Faragher and Ellerth preserved agency and remedial questions and did not previously establish it.

Congress expressly addressed good faith in §1981a(a)(3), barring the section’s damages for specified disability-accommodation practices when the covered entity demonstrates efforts, in consultation with the person who has identified the accommodation need, to identify and make an equally effective accommodation without undue hardship. That provision does not directly govern this sex-discrimination promotion claim. Its bar on the section’s damages differs from the present question of vicarious punitive attribution; it does not eliminate that agency inquiry or itself supply this rule’s burden or predicates.

Implementation matters. Training, reporting, enforcement and corrective operation may bear on genuine compliance, without becoming a mandatory checklist, a universal reporting duty or a guarantee of success. No effective program, preserved defense or compliance failure is found here. Other independently established bases for the employer’s own wrongdoing are not absorbed into the managerial exception. The four Justices joining Part II-C reserve a general burden allocation; Stone-Zsela separately requires an employer showing. Their difference produces no new Court rule placing a duty on the employee to disprove compliance and no presumption that effective measures exist.

##### The punitive judgment is vacated; specific trial and application positions remain distinct

**Holding and operative rule:** The en banc judgment sustaining punitive withholding is vacated and the punitive component returns through the D.C. Circuit for further proceedings under the controlling statutory and agency rules. The liability verdict and backpay remain intact; no punitive award is directed.

**Authority:** Part III-A and the common judgment have eight supporters: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Thomas would affirm. No five-Justice coalition adopts either of the competing more specific application instructions below.

**Controlling explanation:** The erroneous extra-egregiousness rule cannot sustain the punitive judgment. That shared conclusion supplies vacatur and return; it does not erase the different positions on what must occur next. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer would direct a punitive trial on the cumulative evidence and do not join the new compliance limitation. Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia and Kennedy would leave culpability, managerial attribution and any properly available compliance issue to ordinary application before directing submission. Thomas supports the agency rules alternatively but supplies no fifth vote for that remand position.

The controlling propositions govern the returned case, while neither a present punitive-trial command nor a five-Justice determination of whole-record insufficiency is announced. The mandate creates no entitlement to amended pleadings, new discovery or a special compliance hearing. Genuinely preserved independent matters retain ordinary procedural treatment; no preservation or factual success is assumed. The expired Resnick decree is not a current violation or automatically admissible punitive evidence. Instatement and fees receive no new ruling, and there is no reconsideration of established liability.

#### Precedent Treatment

Smith v. Wade informs the disjunctive reckless-indifference standard, permissible overlap in evidence and discretionary punitive award. Hazen Paper Co. v. Biggins is applied by analogy to distinguish knowing or reckless illegality from an intentional employment act without adding outrage; its ADEA rule and different remedy remain unchanged. Thurston and Richland Shoe retain their proper willfulness roles through that analogy.

**Docket or dockets:** No. 97-930; 120 F.3d 1092.
**Decided:** 1999-01-12.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stevens's Court opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg throughout. Those six directly support Parts I, III-A and III-B. O'Connor and Breyer additionally join Part II, giving that component eight full rationale votes. Thomas concurs in every invalidity judgment on separate grounds; his judgment votes are not full joins in the Court's methodology. The rule's limits accompany each component.

##### I. Registered-voter condition

**Holding:** Colorado may not require otherwise qualified resident-adult initiative circulators to be registered voters on this record. Excluding otherwise qualified speakers because they are unregistered substantially burdens protected petition circulation, while the retained identification and enforcement mechanisms serve traceability more closely.

##### II. Personal name displayed during solicitation

**Holding:** Colorado may not compel every circulator to display a personal name on a badge during protected face-to-face petition advocacy when the retained post-circulation identification and enforcement measures serve the asserted interests with materially less speech burden. The personal-name component is invalid; standalone paid-or-volunteer and employer-information requirements are not adjudicated.

##### III-A. Final individual-circulator reports

**Holding:** The challenged final-report requirements publishing each paid circulator's identity, address and individual compensation fail the required substantial relationship to Colorado's asserted informational and enforcement interests on this record. Disclosure of initiative proponents and aggregate spending remains distinct; the decision creates no categorical exemption from initiative-finance disclosure.

##### III-B. Monthly individual-circulator reports

**Holding:** Colorado's challenged monthly requirements publishing each paid circulator's identity, residential and business addresses and individual amounts paid or owed are likewise invalid on this record. The Court decides that individual-information package, not the independent validity of every other monthly-report field.

**Limits and operative remedy:** The reviewed registration, personal-name and individual-disclosure invalidations stand. Age, circulation timing, verification affidavits, fraud and signature-review mechanisms are outside the reviewed invalidations and remain governed by their own terms. This decision does not invalidate standalone paid-status or sponsor attribution, create a replacement initiative code, grant damages, destroy public records, authorize fraud or decide every future disclosure rule. Unreviewed requirements receive no automatic new constitutional endorsement.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- **Meyer v. Grant, 486 U.S. 414:** Applied to the protected political interaction and reduction of available speakers; ordinary properly fitted administration remains possible.
- **McIntyre, April 19, 1995:** Extended to the personal-name demand during live petition advocacy at the stated narrow-fit scope; candidate-finance, organizational and independent post-circulation settings retain their distinctions.
- **Riley v. National Federation of the Blind, 487 U.S. 781:** Applied to protection of paid solicitation and the burden of compelled speech during the encounter; its discussion supplies no adjudication of the reserved badge fields.
- **Anderson v. Celebrezze and Burdick, June 8, 1992:** Their burden-sensitive administrative inquiry remains; the Court applies the actual petition-speech burdens rather than treating every election regulation as equally severe.
- **Timmons, April 28, 1997:** Distinguished as a candidate-nomination setting; it supplies no automatic lesser review for these speaker restrictions.
- **Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1:** Its disclosure scrutiny and legitimate financial-information interests are applied to each report; no categorical initiative-disclosure immunity is adopted.
- **Kentucky Right to Life, September 14, 1998:** Its payer and source-identification distinctions remain; identification of the financer does not automatically justify identifying each messenger.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Registration cannot be used to exclude otherwise qualified resident-adult circulators; personal names cannot be compelled on the challenged solicitation badge; and the reviewed final and monthly individual-disclosure packages cannot be enforced. Governing law preserves distinct authority for lawful administration, verification, fraud controls, sponsor and aggregate disclosures. The independent validity and remedial treatment of unreviewed badge and monthly fields remain outside this judgment. No fractured-opinion or narrowest-ground synthesis is needed: each Court holding has at least six direct joins, and the badge holding has eight.
