# TRW Inc. v. Andrews; No. 00-1045 — 2001-11-13

[Authoritative Record](../records/trw-inc-v-andrews_merits_2001-11-13.md)

## Event

TRW Inc. v. Andrews, No. 00-1045. Argued October 9, 2001; decided November 13, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, which reversed partial summary judgment treating the first two disputed credit disclosures as untimely. The question is whether the then-operative Fair Credit Reporting Act supplies a general discovery extension beyond its express misrepresentation exception.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the Ninth Circuit's general-discovery ruling and remands for further proceedings consistent with the statutory construction.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The first two disclosure claims cannot proceed on the rejected general discovery theory. The mandate supplies no entitlement to reopen the newly presented actual-damages theory, amend to allege misrepresentation, or obtain an accrual or exception hearing. Any request in further proceedings remains subject to the mandate and ordinary preservation, waiver and procedural rules. Independent judgments outside this review retain their force.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Breyer | Supports the judgment | Entire confined statutory opinion |
| Concurrence | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Joins the Court's opinion and judgment | Element-specific accrual; no affirmative reopening entitlement |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Scalia | Thomas | Supports the judgment on a distinct ground | Traditional complete-cause rule and rejection of a general discovery presumption |

## Holdings

**No general discovery extension under the governing FCRA.** The version of 15 U.S.C. §1681p governing these claims requires suit within two years from when liability arises. Its express extension permits suit within two years after discovery only when the defendant materially and willfully misrepresented information the Act required it to disclose to the individual and that information was material to establishing that defendant's liability to the individual; ignorance of a violation alone satisfies neither those conditions nor an additional general exception.

**Authority:** Ginsburg's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer, controls this statutory construction, the exception's application and the limited nonreach below. Scalia and Thomas agree with the judgment on their separately stated ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the specified discovery exception must retain its restrictive operation. A general discovery extension would allow a claimant to bypass its conditions and make it largely redundant. Andrus supports giving an enumerated exception its limiting effect; Menasche requires the enacted parts to perform their assigned work. The words identifying when liability arises do not transform liability into discovered liability. The practical difficulty of detecting identity misuse therefore cannot supply an exception Congress did not enact. Holmberg's fraud and concealment rule and Urie's latent-disease setting do not establish a universal discovery presumption overriding this text. Rotella and Klehr retain their distinct RICO questions and qualifications; they do not rewrite the FCRA. Andrews's complaint does not allege the material and willful misrepresentation of required information specified by §1681p, so that express extension supplies no basis for these claims.

**Precedent treatment:** Holmberg and Urie are distinguished at their stated scopes; Andrus and Menasche are applied to the statutory structure; the distinct Rotella and Klehr rules are preserved.

**Question left undecided.** The Court does not decide Andrews's argument that liability first arose upon actual loss rather than disclosure, because that distinct argument was first presented here and was not raised or briefed below. This nonreach does not resolve the substantive requirements of every willful claim, establish the asserted loss date, or authorize reopening the theory on remand. The confined statutory ruling corrects the general-discovery judgment without deciding a universal federal accrual rule.

## Precedent Treatment

- Andrus v. Glover Construction Co., 446 U.S. 608 (1980): applied for the significance of an expressly enumerated exception.
- United States v. Menasche, 348 U.S. 528 (1955): applied to preserve operative work for the exception's enacted conditions.
- Holmberg v. Armbrecht, 327 U.S. 392 (1946): its fraud/concealment rule is preserved; it does not establish the universal discovery rule urged here.
- Urie v. Thompson, 337 U.S. 163 (1949): its latent-disease setting does not govern this express FCRA structure.
- Rotella v. Wood (February 23, 2000) and Klehr v. A. O. Smith Corp. (June 19, 1997): their distinct RICO accrual, new-injury, unprovable-damage and concealment components remain at their actual scope.

## Law After Decision

The governing version of §1681p has no general discovery extension. Its precise statutory misrepresentation exception remains available when its conditions are met. The Court's ruling does not decide the first-presented actual-loss theory or create a universal accrual rule for federal statutes.

## Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, concurring.** Stone-Zsela joins the Court's statutory ruling and judgment. He adds that liability arises when the elements necessary for the particular statutory claim exist: rejection of general discovery does not erase the need for an element-specific inquiry or an independently applicable preserved exception. Rotella rejects substitution of a later discovery event for the governing accrual inquiry, but does not make disclosure, discovery and actionable injury interchangeable. Properly preserved questions remain subject to ordinary adjudication. The newly presented theory receives no affirmative permission to reopen merely because the Court leaves it undecided.

**Scalia, joined by Thomas, concurring in the judgment.** They begin with the traditional rule that a limitations period ordinarily runs when the cause of action is complete and enforceable, rather than when the claimant learns of it. A general discovery presumption does not follow from Congress's failure expressly to reject discovery. Congress can prescribe a different start and did enact a confined exception here. Traditional fraud/concealment protection and the latent-injury setting remain separate rules. They object that leaving the general background uncertain invites uncertainty under other statutes. Their agreement with reversal and remand does not establish that every disclosure completes every FCRA claim or decide the newly raised actual-loss theory.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns for proceedings consistent with rejection of the general discovery theory, subject to the stated mandate and ordinary procedural limits. The independent dispositions concerning other claims remain intact.

## Source Notes

15 U.S.C. §§1681e(a), 1681n and 1681p (versions governing the challenged disclosures); TRW Inc. v. Andrews, No. 00-1045; Andrews v. TRW Inc., 225 F.3d 1063 (9th Cir. 2000).

# Dusenbery v. United States; No. 00-6567 — 2002-01-08

[Authoritative Record](../records/dusenbery-v-united-states_merits_2002-01-08.md)

## Event

Dusenbery v. United States, No. 00-6567. Merits decision, January 8, 2002. Argued October 29, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Sixth Circuit, which sustained the forfeiture-notice procedure. The FBI sent certified notice to the federal institution holding Dusenbery; notice mailed November 7, 1988 identified the forfeiture, contest process and December 19 claim deadline. An employee acknowledged postal receipt. Testimony concerning the relevant period described collection, certified-mail logging, receipt by the responsible unit employee and ordinary delivery to the inmate. The old log was unavailable under a one-year retention practice, and actual personal receipt was not established. The question is whether this process satisfied due process without proof of actual receipt.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument or submission and decision.

## Public Action

Affirm, 5–4.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 5–4.** Supporting: Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. Opposing: Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. The challenged notice determination stands on the reasonably calculated institutional route. The dissenters would vacate and remand for the consequences of the insufficiently demonstrated final custodial handoff.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stone-Zsela | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Supports affirmance | Entire proved-route and notice opinion |
| Dissent | Stevens | Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Would vacate and remand | Mullane framework accepted; final custodial-handoff application rejected |

## Holdings

### A proved reasonable custodial route can satisfy due process without proof of actual receipt

**Holding and operative rule:** Due process requires means reasonably calculated in the known circumstances to convey forfeiture notice and afford a meaningful opportunity to object, not an absolute guarantee of actual receipt. For a known confined owner, certified delivery coupled with an established, reasonably functioning internal distribution route to that inmate can satisfy the obligation; a signature at the institution's entrance alone is not universally sufficient.

**Authority:** The Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; five direct joins support the standard, its application and affirmance. The four dissenters accept Mullane and reject a guaranteed-receipt requirement but do not join the adequacy application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the proved route was reasonably directed to Dusenbery. Mullane asks prospectively whether the means reasonably communicate in the actual circumstances. The government demonstrated more than dispatch to a prison address: employees collected certified mail, logged it, obtained the receiving unit employee's signature and ordinarily delivered it to inmates. The district court credited that relevant-period practice evidence. The missing old log does not establish an unreasonable system, and testimony about ordinary operation can establish the route without proof of this item's final receipt.

The contrary argument correctly emphasizes the government's exclusive custodial control. The inquiry therefore follows the onward route rather than ending at employee receipt. But neither Mullane nor Mennonite makes a particular additional verification measure or guaranteed success indispensable on this evidence. A feasible improvement alone does not invalidate the earlier reasonable procedure. Other attempted addresses supply no independent cure if the custodial route were deficient.

West Covina's protection of usable notice remains; its rejection of an individualized guide to published law does not excuse a deficient delivery method. Mathews's general calibration does not displace the settled notice-specific standard. This decision concerns the established procedure for the relevant period and the absence of an established known failure; it does not validate every prison mailing or resolve a different record involving unreliability or known nondelivery.

**Precedent treatment:** Mullane and Mennonite are applied to the actual route; West Covina's distinction between usable notice and legal instruction remains; Good's different pre-seizure requirement is not altered.

## Precedent Treatment

- Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co., 339 U.S. 306: reasonable communication in the known circumstances governs; neither ritual dispatch nor guaranteed receipt is sufficient as a universal formula.
- Mennonite Board of Missions v. Adams, 462 U.S. 791: the practical-notice principle is applied without an absolute successful-receipt requirement.
- City of West Covina v. Perkins, No. 97-1230: usable notice and adequate access remain distinct from individualized legal instruction; that decision does not settle this delivery application.
- Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S. 319: its general procedural calibration does not replace Mullane's notice-specific standard.
- United States v. James Daniel Good Real Property: its distinct civil real-property pre-seizure protections remain intact.

## Law After Decision

A known prisoner must receive notice through a reasonably calculated custodial route. The proved certified-mail and internal-distribution process satisfies that standard without proof of actual receipt. The holding is procedure-based and leaves genuinely unreliable or known-failure situations to their own records.

## Separate Writings

Stevens dissents, joined by Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, and agrees that due process does not guarantee receipt. It concludes that the government has not sufficiently demonstrated the final custodial handoff. A confined owner cannot supervise or replace the government's intermediary, so Mullane requires practical attention to how notice reaches that person, including reasonably available safeguards. Evidence of transmission between employees does not adequately establish the final inmate-directed protection. Greene and Mennonite emphasize practical communication; Good and West Covina preserve a meaningful opportunity to respond. The dissent does not infer nonreceipt from the denial or the missing old records, invent a failure rate, demand one particular signature form, or apply later prison practices retroactively. It would set aside the notice judgment and return for the proper consequences of the defect, without treating inadequate notice as proof of ownership or automatic entitlement to the property.

## Procedure After Action

The Sixth Circuit's determination sustaining the challenged forfeiture notice stands. The judgment establishes constitutional adequacy of the proved procedure, without finding Dusenbery's actual receipt.

## Source Notes

U.S. Const. amend. V; Dusenbery v. United States, No. 00-6567, 534 U.S. 161; J.A. 18–21, 36–38, 46–54, 59–62 and 67–71, as identified in the United States' merits presentation, for the relevant-period mailing practice. Personal receipt is not established.

# Great-West Life & Annuity Insurance Co. v. Knudson; No. 99-1786 — 2002-01-08

[Authoritative Record](../records/great-west-life-annuity-insurance-co-v-knudson_merits_2002-01-08.md)

## Event

Great-West Life & Annuity Insurance Co. v. Knudson, No. 99-1786. Merits decision, January 8, 2002. Argued October 1, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, which affirmed rejection of the plan's reimbursement claim. The question is whether this demand for repayment of medical benefits after a tort settlement is appropriate equitable relief under ERISA §502(a)(3). The settlement property was distributed to a special-needs trust and an attorney rather than held by the named beneficiaries in the manner required by the asserted property remedy. The claim seeks personal payment from those beneficiaries; the plan's contractual entitlement and the available federal remedy are distinct questions.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument or submission and decision.

## Public Action

Affirm, 5–4.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 5–4.** Supporting: Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. Opposing: Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. The particular reimbursement demand is unavailable under §502(a)(3), and its dismissal stands. The four dissenters would vacate the statutory-remedy rejection and remand for plan construction and the necessary restitution predicates.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Thomas | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy | Supports affirmance | Entire traditional-remedy and property-defendant rationale |
| Dissent | Souter | Stevens, Ginsburg, Breyer | Would vacate and remand | Gain-restoration restitution category, with plan entitlement and actual remedial predicates left for adjudication |

## Holdings

### A contractual payment obligation does not establish an equitable remedy

**Holding and operative rule:** ERISA §502(a)(3) authorizes appropriate traditionally equitable relief, not personal payment of a past-due contractual debt merely because a claimant calls its demand an injunction, specific performance or restitution. A constructive trust or equitable lien ordinarily requires specifically identifiable property or proceeds, an equitable entitlement and the required connection to the defendant holding that property, subject to applicable defenses.

**Authority:** The Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; five direct joins establish the entire rule and its application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the remedy's actual predicates, not its label, determine whether it is equitable. Mertens gives the statutory limitation independent force by distinguishing traditional equitable categories from all relief once obtainable in a trust court. An injunction to pay an accrued debt does not avoid that distinction. Equity's exceptional use of specific performance to prevent otherwise inadequately remediable future losses does not supply this demand for an accrued sum.

Restitution has legal and equitable forms. The property-based form ordinarily restores identifiable property or proceeds from the defendant who holds them; an obligation to repay a benefit from other assets is different. Here settlement property went to the separate trust and attorney. The named beneficiaries' asserted contractual debt cannot substitute for the connection required by the remedy invoked against them. A charge against a beneficiary's trust interest also differs from the personal money judgment sought here. This is not a universal tracing formula for every equitable remedy; an accounting for profits has distinct predicates and is not this claim.

The contrary gain-restoration argument correctly recognizes that some restitution is equitable. It does not establish that this particular demand satisfies the traditional remedy's conditions. Harris Trust confirms genuine recipient equity within its own predicates and defenses, while Varity preserves appropriate individual relief within statutory categories. Neither turns every omitted remedy into authorized equity. The statutory ground sustains dismissal without deciding the alternative interpretation of the plan terms.

**Precedent treatment:** Mertens's traditional-category construction governs; Harris Trust's recipient equity is preserved rather than converted to personal collection; Varity retains its individual-equity channel and ordinary adequacy limits.

## Precedent Treatment

- Mertens v. Hewitt Associates, 508 U.S. 248: its traditional-equitable-category construction controls; neither its compensation exclusion nor preserved genuine monetary equity is displaced.
- Harris Trust and Savings Bank v. Salomon Smith Barney Inc., 530 U.S. 238: equitable recipient relief remains available on its actual predicates and defenses; it supplies neither this missing property connection nor a universal tracing formula.
- Varity Corp. v. Howe, 516 U.S. 489: appropriate individual equity remains within statutory categories and ordinary adequate-remedy limits.

## Law After Decision

Section 502(a)(3) does not authorize this demand for personal payment merely from the reimbursement clause. Genuine property-based equity remains available when its defendant, property and defense requirements are met. A different claim against another holder requires its own proper pleading, jurisdiction, process and equitable basis; the present judgment decides no such claim.

## Separate Writings

Souter dissents, joined by Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer, and would recognize the asserted return of benefits from a third-party recovery as the traditionally equitable restitution category. The claim is measured by alleged gain rather than the plan-loss compensation rejected in Mertens. A category need have been typically available in equity, not exclusive to equity, and direct possession should not control every restorative theory. Varity supports giving the redress provision practical effect within its limits, while Harris Trust recognizes that appropriate restoration can reach beyond fiduciaries. The government's trust-beneficiary analogy supports that characterization without establishing new property facts. The dissent would remove the categorical remedy bar and return plan interpretation and the actual remedial predicates for adjudication. It would award no reimbursement amount and would not adjudicate absent recipients' property.

## Procedure After Action

The dismissal of the reviewed federal reimbursement claim stands. An independently available claim against an appropriate holder remains subject to ordinary procedural rules; this judgment orders neither amendment nor joinder of the trust and decides no absent recipient's property rights.

## Source Notes

29 U.S.C. §1132(a)(3); Great-West Life & Annuity Insurance Co. v. Knudson, No. 99-1786, 534 U.S. 204, for settlement allocation, claim and procedural record.

# Searles v. Van Bebber; 251 F.3d 869 (10th Cir. 2001) — 2002-01-08

[Authoritative Record](../records/searles-v-van-bebber_merits_2002-01-08.md)

## Event

Searles v. Van Bebber. Merits decision, January 8, 2002. Review of the Tenth Circuit judgment reported at 251 F.3d 869. The questions concern original 42 U.S.C. §1997e(e)'s restriction on mental or emotional compensation absent physical injury, the availability of nominal and punitive damages for the proved religious-diet violation, the consequence of a punitive instruction tied to invalid compensation; and the preserved court-access and due-process objection to the restriction at this remedial scope. The jury found liability, no physical injury and a separate culpable state of mind supporting punitive eligibility; it awarded $3,650 compensation and $42,500 punishment and returned verdicts for the other officials.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument or submission and decision.

## Public Action

Affirm the reviewed remedial judgment, 9–0.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 9–0, at the reviewed remedial scope.** Each component is supported by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

| Judgment component | Disposition | Operative remedy |
|---|---|---|
| Mental or emotional compensation | Affirm vacatur | The $3,650 award is vacated |
| Nominal damages | Affirm entitlement | Enter one dollar for the established violation |
| Punitive availability and culpability | Affirm | Punitive relief remains available; the separate culpability finding survives |
| Punitive amount | Affirm vacatur and remand | The $42,500 amount is vacated; determine punishment under instructions independent of the invalid compensation |
| Dependent attorney fees | Affirm reconsideration | Reconsider fees in light of lawful recovery |
| Preserved court-access and due-process objection | Reject | No additional constitutional relief; Stone-Zsela agrees in this component's disposition without joining its rationale |

Constitutional liability, the no-physical-injury finding, the punitive-culpability finding and the judgments for the other officials remain intact.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Scalia | Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer throughout; Stone-Zsela except the constitutional-objection holding | All nine support every judgment component | All nine join statutory compensation, nominal and punitive holdings and remedies; eight Associates join court-access and due-process rationale; Stone-Zsela agrees only in that component's disposition |

## Holdings

### The named injury restriction bars the compensatory award, not every remedy

**Holding and operative rule:** Original 42 U.S.C. §1997e(e) bars an action by a prisoner confined at filing for mental or emotional injury suffered in custody without the required prior showing of physical injury. That restriction bars the actual distress compensation awarded here even though the underlying wrong is constitutional; it does not erase the violation or compensation-independent remedies.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join this statutory holding in the Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress restricted recovery for a named injury category. Harris v. Garner establishes the confinement-at-filing trigger and the mental-or-emotional-injury limit; it does not convert that limit into immunity from every remedy. The jury found no physical injury, and the $3,650 award compensated mental or emotional harm. The religious source of the right does not exempt that recovery. Carey and Stachura also prevent compensation based merely on an abstract valuation of the constitutional right. The all-damages reading goes too far because it ignores the distinct functions of nominal recognition and punishment, while the contrary compensation argument cannot save a jury award for the expressly barred injury.

**Precedent treatment:** Harris is applied at its actual injury-and-filing scope; Carey and Stachura retain the distinction between a violation and compensable loss.

### Nominal damages recognize the established violation

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1997e(e) does not bar nominal damages recognizing an established constitutional violation without compensable injury. On the preserved violation and lack of compensable injury here, the plaintiff is entitled to one dollar.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join this holding in the Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** Nominal relief does not compensate the mental or emotional injury named in the statute. Carey permits recognition of an established constitutional wrong without inventing injury or valuing the right itself as compensatory loss. The preserved liability verdict therefore requires the one-dollar award even though the distress compensation cannot stand.

**Precedent treatment:** Carey v. Piphus supplies nominal recognition; Stachura's prohibition on abstract-right compensation remains.

### Punitive eligibility survives, but the amount requires a lawful redetermination

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1997e(e) does not categorically bar punitive damages for conduct satisfying Smith v. Wade's evil-motive-or-intent or reckless-or-callous-indifference standard. Punishment remains discretionary even when that culpability is established; an amount expressly tied to invalid compensation must be redetermined independently, preserving separate liability and culpability findings.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join this holding and remedy in the Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** Punitive damages punish culpable conduct and deter its repetition; they do not compensate distress. Smith supplies a distinct remedial predicate rather than a rule that punitive fault must invariably exceed every substantive violation standard. The jury separately made the required culpability finding, which survives the compensation defect. But the instruction expressly relied on the invalid compensatory award in setting the punitive amount. That linkage prevents preserving $42,500 as an independently sound calculation. The corrected proceeding concerns amount, without retrying established liability or punitive culpability. A positive punitive award is not compelled, punishment may not reproduce barred compensation, and no automatic punitive-to-nominal ratio applies. Fees must then be reconsidered in light of the lawful recovery.

**Precedent treatment:** Smith v. Wade's culpability requirement and discretionary punishment are applied; the distinct remedial functions recognized in Carey and Stachura prevent relabeling barred compensation.

### The preserved constitutional objection fails at this remedial scope

**Holding and operative rule:** Applying the physical-injury restriction to this distress award does not deny access to court or due process where the established right remains enforceable through nominal relief and punitive relief on its distinct predicates. This ruling resolves the preserved challenge in these circumstances, without validating every possible application of the statute.

**Authority:** Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer join this portion of the Opinion of the Court. Stone-Zsela agrees in its disposition only.

**Controlling explanation:** The statute restricts one injury-based recovery rather than withdrawing adjudication of the underlying right. Searles has established liability, receives nominal relief and retains punitive relief subject to its lawful standard. At this actual scope, the constitutional objection therefore supplies no reason to restore barred compensation or upset the preserved remedial distinctions.

**Precedent treatment:** Harris's bounded restriction and Carey and Smith's distinct remedies operate together; no remedy-wide immunity follows.

## Precedent Treatment

- Harris v. Garner: confinement at filing and the restriction to mental or emotional injury remain controlling; the nominal and punitive questions it left open are resolved here.
- Carey v. Piphus, 435 U.S. 247: nominal damages recognize a proved wrong without compensable injury.
- Memphis Community School District v. Stachura, 477 U.S. 299: abstract value of a constitutional right cannot substitute for actual compensatory injury.
- Smith v. Wade, 461 U.S. 30: evil motive or intent, or reckless or callous indifference, supplies punitive culpability; a qualifying award remains discretionary.

## Law After Decision

Original section 1997e(e) bars the named distress compensation absent physical injury while preserving nominal recognition and punitive relief under their own predicates. Established liability and punitive culpability survive a separate damages-calculation defect. The lawful punitive amount and consequential fee decision remain ordinary remedial work under those settled rules.

## Procedure After Action

The lower court must enter one dollar nominal damages, redetermine punitive amount under instructions independent of invalid compensation, and reconsider fees. The constitutional-liability, no-physical-injury and punitive-culpability findings and verdicts for the other officials remain intact.

# Thomas v. Chicago Park District; No. 00-1249; 534 U.S. 316. — 2002-01-15

[Authoritative Record](../records/thomas-v-chicago-park-district_merits_2002-01-15.md)

## Event

Thomas v. Chicago Park District, No. 00-1249; 534 U.S. 316. Merits decision, January 15, 2002; October Term 2001, chunk 2. Argued December 3, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Seventh Circuit. The ordinance requires permits for events involving more than fifty people and activities including amplified sound. Applicants seeking expressive events, including marijuana-policy advocacy, challenge its facial standards and procedures. The Seventh Circuit sustained the system. The questions concern the full censorship safeguards, constrained discretion, and timely action and review.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

## Public Action

The Seventh Circuit judgment rejecting the facial challenge is affirmed.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The Seventh Circuit's rejection of the facial challenge stands, and the permit ordinance remains operative. Properly presented challenges to discriminatory, delayed or content-based administration retain their ordinary availability; no particular permit denial is adjudicated here.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Scalia | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Affirms the facial judgment | All three holdings, thirteen qualified denial grounds, actual deadlines/review and limits on administration |

## Holdings

### I. Neutral park coordination does not require the entire censorship procedure

**Holding and operative rule:** A genuinely content-neutral permit system for allocating shared park space and coordinating activities need not adopt Freedman's entire procedure for censorship of expressive content, including a requirement that the government initiate judicial proceedings and bear the censorship burden after each denial. Such a system still requires real constraints on discretion, bounded administrative action and meaningful access to review; a logistical label alone does not establish validity.

**Authority:** Justice Scalia's opinion is joined throughout by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine directly adopt this and the following two holdings with their qualifications.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the safeguards must address the function officials may exercise. Freedman concerns official censorship of acceptable expressive content. Allocating park uses under definite scheduling, capacity and safety rules presents a different recurring risk. Cox recognizes neutral coordination of shared public space; Ward requires actual content independence rather than a benign label. The need to obtain a permit does not itself erase that distinction.

The challengers correctly stress that delay and favoritism can suppress speech even without an express message category. The answer is not to presume good administration, but to examine the authorized criteria and the concrete timing and review protections. Here those protections constrain park management rather than authorize approval of ideas. Requiring the District to commence a judicial case after every scheduling refusal would import a censorship procedure without its predicate. Freedman and FW/PBS remain in force in their own settings. Because the full censorship procedure does not apply, the Court need not decide whether Freedman's expeditious-review requirement there demands prompt commencement of proceedings or a prompt final judicial determination.

### II. The ordinance confines denial to objective management grounds

**Holding and operative rule:** The ordinance's two qualified prior-conduct predicates and eleven further grounds, set out below, sufficiently confine denial to neutral park-management subjects for this facial challenge. It permits denial on a listed ground but does not require denial whenever a ground occurs; permission to excuse a disqualifying circumstance authorizes neither an invented ground nor selective treatment by viewpoint.

**Authority:** The unanimous Court opinion adopts this standards determination and its limits.

**Denial grounds governed by this holding:**

| Ordinance basis | Permitted ground, with its condition |
|---|---|
| Prior conduct | To the extent permitted by law, prior material misrepresentation about the nature or scope of a permitted event, or violation of an earlier permit's terms, by the applicant or the person represented. |
| 1 | An application, including required attachments and submissions, is incompletely filled out or executed. |
| 2 | The application fee was not tendered with the application, or the user fee, indemnification agreement, insurance certificate or security deposit was not tendered within the General Superintendent's prescribed times. |
| 3 | The application contains a material falsehood or misrepresentation. |
| 4 | The applicant lacks legal capacity to contract or to sue and be sued. |
| 5 | The applicant or represented person has unpaid prior damage to District property or another outstanding unpaid District debt. |

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the grounds must be understood with the receipt-order requirement, written reasons and review. Forsyth, Shuttlesworth and City of Lakewood require definite limits on expressive licensing rather than confidence in official goodwill. These provisions identify the matters officials may assess and permit a reviewing court to identify a refusal resting on some different reason.

The strongest objection concerns the judgment involved in assessing danger, applying cross-references or forgiving defects. But permission to decline denial does not confer permission to invent a disqualifying reason. Nor must every technical defect compel refusal to avoid facial invalidity. The criteria remain neutral management limits; they do not authorize message approval, viewpoint favoritism or an audience-hostility burden. Forsyth's separate rule against hostility-based fees continues to govern any covered fee. This case does not adjudicate an unpresented fee amount or validate every financial condition in every application. A materially different or discriminatory administration remains subject to challenge on its own facts, without a new numerical-pattern prerequisite to proving viewpoint discrimination.

### III. The actual deadlines, reasons and review support the facial judgment

**Holding and operative rule:** The District must decide within fourteen days unless written notice extends the period for fourteen additional days; a denial must state its grounds in writing, propose feasible cures, and suggest alternative times or places when based on an earlier competing application. An applicant has seven days to appeal in writing to the General Superintendent, who must act within seven days, followed by available state common-law certiorari review; together with the objective grounds, these protections sustain the ordinance against the facial challenge.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join this procedural-sufficiency holding and affirmance.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that these actual protections answer the presented structural objection without a universal rule for every neutral permit system. Receipt order governs processing; the bounded decision period prevents open-ended administrative withholding; written reasons identify the decision for challenge; feasible correction and alternative scheduling address curable defects; and administrative and judicial review provide avenues to contest an improper refusal. Content neutrality alone would not cure unrestricted discretion or ineffective review.

No automatic-issuance rule is established on this record, and no fixed court-adjudication deadline is supplied. The Court adds neither. It also does not resolve an actual late application or discriminatory denial absent a proper claim and record. Those are applications of continuing First Amendment constraints, not a license for indefinite delay. The Seventh Circuit's facial judgment is affirmed, leaving the ordinance operative while preserving properly presented claims about actual administration.

- **Freedman v. Maryland, 380 U.S. 51:** Distinguished as content censorship; its safeguards remain effective in that setting, and the meaning of its judicial-timing requirement is unnecessary to resolve here.
- **FW/PBS, Inc. v. Dallas, 493 U.S. 215:** Its application of censorship-related safeguards to the licensing mechanism there is preserved; it does not make this neutral park procedure identical.
- **Forsyth County v. Nationalist Movement, 505 U.S. 123:** Applied for objective limits on licensing and preserved for its independent prohibition of audience-hostility charges; no fee amount is approved here.
- **Cox v. New Hampshire, 312 U.S. 569:** Applied for lawful neutral coordination of shared public space, subject to nondiscriminatory administration.
- **Ward v. Rock Against Racism, 491 U.S. 781:** Applied for genuine content-independent time, place and manner management rather than acceptance of a stated benign purpose alone.

- **Hill v. Colorado, June 28, 2000, and Schenck v. Pro-Choice Network, February 19, 1997:** Their actual-mechanism and practical-scope limits remain; neither creates blanket immunity for purportedly neutral restraints.

Genuinely neutral park coordination may use the ordinance's objective grounds, bounded administrative timetable, written reasons, correction and alternative-scheduling provisions, and review without requiring the District to initiate a censorship proceeding after each denial. Permissive forgiveness of a listed defect does not itself create facially unbounded discretion. The same structure remains subject to meaningful review and to properly presented challenges to viewpoint discrimination, actual delay or other unauthorized administration. No automatic-issuance rule or universal court-decision deadline is established.

# Swierkiewicz v. Sorema N.A.; No. 00-1853 — 2002-02-26

[Authoritative Record](../records/swierkiewicz-v-sorema-na_merits_2002-02-26.md)

## Event

Swierkiewicz v. Sorema N.A., No. 00-1853; 534 U.S. 506. Merits decision, February 26, 2002; October Term 2001, chunk 3. Argued January 15, 2002. On writ of certiorari to the Second Circuit, which affirmed dismissal of Title VII and ADEA claims because the complaint failed to establish the McDonnell Douglas prima facie case. The question is whether that evidentiary framework supplies an additional pleading prerequisite. The complaint identifies demotion, discharge, national origin and age as the alleged prohibited grounds, relevant dates and circumstances involving a younger French employee. Swierkiewicz seeks reinstatement of the claims; Sorema seeks affirmance.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

## Public Action

The judgment sustaining dismissal under the prima facie pleading requirement is reversed, and the case is remanded.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The complaint's dismissal is set aside and the Title VII and intentional-age-discrimination claims return for ordinary proceedings under Rule 8. Rule 11, lawful discovery management and Rule 56 remain available under their own requirements.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Scalia | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports the judgment | Entire opinion; all controlling propositions and stated limits |

## Holdings

### The prima facie proof framework is not a pleading checklist

**Holding and operative rule:** A Title VII or ADEA complaint need not allege facts establishing every element of the McDonnell Douglas prima facie evidentiary framework. A short and plain account identifying the challenged employment decisions, their alleged prohibited basis and the grounds of the claim satisfies Rule 8 when it gives fair notice; this complaint does so.

**Authority:** Scalia's opinion, joined in full by the other eight Justices, supplies nine direct votes for both the rejection of the added pleading requirement and this complaint's sufficiency.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a method of proving discrimination is not an additional requirement for commencing an action. McDonnell Douglas organizes circumstantial proof when direct evidence is unavailable. Thurston explains why direct evidence can make that inferential route unnecessary. Requiring its every element before discovery would therefore demand a particular route that the claimant may never need to use.

Rule 8 requires a short and plain statement giving fair notice. Rule 9 identifies heightened categories and supplies no employment-discrimination category. Leatherman rejects judicial creation of a heightened pleading code outside the Rules, and Crawford-El rejects a specially invented motive burden and a circular barrier to discovery. Reeves addresses the sufficiency of evidence, not the allegations required at filing.

The complaint describes the relevant employment acts, their timing and surrounding personnel circumstances, and the prohibited national-origin and age grounds asserted. That supplies fair notice. Sorema's concern about costly unsupported litigation does not authorize a new pleading amendment: Rule 11, the courts' lawful powers over discovery and Rule 56 address unsupported claims at their proper stages. The claimant retains the burden of proving unlawful discrimination. This holding applies the ordinary Rules to the actual complaint and does not make every conclusory accusation sufficient.

## Precedent Treatment

McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green remains a conditional framework for circumstantial proof; its elements are not transformed into universal pleading requirements.

Trans World Airlines, Inc. v. Thurston supplies the direct-evidence distinction that makes the proposed universal checklist unsound.

Leatherman v. Tarrant County Narcotics Intelligence and Coordination Unit is extended in its source-based procedural principle to this employment pleading requirement; its preserved immunity-pleading limits are unchanged.

Crawford-El v. Britton is applied for ordinary procedural control without an invented motive burden or circular discovery barrier.

Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc. remains an evidentiary-sufficiency decision and does not supply an additional complaint requirement.

Conley v. Gibson supplies the governing notice-pleading background.

## Law After Decision

From February 26, 2002, the ordinary Federal Rules govern these employment-discrimination complaints without a compulsory McDonnell Douglas checklist. Pleading sufficiency permits the case to proceed to the appropriate next stage; liability and evidentiary sufficiency remain governed by their distinct substantive and procedural rules.

# Young v. United States; No. 00-1567; 535 U.S. 43 — 2002-03-04

[Authoritative Record](../records/young-v-united-states_merits_2002-03-04.md)

## Event

Young v. United States, No. 00-1567; 535 U.S. 43. Argued January 9, 2002; decided March 4, 2002; October Term 2001, chunk 3. On writ of certiorari to the First Circuit, which affirmed the determination that the relevant federal income-tax debt survived the Chapter 7 discharge. The question is whether the three-year priority lookback is suspended while a prior bankruptcy stay legally prevents collection of the same tax liability. The taxpayers seek a declaration of discharge in a reopened case; the United States seeks affirmance. Their 1992 return was due and filed October 15, 1993 without payment. They filed Chapter 13 on May 1, 1996, and Chapter 7 on March 12, 1997, one day before dismissal of the earlier proceeding. The prior stay prevented collection; its material duration and coverage are not disputed.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

## Public Action

The First Circuit's judgment that the tax debt was not discharged is affirmed.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The prior legally disabling stay is excluded from the three-year calculation. The tax debt remains collectible through otherwise lawful procedures.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Breyer | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | Supports the judgment | Entire stay-period tolling rule, statutory limits, application and affirmance |

## Holdings

### The three-year tax lookback excludes a prior legally disabling stay

**Holding and operative rule:** The three-year income-tax priority lookback in the applicable 11 U.S.C. section 507(a)(8)(A)(i), carried into dischargeability by section 523(a)(1)(A), is suspended for the interval during which a prior bankruptcy stay legally prevented collection of that same liability. This traditional suspension requires no bad-faith finding and does not extend the period for ordinary collection difficulty, every successive petition or every deadline in the Bankruptcy Code.

**Authority:** Breyer's Opinion of the Court is joined in full by all eight other Justices. Their direct unanimous agreement controls the rule, its statutory limits and the affirmance.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Code's temporary prohibition cannot consume the collection interval that its priority and discharge rules protect. The lookback is backward-looking, but its finite protection performs a limitations function; other remedies surviving the interval do not eliminate that function. Irwin supplies a rebuttable traditional tolling background, not a power to disregard statutory terms. The present suspension removes only time during which the Code legally disabled collection.

The taxpayers emphasize the absence of an express tolling clause and Congress's extensions elsewhere. Those provisions do different work. Section 523(b) addresses debts actually excepted from an earlier discharge, not this dismissed Chapter 13 proceeding. Section 108(c) addresses covered proceedings outside bankruptcy; its distinct setting does not forbid this suspension. The different 240-day rule's express treatment of offers in compromise does not make a collection stay usable time under the three-year rule. A broader construction of section 108(c)(1)'s suspension language is unnecessary to resolve this interaction.

Brockamp's detailed tax-refund filing and amount structure rebutted the incapacity extension sought there; it does not make every tax-related period untollable. Noland forbids changing statutory priority categories by general fairness, but this rule preserves the legislated interval and category. Raleigh keeps the substantive tax burden distinct from federal dischargeability. TRW's refusal to add a general discovery extension to the FCRA does not govern a later statutory prohibition on collection. No fraud, concealment or abuse premise supplies the rule.

The return was due October 15, 1993. The May 1, 1996 Chapter 13 filing imposed the stay that continued when the Chapter 7 petition followed on March 12, 1997. Excluding that interval places this debt within the three-year lookback. Because the legal disability operated regardless of motive, affirmance requires no finding that the taxpayers abused bankruptcy.


## Precedent Treatment

- Irwin v. Department of Veterans Affairs: its rebuttable traditional background is applied to the particular stay and lookback interaction; no universal deadline extension follows.
- United States v. Brockamp: distinguished by the integrated tax-refund filing, allowance and payment-window structure and the incapacity extension there; both of its statutory bars retain their force.
- United States v. Noland: applied to preserve Congress's priority categories while allowing lawful equity within the Code; no creditor class receives a new rank.
- Raleigh v. Illinois Department of Revenue: its preservation of the substantive tax burden remains distinct from this federal dischargeability calculation.
- TRW Inc. v. Andrews: its rejection of a general discovery extension under the governing FCRA remains; accrual or discovery and a later legal disability to collect are different questions.


## Law After Decision

The stated three-year lookback excludes a prior bankruptcy stay that actually prohibited collection of the covered tax debt. Motive is immaterial to that legal disability. The tax's substantive burden and statutory priority categories remain unchanged, and other deadlines retain their own text and structure.

## Separate Writings



## Procedure After Action

The First Circuit's nondischargeability judgment is affirmed. The prior stay is excluded from the lookback, and the surviving liability may be collected through otherwise lawful procedures. The reviewed dischargeability issue is concluded.

The stipulated chronology establishes the collection stay; no finding of abusive or fraudulent filing is part of the judgment.

# Edelman v. Lynchburg College; No. 00-1072. — 2002-03-19

[Authoritative Record](../records/edelman-v-lynchburg-college_merits_2002-03-19.md)

## Event

Edelman v. Lynchburg College, No. 00-1072. Argued January 8, 2002; decided March 19, 2002. October Term 2001, chunk 3. On certiorari to the Fourth Circuit. After denial of tenure, Edelman submitted an unverified November 1997 letter within the applicable 300-day period and a verified form on day 313. The Fourth Circuit sustained dismissal because it considered the EEOC's relation-back regulation incompatible with Title VII. Edelman seeks reversal; the College seeks affirmance and preserves the distinct objection that the letter was not a charge. The reviewed question concerns verification timing, not the merits of the discrimination claim.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

## Public Action

The judgment is reversed and the case is remanded.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Disposition:** Reversed and remanded, 9–0.

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The Fourth Circuit's rule invalidating later verification is reversed. The original letter's status as a charge and the other preserved defenses remain for decision under the lawful relation-back rule.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Breyer | Reverses and remands | Independent statutory construction sustaining later verification of an otherwise sufficient timely charge; Thomas joins with his stated procedural-authority understanding |
| Concurring opinion | Thomas | No other Justice | Joins the Court and judgment | Express procedural delegation, reasonableness, consistency with Title VII and compliance with the Administrative Procedure Act |
| Opinion concurring in the judgment | O'Connor | Scalia | Reverses and remands | Reasonable delegated procedural choice within statutory ambiguity; does not join the Court's independent best-reading rationale |

## Holdings

### A timely charge may receive later verification

**Holding and operative rule:** An otherwise sufficient Title VII charge filed within the applicable filing period may receive its required verification afterward through 29 C.F.R. section 1601.12(b), with the verification relating back to the timely charge. Verification remains mandatory and must protect the employer before it is obliged to answer the substance; the cure does not extend the filing deadline or turn a document that otherwise is not a charge into one.

**Authority:** Ginsburg's opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Breyer, giving seven direct votes for this independent statutory construction. O'Connor and Scalia support the same operative cure and judgment on their distinct delegated-choice ground; that rationale is not added to the Court's reasoning through vote aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that filing and verification are distinct requirements whose performance need not be simultaneous. Section 706(b) requires a written charge under oath or affirmation. Section 706(e)(1) supplies the filing period. Neither provision defines a charge as necessarily verified at the instant of its first timely submission. Prompt filing addresses staleness; verification assures that the complainant adopts the accusation under oath before the employer must respond on the merits.

The College would treat the verification requirement as part of the filing deadline merely by combining the two provisions. That reading adds a simultaneity command that Congress did not state. The regulation instead preserves both obligations: a timely charge initiates the process, and an actual later oath completes the formal requirement. The process is commonly initiated by laypersons, as Love and Commercial Office Products recognize, but ease of filing does not excuse either enacted obligation.

Section 713(a) expressly authorizes procedural rules. This relation-back rule is procedural, reasonable, consistent with the statute and promulgated in conformity with the Administrative Procedure Act. Becker provides a useful analogy for completing a timely filing through an authorized cure; Title VII and this procedural grant, rather than Becker itself, authorize this cure. The independent reading is sufficient without resolving the full degree of administrative deference.

The November letter preceded the applicable 300-day deadline; the verified form arrived on day 313. Those dates make the verification issue concrete but do not decide whether the letter otherwise constituted a charge. That independently preserved question, the subsequent correspondence and the effect of the agency's failure to give notice within ten days require the lower court's analysis on remand. Agency delay is not itself an adjudicated bar or proof that the initial document failed.

## Precedent Treatment

Becker v. Montgomery is applied by analogy to distinguish completion of an existing timely filing from initiation of a late proceeding; its express cure rule is not transplanted into Title VII as independent authority.

Zipes v. Trans World Airlines, Inc. supplies the distinction between the timely-charge condition and subject-matter jurisdiction, without deciding every processing requirement's classification.

Love v. Pullman Co. and EEOC v. Commercial Office Products Co. inform the lay-initiated administrative process; they do not authorize disregard of the actual filing deadline or oath requirement.

United States v. Mead Corp. and Christensen v. Harris County retain their distinctions between delegated legal force and persuasive interpretation. The Court's independent construction makes a broader deference ruling unnecessary; O'Connor and Scalia rely on the actual procedural delegation for their separate ground.

Leatherman v. Tarrant County Narcotics Intelligence and Coordination Unit retains its source-based rejection of invented pleading requirements in its own field. Swierkiewicz v. Sorema N.A. governs the later federal complaint, not what constitutes a timely administrative charge.

EEOC v. Waffle House, Inc. retains the distinction between statutory enforcement authority and the actual prerequisites for relief; it supplies no independent verification-timing rule.

## Law After Decision

An otherwise sufficient timely Title VII charge may be verified after the filing period under the lawful relation-back regulation. Seven Justices adopt the independent statutory explanation; two concur in the judgment on a reasonable delegated-choice rationale. Actual verification, timely initiation and charge sufficiency remain separate requirements. The ruling governs verification cure, leaving the underlying employment-practice accrual question to its own statutory analysis.

## Separate Writings

**Thomas, concurring.** He joins the Court on the understanding that section 713(a) expressly delegates procedural rulemaking, this regulation is reasonable, Title VII does not prohibit it and its promulgation complies with the Administrative Procedure Act. All four conditions matter. The rule completes verification of an existing timely charge; it neither waives a statutory deadline nor recognizes an unrestricted equitable power to excuse procedural requirements. His qualification accompanies his full join in the Court's explanation, rather than substituting a judgment-only ground.

**O'Connor, joined by Scalia, concurring in the judgment.** Reading the provisions together most naturally calls for a verified charge within the filing period. But the statute does not expressly define charge in a way that unambiguously compels that reading. Section 713(a)'s procedural delegation therefore permits the EEOC's reasonable alternative, which preserves both timely initiation and later verified accountability. The rule's binding operation and notice-and-comment repromulgation in 1977 distinguish it from the revisable, nonbinding tariff letters addressed in Mead. Commercial Office Products supports deference on this technical agency procedure. The concurrence also questions how recognition of a possible future reasonable agency change can be reconciled with wholly reserving the deference issue. Neither Justice joins the assertion that the current rule is the best independent statutory reading. Both preserve actual verification, the employer's protection before a required merits response and the separate inquiry into whether the initial letter was a charge.

The case returns through the Fourth Circuit for analysis of whether the original letter was a charge, the preserved significance of later correspondence and the agency's ten-day notice failure, and any remaining defenses under the correct verification rule. Ordinary mandate procedure applies.

The original document's independent charge status and the preserved processing issues were not resolved by the verification ruling.

# Verizon Maryland Inc. v. Public Service Commission of Maryland; United States v. Public Service Commission of Maryland; Nos. 00-1531 and 00-1711; 535 U.S. 635. — 2002-05-20

[Authoritative Record](../records/verizon_maryland_v_public_service_commission_merits_2002-05-20.md)

## Event

**Cases:** Verizon Maryland Inc. v. Public Service Commission of Maryland, No. 00-1531, and United States v. Public Service Commission of Maryland, No. 00-1711; 535 U.S. 635.
**Event:** Consolidated merits decision.
**Argued:** December 5, 2001.
**Decided:** May 20, 2002.
**Route and posture:** Certiorari to the Fourth Circuit, 240 F.3d 279, affirming dismissal of the federal challenge to Maryland's later commission order concerning reciprocal compensation for Internet-bound traffic. A 1997 commission order had been upheld in Maryland circuit court. Following the FCC's 1999 ruling, Verizon sought another commission determination; the June 1999 order denied relief and addressed continued payment duties. The FCC ruling was vacated in 2000, and the 2001 remand order and its transition limits are part of the dispute.
**Question granted:** Question 2 in each petition: assuming §252(e)(6) does not apply, whether §1331 independently supplies jurisdiction to determine whether interpretation or enforcement of an interconnection agreement violates the Telecommunications Act. The Court considers officer-relief and indispensability only as necessary alternative grounds for sustaining dismissal.

The prayers seek a declaration of federal-law violation, restraint of enforcement and a direction requiring the Commission to direct private carriers to refund compensation. ## Public Action

The Court vacates the Fourth Circuit's judgments in both consolidated dockets and remands.

## Holdings

### I. Section 1331 remains available for this substantial federal claim

**Controlling proposition:** Assuming without deciding that the determination-review sentence of 47 U.S.C. §252(e)(6) does not cover this enforcement decision, it does not withdraw 28 U.S.C. §1331 jurisdiction over the substantial claim that the later commission order violates the Telecommunications Act or applicable federal rules. A purported federal claim fails this jurisdictional threshold if it clearly appears immaterial and made solely to obtain jurisdiction, or wholly insubstantial and frivolous; this complaint falls within neither exception.

**Authority:** Parts I–III of Thomas's Opinion of the Court are joined by all eight other Justices. Their jurisdictional and officer-relief propositions have nine direct supporters, independent of the disagreement over the form of the mandate.

**Controlling explanation:** Bell v. Hood separates a substantial federal controversy from eventual entitlement to relief. This complaint asserts a federal limitation on the commission's continuing order; it does not become nonfederal simply because agreement interpretation is involved. Whether the asserted private statutory cause ultimately exists remains a merits question, not a reason to deny jurisdiction over this substantial claim.

Section 252(e)(6) contains two different sentences. The first makes FCC substitution under paragraph (5), and judicial review of FCC action, exclusive remedies for a state commission's failure to carry out its section 252 responsibilities in a proceeding or other matter under that section. Paragraph (5) requires FCC preemption and assumption of responsibility within ninety days after notification or notice of that failure. The second sentence permits any party aggrieved by a state commission determination under section 252 to bring an action in an appropriate federal district court to determine whether the agreement or statement meets sections 251 and 252; it does not repeat that exclusivity. This case challenges action, not an established paragraph (5) failure.

Paragraph (e)(4) expressly excludes state-court review of commission approval or rejection of an agreement. Its separate timing rule deems an agreement approved if the commission does not approve or reject it within ninety days after the parties submit a negotiated agreement, or thirty days after they submit an arbitrated agreement. Those provisions do not withdraw ordinary federal-question jurisdiction over the present federal challenge. Paragraph (e)(3) preserves other state-law requirements in review, notwithstanding paragraph (2) and subject to §253. The Court preserves each condition rather than treating the review structure as universally exclusive or every contract dispute as federal. Full coverage of the determination-review sentence is outside the granted alternative question and unnecessary to its answer.

### II. The later administrative order is not a state-court judgment

**Controlling proposition:** The restriction on lower federal appellate review of state-court judgments does not bar this original federal challenge to the later commission action. Actual claim or issue preclusion remains a distinct question for adjudication at its proper scope.

**Authority:** Part II of the Court's opinion; all nine Justices join.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 1331 does not confer appellate jurisdiction over a state-court judgment. But the complaint attacks the commission's later order, entered after the intervening federal development, rather than seeking appellate reversal of the earlier Maryland judgment. A state administrative order does not become a judicial judgment merely because an earlier related commission decision was reviewed in state court. The Court therefore rejects the categorical state-court-review objection. It neither vacates that prior judgment nor decides the separate preclusive effect of its actual determinations.

### III. Necessary officer-relief alternatives do not sustain categorical dismissal

**Controlling proposition:** A claim to restrain responsible commissioners from continuing enforcement of an order alleged to violate federal law may proceed through the prospective officer-relief route. Private intercarrier financial consequences do not by themselves convert that restraint into retrospective compensation from the State, and the commission's absence does not categorically make such relief impossible.

**Authority:** Part III of the Court's opinion; all nine Justices join this confined rule and its remedial limits.

**Controlling explanation:** The grant concerns the §1331 alternative, but immunity and indispensability were separate grounds for sustaining dismissal. Florida Prepaid permits consideration of a respondent's properly presented unchanged-judgment alternative; resolving those grounds only as necessary does not enlarge the grant to every telecommunications or immunity question.

Ex parte Young permits prospective restraint of connected officers' ongoing federal violations. The threshold examines the allegation and requested relief, not whether Verizon has proved its federal claim. This statutory scheme does not impose a restrictive substitute remedial sequence that the prospective action would evade. Seminole Tribe permits its officer route within enacted remedial limits; Coeur d'Alene preserves the properly limited route without generalized sovereign-interest balancing as a bar. Schacht keeps jurisdiction, defendants and immunity distinct.

Restraint of continued enforcement is prospective even when it changes private carriers' financial positions. Effective restraint may operate against the enforcing commissioners without the commission itself. That conclusion defeats categorical dismissal; it does not decide all other parties or prayers. A declaration affecting accrued private obligations and a direction concerning private-carrier refunds require their own analysis below. No refund entitlement is established. Edelman's boundary against retrospective State compensation and Pennhurst's boundary against federal commands enforcing state law remain.

Direct Commission amenability, regulatory-participation consent, sufficient authorization of direct-State suit and the private statutory cause need not be decided to preserve this officer claim. Florida Prepaid's Article I rule remains available on its own constitutional and unmistakable-authorization predicates; the Court finds none of those predicates here merely from participation. College Savings Bank does not equate continued ordinary commercial activity with consent or determine how this regulatory role should be classified, and Lapides's voluntary-removal circumstances are not present. The lower court must adjudicate federal compliance and tailor any permissible remedy rather than assume the compensation claim succeeds.

### IV. The judgments are vacated and the cases returned

**Controlling proposition:** The Fourth Circuit's judgments in both consolidated dockets are vacated and the cases remanded for adjudication under the jurisdictional and prospective-officer rules stated above. Remaining defendant, claim, preclusion and remedy questions must be addressed at their actual scope; the compensation merits are not resolved by this threshold decision.

**Authority:** Part IV of Thomas's opinion is joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplying eight direct votes. Stone-Zsela joins Parts I–III but would reverse and remand rather than vacate.

**Controlling explanation:** The categorical barriers to federal adjudication cannot sustain dismissal, while distinct issues remain for the lower courts under the governing rules. Vacatur removes the judgments supporting that dismissal without resolving the remaining claims and prayers. The available prospective route is a right to adjudication on the stated conditions, not a present adjudication of federal unlawfulness or an award of intercarrier payments.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Bell v. Hood, 327 U.S. 678 (1946):** Applies its substantial-federal-claim distinction with both complete exceptions; eventual merits failure is separate from jurisdiction.
- **Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908):** Applies the route against connected officers for prospective restraint of an alleged ongoing federal violation.
- **Seminole Tribe of Florida v. Florida, March 27, 1996:** Applies the officer-route holding within enacted remedial limits; it supplies no categorical exclusion of this action or Article I bar.
- **Idaho v. Coeur d'Alene Tribe of Idaho, June 23, 1997:** Preserves the established officer route and rejects generalized sovereign-interest balancing as an independent bar; the State-property setting does not transform these intercarrier consequences into State damages.
- **Wisconsin Department of Corrections v. Schacht, June 22, 1998:** Applies the separation between federal jurisdiction and a particular defendant's immunity.
- **Florida Prepaid Postsecondary Education Expense Board v. College Savings Bank, June 23, 1999:** Applies its unchanged-judgment alternative-ground rule; preserves its Article I enforcement rule without finding the predicates of direct-State suit here.
- **College Savings Bank v. Florida Prepaid Postsecondary Education Expense Board, June 23, 1999:** Preserves the rejection of consent inferred from continued ordinary commercial activity; classification of this regulatory role remains undecided, and no automatic waiver follows.
- **Lapides v. Board of Regents, May 13, 2002:** Its voluntary-removal rule does not establish consent through the regulatory participation presented here.
- **Edelman v. Jordan; Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman:** Preserve, respectively, the retrospective State-compensation and state-law-command boundaries.
- **AT&T Corp. v. Iowa Utilities Board, January 25, 1999; Verizon Communications Inc. v. FCC, May 13, 2002:** Preserve the distinct federal/state allocation, methodology and combination holdings; they do not determine this review channel or Internet-bound reciprocal compensation.

## Law After Decision

Section 1331 remains available for the substantial federal enforcement challenge even on the assumption that §252(e)(6)'s determination-review sentence does not apply. Its separate failure-to-act exclusivity remains intact. Responsible commissioners may face prospective restraint of ongoing enforcement alleged to violate federal law, without treating private financial effects alone as State damages or the Commission's absence as an automatic bar. Full determination-review coverage, direct Commission amenability, consent and the private statutory cause remain undecided because this limited review does not require them. Federal compliance, compensation merits and the availability and scope of the actual requested remedies require ordinary adjudication below under the stated rules; establishing the forum does not decide those matters.

## Separate Writings

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela concurs in Parts I–III and dissents from the form of the judgment. He would reverse and remand because §1331 and the prospective officer-relief route permit the claim against the responsible commissioners to proceed. He agrees that federal jurisdiction, statutory authority, consent and remedy do separate work; participation does not establish constructive consent, and intercarrier financial consequences do not make the requested future-enforcement restraint a State-damages action. His reversal would reinstate adjudication of the federal claim, leaving federal compliance and the precise injunction for decision below. He would not determine direct Commission waiver or the compensation merits. His disagreement concerns reversal rather than vacatur, not the controlling threshold rules or the requirement of further adjudication.

## Procedure After Action

The Fourth Circuit's judgments in both dockets are vacated and the cases remanded. The lower courts must permit adjudication consistent with the §1331 and prospective-officer holdings, determine federal compliance and address the actual remaining party, preclusion and remedial issues. Any injunction must follow that adjudication and observe the stated prospective and federal-law limits. The earlier Maryland state-court judgment is not vacated.

## Holdings

### I. The asserted regulation-created effects right is not enforceable under §1983

**Controlling proposition:** Section 601's intentional-discrimination protection and §602's agency instructions do not confer the additional individual effects right asserted here. A regulation may elaborate a statutory entitlement, but cannot create this missing broader right for §1983; Sandoval's separate authorization of direct prospective Title VI enforcement does not alter that rule.

**Authority:** Part I of O'Connor's opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, including the author in that five-Justice coalition. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer disagree. The proposition has direct majority support despite the different formal appellate dispositions; no aggregation of distinct rationales is required.

**Controlling explanation:** Powell resolved the asserted §1983 right independently of the source of a direct statutory action. Sandoval overruled only the direct-action prohibition and expressly preserved the right-creation holding. Blessing requires a particular congressional entitlement, not simply a beneficial program or a mandatory agency command. The residents' regulatory protection may be enforceable through the direct channel without becoming the different right asserted under §1983. Treating every valid implementing regulation as a new individual entitlement would erase that distinction; treating Powell's direct-action prohibition as unchanged would disregard Sandoval. Wright and Wilder preserve regulatory elaboration of entitlements the statutes themselves confer; they do not supply the missing broader effects right. This conclusion does not foreclose an independently conferred statutory right or the original intentional-discrimination claim.

**Precedent treatment:** Powell v. Ridge, April 3, 2000, is applied only at its surviving §1983 holding. Alexander v. Sandoval, April 24, 2001, retains its express limited overruling and separate direct action. Blessing v. Freestone, 520 U.S. 329 (1997), Wright v. Roanoke Redevelopment and Housing Authority, 479 U.S. 418 (1987), and Wilder v. Virginia Hospital Association, 496 U.S. 498 (1990), retain the distinction between identifying and elaborating a statutory right.

### II. The retained prospective Title VI claim must receive its own adjudication

**Controlling proposition:** Title VI beneficiaries may seek prospective compliance by a proper covered defendant with valid, sufficiently definite §602 requirements implementing their statutory discrimination protection. The original permitting claim must be adjudicated through that existing route, applying the actual recipient, program, regulatory duty, prohibited effect, causal relationship and fitted-remedy requirements; disparity alone is not an automatic violation.

**Authority:** Part II is joined by all nine Justices. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas apply Sandoval as controlling precedent while retaining their original interpretive disagreement; they do not join a new §1983 effects right.

**Controlling explanation:** Sandoval authorizes this category of prospective action; its existence is settled law, not a question postponed for remand. The original direct theory was not shown abandoned by adding §1983. Under 40 C.F.R. §7.35(b), the court must identify criteria or methods of administering the covered program that have the prohibited discriminatory effect, or defeat or substantially impair the program's objectives for protected individuals. Section 7.35(c) separately governs site or facility selection under its own purpose-or-effect terms; its distinct claim requires its own pleading and proof and is not established here merely by the separate criteria-or-methods claim. Cumulative effects matter when the actual regulatory duty reaches them, not because courts possess general authority to equalize environmental conditions. The accepted emissions analysis neither establishes nor disproves the discrimination claim. Existing findings retain their ordinary status for review; the remaining factual, regulatory and equitable applications must be decided under the governing rule.

**Precedent treatment:** Sandoval is applied to the retained EPA permitting claim at the reach of its prospective enforcement rule, with valid and definite duties, actual elements and fitted relief; it creates no damages remedy for unintentional effects.

### III. Jurisdiction, proper defendants and interim relief require their distinct inquiries

**Controlling proposition:** A substantial federal controversy supports federal adjudication independently of eventual success. A connected state officer may face prospective relief against an alleged continuing federal violation within the enacted remedial limits, but direct agency amenability, actual officer connection and fitted equitable relief require their own predicates; the original §601 claim retains its intent requirement and actual procedural footing.

**Authority:** Part III is joined by all eight Associate Justices. Stone-Zsela joins the proper-defendant, prospective-remedy and direct-claim application; he agrees in judgment only with further adjudication of the separately pleaded intentional claim and adds no ground on that component.

**Controlling explanation:** Verizon Maryland separates substantial federal jurisdiction, defendant immunity and ultimate entitlement. Young permits prospective relief against an actually connected officer; Commissioner Shinn is named, but naming him does not establish that connection or the scope of relief. NJDEP's direct amenability cannot be assumed from participation or consent not established here. The intentional claim remains distinct from the regulatory effects claim. Once §1983's asserted effects right fails, residents must receive prompt consideration of the retained statutory route and requested protection, with the required merits and equitable findings addressing the threatened injury and lawful limits of an order. The existing injunction was suspended, and vacatur does not revive it. Actual findings receive ordinary review; remand does not direct a new hearing or wholesale reopening.

**Precedent treatment:** Verizon Maryland Inc. v. Public Service Commission of Maryland, May 20, 2002, and Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908), are applied to jurisdiction and prospective connected-officer relief; neither decides this permitting violation or direct agency amenability.

## Precedent Treatment

Powell's regulation-created-right holding remains controlling; its direct-action prohibition remains overruled only to Sandoval's stated extent. Sandoval governs this category of prospective claim and does not supply intent, damages for unintentional effects or a new §1983 right. Blessing, Wright and Wilder retain their particular-right and regulatory-elaboration principles. Verizon Maryland and Young preserve the separation among jurisdiction, defendant, merits and remedy. No further precedent is overruled.

## Law After Decision

Title VI's direct prospective action and §1983's individual-right inquiry remain distinct. This permitting claim may proceed under the established direct route upon the actual regulatory and defendant predicates, while the asserted regulation-created §1983 effects right remains unavailable. Section 601's intentional-discrimination route retains its independent requirement. The legal route is settled; coverage, violation, causation and relief require adjudication on the record.

## Separate Writings

Justice Stevens, joined by Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, concurs in the direct-route remand and dissents from Part I. He would reconsider Powell's §1983 holding to the extent it rejects this identified entitlement. The statutory beneficiaries, nondiscrimination protection, express implementation authority and a valid, sufficiently definite implementing requirement can, in his view, define the individual protection that §1983 enforces. Blessing's particular-right inquiry and Wright's and Wilder's recognition of authorized elaboration support that conclusion. He does not treat every regulation or administrative command as a right or establish a general entitlement to equal environmental burdens. Sandoval independently requires consideration of the direct claim, so his §1983 disagreement does not undermine remand, actual causation, proper-defendant or equitable requirements.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela concurs in the §1983 rejection and direct prospective adjudication but dissents from vacating the appellate judgment in full. He would affirm the rejected §1983 basis and vacate only the foreclosure of the retained Title VI theory. The two channels answer different questions, and Sandoval's direct-action rule leaves Powell's §1983 rule intact. He would require prompt determination of the covered recipient, actual regulatory duty, causal effect, proper prospective defendant and interim-relief requirements. Protection requires a supported lawful order; his disposition would not automatically restore the injunction or decide the permit's validity.

The case returns for prompt adjudication of the retained direct prospective claim, the distinct intentional claim and the actual defendant and equitable inquiries. The injunction remains suspended unless lawful preliminary relief is supported and entered. Existing findings receive ordinary review; there is no categorical new-hearing requirement. Regulatory invalidity or an independent permit-law claim not preserved in this appeal is not decided.
