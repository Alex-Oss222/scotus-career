# Hope v. Pelzer: exact public-law reading copy

Mechanically copied bounded Public Projection. The Record controls. Effective June 27, 2002.

## Event

Hope v. Pelzer, No. 01-309. Argued April 17, 2002; decided June 27, 2002. On certiorari to the Eleventh Circuit, which affirmed summary judgment for three prison officers on qualified-immunity grounds while accepting that the alleged punitive restraint violated the Eighth Amendment. The granted issue concerns its materially-similar-precedent requirement and fair warning; ultimate individual responsibility was not taken for review. Hope seeks reversal and continuation of his action under 42 U.S.C. §1983. The officers seek preservation of their personal-immunity judgment.

The favorable summary-judgment account concerns May 11 and June 7, 1995. In May, Hope was attached for two hours after an altercation; the raised position caused pain and wrist discomfort. Water and bathroom breaks were offered every fifteen minutes and recorded. McClaran allegedly ordered attachment, and Pelzer and Gates allegedly attached him. The June account describes approximately seven hours after Hope had been subdued, with scant water, no bathroom break and other aggravations. Gates is the only remaining respondent connected to June, and the alleged act attributed to him is attachment; the account does not itself allege his supervision of the seven hours, removal of clothing, denial of water or taunting. The attribution and even identity of the persons involved remain disputed. These are legal-review assumptions and alleged acts, not liability findings.

The governing assumption for each occasion is extended, painful attachment without continuing danger or work refusal. June's greater severity is distinct from that common premise and is not attributed to May-only officers. The constitutional characterization is accepted for this granted immunity inquiry.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The Eleventh Circuit judgment is reversed, 7–2. The legal fair-warning issue is resolved on the accepted account of each occasion, with ordinary proceedings on individual responsibility and independent defenses.

## Holdings

### I. The accepted constitutional premise remains individual and conduct-specific

**Holding and operative rule:** The granted immunity review takes the supported, plaintiff-favorable premise that each occasion involved knowingly imposed painful restraint continued after a security need and any work refusal ended, as gratuitous punishment. That conduct violates the existing Eighth Amendment rule, but personal liability requires each defendant's own supported participation, actual culpable awareness and causal contribution; a reasonable response to a known risk remains protected under Farmer.

**Authority:** Part I of Souter's Opinion of the Court is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer. The same seven directly join each following holding; neither dissenter joins the opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court applies the accepted constitutional premise rather than deciding an ungranted challenge to it. Rhodes and Whitley distinguish justified control from unnecessary punitive suffering. Farmer requires actual appreciation of a substantial danger and unreasonable disregard; obviousness permits an inference of awareness but does not compel it or aggregate knowledge across guards. Hudson's established prohibition of nontrivial malicious beating does not become a universal rule for every restraint or eliminate the conditions inquiry.

May's regular offers of necessities mitigate the conditions but do not justify the assumed two hours of painful punishment after necessity ceased. June's aggravated conditions strengthen its account without supplying missing acts by McClaran, Pelzer or Gates. The dissent's personal-role objection matters to ultimate responsibility. It cannot change the granted legal assumption into mere initial attachment for a continuing lawful purpose. The Court leaves factual sufficiency and actual attribution to ordinary proceedings because they fall outside the accepted question.

**Precedent treatment:** Farmer's actual-knowledge and reasonable-response requirements are applied. Hudson retains its limited force holding. Rhodes and Whitley supply the distinction between necessary control and gratuitous punishment; Delaney's assumed-fact method supplies no missing allegations or 1995 notice.

### II. Fair warning can be clear without a previously litigated replica

**Holding and operative rule:** An officer has qualified immunity unless the law when he acted made the unlawfulness of his actual conduct apparent to a reasonable official. An established rule can give that warning through an obvious application without a materially identical prior case; context, contrary authority and judicial hierarchy matter, and an earlier express reservation of the particular application may require greater factual specificity.

**Authority:** Part II has the same seven direct joins. Scalia and Thomas's agreement with the possible obvious-violation route does not join the Court's reading or application of the appellate judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** Harlow supplies an objective personal defense, and Anderson requires particularized clarity while rejecting immunity merely because the precise act has not been litigated. Lanier explains why civil clearly-established law and criminal fair warning share a notice inquiry. General moral disapproval alone is insufficient, but a settled prohibition can plainly cover new apparatus. A more particular precedent is especially important where earlier law expressly left this application open. Lanier's criminal willfulness element does not become a civil element.

The reviewed judgment rejected Gates and Ort as insufficiently similar despite the accepted after-necessity punishment. The dissent reads circuit law as already allowing obvious applications. That general allowance does not cure this judgment's failure to apply the governing warning to its accepted facts. The inquiry asks what preconduct law communicated, not whether an official actually consulted a favorable precedent. Wilson retains context-sensitive immunity; Saucier establishes no replacement defense or compulsory merits-first sequence. Lanier, Wilson and later decisions explain the method without creating notice retroactively. State policy and administrative warnings have contextual roles, not independent authority to create or erase the constitutional right.

**Precedent treatment:** Harlow and Anderson are retained; Lanier's warning method is applied without importing criminal willfulness. Wilson, Denius and Saucier preserve objective conduct-date inquiry. A categorical factual-twin prerequisite is rejected.

### III. Pre-1995 law warned against the assumed gratuitous restraint on both occasions

**Holding and operative rule:** By May 11 and June 7, 1995, Gates v. Collier and Ort v. White gave fair warning that knowingly prolonging painful restraint as punishment after the prisoner was controlled and no work refusal continued violated the Eighth Amendment. The assumed conduct on each occasion therefore cannot receive qualified immunity merely because no earlier case involved this precise device, duration or combination of conditions.

**Authority:** Part III has the same seven direct joins, covering both May and June at the stated legal assumption. It establishes no collective or final personal liability.

**Controlling explanation:** Gates condemned prolonged handcuffing and awkward confinement as corporal punishment. Its place in the binding circuit line, through Bonner, matters more than the name of the apparatus. Ort upheld necessary coercion during work refusal while explaining the different constitutional position of later punishment and measures severe enough to endanger health. That distinction directly addresses the assumed end of necessity here.

Lane, Whitson and the other preconduct district decisions are the strongest contrary evidence. Their accounts involved continued refusal, opportunities to resume work, necessities or shorter duration. May shared some mitigating conditions but, on the accepted premise, lacked the continuing justification. Such lower-court decisions cannot override the superior circuit warning against this gratuitous punishment. The contrary interpretation does not establish immunity by treating the assumed prolonged punitive act as only lawful initial attachment.

The state regulation authorized restraints for refusal or disruption and prescribed return to work when an inmate was ready, quarter-hour necessities and a log. Those safeguards reinforce the coercion/punishment distinction; compliance with some safeguards is not a license for knowingly needless pain, and breach alone is not a federal violation. The preconduct federal report corroborates the warning without proving receipt by any respondent. Later district approval cannot retroactively erase the earlier rule. Each officer's actual knowledge and causal role remain necessary under Part IV.

**Precedent treatment:** Gates and Ort are applied at their specific punishment/coercion boundary; Bonner supplies circuit hierarchy. The district cases are distinguished, not treated as nonexistent. Later cases and the administrative report are not substitutes for preconduct constitutional authority.

### IV. Reversal resolves notice while preserving the ungranted responsibility issues

**Holding and operative rule:** The reviewed similarity-based immunity judgment is reversed because the legal warning was sufficient on the accepted gratuitous-punishment account of both occasions. Ordinary further proceedings determine each respondent's supported acts, knowledge, causation and independent defenses; the June aggravations may not be attributed to May-only defendants or to Gates without a sufficient personal basis.

**Authority:** Part IV has the same seven direct joins in reversal and ordinary further proceedings. Stone-Zsela separately calls for an express remand; the six Associate reversal votes do not depend on that additional formal direction.

**Controlling explanation:** The grant reaches the legal notice issue, not final sufficiency of the pleadings and evidence concerning each named officer. Deciding notice on the court below's accepted premise therefore neither decides the factual dispute nor requires an open-ended repetition of the legal warning inquiry. The dissent would regard the limited attributed acts as enough to affirm immunity now. That inquiry exceeds the issue accepted for review; it remains available in the appropriate proceedings on an actual individual record.

The lower court must separate McClaran's alleged May order, Pelzer's alleged May attachment, and Gates's alleged attachments on the two occasions. It must apply Farmer's personal awareness and reasonable-response limits, ordinary causation requirements and lawful independent defenses. Later supported facts may materially differ from the assumed punitive conduct. This disposition preserves that work rather than ordering collective liability. The withdrawn claims against absent guards remain withdrawn.

**Precedent treatment:** Delaney confirms the distinction between a resolved legal issue and actual responsibility, without making missing allegations into a genuine factual dispute. Farmer and the ordinary summary-judgment rules govern the remaining individual inquiry.

## Precedent Treatment

- Harlow v. Fitzgerald and Anderson v. Creighton: objective, particularized immunity is retained; obvious applications can give notice without a factual replica or proof of actual consultation.
- United States v. Lanier: applies the common warning inquiry, including the need for greater specificity where earlier law expressly reserved an application; criminal willfulness remains separate.
- Wilson v. Layne, Denius v. Dunlap, Elder v. Holloway and Saucier v. Katz: retain context, all relevant preconduct law and separate legal immunity analysis; later decisions create no retrospective warning or new reliance defense.
- Gates v. Collier, Ort v. White and Bonner v. City of Prichard: apply the binding preconduct prohibition of prolonged punitive restraint and distinguish necessary coercion from gratuitous punishment. Ort's operative holding sustained coercion during work refusal; its reasoning explains the punishment boundary.
- Farmer v. Brennan: retains actual awareness, permissible but noncompulsory inference, individual responsibility and reasonable-response protection.
- Hudson v. McMillian: retains the narrow nontrivial malicious-beating holding; it does not supply a universal force test or displace conditions law.
- Rhodes v. Chapman and Whitley v. Albers: supply the boundary between justified prison control and unnecessary punitive suffering.
- Lane v. Findley, Whitson v. Gillikin, Ashby v. Dees, Vinson v. Thompson, Hollis v. Folsom, Williamson v. Anderson and Dale v. Murphy: considered for preconduct notice, distinguished by purpose, continuing refusal or conditions, and subordinate to contrary controlling circuit law on the assumed punitive use.
- Delaney v. DeTella and Leventhal v. Knapek: maintain assumed-fact review and distinct objective immunity; neither supplies 1995 notice or an allegation of personal involvement. Searles v. Van Bebber remains confined to any properly presented injury/remedy issue.

## Law After Decision

Conduct-specific fair warning can arise from an obvious application of established law without a factual twin. The rule resolves immunity against the accepted knowingly gratuitous restraint on both 1995 occasions, while leaving actual defendant-specific responsibility and lawful independent defenses for ordinary adjudication. Needed control, reasonable responses and materially different circumstances remain subject to the existing constitutional and immunity standards.

## Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, concurring.** He joins the Court's notice rule, both-occasion application and reversal, and expressly directs remand for remaining individual-responsibility and factual issues. The legal fair-warning conclusion is settled on the accepted account; the next court must test each named officer's own participation, knowledge and causation. May's offered necessities do not justify assumed gratuitous punishment, and June's aggravations cannot be imputed across officers. His express remand gives effect to that remaining work without changing the Court's substantive standard.

**Thomas, dissenting, with Scalia joining the immunity and attribution grounds.** They would affirm personal immunity for these respondents. Harlow, Anderson and Malley protect reasonable legal mistakes, and both accept that obvious illegality can defeat immunity without a factual twin. They dispute the Court's reading of the appellate decision: considering factual similarity is not necessarily making it indispensable, and the circuit's own obvious-violation cases belie a categorical rule.

The dispositive question concerns the alleged acts of McClaran, Pelzer and Gates. McClaran allegedly ordered the May attachment; Pelzer and Gates allegedly performed it; Gates alone is linked to June. The seven-hour prolongation, shirt removal, water deprivation and taunting were not attributed to him. May had quarter-hour necessities and a log. Identity, clothing and bathroom accounts are disputed, and a generalized narrative of the worst treatment cannot establish these officers' clearly unlawful acts.

The authorizing regulation, Lane and Whitson, and the other pre-1995 district decisions would permit reasonable disagreement. The policy addressed disruption as well as refusal; the dissent disputes that these officers were alleged to have denied a request to return to work or breached a supervisory duty. The federal report was not shown to have reached them and could not settle a legal disagreement with courts. Ort upheld coercion and only reasoned about later punishment; Gates supplied no precise duration, purpose or necessities rule and preceded Farmer's clarified knowledge requirement. They find no sufficiently clear prohibition of the attributed initial attachment, even though prolonged knowingly gratuitous punishment may be unlawful. Later decisions cannot supply missing notice.

Thomas additionally preserves his view that conditions not imposed as part of a sentence are not punishment in the original sense. He applies the binding contrary conditions law here and remains open to reconsideration in an appropriate case. That reservation does not decide the granted immunity issue or change the Court's existing rule.

## Procedure After Action

The appellate judgment is reversed. Ordinary proceedings address the actual supported conduct and responsibility of each remaining defendant under the settled notice rule and the governing personal-liability requirements; lawful independent defenses and later dispositive motions remain available on an appropriate record. The Chief Justice expressly directs remand for that work. Withdrawn claims remain withdrawn.
