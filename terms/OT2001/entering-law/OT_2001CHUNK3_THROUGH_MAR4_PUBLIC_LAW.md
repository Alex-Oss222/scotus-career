# Brown v. City of Oneonta — 2001-11-13

[Authoritative Record](../records/brown-v-city-of-oneonta_merits_2001-11-13.md)

## Event

Brown v. City of Oneonta. Decided November 13, 2001. On review of the Second Circuit's amended judgment, 221 F.3d 329. The question is whether deliberate investigative selection by race escapes racial-classification review when the racial feature comes from a victim's description and additional hostility is not shown, and what constitutional standard governs that selection.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the affected equal-protection dismissal and remands.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed in part and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The reversal reaches the rejection of the equal-protection claim insofar as it rests on a categorical victim-description exemption. The affected claim is restored for application of the governing racial-classification standard to the actual selection, scope and burdens. Independently resolved claims, existing Fourth Amendment dispositions and the earlier qualified-immunity judgment protecting university officials for release of the list remain undisturbed.

## Holdings

**Deliberate racial selection requires justification.** When officials intentionally select people for investigation by race, they use a racial classification even if a victim supplied that feature and no hostility is shown. The government must establish an important lawful investigative objective and a substantial, evidence-supported relationship between that objective and the actual selection, its coverage and burdens, including a supported response to concrete practicable alternatives offering comparable investigative value with materially less racial disadvantage.

**Authority:** O'Connor's opinion for all nine Justices controls the classification rule, its standard and the bounded remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the source of identifying information can justify an official racial choice; it does not erase that choice. Adarand Constructors, Inc. v. Peña supplies the common justification standard; Engineering Contractors and City of Dallas require examination of the evidence connecting the objective to the actual measure and burden. Solving the reported violent attack is an important lawful objective. A concrete description linking race to age, sex, an injured hand, location and timing may support appropriately limited measures. The Court therefore rejects the contention that classification review requires police to ignore useful descriptions. It also rejects a categorical permission to investigate a racial community without examining the description's other features and the practical scope of the operation. The wider sweep and alleged encounter with a woman require that examination. Testimony, investigative records and reasonable inferences can establish fit; no commissioned study or universal least-restrictive-means requirement follows. Consensual questioning and a Fourth Amendment seizure are distinct, but consent alone does not remove an intentionally racial burden from equal-protection review. Membership in a described group does not establish an individual encounter, injury or defendant's responsibility.

**Precedent treatment:** Peña's common standard is extended to purposeful investigative selection; its evidentiary and remedial limits remain.

**Remand rule.** The lower courts must examine the actual selection criteria, scope and burdens under that standard, and address personal responsibility, coercion where relevant, immunity and relief through their distinct governing rules. Immunity remains measured against the law at the September 1992 conduct; this decision does not retrospectively supply clearly established notice.

**Authority and explanation:** The same unanimous opinion controls. Lesage separates unequal-process injury from consequential loss and appropriate relief. The categorical exemption prevented the necessary examination; removing it restores the affected claim without reopening independently sustained dispositions.

## Precedent Treatment

- Adarand Constructors, Inc. v. Peña (June 12, 1995): extended to deliberate investigative selection; the common substantial-justification standard governs rather than strict scrutiny.
- Engineering Contractors (1998) and City of Dallas (1999): applied for evidence-supported fit, actual burdens and concrete practicable alternatives.
- Eisenberg (May 30, 2000): its acknowledgment of the binding common standard is preserved; its distinct educational component is not extended to criminal investigation.
- Lesage (November 29, 1999): applied to keep the classification inquiry separate from individual causation, immunity and consequential relief.

## Law After Decision

Purposeful investigative selection by race is subject to the common substantial-justification standard. Victim-supplied identifying information may justify fitted measures, but its source and the absence of hostility create no categorical exemption. The rule governs the classification and its justification; individual liability and remedies remain subject to their existing requirements.

## Separate Writings

No separate opinion.

## Procedure After Action

The affected equal-protection claim returns for proceedings under the stated rule. Unchallenged and independently sustained dispositions retain their force.

# Nebraska v. Wyoming — 2001-11-13

[Authoritative Record](../records/nebraska-v-wyoming_decree_2001-11-13.md)

## Event

Nebraska v. Wyoming, No. 108, Original. Modified North Platte Decree entered November 13, 2001. On the Special Master's final report and the parties' joint request to approve the Final Settlement Stipulation dated March 13, 2001 and presented March 15, 2001, and to replace the decree entered October 8, 1945, as modified June 15, 1953.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated.

## Public Action

The Special Master's Final Report is received and ordered filed. The Court approves the parties' Final Settlement Stipulation, enters the complete Modified North Platte Decree below in place of the October 8, 1945 decree as modified June 15, 1953, and dismisses with prejudice all claims, counterclaims and cross-claims brought in this case, including the settled claims for which leave to file was or could have been sought. The parties shall share litigation costs as the Court orders after entry of the modified decree. No individual tally is published.

## Judgment & Remedy

The stipulation is approved and the complete replacement decree is entered. The specified claims, counterclaims and cross-claims are dismissed with prejudice. Paragraph XIV confirms costs apportioned and paid under previous orders; the outer order preserves a further allocation of litigation costs. Paragraph XIII retains decree jurisdiction and its committee-first procedure.

## Holdings

**Operative rule and authority:** The Court enters the following complete Modified North Platte Decree. Each injunction, limit, definition, exception, accounting requirement, reserved right and retained power operates according to its own terms. The Court's order and decree bind the parties; they establish no new general equitable-apportionment standard.

**Controlling explanation:** The parties have agreed to resolve this existing interstate controversy through specified obligations. The earlier Nebraska decisions distinguish enforcement of an established entitlement from contested alteration of an allocation; they do not prevent this lawful consensual modification. The decree preserves independent storage and other enumerated rights, makes its terms superior to committee procedures, and retains judicial authority over implementation, amendment and unresolved committee matters. Those limits answer the concern that administrative implementation could become an uncontrolled power to change allocations. Consent settles the claims without converting their disputed allegations into findings.

**Precedent treatment:** Nebraska v. Wyoming (April 20, 1993 and May 30, 1995) remains governing for its enforcement, priority and modification distinctions; the present decree supplies the parties' exact agreed replacement obligations.

# TRW Inc. v. Andrews — 2001-11-13

[Authoritative Record](../records/trw-inc-v-andrews_merits_2001-11-13.md)

## Event

TRW Inc. v. Andrews, No. 00-1045. Argued October 9, 2001; decided November 13, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, which reversed partial summary judgment treating the first two disputed credit disclosures as untimely. The question is whether the then-operative Fair Credit Reporting Act supplies a general discovery extension beyond its express misrepresentation exception.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the Ninth Circuit's general-discovery ruling and remands for further proceedings consistent with the statutory construction.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The first two disclosure claims cannot proceed on the rejected general discovery theory. The mandate supplies no entitlement to reopen the newly presented actual-damages theory, amend to allege misrepresentation, or obtain an accrual or exception hearing. Any request in further proceedings remains subject to the mandate and ordinary preservation, waiver and procedural rules. Independent judgments outside this review retain their force.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Breyer | Supports the judgment | Entire confined statutory opinion |
| Concurrence | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Joins the Court's opinion and judgment | Element-specific accrual; no affirmative reopening entitlement |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Scalia | Thomas | Supports the judgment on a distinct ground | Traditional complete-cause rule and rejection of a general discovery presumption |

## Holdings

**No general discovery extension under the governing FCRA.** The version of 15 U.S.C. §1681p governing these claims requires suit within two years from when liability arises. Its express extension permits suit within two years after discovery only when the defendant materially and willfully misrepresented information the Act required it to disclose to the individual and that information was material to establishing that defendant's liability to the individual; ignorance of a violation alone satisfies neither those conditions nor an additional general exception.

**Authority:** Ginsburg's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer, controls this statutory construction, the exception's application and the limited nonreach below. Scalia and Thomas agree with the judgment on their separately stated ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the specified discovery exception must retain its restrictive operation. A general discovery extension would allow a claimant to bypass its conditions and make it largely redundant. Andrus supports giving an enumerated exception its limiting effect; Menasche requires the enacted parts to perform their assigned work. The words identifying when liability arises do not transform liability into discovered liability. The practical difficulty of detecting identity misuse therefore cannot supply an exception Congress did not enact. Holmberg's fraud and concealment rule and Urie's latent-disease setting do not establish a universal discovery presumption overriding this text. Rotella and Klehr retain their distinct RICO questions and qualifications; they do not rewrite the FCRA. Andrews's complaint does not allege the material and willful misrepresentation of required information specified by §1681p, so that express extension supplies no basis for these claims.

**Precedent treatment:** Holmberg and Urie are distinguished at their stated scopes; Andrus and Menasche are applied to the statutory structure; the distinct Rotella and Klehr rules are preserved.

**Question left undecided.** The Court does not decide Andrews's argument that liability first arose upon actual loss rather than disclosure, because that distinct argument was first presented here and was not raised or briefed below. This nonreach does not resolve the substantive requirements of every willful claim, establish the asserted loss date, or authorize reopening the theory on remand. The confined statutory ruling corrects the general-discovery judgment without deciding a universal federal accrual rule.

## Precedent Treatment

- Andrus v. Glover Construction Co., 446 U.S. 608 (1980): applied for the significance of an expressly enumerated exception.
- United States v. Menasche, 348 U.S. 528 (1955): applied to preserve operative work for the exception's enacted conditions.
- Holmberg v. Armbrecht, 327 U.S. 392 (1946): its fraud/concealment rule is preserved; it does not establish the universal discovery rule urged here.
- Urie v. Thompson, 337 U.S. 163 (1949): its latent-disease setting does not govern this express FCRA structure.
- Rotella v. Wood (February 23, 2000) and Klehr v. A. O. Smith Corp. (June 19, 1997): their distinct RICO accrual, new-injury, unprovable-damage and concealment components remain at their actual scope.

## Law After Decision

The governing version of §1681p has no general discovery extension. Its precise statutory misrepresentation exception remains available when its conditions are met. The Court's ruling does not decide the first-presented actual-loss theory or create a universal accrual rule for federal statutes.

## Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, concurring.** Stone-Zsela joins the Court's statutory ruling and judgment. He adds that liability arises when the elements necessary for the particular statutory claim exist: rejection of general discovery does not erase the need for an element-specific inquiry or an independently applicable preserved exception. Rotella rejects substitution of a later discovery event for the governing accrual inquiry, but does not make disclosure, discovery and actionable injury interchangeable. Properly preserved questions remain subject to ordinary adjudication. The newly presented theory receives no affirmative permission to reopen merely because the Court leaves it undecided.

**Scalia, joined by Thomas, concurring in the judgment.** They begin with the traditional rule that a limitations period ordinarily runs when the cause of action is complete and enforceable, rather than when the claimant learns of it. A general discovery presumption does not follow from Congress's failure expressly to reject discovery. Congress can prescribe a different start and did enact a confined exception here. Traditional fraud/concealment protection and the latent-injury setting remain separate rules. They object that leaving the general background uncertain invites uncertainty under other statutes. Their agreement with reversal and remand does not establish that every disclosure completes every FCRA claim or decide the newly raised actual-loss theory.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns for proceedings consistent with rejection of the general discovery theory, subject to the stated mandate and ordinary procedural limits. The independent dispositions concerning other claims remain intact.

15 U.S.C. §§1681e(a), 1681n and 1681p (versions governing the challenged disclosures); TRW Inc. v. Andrews, No. 00-1045; Andrews v. TRW Inc., 225 F.3d 1063 (9th Cir. 2000).

# United States Postal Service v. Gregory — 2001-11-13

[Authoritative Record](../records/united-states-postal-service-v-gregory_merits_2001-11-13.md)

## Event

United States Postal Service v. Gregory, No. 00-758. Argued October 9, 2001; decided November 13, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Federal Circuit. The question is whether an agency and the Merit Systems Protection Board may consider prior discipline while contractual grievances challenging it remain pending. The judgment below imposed a categorical pending-grievance exclusion.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated.

## Public Action

The Court vacates the Federal Circuit's judgment and remands for further proceedings under the proper rule.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Vacated and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The Federal Circuit must reject categorical exclusion based solely on grievance pendency and address the available consequences of the warning's completed arbitral expungement, including its procedural availability and material effect on penalty review. Where necessary, the penalty must return to the Board for reconsideration without an invalidated predicate. The mandate does not automatically restore the removal, require reopening, or select a replacement penalty.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Breyer | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter; Thomas except the nonreach rationale | Supports the judgment | Breyer, Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter join the entire limited opinion; Thomas joins statutory authority, incidental review, conditional adequacy and the remand, but not the reason for leaving statutory sufficiency undecided |
| Concurrence | Thomas | No other Justice | Joins the Court's opinion at the specified scope and the judgment | Would additionally decide and uphold Bolling's statutory sufficiency |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Ginsburg | No other Justice | Supports vacatur and remand on separate grounds | Reopening power, removal election, adequacy reservation and rejection of an absolute agency prohibition |

## Holdings

**Pending grievance review does not itself exclude earlier discipline.** When reviewing a removal or other serious adverse action, the agency and the Board may consider earlier discipline while a contractual grievance remains pending, provided the independent review affords a meaningful opportunity to challenge the predicate and its material use. The Board's authority over the later adverse action includes incidental examination of relevant earlier discipline even when that minor action was not itself directly appealable to the Board.

**Authority:** Breyer's opinion on these propositions is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, supplying eight direct supporters. Ginsburg agrees with the judgment on her separate ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that neither the adverse-action statutes nor the review statute makes completion of contractual grievances a condition of considering disciplinary history. The statutory channels may proceed in parallel. Reviewing the later removal necessarily includes examining the discipline relevant to its penalty; absence of a direct appeal from a minor action does not defeat that incidental authority. Darby supports, by analogy, refusal to add a procedural prerequisite absent from the enacted scheme. The strongest contrary concern is that unreliable prior discipline may magnify a removal penalty. That concern requires adequate review, not categorical exclusion of every action still under challenge. Independent review may satisfy the agency's obligations without awaiting the grievance. The conditional premise matters: this holding does not pronounce Bolling's particular mechanism adequate in every statutory or constitutional application.

**Judicial review and proof have different work.** Under §7703(c), a court reviews the Board's administrative choice for arbitrary or capricious action, abuse of discretion, legal error, required procedures and substantial evidence; it may not replace a permissible review policy with its preferred categorical rule. That standard does not diminish the agency's own statutory proof obligation or make agency expertise controlling on statutory meaning.

**Authority and explanation:** The same eight Justices join this proposition. Overton Park and State Farm supply the narrow review of administrative judgment while requiring lawful reasoned action. Greenwich Collieries keeps the §7701(c)(1)(B) preponderance obligation distinct from judicial review and from the Board's collateral-review formula. Respect for disciplinary administration and the presumption of regularity do not prove every predicate valid. Brown v. Gardner, Christensen and Mead preserve judicial interpretation within their respective limits.

**Adequacy reserved; the completed reversal receives lawful effect.** The Court leaves Bolling's actual statutory and constitutional adequacy undecided because Gregory did not seek another mechanism below or here and the categorical judgment can be corrected without resolving it. Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer adopt that nonreach rationale; Thomas does not join it. The completed arbitral expungement is distinct from mere pendency and must receive its legally required effect through the governing agreement and review mechanisms. Section 1201.118 permits discretionary Board reopening on its own motion; it does not compel reopening after every changed predicate. The remand requires examination of the warning's material role and the procedural consequences of its late presentation, rather than a judicial finding of dependence or automatic waiver.

## Precedent Treatment

- Darby v. Cisneros (June 21, 1993): applied by analogy against an extra procedural prerequisite; its APA provision is not directly imposed on this personnel statute.
- Citizens to Preserve Overton Park v. Volpe, 401 U.S. 402 (1971), and Motor Vehicle Manufacturers Assn. v. State Farm, 463 U.S. 29 (1983): applied to the bounds of judicial review of administrative judgment.
- Director, OWCP v. Greenwich Collieries (June 20, 1994): applied to keep proof burdens distinct from reviewing deference.
- Brown v. Gardner (December 12, 1994), Christensen v. Harris County (May 1, 2000), and United States v. Mead Corp. (June 18, 2001): preserved at their actual limits on administrative interpretation; no general conclusive-deference rule is created.
- United States v. Chemical Foundation, 272 U.S. 1 (1926): regularity does not establish that a particular predicate or collateral-review formula is valid.

## Law After Decision

A pending contractual grievance creates no categorical statutory bar to considering earlier discipline in later adverse-action review. Adequate independent review remains essential, and the agency's proof burden is separate from judicial review. Bolling's actual adequacy remains undecided by the Court. A completed reversal must receive the effect required by the applicable agreement and review framework through the available procedural mechanisms.

## Separate Writings

**Thomas, concurring.** Thomas joins the limited statutory opinion at the scope shown in the table, but would also decide statutory sufficiency because the Postal Service's reliance on Bolling makes that ground part of the presented dispute. Under Bolling, full fresh review applies if written notice, a recorded action, or an opportunity to challenge above the original imposing authority was absent. If all three exist, the employee may show clear error on the earlier record. Thomas considers that framework consistent with §7503's protections for minor discipline and its absence of a direct Board appeal. The statute does not require retrying the facts of every earlier action merely because it bears on a later penalty. The agency must establish the earlier action, while the appealed charge and penalty retain their own proof requirements. The contractual and Board routes protect different rights; one route's conclusion is not automatically a prerequisite for the other. He accepts remand concerning the overturned warning without commanding reopening. His affirmative statutory view neither becomes the Court's holding nor resolves every constitutional application.

**Ginsburg, concurring in the judgment.** Ginsburg questions whether restricted clear-error review can satisfy the statutory preponderance requirement when the earlier merits remain unadjudicated. The opportunity to contest a recorded action does not itself prove adequate review. She therefore withholds assent to the Court's independent-review rationale, without holding Bolling invalid or requiring fresh review of every final disciplinary action. Her remand rests on the Board's actual reopening power, Gregory's election of Board review of the removal, the reservation of Bolling's adequacy, and rejection of an absolute prohibition on agency consideration of pending discipline. Reopening provides a route to request consideration of the changed disciplinary record, not guaranteed relief. A refusal to address a material invalidated predicate could present an arbitrary-and-capricious question. The removal election does not abandon earlier grievances, establish personal control over union arbitration, or substitute for adequate Board process. Her distinction between final discipline and an action still being challenged remains a noncontrolling qualification.

## Procedure After Action

The Federal Circuit resumes review under the stated rule, addresses the expungement and available procedural consequences, and returns the penalty to the Board where legally necessary. The Board's reopening and procedural options retain their statutory and regulatory limits.

# Adarand Constructors, Inc. v. Mineta — 2001-11-27

[Authoritative Record](../records/adarand-constructors-inc-v-mineta_dig_2001-11-27.md)

## Event

Adarand Constructors, Inc. v. Mineta, No. 00-730. Argued October 31, 2001; decided November 27, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Tenth Circuit. The petition sought review of the judgment concerning the program governing state and local transportation contracting; the merits presentation pressed the different direct federal procurement regime.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated.

## Public Action

The writ of certiorari is dismissed as improvidently granted. The Court issues a per curiam explanation; no individual tally is published.

## Judgment & Remedy

Review is dismissed because the direct federal procurement regime urged here is materially different from the state and local transportation program whose merits were decided below, and the petition did not challenge the adverse standing determination governing the different regime. The lower-court judgment remains in place at its actual scope. This disposition does not adopt its constitutional reasoning.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Per curiam procedural explanation | The Court | No individual joins published | Explains dismissal of the writ | Program identity, preserved judgment and the omitted standing challenge |

## Holdings

**Procedural disposition; no substantive constitutional holding.** This proceeding does not permit decision of the direct federal procurement regime urged at the merits stage: the petition did not bring forward the adverse standing ruling governing that regime, and the court below did not decide its constitutional merits. The Court therefore dismisses the writ rather than decide the constitutionality of a materially different program.

**Public explanation:** A common contracting policy does not make two programs interchangeable when their operation, asserted injury, adjudicated questions and remedial targets differ. Slater removed an inadequate mootness premise; it did not eliminate this distinct standing ruling or expand the judgment under review. The governing racial-classification standard likewise cannot supply an omitted challenge or program-specific record. The Court leaves both the direct procurement program's constitutionality and the unpresented standing challenge undecided because they are outside the properly presented review. Dismissal approves neither program and creates no new equal-protection rule.

## Precedent Treatment

- Adarand Constructors, Inc. v. Peña (June 12, 1995): its common racial-justification standard and bounded program-specific remand remain in force; the dismissal does not decide their application to either program.

# Chickasaw Nation v. United States — 2001-11-27

[Authoritative Record](../records/chickasaw-nation-v-united-states_merits_2001-11-27.md)

## Event

Chickasaw Nation v. United States, No. 00-507, encompassing the Chickasaw and Choctaw Nations. Argued October 2, 2001; decided November 27, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Tenth Circuit's judgments rejecting the tribes' claimed exemption from federal wagering excise and occupational taxes. The question is whether 25 U.S.C. §2719(d)(1) carries chapter 35's state wagering-tax exemption into covered tribal gaming.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated.

## Public Action

The Court affirms the judgments rejecting the asserted §2719(d)(1) exemption for both tribes.

## Judgment & Remedy

## Holdings

**IGRA's reporting and withholding direction does not confer the claimed substantive tax exemption.** For Indian gaming conducted under IGRA or an effective Tribal-State compact entered under §2710(d)(3), §2719(d)(1) directs state-equivalent treatment of the Code provisions concerning reporting and withholding of taxes on gaming or wagering winnings. Its parenthetical chapter 35 reference does not extend that defined subject to chapter 35's substantive wagering excise and occupational taxes. Section 2719(d)(2) makes the subsection govern notwithstanding other law unless that law specifically cites the subsection; this priority applies only within (d)(1)'s scope.

**Authority:** Kennedy's opinion, joined by Stevens, Scalia, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies six direct votes for the complete construction and both affirmances.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court reads the operative description and its examples together. National Bank of Oregon permits the complete enactment to establish meaning rather than making an isolated textual feature decisive. Here the parenthetical illustrates the class defined by the reporting-and-withholding words; it does not create a second, broader class. The tribes' strongest objection is that this reading deprives the express chapter reference of its apparent substantive work. That is a real textual difficulty, but an inconsistent example cannot expand the enacted subject it purports to illustrate. State-equivalent treatment still governs the reporting and withholding provisions within the defined class. The Indian-law canon resolves genuine remaining ambiguity; it does not require the opposite construction after the whole clause fixes that scope. This conclusion rests on text and context, not a claimed power to delete any inconvenient reference, a fabricated drafting sequence, or hostility to protective tribal canons. The state-tax and treaty rules of Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Chickasaw Nation and Mille Lacs do not supply an independent federal tax exemption.

**Precedent treatment:** National Bank of Oregon is applied for contextual construction; the distinct state-tax and tribal-instrument precedents remain confined to their own predicates. Chapter 35's independent definitions, exclusions and liability-linked provisions remain operative; today's construction rejects the asserted IGRA exemption rather than erasing those provisions.

## Precedent Treatment

- United States National Bank of Oregon v. Independent Insurance Agents of America (June 7, 1993): applied to read the whole enacted provision; no general authority to rewrite statutory text is adopted.
- Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Chickasaw Nation (June 14, 1995): its state-tax incidence, residence, source and treaty rules are distinguished from this federal statutory exemption.
- Minnesota v. Mille Lacs Band of Chippewa Indians (March 24, 1999): its protective interpretation of retained tribal rights remains; it creates no exemption outside the scope of this resolved federal text.

## Law After Decision

Section 2719(d)(1) does not give covered tribal gaming chapter 35's state wagering-tax exemption. Reporting and withholding parity operates within the provision's described subject. Other Code exclusions, the actual tax base and the occupational tax's independent linkage remain governed by their own terms; no general tribal federal-tax immunity is recognized.

## Separate Writings

**Souter, joined by Stone-Zsela and O'Connor, dissenting.** The dissent would give the expressly named chapter operative effect. The reporting-and-withholding description and the substantive chapter reference pull in different directions; calling the reference an accident is not the only possible reading. Punctuation does not make text inside parentheses legally inferior, and specific inclusion may show that the general description is too narrow. National Bank requires integrated interpretation, not a license to discard the inconvenient side of an enacted conflict. At least genuine ambiguity remains after ordinary reading, and the Indian-law canon resolves it in favor of the tribes; tribal economic-development purposes support that reading without independently granting immunity.

The dissent would apply state-equivalent treatment to chapter 35's pertinent exemption for a sweepstakes, wagering pool or lottery conducted by a state agency under state law, where the wager is placed with that agency or its authorized employee or agent. Section 4411's occupational tax would then follow its own liability-linked terms. This is a construction of the IGRA cross-reference for covered operations, not an exemption for every tribe, member, game or federal tax. The dissent would reverse and remand for application to each tribe's actual assessments and relief, leaving transaction coverage and calculations to the tax record.

The Tenth Circuit judgments rejecting the asserted IGRA exemption are affirmed. No Supreme Court remand is ordered.

# Church of Scientology International v. Behar — 2001-11-27

[Authoritative Record](../records/church-of-scientology-international-v-behar_merits_2001-11-27.md)

## Event

Church of Scientology International v. Behar. Decided November 27, 2001. On review of the Second Circuit judgment, 238 F.3d 168, concerning the May 6, 1991 Time article. The question is whether the First Amendment supplies an independent subsidiary-meaning immunity for a challenged statement because actual malice was not proved for other accusations in the same publication.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated.

## Public Action

The Court vacates the judgment insofar as it dismisses the three Vancouver statements through an independent subsidiary-meaning immunity, and remands.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Vacated in part and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The affected statements are 5, 6 and 7 in the appellate opinion. The remand permits statement-specific adjudication of material falsity, materially distinct defamatory meaning, identification and actual malice, together with properly available alternative defenses. Independently supported dismissals of other statements, unappealed matters and the amendment request's independent procedural limits remain undisturbed. The remand guarantees neither trial nor additional discovery.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Kennedy | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports the judgment | Entire opinion |

## Holdings

**Contextual materiality does not create automatic immunity for a distinct accusation.** A challenged statement may be dismissed when, read in context, it conveys no materially false defamatory meaning. But the First Amendment does not immunize a materially distinct factual accusation merely because it is subsidiary to other serious accusations for which actual malice was not proved; the required fault inquiry concerns the distinct accusation itself.

**Authority:** Kennedy's opinion, joined by all eight other Justices, controls the complete rule and bounded remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court preserves the demanding protection New York Times v. Sullivan supplies: a public figure must prove a materially false defamatory assertion of and concerning it, published with knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard, with clear-and-convincing proof of actual malice. Context remains essential. Masson permits immaterial differences to be excluded from actionable defamation, but rejects a constitutionally compelled incremental-harm defense and keeps meaning, fault and injury distinct. The strongest defense of subsidiary-meaning immunity is that a supported general portrayal leaves an additional accusation with no meaningful defamatory work. Ordinary contextual analysis may establish that premise in a particular case; protection of one accusation does not itself establish it for another. Nor does absence of actual malice about one assertion prove the publisher's belief about a separate assertion. The Vancouver statements concern a source of funds and particular takeover, stock and property conduct capable of adding a distinct charge. The panel's reliance on the article's general portrayal did not resolve all their separate elements. Statement-specific adjudication is therefore required through the ordinary procedures, including an adequately supported alternative summary-judgment ground.

**Precedent treatment:** Sullivan's fault burden remains unchanged; Masson's separation of materiality, fault and incremental injury governs. The Second Circuit's Herbert subsidiary-meaning rule is rejected only insofar as it supplies the independent constitutional immunity described here.

**Limits and remaining adjudication.** Ordinary state rules governing injury, causation and damages retain the role Masson left them. The of-and-concerning defense and other undecided statement-specific elements remain for the lower courts because the panel did not resolve them and the record does not compel their resolution here. Nominal damages do not eliminate the conceded public-figure fault requirement. The distinct libel-proof-plaintiff doctrine lies outside the question decided.

## Precedent Treatment

- New York Times Co. v. Sullivan, 376 U.S. 254 (1964): reaffirmed for the public-figure requirement of knowing falsity or reckless disregard and constitutionally sufficient proof.
- St. Amant v. Thompson, 390 U.S. 727 (1968), and Harte-Hanks Communications v. Connaughton, 491 U.S. 657 (1989): applied for subjective fault rather than mere hostility or poor journalism.
- Masson v. New Yorker Magazine, Inc., 501 U.S. 496 (1991): applied to separate material falsity, contextual meaning, fault and injury, reject a constitutionally compelled incremental-harm defense, and preserve ordinary state tort rules.
- Bartnicki v. Vopper (May 21, 2001): its accuracy-dependent protection at its stated factual scope supplies no article-wide immunity for distinct false accusations.
- The Second Circuit's Herbert subsidiary-meaning rule: displaced to the extent it insulates a materially distinct accusation from the required fault inquiry solely because other accusations are protected.

## Law After Decision

The First Amendment supplies no automatic subsidiary-meaning immunity for a materially distinct defamatory factual accusation. Context may establish absence of actionable material falsity, and the public-figure burden remains demanding and statement-specific. Protected criticism elsewhere in a publication cannot replace those inquiries. State-law injury, causation and damages rules remain separately operative.

# Correctional Services Corp. v. Malesko — 2001-11-27

[Authoritative Record](../records/correctional-services-corp-v-malesko_merits_2001-11-27.md)

## Event

Correctional Services Corp. v. Malesko, No. 00-860. Argued October 1, 2001; decided November 27, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Second Circuit, which reversed dismissal of the constitutional damages claim against the private operator of a federal halfway house. The question is whether a private federal custodial corporation may answer for an Eighth Amendment injury caused by its own unconstitutional policy, practice or authorized decision.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated.

## Public Action

The Court affirms the availability of a confined constitutional damages claim against the private federal custodial operator for its own unconstitutional conduct and remands for application to the pleading.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed at the stated scope and remanded, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

The lower courts must determine whether the pleading identifies a corporate policy, established practice or authorized corporate decision causing deliberate indifference and injury in the exercise of federal custody, permitting the ordinary pleading process under applicable rules. Automatic responsibility for an employee's wrong is excluded. The independent limitations judgments concerning individual employees remain in place.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stevens | Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Affirms the confined cause and remands | Entire opinion |
| Dissent | O'Connor | Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Would reverse and dismiss the corporate claim | Entire common new-defendant/judicial-remedy objection |
| Separate dissent | Scalia | Thomas | Also joins O'Connor's dissent | Distinct refusal of further constitutional-damages extensions beyond Bivens, Davis and Carlson |

## Holdings

**A private federal custodian answers for its own constitutional misconduct.** A private corporation entrusted with federal custody may be sued in damages for an Eighth Amendment violation caused by its own policy, established practice or authorized corporate decision in performing that custodial function. The claim requires deliberate indifference, direct corporate attribution and causation; employee negligence or automatic vicarious responsibility alone is insufficient.

**Authority:** Stevens's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies five direct votes for the full cause, its limitations and the remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court recognizes a limited extension of the custodial damages remedy preserved by Carlson. Custody gives the operator control over basic conditions and access to necessary care; when the company itself makes the unconstitutional decision, an employee-only remedy can miss the responsible actor. Meyer does not mechanically resolve this different defendant. It rejects direct agency liability and a new public fiscal obligation; a private operator's own decision lacks that agency and fiscal premise. The Court nevertheless accepts Meyer's distinction between having a constitutional duty and having a cause of action. The extension rests on Carlson's established protection for persons in federal custody and on direct private responsibility, not on a waiver of immunity or federal contracting alone. The dissent correctly identifies a new entity remedy and asks why useful compensation should authorize it. Here the established custodial injury, the corporation's own decision and the absence of the agency obligation provide the bounded remedial justification. State negligence remedies and prospective relief remain relevant, but neither necessarily addresses the corporation's own completed, deliberate constitutional wrong. Richardson distinguishes private custodial operation from automatic public immunity; it does not itself create this cause. The own-conduct requirement prevents the remedy from becoming liability for every employee mistake or every federal contractor.

**Precedent treatment:** Carlson's custodial remedy is extended to the private operator's own culpable conduct; Meyer remains controlling for federal agencies and is distinguished at its actual grounds; Richardson's separate immunity rule is preserved.

**Application and limit.** The elevator allegation must be assessed for an actual corporate policy, practice or authorized decision, deliberate indifference and causation. The seriousness of an alleged injury does not establish those elements. The recognized cause is confined to direct corporate responsibility in federal custody; individual-employee liability is outside the question presented. Ordinary pleading and proof govern the remand, and the time-barred employee claims are not revived.

## Precedent Treatment

- Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents, 403 U.S. 388 (1971), and Davis v. Passman, 442 U.S. 228 (1979): their recognized individual constitutional damages settings remain operative.
- Carlson v. Green, 446 U.S. 14 (1980): its federal custodial damages function supports the confined extension to a private custodian's own unconstitutional policy, practice or authorized decision.
- FDIC v. Meyer (February 23, 1994): its independent agency-cause and public fiscal/remedial grounds remain; they do not automatically determine the different private own-conduct claim.
- Richardson v. McKnight (June 23, 1997): its immunity rule is distinguished from the separate cause-of-action question and is not converted into a holding on corporate liability.
- Alexander v. Sandoval (April 24, 2001): its limited prospective statutory remedy remains distinct; it supplies neither a universal prohibition nor automatic authorization of constitutional damages.

## Law After Decision

A confined federal constitutional damages cause is available against a private corporation exercising federal custody for its own deliberate constitutional misconduct through policy, established practice or authorized decision. Corporate attribution and causation must be proved; ordinary negligence and respondeat superior do not suffice. Meyer's federal-agency bar and existing individual-action rules remain in force.

## Separate Writings

**O'Connor, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, dissenting.** The dissent would reverse and dismiss the corporate constitutional claim. Meyer gives independent force to the distinction between an established officer action and judicial creation of a new entity remedy. A federal custodial duty, useful compensation and the absence of sovereign immunity do not themselves supply that cause. The private defendant weakens the public-fisc concern, but does not eliminate the separate question of who may create a corporate constitutional remedy. Richardson addresses immunity and expressly does not resolve corporate liability or cause creation. Individual actions, state tort remedies, prospective relief and contractual oversight retain their actual scope and reinforce remedial restraint; the dissent does not need to assume that tort relief exactly duplicates every constitutional injury. Limiting the proposed action to corporate policy narrows liability but still adds the disputed defendant. The dissent's objection concerns creation of that cause, not a finding that the company complied with the Constitution or a grant of sovereign status to every contractor.

**Scalia, joined by Thomas, dissenting separately.** They join O'Connor's dissent and add that the Court should not extend implied constitutional damages beyond the recognized Bivens, Davis and Carlson settings even when a proposed extension can be analogized to their reasoning. That distinct objection reaches the Court's claim of a sufficiently close custodial analogy: accepting the analogy does not, in their view, authorize another judicially created damages action. Existing recognized causes remain operative. They do not rely on treating Sandoval as a controlling general abandonment of statutory implication, because its actual statutory holding supplies no such premise. Their separate ground neither decides private employee liability nor overrules the recognized constitutional actions.

The case returns for application of the confined cause to the actual pleading and, if the claim proceeds under ordinary rules, proof of its elements. The independent employee-limitations dispositions retain their force.

# J. E. M. Ag Supply, Inc. v. Pioneer Hi-Bred International, Inc. — 2001-12-10

[Authoritative Record](../records/j-e-m-ag-supply-inc-v-pioneer-hi-bred-international-inc_merits_2001-12-10.md)

## Event

J. E. M. Ag Supply, Inc. v. Pioneer Hi-Bred International, Inc., No. 99-1996. Merits decision, December 10, 2001. Argued October 3, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Federal Circuit. Pioneer asserted utility patents concerning corn varieties against unauthorized seed sellers. The question is whether the Plant Patent Act and Plant Variety Protection Act categorically exclude newly developed plants from utility-patent protection under §101. The accepted defense concerns statutory eligibility and exclusivity, with patent-specific and transaction-specific issues remaining distinct.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument or submission and decision.

## Public Action

Affirm, 7–2.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 7–2.** Supporting: Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg. Opposing: Stevens and Breyer. The categorical ineligibility defense is rejected. Patent litigation may continue under the independent requirements of the protection actually invoked.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Souter | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg | Supports affirmance | Entire eligibility and coexistence opinion |
| Concurrence | Scalia | No other Justice | Joins the Court in full | Additional statutory-canon and precedential-construction rationale |
| Dissent | Stevens | Breyer | Would reverse and remand | Specific plant-regime exclusivity and preservation of seed and research protections |

## Holdings

### The plant statutes permit concurrent utility-patent protection

**Holding and operative rule:** Newly developed plants, including the corn varieties at issue, are not categorically excluded from 35 U.S.C. §101 because the Plant Patent Act or Plant Variety Protection Act also covers the subject. A claimant invoking utility protection must meet that statute's independent requirements; certificate-specific exceptions govern their own statutory right and do not create a general immunity from a valid utility patent.

**Authority:** The Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg; seven direct joins support the complete eligibility and coexistence rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress's plant-specific protections can operate alongside the broadly expressed utility-patent categories. Chakrabarty rejects a categorical living-subject exclusion and gives effect to human invention within the statutory language. The Plant Patent Act's limited reproduction right and relaxed description requirement address obstacles to protection without declaring that a plant satisfying the more demanding general statute loses that protection. The Plant Variety Protection Act likewise supplies a distinct right with distinct requirements and exceptions.

The contrary argument has practical force: utility rights can restrict seed saving or research that the certificate regime permits. But different rights and eligibility requirements are not an irreconcilable statutory conflict. Neither plant statute expressly makes its protection exclusive, and enactment or recodification does not require exclusion from §101. Each statute retains work; a court may not create a hybrid by importing certificate exceptions into utility-patent law.

Asgrow construes the former certificate saved-seed provision. It does not answer utility-patent exclusivity or supply a universal agricultural exemption. Its former-law limits and the enacted transition remain effective within the certificate regime. Administrative practice and legislative silence are not treated as binding interpretations. The present category-wide answer leaves novelty, nonobviousness, disclosure and other patent requirements independently operative; it establishes neither validity of every asserted claim nor enforceability of every sales restriction.

**Precedent treatment:** Chakrabarty's broad statutory reading is applied; Asgrow remains confined to its certificate setting; Deuel and State Street's independent requirements and limits remain intact.

### Certificate protections retain their own statutory version and exceptions

**Holding and operative rule:** Concurrent utility eligibility does not rewrite the protection supplied by a plant-variety certificate. Former-law saved-seed protection and the amendments effective April 4, 1995 retain their enacted conditions and transition; no specific certificate, seed transaction or exception entitlement is adjudicated here.

**Authority:** The same seven-Justice Opinion of the Court preserves these boundaries as part of the coexistence rule.

**Controlling explanation:** Under Asgrow, former §2543 permits reproductive sale only of seed genuinely saved for the farmer's own planting, produced from seed obtained with the owner's authority or its descendants. Seller and purchaser must primarily farm for nonreproductive crop sales, and the sale must comply with state seed-sale law. Changed planting plans may permit disposition of genuinely retained seed; intentional multiplication for marketing is not exempt. The independent protection for bona fide nonreproductive sales in customary channels remains; a buyer who diverts seed sold through those channels to planting is deemed to have section 2567 notice that the diversion infringes. Section 2544's exemption for use and reproduction for plant breeding or other bona fide research likewise belongs to that certificate regime.

The 1994 amendments delete the former farmer-sales proviso for the amended regime from April 4, 1995. Except as section 14 itself provides through the refiling rule of section 14(b) and the notice rule of section 14(c) described next, section 14(a) keeps varieties with certificates issued before April 4, 1995, or with applications pending on that date, under the Plant Variety Protection Act as in effect the day before. Section 14(b) permits refiling under amended terms while retaining the original filing date for §42. Section 14(c) requires identification of amended protection in a notice claiming it and retains sanctions for false or misleading claims or labels. These rules continue to govern the certificate right; none is converted into an exception to a separate utility patent. The Court makes no transaction-specific exception or transition finding.

**Precedent treatment:** Asgrow's saved-seed construction and former-law force are preserved; the admitted amendment's express transition remains unchanged.

## Precedent Treatment

- Diamond v. Chakrabarty, 447 U.S. 303: its broad reading of §101 applies to qualifying human invention rather than a categorical exclusion of living subject matter.
- Asgrow Seed Co. v. Winterboer, No. 92-2038: its former certificate saved-seed construction and transition limits remain; no utility-patent exception is derived from it.
- In re Deuel, January 14, 1997: ordinary supported nonobviousness requirements remain distinct from category eligibility.
- State Street Bank & Trust Co. v. Signature Financial Group, Inc., January 14, 1999: broad category language does not remove genuine statutory limitations or independent proof requirements.

## Law After Decision

Plants meeting the utility statute's requirements may receive utility patents alongside the plant-specific regimes. Each right carries its own requirements and exceptions. The certificate amendments and Asgrow's surviving former-law operation retain their actual reach; the decision supplies no generalized saved-seed or research exemption from utility-patent rights.

## Separate Writings

Scalia concurs and joins the Court in full. Chakrabarty already authoritatively construed the general subject-matter language to reject the supposed ambiguity based on living subject matter. The plant-specific statutes therefore cannot merely be used to clarify that language into the opposite categorical exclusion; a claimed change must satisfy the more demanding inquiry into displacement of the existing rule. This canon explanation supplements rather than narrows the Court's eligibility and coexistence rationale.

Stevens dissents, joined by Breyer, and would treat the plant-specific enactments as the exclusive protection for their covered plant categories. The 1930 Act expressly amended the general patent provision, conditioned protection on asexual reproduction and limited the reproduction right; the later variety statute supplied seed-reproduction protection with saved-seed and research limits. In their view, the utility route bypasses those particular congressional choices, and recodification did not undo them. Different eligibility thresholds do not explain why increased originality should eliminate protections Congress deliberately conferred on agricultural users. Chakrabarty's human-made microorganism lay outside those specialized categories, so the dissent preserves its holding and does not exclude all living inventions or independently qualifying processes. The dissent would reverse on the category defense.

## Procedure After Action

The Federal Circuit's rejection of the categorical defense stands. Remaining patent-specific validity, exhaustion, infringement, competition and contract issues proceed only within their ordinary preserved scope; this decision resolves none of them by the eligibility answer alone.

**Authority:** The Opinion of the Court on the merits, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer; five direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that support sufficient to place a congressional candidate on the ballot does not itself establish the separate statewide party status represented by Ohio's official nomination cue. Anderson and Burdick require attention to the actual burden and the fit of the State's interests, rather than treating the designation as immune from review. Timmons permits proportionate regulation of official nomination machinery within a system that leaves meaningful political participation.

Schrader's verified nomination and affiliation make his associational interest real. They do not compel Ohio to redesign its official designation to distinguish affiliation from recognized-party nomination whenever an alternative wording is feasible. Independent access and campaigning reduce the burden; they do not make it disappear. The State may use reasonable preventive categories without waiting for actual disruption.

Norman protected authorized geographic party expansion and independently qualified candidates from disqualification. This candidate remains on the ballot through a functioning nomination route, and the different party-recognition designation serves a distinct purpose. Tashjian and Eu's associational protection does not settle every official ballot format. The Court adopts neither pure rational-basis review nor a presumption validating every affiliation prohibition. More restrictive access systems and unsupported burdens remain subject to the governing calibrated inquiry.

**Precedent treatment:** Anderson and Burdick supply the calibrated framework; Timmons's nomination-system reasoning is applied to this distinct designation burden; Norman is distinguished by the preserved candidate access and different state-status cue; Tashjian and Eu retain their associational protections.

## Precedent Treatment

- Anderson v. Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780, and Burdick v. Takushi, 504 U.S. 428: the actual burden, precise interests and proportional fit remain controlling; no write-in presumption is transferred to labels.
- Timmons v. Twin Cities Area New Party, No. 95-1608: its contextual nomination-system reasoning supports this bounded application, without universal approval of recognition restrictions.
- Norman v. Reed, 502 U.S. 279: authorized expansion and independently qualified slate access remain protected; the present official status designation is distinguished.
- Tashjian v. Republican Party of Connecticut, 479 U.S. 208, and Eu v. San Francisco County Democratic Central Committee, 489 U.S. 214: associational protection remains, without compelling every requested ballot format.

## Law After Decision

The official party designation may remain linked to Ohio's recognition system in these circumstances. The holding applies burden-and-fit review to a designation restriction; it supplies no categorical approval of every recognition threshold or prohibition of truthful affiliation.

## Separate Writings

Stevens dissents, joined by Stone-Zsela, Souter and Ginsburg, and would distinguish verified affiliation from the privileges and official representation of recognized-party nomination. Because the candidate has independently qualified and the party actually nominated him, the State must explain why every accurately qualified affiliation cue must be suppressed. Anderson-Burdick requires that fit even when the burden is less than severe; campaign speech does not eliminate the distinct ballot burden. Norman, Tashjian and Eu give weight to the real association and supported standard bearer. Timmons concerned fusion nomination and does not decide this separate informational burden. The dissent would permit the State to verify affiliation and choose accurate wording, and would recognize demonstrated administrative or effective-governing interests rather than impose a universal preferred-label right. # United States v. Knights — 2001-12-10

[Authoritative Record](../records/united-states-v-knights_merits_2001-12-10.md)

## Event

United States v. Knights, No. 00-1260. Merits decision, December 10, 2001. Argued November 6, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, which affirmed suppression because the residence search pursued ordinary criminal investigation. The question is whether a known probation search condition and individualized reasonable suspicion justify the search notwithstanding that purpose. The written condition permitted searches by probation or law-enforcement officers, and the detective had reasonable suspicion connecting the residence to criminal activity.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.** Supporting: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. The categorical investigative-purpose ground for suppression is rejected. The lower courts apply the condition-and-reasonable-suspicion rule; evidence is admissible if no independently preserved defect remains. Any genuinely open scope or factual issue must be resolved on its own record.

## Opinion Topology

## Holdings

### The known search condition and individualized suspicion justify this probation search

**Holding and operative rule:** A warrantless search of a probationer's residence within a known, lawful search condition is reasonable when supported by particularized reasonable suspicion of criminal activity in the circumstances presented. Edmond and Ferguson retain their purpose inquiries in their suspicionless and special-needs settings. Scott governs the consequence of an unconstitutional supervisory search, not the antecedent validity of this condition-and-suspicion search; its exclusion rule remains intact. Any ultimate suspicion question properly remaining is independently reviewed under Ornelas, with its protection for historical findings and due weight to grounded contextual inferences.

The holding rests on reasonableness, not complete contractual consent or forfeiture of Fourth Amendment protection. Suspicionless residential searches and blanket consent are outside the question necessarily resolved because individualized suspicion supplies the sufficient ground here.

**Precedent treatment:** Griffin's supervisory-relationship principle is applied without making its rationale exclusive; Whren's objective method is applied within its source-specific limits; Edmond and Ferguson are distinguished; Scott's exclusion rule and Ornelas's review allocation remain operative.

# Dusenbery v. United States — 2002-01-08

[Authoritative Record](../records/dusenbery-v-united-states_merits_2002-01-08.md)

## Event

Dusenbery v. United States, No. 00-6567. Merits decision, January 8, 2002. Argued October 29, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Sixth Circuit, which sustained the forfeiture-notice procedure. The FBI sent certified notice to the federal institution holding Dusenbery; notice mailed November 7, 1988 identified the forfeiture, contest process and December 19 claim deadline. An employee acknowledged postal receipt. Testimony concerning the relevant period described collection, certified-mail logging, receipt by the responsible unit employee and ordinary delivery to the inmate. The old log was unavailable under a one-year retention practice, and actual personal receipt was not established. The question is whether this process satisfied due process without proof of actual receipt.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument or submission and decision.

## Public Action

Affirm, 5–4.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 5–4.** Supporting: Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. Opposing: Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. The challenged notice determination stands on the reasonably calculated institutional route. The dissenters would vacate and remand for the consequences of the insufficiently demonstrated final custodial handoff.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stone-Zsela | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Supports affirmance | Entire proved-route and notice opinion |
| Dissent | Stevens | Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Would vacate and remand | Mullane framework accepted; final custodial-handoff application rejected |

## Holdings

### A proved reasonable custodial route can satisfy due process without proof of actual receipt

**Holding and operative rule:** Due process requires means reasonably calculated in the known circumstances to convey forfeiture notice and afford a meaningful opportunity to object, not an absolute guarantee of actual receipt. For a known confined owner, certified delivery coupled with an established, reasonably functioning internal distribution route to that inmate can satisfy the obligation; a signature at the institution's entrance alone is not universally sufficient.

**Authority:** The Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; five direct joins support the standard, its application and affirmance. The four dissenters accept Mullane and reject a guaranteed-receipt requirement but do not join the adequacy application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the proved route was reasonably directed to Dusenbery. Mullane asks prospectively whether the means reasonably communicate in the actual circumstances. The government demonstrated more than dispatch to a prison address: employees collected certified mail, logged it, obtained the receiving unit employee's signature and ordinarily delivered it to inmates. The district court credited that relevant-period practice evidence. The missing old log does not establish an unreasonable system, and testimony about ordinary operation can establish the route without proof of this item's final receipt.

The contrary argument correctly emphasizes the government's exclusive custodial control. The inquiry therefore follows the onward route rather than ending at employee receipt. But neither Mullane nor Mennonite makes a particular additional verification measure or guaranteed success indispensable on this evidence. A feasible improvement alone does not invalidate the earlier reasonable procedure. Other attempted addresses supply no independent cure if the custodial route were deficient.

West Covina's protection of usable notice remains; its rejection of an individualized guide to published law does not excuse a deficient delivery method. Mathews's general calibration does not displace the settled notice-specific standard. This decision concerns the established procedure for the relevant period and the absence of an established known failure; it does not validate every prison mailing or resolve a different record involving unreliability or known nondelivery.

**Precedent treatment:** Mullane and Mennonite are applied to the actual route; West Covina's distinction between usable notice and legal instruction remains; Good's different pre-seizure requirement is not altered.

## Precedent Treatment

- Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co., 339 U.S. 306: reasonable communication in the known circumstances governs; neither ritual dispatch nor guaranteed receipt is sufficient as a universal formula.
- Mennonite Board of Missions v. Adams, 462 U.S. 791: the practical-notice principle is applied without an absolute successful-receipt requirement.
- City of West Covina v. Perkins, No. 97-1230: usable notice and adequate access remain distinct from individualized legal instruction; that decision does not settle this delivery application.
- Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S. 319: its general procedural calibration does not replace Mullane's notice-specific standard.
- United States v. James Daniel Good Real Property: its distinct civil real-property pre-seizure protections remain intact.

## Law After Decision

A known prisoner must receive notice through a reasonably calculated custodial route. The proved certified-mail and internal-distribution process satisfies that standard without proof of actual receipt. The holding is procedure-based and leaves genuinely unreliable or known-failure situations to their own records.

## Separate Writings

Stevens dissents, joined by Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, and agrees that due process does not guarantee receipt. It concludes that the government has not sufficiently demonstrated the final custodial handoff. A confined owner cannot supervise or replace the government's intermediary, so Mullane requires practical attention to how notice reaches that person, including reasonably available safeguards. Evidence of transmission between employees does not adequately establish the final inmate-directed protection. Greene and Mennonite emphasize practical communication; Good and West Covina preserve a meaningful opportunity to respond. The dissent does not infer nonreceipt from the denial or the missing old records, invent a failure rate, demand one particular signature form, or apply later prison practices retroactively. It would set aside the notice judgment and return for the proper consequences of the defect, without treating inadequate notice as proof of ownership or automatic entitlement to the property.

## Procedure After Action

The Sixth Circuit's determination sustaining the challenged forfeiture notice stands. The judgment establishes constitutional adequacy of the proved procedure, without finding Dusenbery's actual receipt.

U.S. Const. amend. V; Dusenbery v. United States, No. 00-6567, 534 U.S. 161; J.A. 18–21, 36–38, 46–54, 59–62 and 67–71, as identified in the United States' merits presentation, for the relevant-period mailing practice. Personal receipt is not established.

# Great-West Life & Annuity Insurance Co. v. Knudson — 2002-01-08

[Authoritative Record](../records/great-west-life-annuity-insurance-co-v-knudson_merits_2002-01-08.md)

## Event

Great-West Life & Annuity Insurance Co. v. Knudson, No. 99-1786. Merits decision, January 8, 2002. Argued October 1, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, which affirmed rejection of the plan's reimbursement claim. The question is whether this demand for repayment of medical benefits after a tort settlement is appropriate equitable relief under ERISA §502(a)(3). The settlement property was distributed to a special-needs trust and an attorney rather than held by the named beneficiaries in the manner required by the asserted property remedy. The claim seeks personal payment from those beneficiaries; the plan's contractual entitlement and the available federal remedy are distinct questions.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument or submission and decision.

## Public Action

Affirm, 5–4.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 5–4.** Supporting: Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. Opposing: Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. The particular reimbursement demand is unavailable under §502(a)(3), and its dismissal stands. The four dissenters would vacate the statutory-remedy rejection and remand for plan construction and the necessary restitution predicates.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Thomas | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy | Supports affirmance | Entire traditional-remedy and property-defendant rationale |
| Dissent | Souter | Stevens, Ginsburg, Breyer | Would vacate and remand | Gain-restoration restitution category, with plan entitlement and actual remedial predicates left for adjudication |

## Holdings

### A contractual payment obligation does not establish an equitable remedy

**Holding and operative rule:** ERISA §502(a)(3) authorizes appropriate traditionally equitable relief, not personal payment of a past-due contractual debt merely because a claimant calls its demand an injunction, specific performance or restitution. A constructive trust or equitable lien ordinarily requires specifically identifiable property or proceeds, an equitable entitlement and the required connection to the defendant holding that property, subject to applicable defenses.

**Authority:** The Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; five direct joins establish the entire rule and its application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the remedy's actual predicates, not its label, determine whether it is equitable. Mertens gives the statutory limitation independent force by distinguishing traditional equitable categories from all relief once obtainable in a trust court. An injunction to pay an accrued debt does not avoid that distinction. Equity's exceptional use of specific performance to prevent otherwise inadequately remediable future losses does not supply this demand for an accrued sum.

Restitution has legal and equitable forms. The property-based form ordinarily restores identifiable property or proceeds from the defendant who holds them; an obligation to repay a benefit from other assets is different. Here settlement property went to the separate trust and attorney. The named beneficiaries' asserted contractual debt cannot substitute for the connection required by the remedy invoked against them. A charge against a beneficiary's trust interest also differs from the personal money judgment sought here. This is not a universal tracing formula for every equitable remedy; an accounting for profits has distinct predicates and is not this claim.

The contrary gain-restoration argument correctly recognizes that some restitution is equitable. It does not establish that this particular demand satisfies the traditional remedy's conditions. Harris Trust confirms genuine recipient equity within its own predicates and defenses, while Varity preserves appropriate individual relief within statutory categories. Neither turns every omitted remedy into authorized equity. The statutory ground sustains dismissal without deciding the alternative interpretation of the plan terms.

**Precedent treatment:** Mertens's traditional-category construction governs; Harris Trust's recipient equity is preserved rather than converted to personal collection; Varity retains its individual-equity channel and ordinary adequacy limits.

## Precedent Treatment

- Mertens v. Hewitt Associates, 508 U.S. 248: its traditional-equitable-category construction controls; neither its compensation exclusion nor preserved genuine monetary equity is displaced.
- Harris Trust and Savings Bank v. Salomon Smith Barney Inc., 530 U.S. 238: equitable recipient relief remains available on its actual predicates and defenses; it supplies neither this missing property connection nor a universal tracing formula.
- Varity Corp. v. Howe, 516 U.S. 489: appropriate individual equity remains within statutory categories and ordinary adequate-remedy limits.

## Law After Decision

Section 502(a)(3) does not authorize this demand for personal payment merely from the reimbursement clause. Genuine property-based equity remains available when its defendant, property and defense requirements are met. A different claim against another holder requires its own proper pleading, jurisdiction, process and equitable basis; the present judgment decides no such claim.

## Separate Writings

Souter dissents, joined by Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer, and would recognize the asserted return of benefits from a third-party recovery as the traditionally equitable restitution category. The claim is measured by alleged gain rather than the plan-loss compensation rejected in Mertens. A category need have been typically available in equity, not exclusive to equity, and direct possession should not control every restorative theory. Varity supports giving the redress provision practical effect within its limits, while Harris Trust recognizes that appropriate restoration can reach beyond fiduciaries. The government's trust-beneficiary analogy supports that characterization without establishing new property facts. The dissent would remove the categorical remedy bar and return plan interpretation and the actual remedial predicates for adjudication. It would award no reimbursement amount and would not adjudicate absent recipients' property.

## Procedure After Action

The dismissal of the reviewed federal reimbursement claim stands. An independently available claim against an appropriate holder remains subject to ordinary procedural rules; this judgment orders neither amendment nor joinder of the trust and decides no absent recipient's property rights.

# Searles v. Van Bebber — 2002-01-08

Searles v. Van Bebber. Merits decision, January 8, 2002. Review of the Tenth Circuit judgment reported at 251 F.3d 869. ## Holdings

### The named injury restriction bars the compensatory award, not every remedy

**Holding and operative rule:** Original 42 U.S.C. §1997e(e) bars an action by a prisoner confined at filing for mental or emotional injury suffered in custody without the required prior showing of physical injury. That restriction bars the actual distress compensation awarded here even though the underlying wrong is constitutional; it does not erase the violation or compensation-independent remedies.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join this statutory holding in the Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress restricted recovery for a named injury category. Harris v. Garner establishes the confinement-at-filing trigger and the mental-or-emotional-injury limit; it does not convert that limit into immunity from every remedy. The jury found no physical injury, and the $3,650 award compensated mental or emotional harm. The religious source of the right does not exempt that recovery. Carey and Stachura also prevent compensation based merely on an abstract valuation of the constitutional right. The all-damages reading goes too far because it ignores the distinct functions of nominal recognition and punishment, while the contrary compensation argument cannot save a jury award for the expressly barred injury.

**Precedent treatment:** Harris is applied at its actual injury-and-filing scope; Carey and Stachura retain the distinction between a violation and compensable loss.

### Nominal damages recognize the established violation

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1997e(e) does not bar nominal damages recognizing an established constitutional violation without compensable injury. On the preserved violation and lack of compensable injury here, the plaintiff is entitled to one dollar.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join this holding in the Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** Nominal relief does not compensate the mental or emotional injury named in the statute. Carey permits recognition of an established constitutional wrong without inventing injury or valuing the right itself as compensatory loss. The preserved liability verdict therefore requires the one-dollar award even though the distress compensation cannot stand.

**Precedent treatment:** Carey v. Piphus supplies nominal recognition; Stachura's prohibition on abstract-right compensation remains.

### Punitive eligibility survives, but the amount requires a lawful redetermination

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1997e(e) does not categorically bar punitive damages for conduct satisfying Smith v. Wade's evil-motive-or-intent or reckless-or-callous-indifference standard. Punishment remains discretionary even when that culpability is established; an amount expressly tied to invalid compensation must be redetermined independently, preserving separate liability and culpability findings.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join this holding and remedy in the Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** Punitive damages punish culpable conduct and deter its repetition; they do not compensate distress. Smith supplies a distinct remedial predicate rather than a rule that punitive fault must invariably exceed every substantive violation standard. The jury separately made the required culpability finding, which survives the compensation defect. But the instruction expressly relied on the invalid compensatory award in setting the punitive amount. That linkage prevents preserving $42,500 as an independently sound calculation. The corrected proceeding concerns amount, without retrying established liability or punitive culpability. A positive punitive award is not compelled, punishment may not reproduce barred compensation, and no automatic punitive-to-nominal ratio applies. Fees must then be reconsidered in light of the lawful recovery.

**Precedent treatment:** Smith v. Wade's culpability requirement and discretionary punishment are applied; the distinct remedial functions recognized in Carey and Stachura prevent relabeling barred compensation.

**Holding and operative rule:** Applying the physical-injury restriction to this distress award does not deny access to court or due process where the established right remains enforceable through nominal relief and punitive relief on its distinct predicates. This ruling resolves the preserved challenge in these circumstances, without validating every possible application of the statute.

**Authority:** Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer join this portion of the Opinion of the Court. Stone-Zsela agrees in its disposition only.

**Controlling explanation:** The statute restricts one injury-based recovery rather than withdrawing adjudication of the underlying right. Searles has established liability, receives nominal relief and retains punitive relief subject to its lawful standard. At this actual scope, the constitutional objection therefore supplies no reason to restore barred compensation or upset the preserved remedial distinctions.

**Precedent treatment:** Harris's bounded restriction and Carey and Smith's distinct remedies operate together; no remedy-wide immunity follows.

This is an individualized standard requiring considerable functional limitation, not complete incapacity or loss of virtually every manual ability; diagnosis alone is insufficient, and no numerical duration threshold applies.

**Authority:** The Opinion of the Court's manual-task-standard and application portions, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Eight direct joins establish the rule; Stone-Zsela does not join these portions.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that substantial limitation concerns a considerable restriction in the major activity asserted. The ADA's common definition is not confined to employment, so specialized assembly requirements cannot alone define manual-task disability. The original text and contemporaneous guidance concerning nature, severity, expected duration and enduring impact support the stated threshold.

Bragdon requires individual proof and rejects complete inability as an added condition. It does not erase substantiality or prohibit this activity-specific duration requirement. A permanent or long-term functional impact need not mean an incurable diagnosis; the Court sets no numerical minimum. Likewise, identifying central daily-life manual tasks does not restrict every major life activity to something performed each day or displace Bragdon's reproduction holding.

Williams is correct that sustained gripping, reaching and repetition may show important general capacities even if some household tasks remain possible. The standard admits that evidence; it rejects treating a class of demanding occupational tasks as sufficient by category alone. Household and self-care evidence also matters in its actual extent, without creating an automatic defense from the ability to perform some tasks. Ordinary summary-judgment principles govern competing evidence. The Court consults contemporaneous guidance without deciding general binding agency authority.

**Precedent treatment:** Bragdon's individual-proof and no-complete-incapacity principles remain; Sutton and Murphy's distinct occupational-breadth inquiry is not transferred to every activity; Kirkingburg supplies no additional disability method.

### Occupational classification is not a substitute for the claimed activity

**Holding and operative rule:** Inability to perform a class of occupational tasks does not by itself establish substantial limitation in the separate major life activity of performing manual tasks. Workplace evidence remains relevant to the extent it proves limitation of the general manual functions at issue; ordinary household and self-care evidence must likewise be assessed without categorical conclusions.

**Authority:** All nine Justices directly join this severable proposition in the Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** Sutton and Murphy's class-of-jobs inquiry addresses working. A court cannot use that inquiry as a shortcut to establish a different activity. The Sixth Circuit's occupational category therefore does not establish Williams's manual-task disability as a matter of law. The relevant functional evidence must be assessed under the rule governing the particular activity, rather than excluded because it arises at work or treated as dispositive because it concerns a class of job tasks.

**Precedent treatment:** Sutton and Murphy's working rules remain confined to their actual activity; individual functional proof remains necessary.

### Beneficial mitigation does not eliminate the underlying limitation

**Holding and operative rule:** The manual-task inquiry assesses the individual's underlying substantial limitation without treating beneficial correction or medication as eliminating it. Individual proof remains required, and coverage remains distinct from qualification, accommodation and safety; the claimant need not stop treatment.

**Authority:** All nine Justices directly join this severable application of Sutton and Murphy in the Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** Sutton and Murphy settle the mitigation baseline under the original ADA. Their rule identifies the functional condition assessed; it does not automatically establish its substantiality. The manual-task threshold applies to that legally defined underlying condition. Successful management does not remove protection by itself, while evidence still must establish this individual's substantial limitation. The Court therefore rejects neither the governing mitigation rule nor the independent need to prove disability.

**Precedent treatment:** Sutton and Murphy are applied unchanged; their underlying-impairment rule controls the remand.

## Precedent Treatment

- Sutton v. United Air Lines, Inc., No. 97-1943, and Murphy v. United Parcel Service, Inc., No. 97-1992: the underlying-impairment rule is applied unchanged; beneficial mitigation does not defeat coverage, and the working-specific class inquiry does not define manual tasks.
- Bragdon v. Abbott, 524 U.S. 624: individualized substantiality and the distinction from complete inability remain; its reproduction and direct-threat holdings are intact.
- Albertson's, Inc. v. Kirkingburg, No. 98-591: its distinct safety-qualification rule remains; it supplies no manual-task or mitigation methodology.

## Law After Decision

The original ADA now requires the stated severe restriction in central daily-life manual tasks and permanent or long-term impact, assessed individually under the existing underlying-impairment mitigation rule. Occupational categories alone do not establish disability; workplace and nonwork functional evidence remain relevant. The lower courts apply those settled rules to the reviewed partial judgment, with other claims retaining their existing procedural status.

## Separate Writings

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela concurs in the occupational-shortcut and mitigation propositions and in setting aside the partial judgment, but rejects a categorical severe-and-permanent threshold. He would assess individualized substantial limitation of important manual functioning through breadth, frequency, duration and practical effect. These are relevant considerations, not separate mandatory elements. Important work-related functions cannot be excluded because they occur at work, and retained household abilities do not automatically defeat coverage. Expected duration informs substantiality without making permanence indispensable. Sutton and Murphy preserve the underlying-impairment inquiry; the difference between working and manual tasks requires correction of the appellate shortcut, not a restriction to only the most pervasive daily-life limitations. His proposed standard still requires proof rather than diagnosis alone, preserves distinct qualification and accommodation issues, and would not grant Toyota automatic judgment. That broader standard is noncontrolling.

The limited grant does not reopen an independently unreviewed judgment or forfeited coverage branch. ## Holdings

### Other-agency displacement requires actual exercise over the relevant working conditions

**Holding and operative rule:** Under Occupational Safety and Health Act §4(b)(1), another federal agency displaces OSHA only by actually exercising statutory authority to prescribe or enforce occupational-safety or health standards affecting the relevant working conditions. Pertinent specific regulation or actual comprehensive regulation of the relevant category can suffice without word-for-word duplication; potential authority, unrelated vessel regulation or casualty investigation alone cannot establish whole-vessel or industry-wide displacement.

**Authority:** The unanimous Opinion of the Court; all nine Justices directly join the rule, application and limits.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress made exercise of power, rather than its mere possession, decisive. The Coast Guard had not prescribed pertinent standards or comprehensively regulated the evacuation and emergency-response working conditions OSHA cited on this uninspected inland barge. Its general maritime responsibilities and investigation cannot supply that missing regulatory exercise.

Mallard Bay's coordination concern has force where actual regulatory regimes overlap. It does not justify leaving working conditions outside OSHA solely because another agency could have regulated them. The statute already accommodates genuine comprehensive regulation; the Court need not demand identical wording in the two agencies' rules. Conversely, regulation of unrelated vessel matters does not establish comprehensive occupational-safety control.

The result does not depend on choosing between hazard-based and area-based descriptions of working conditions: neither pertinent nor comprehensive actual regulation is established under either description here. Uninspected status is not itself proof of no Coast Guard regulation, and the holding does not allocate every maritime setting to OSHA. Statutory text resolves the dispute without a new deference holding or an inference of power from the accident's gravity.

**Precedent treatment:** The statutory-boundary principle is consistent with Mead's distinction between possession of authority and legally relevant exercise; Mead supplies no displacement merely from agency expertise.

### Employment on this Louisiana barge is within the Act's workplace coverage

**Holding and operative rule:** Employment aboard this barge within Louisiana is employment in a workplace in a State under §4(a). Anchoring in navigable waters does not remove that workplace from the Act's geographic coverage; the independent other-agency exclusion remains governed by §4(b)(1)'s actual-exercise requirement.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join this independent holding in the Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** The Act's geographic and employment terms reach this Louisiana workplace. No general maritime exception appears in §4(a). Treating the Coast Guard's potential authority as a geographic exclusion would merge two distinct provisions and bypass §4(b)(1)'s condition. The Court therefore rejects the alternative workplace objection as well as the displacement theory. The receiving court must implement those holdings in the actual administrative posture, with the unchallenged citation merits retaining their status.

**Precedent treatment:** The decision construes the two statutory provisions independently; it creates no new maritime exclusivity or deference rule.

## Precedent Treatment

- United States v. Mead Corp.: the distinction between statutory authority and its legally relevant exercise remains; agency expertise alone supplies no displacement. The present holdings rest on §§4(a) and 4(b)(1), not a new rule of interpretive deference.

Work on a barge in Louisiana falls within §4(a), subject independently to any displacement actually established under §4(b)(1). Actual overlap, rather than vessel status alone, governs other settings.

## Holdings

### Part I. The actual punishment choice and complete presentation establish a parole-information violation

**Holding and operative rule:** When the State's evidence or argument materially places future dangerousness in issue and, after the required aggravating finding, the jury can select only death or life without ordinary parole, due process requires an effective opportunity to explain that ineligibility through accurate defense presentation or an adequate instruction. The prospective message of the complete presentation controls despite a character label or the absence of prescribed words; on this record the permitted duration arguments and ordinary-meaning instruction did not convey the legally unavailable parole alternative adequately.

**Authority:** Part I of Justice Souter's opinion, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies six direct votes for the complete rule and its application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the jury must be able to evaluate the State's future-danger case against its actual sentencing alternatives. Simmons supplies the effective opportunity to answer that case, and Shafer establishes that the judge's earlier conditional power to impose a term of years does not create a third option when the jury chooses punishment after finding an aggravator. Gardner protects the opportunity to answer material capital-sentencing information; Skipper protects a response to the State's future-danger premise.

The evidence of a proposed hostage attack and armed escape, considered with the prosecutor's portrayal of an enduring dangerous character, invited a prospective inference. The same material could address past conduct, retribution or prison adaptability. Those uses did not erase its future-danger message. Limiting misconduct evidence to character rather than statutory aggravators likewise did not remove that message from punishment selection. The State need not use a particular phrase or present an expert prediction. This conclusion does not make every brutal crime sufficient, or rest on a rule that escape alone necessarily predicts further violence.

The State separately contends that counsel's descriptions of permanent confinement, the judge's ordinary-meaning charge, the absence of a jury parole inquiry or an instruction excluding parole consideration, and the good-time testimony adequately informed the jury. Taken together, those features did not explain the legal unavailability of ordinary parole. Rhetorical duration and an undefined ordinary meaning did not supply that answer. A jury question or proof of actual confusion is unnecessary. An effective defense explanation can suffice without a judicial instruction or fixed script. The State remains free to present truthful danger within prison or through escape; parole ineligibility does not make that danger impossible. The violation concerns the withheld legal answer, not a prohibition on that evidence.

### Part II. Error effect remains for the state court under Chapman's burden

**Holding and operative rule:** On remand, the State must prove beyond a reasonable doubt on the existing sentencing record that the omission did not contribute to selection of death; the future-danger predicate and informational inadequacy are settled. If the State cannot satisfy that burden, it must provide a new lawful penalty proceeding if it continues to seek death, or obtain a lawful noncapital sentence; the present judgment returns that inquiry without itself vacating the death sentence or ordering an immediate penalty hearing.

**Authority:** Part II of Justice Souter's opinion, joined by Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies five direct votes. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela does not join this part and would require the new penalty proceeding now if death is pursued. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would affirm on their distinct no-violation grounds.

**Controlling explanation:** Chapman supplies the State's burden on direct review. Simmons applies that standard to the particular sentencing presentation rather than classifying denial of parole information as structural error. Shafer preserves the distinction between identifying the violation and determining whether it affected selection of death. The state court rejected this claim without performing the required error-effect inquiry. The Court returns that inquiry under the settled danger and adequacy determinations.

The State's adequate-information contention is a claim of compliance, not a concession about the effect of an established violation. The Court infers neither waiver nor harmlessness from it. Substantial aggravating evidence alone does not answer whether the missing legal explanation contributed to the sentence. The Chief Justice would find the State's burden unsatisfied on this record. The controlling opinion instead requires the state court to assess the existing presentation under Chapman, without treating the violation itself as the answer. Guilt and the independent sentences remain intact because this claim concerns punishment selection.

## Holdings

### I. The bounded assigned-claim mechanism satisfies the Take Care Clause

**Holding and operative rule:** A relator may prosecute the False Claims Act's partially assigned federal pecuniary claim after initial governmental declination without violating the Take Care Clause, where the relator has the Act's case-specific claimant role and the government retains the actual intervention, dismissal, settlement, participation, discovery and alternate-remedy powers with the qualifications set out below. The holding rests on that combined structure, not a general authority to transfer executive functions to private persons, and initial declination does not constitute approval of every later litigating act.

**Authority:** Justice Breyer's opinion, joined throughout by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg, supplies seven direct votes for both constitutional holdings and their limits.

**Statutory conditions incorporated in this holding:**

| Provision | Governmental power and controlling qualification |
|---|---|
| Section 3730(b)(1) | Voluntary dismissal requires written consent and reasons from both the court and the Attorney General. |

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this defined financial assignment and the government's retained protections permit the action. Vermont Agency establishes the relator's assigned financial injury for Article III but expressly leaves Article II unanswered. Early qui tam practice informs the nature of that claimant's interest; neither that practice nor the civil label conclusively resolves executive responsibility.

The dissent correctly identifies a public interest beyond the relator's personal share: litigation may bind federal interests, and the Executive does not select, direct or remove the relator as an ordinary prosecutor. The statute nevertheless gives the government the first opportunity to take over and preserves meaningful later means of protecting the claim, investigation and enforcement interests. The table's conditions are part of those powers. A hearing requirement is not an unrestricted executive veto, and conditioned intervention is not at-will supervision. Those protections must be assessed with the relator's limited proprietary role rather than tested as though Congress had created a general prosecutor.

Morrison supplies a functional inquiry into interference with executive performance; its independent criminal office and supervisory arrangement are not identical to this assigned civil claim or a mandatory checklist for every claimant. The conditions also protect the assignee's existing procedural and financial interests and the court's adjudicative function. ### II. A case-specific relator is not a continuing federal officer

**Holding and operative rule:** A relator enforcing this partially assigned pecuniary claim under the False Claims Act is a private claimant with a defined litigation role, not the occupant of a continuing federal office requiring appointment under the Appointments Clause. The distinction turns on the position and actual functions: it is not established merely by the absence of salary, formal title, appointment or final adjudicative power, and it authorizes no private criminal prosecution, rulemaking or unrestricted sovereign coercive power.

**Authority:** The seven-Justice opinion identified above directly adopts this independent Appointments Clause holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that significant consequences for government interests do not turn every statutory civil claimant into a federal officeholder. Vermont Agency establishes a real financial assignment. The Act permits this claimant to seek a judicial remedy and a contingent personal share; it does not establish an enduring governmental position with continuing jurisdiction and general duties to adjudicate, regulate or prosecute public matters.

Freytag requires attention to significant statutory duties. Landry applies that principle to an actual continuing hearing office and rejects final-decision power as indispensable. Edmond and Hilario examine the combined hierarchy governing actual offices rather than making one factor universally decisive. Those cases do not eliminate the antecedent distinction between an office and a litigant. Morrison also shows why limited duration alone does not disprove officeholding: its independent counsel occupied a governmental office even though its jurisdiction and tenure were limited.

The dissent views litigation of the retained federal claim as governmental prosecution through a role continuing until judgment. That identifies the action's public consequence but does not establish a governmental position independent of the particular assigned claim. The relator's statutory status and party powers persist to enforce that claim, subject to the government controls described above. The office conclusion therefore rests on the entire function and structure, not on relabeling executive authority as private or on denying its importance. The two Article II inquiries are distinct; Article III standing resolves neither by itself.

### III. The rejection of the two constitutional dismissal grounds stands

**Holding and operative rule:** The Fifth Circuit's judgment rejecting the Take Care and Appointments Clause challenges is affirmed, preserving its reversal of dismissal and remand for ordinary adjudication of the false-claims action under the applicable statutory and merits requirements. The government remains a nonintervenor on the merits unless it lawfully exercises its intervention power, and Vermont Agency's sovereign-State defendant limitation remains applicable within its own scope.

**Authority:** The same seven Justices adopt the disposition and its statutory limits.

**Controlling explanation:** Both asserted constitutional grounds for dismissal fail under the assigned-claim structure presented. The allegations of health-care fraud remain allegations for ordinary litigation. The government's appellate defense of the statute does not become merits intervention, and rejection of Article II challenges does not establish statutory coverage of every defendant. Vermont Agency's distinct defendant-coverage rule survives. The appellate remand thus proceeds under the actual allocation of party powers rather than a judicially created governmental takeover.

- **Vermont Agency of Natural Resources v. United States ex rel. ## Law After Decision

The FCA's bounded assigned-claim mechanism may constitutionally continue after initial governmental declination. Its private claimant does not occupy a continuing federal office, and the actual retained government powers, with their distinct statutory conditions, are sufficient for the arrangement presented. These Article II conclusions are separate from assigned-injury standing and statutory defendant coverage. They authorize no general private executive office and do not decide an actual judicial override of a supported government request absent from this record.

## Separate Writings

**Justice Scalia, dissenting, joined by Justice Thomas.** They would reverse on both Article II grounds and bar this nonintervened prosecution while preserving lawful governmental enforcement. A partial financial assignment does not eliminate the sovereign's retained claim, penalties, settlement interests or exposure to a binding judgment. In their view, initiation and prosecution of that public component exercise executive power through a statutory role continuing until the suit ends. The Executive neither selected this prosecutor nor possesses the ordinary chain of direction that execution of the laws requires.

Scalia's Morrison dissent supplies his executive-control premise; Vermont Agency's assignment-based standing opinion expressly reserves this distinct Article II question. His and Thomas's published Laidlaw concern about private public-law enforcement likewise distinguishes an injured claimant from uncontrolled governmental enforcement. Edmond, Hilario and Landry require attention to actual authority, appointment and supervision rather than final judgment power alone. The relator's financial incentive and title do not remove the significant public function. Early qui tam practice and a court-bound assigned claim are substantial contrary considerations, but they do not cure prosecution of the retained public interest without lawful appointment and constitutionally adequate direction.

The statutory safeguards are real but conditional. Later intervention requires good cause, dismissal requires notice and a hearing opportunity, settlement has its separate judicial standard, and discovery protection depends on its stated showing. These measures do not supply the missing selection and direction. The dissent does not demand presidential at-will removal of every inferior officer or decide every private statutory action. It would return for the procedural consequence of disallowing Riley's nonintervened prosecution, including any lawful governmental election, without invalidating section 3730(a), dismissing government claims with prejudice, presuming intervention or appointment, or treating the entire Act as inseverable.

The Fifth Circuit's reversal of dismissal and remand stand. The government's appellate constitutional participation does not alter its nonintervenor merits status.

# EEOC v. Waffle House, Inc. — 2002-01-15

[Authoritative Record](../records/eeoc-v-waffle-house-inc_merits_2002-01-15.md)

## Event

EEOC v. Waffle House, Inc., No. 99-1823; 534 U.S. 279. Merits decision, January 15, 2002; October Term 2001, chunk 2. Argued October 10, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Fourth Circuit. The question is whether Baker's employment arbitration agreement prevents the EEOC from seeking individual relief in its own ADA action. Baker experienced a seizure, was discharged and filed a charge; disability discrimination remains alleged. The Fourth Circuit allowed prospective public relief but barred victim-specific remedies. The agency seeks reversal of that restriction.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

## Public Action

The restriction on victim-specific relief is reversed, and the case is remanded.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Restriction on victim-specific relief reversed and remanded, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Scalia and Thomas, who would vacate the FAA-based restriction and remand for consideration of any properly available independent state-law contract ground.

All nine reject FAA coverage and agree that actual recovery remains subject to statutory predicates and prevention of duplicate satisfaction. The Commission's public enforcement action continues; the majority permits pursuit of individual remedies without the dissenters' preliminary contract-enforceability inquiry.

## Holdings

### Part I. The FAA supplies no enforcement ground for this employment agreement

**Holding and operative rule:** Circuit City's section 1 employment-contract exclusion covers Baker's employment arbitration agreement, so the FAA supplies no enforcement command or federal arbitration-policy ground for restricting this EEOC action or its remedies. The exclusion does not itself determine an independent state-law basis for enforcement between Baker and Waffle House.

**Authority:** Part I of Justice Stevens's opinion is joined by all eight other Justices; nine direct joins control this ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the governing employment exclusion resolves the antecedent FAA question. Circuit City excludes employment contracts within the commerce reach asserted for the Act, including nontransportation employment. Baker's agreement belongs to that category. A general federal preference for arbitration cannot replace statutory coverage. The Fourth Circuit's FAA-based restriction consequently cannot stand. Gilmer concerns a securities-registration agreement and does not supply a contrary answer about this employment contract. Whether another source binds the employee remains distinct from the FAA holding.

### Part II. The Commission's independent action retains its individual-remedy authority

**Holding and operative rule:** An employee's private agreement selecting arbitration does not bind the nonparty EEOC or categorically eliminate victim-specific remedies Congress authorizes in the Commission's own ADA enforcement action. Even if an independent state-law ground makes the promise enforceable against the employee, that fact alone neither makes the Commission a contracting party nor removes those federal remedy categories; this is an independently sufficient ground for rejecting the relief bar.

**Authority:** Part II of Justice Stevens's opinion is joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, seven direct votes. Scalia and Thomas withhold assent to this ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the agency enforces its own statutory cause, rather than an assignment of Baker's claim. Section 12117(a) gives the Commission the enumerated Title VII enforcement powers, procedures and remedies. Section 706(f)(1) authorizes its action after the statutory process; section 706(g) authorizes appropriate individual equitable relief; and section 1981a expressly includes the Commission among plaintiffs who may seek the specified damages. Compensation and reinstatement can advance that public enforcement function.

First Options supplies the actual-assent principle, but the statutory enforcement structure completes the answer. General Telephone recognizes the independent public action including individual relief, and Gilmer preserves an agency enforcement role alongside an individual's arbitration obligation. Baker could commit himself to a forum; he did not undertake a promise for the Commission, which never agreed to arbitrate.

The dissent's strongest objection concerns the beneficiary's valid commitments, not direct compulsion of the agency. It would condition individual recovery on a state-law-contract determination. But the statutory permission for appropriate relief does not create a categorical exception whenever an employee chose an arbitral forum. Enforceability against Baker alone does not diminish the agency's enacted remedies. This conclusion decides the live private-contract argument without resolving the contract's state-law enforceability or a hypothetical FAA-covered agreement. An actual adjudication, settlement, release or satisfaction differs from the forum promise presented here and requires its own record.

### Part III. Statutory conditions govern any actual award

**Holding and operative rule:** The EEOC's authority to seek relief does not establish liability or an entitlement to an award: the applicable disability, qualification, unlawful-discrimination, injury, causation and remedy predicates must be proved, and the court must apply each remedy's conditions below. The court must prevent duplicate satisfaction of the same injury when actual overlapping recovery is established; no completed arbitration, settlement, release or prior satisfaction is established here.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join Part III's ordinary statutory and nonduplication requirements. Scalia and Thomas separately advocate the additional contractual limitation rejected by Part II; their Part III join does not adopt Part II.

**Statutory conditions incorporated in this holding:**

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the enacted remedial menu remains conditional. Toyota supplies the operative disability rules; a seizure by itself establishes none of their required findings. Section 706(g)'s discretion concerns justified relief on proved facts, and section 1981a separately supplies damages on its own terms. Great-West interprets a different ERISA provision and does not delete this express damages authorization. The distinct limitations in section 706(g)(2)(A), section 706(g)(2)(B) and section 1981a must remain distinct rather than being combined into a new general exception. The employee's conduct can matter to mitigation, actual satisfaction or a properly established judgment or settlement. Without such a record, possible future duplication cannot justify removing every victim-specific remedy at the outset. Employer size and the other damages predicates remain matters for ordinary adjudication.

### Part IV. The categorical restriction is reversed without a contract-enforceability prerequisite

**Holding and operative rule:** The Fourth Circuit's categorical prohibition on victim-specific relief is reversed, and the action is remanded for adjudication of liability and appropriate statutory relief. The Commission may pursue that relief without first establishing that Baker's private forum promise is unenforceable under state law.

**Authority:** The same seven Justices who join Part II join Part IV; Scalia and Thomas instead support vacatur and a bounded inquiry into any properly available independent contract ground.

**Controlling explanation:** Both the absence of FAA coverage and the Commission's independent statutory authority defeat the restriction as entered. Because the second ground resolves the private forum agreement's asserted effect on the Commission's remedy categories, an antecedent state-law enforcement proceeding is unnecessary. The remand returns the ordinary statutory merits and remedy work under Parts II and III.

The FAA supplies no bar for this excluded employment agreement. Independently, an employee's private forum promise does not remove Congress's individual-remedy categories from the EEOC's own enforcement action, even if the promise binds the employee under an independent source. **Justice Thomas, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by Justice Scalia.** They join Part I because Circuit City's governing employment exclusion removes the FAA as a source of enforcement, despite their continuing disagreement with that precedent. # In re Cybernetic Services, Inc. — 2002-01-15

[Authoritative Record](../records/in-re-cybernetic-services-inc_merits_2002-01-15.md)

## Event

In re Cybernetic Services, Inc., 252 F.3d 1039 (9th Cir. 2001). Merits decision, January 15, 2002; October Term 2001, chunk 2. On merits review of the Ninth Circuit's affirmance of the bankruptcy priority and stay-relief rulings. The question is whether federal patent recording or the governing California withdrawal provisions defeat the creditors' earlier state perfection against the trustee's hypothetical judicial-lien claim. Nonownership transfer and proper, timely California filing are stipulated. The trustee seeks avoidance or subordination; the creditors seek preservation of priority and stay relief.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |

| Opinion of the Court | Stone-Zsela | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Affirms the priority and stay-relief judgment | All five holdings, including both independent federal grounds and both separate state-law questions |

## Holdings

### I. The nonownership collateral interest falls outside section 261's recording protection

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 261's recording rule governs transfers of patent ownership rights, including a title-transferring mortgage, but does not require federal recording to perfect this stipulated nonownership collateral interest against the trustee's judicial-lien claim. A covered assignment, grant or conveyance is ineffective against a subsequent purchaser or mortgagee for value without notice unless recorded within three months of its date or before the subsequent purchase or mortgage; those conditions do not establish a universal federal perfection system for all patent encumbrances.

**Authority:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's opinion is joined throughout by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine directly adopt this and each following holding, including the independent grounds and limits.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the rights transferred determine section 261's operation. Waterman distinguishes transfers of title from lesser interests; its mortgage actually transferred title and therefore does not establish federal recording for this different stipulated transaction. Littlefield likewise distinguishes ownership conveyance from retained lesser rights. Neither the modern label security interest nor an old mortgage label resolves the inquiry by itself.

The trustee's national-notice argument has practical force. But Congress's ownership-transfer rule does not enact a comprehensive substitute for ordinary credit-priority law. State perfection of this interest changes neither patent validity, duration nor the scope of the exclusionary right. O'Melveny requires an identified legal conflict, not federal displacement based on administrative convenience or uniformity alone. No such conflict is established. The Court does not classify every patent financing instrument as a license or hold that mortgages never come within section 261. Actual ownership transfers and their competing transferee rights remain governed by the statute at its full scope.

### II. The trustee's judicial-lien status independently supplies no section 261 protection

**Holding and operative rule:** A trustee invoking section 544(a)(1) has the position of a hypothetical judicial lien creditor at commencement, without regard to knowledge and whether or not such a creditor actually exists; that status is not a subsequent ownership-taking purchaser or title mortgagee protected by section 261. Independently of transaction coverage, the trustee's preserved status therefore supplies no federal recording priority over this earlier perfected interest.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join this independently sufficient federal ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the competing claimant's legal status matters separately from the instrument's coverage. Even assuming a covered transaction, section 261 protects the stated purchasers and mortgagees, not every creditor. Section 544(a)(1)'s disregard of knowledge does not transform a judicial lien into ownership bought for consideration. The trustee's contrary approach would replace the actual strong-arm status with a different statutory class.

The section 544(a)(2) unsatisfied-execution theory was not raised below and no exceptional circumstance was identified; purchaser status likewise was not preserved. Those distinct theories are not adjudicated. Section 544(a)(3) addresses real property and supplies no automatic patent-purchaser status. The Court resolves the federal recording contention under the status actually presented.

### III. Former section 9104(a) does not withdraw California's perfection rules here

**Holding and operative rule:** Former California Commercial Code section 9104(a) withdraws Article 9 only to the extent a federal statute governs the rights of the parties and affected third parties in the relevant transaction. Because section 261 does not govern this nonownership collateral interest against this judicial lien creditor, section 9104(a) does not displace the state perfection rules in this case.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join this separate state-law holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the state provision must receive its own operation. Absence of federal preemption does not by itself answer every state-law direction to step back. But section 9104(a)'s trigger requires federal governance of the relevant rights. The ownership-recording protection at issue does not supply that governance for this lien and adversary. The State's qualified withdrawal therefore does not make the otherwise compliant California filing ineffective. This conclusion does not resolve the provision's outer reach in transactions subject to an actual federal security-interest system.

### IV. Former sections 9302(3)(a) and 9302(4) require an applicable outside perfection system

**Holding and operative rule:** Former California Commercial Code sections 9302(3)(a) and 9302(4) replace Article 9 filing only with an adequate national-registration or other outside filing system applicable to perfection of the relevant security interest. A register for patents and ownership transfers, even when collateral documents may be accepted voluntarily, does not provide that system for this nonownership lien and does not require an additional PTO filing here.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join this independent state-law issue resolution.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the national-registration words must be read with the filing-substitution function and subsection (4)'s perfection consequence. The trustee's broader reading would make a register's mere existence enough even though it supplies no governing method to perfect this interest. The Article 9 comments identify an adequate outside system for the relevant security interest. Section 261's ownership regime is not that system here.

Discretionary PTO acceptance establishes administrative availability, not a statutory filing duty. No challenge to the recording regulation's validity is presented, and the Court decides none. The copyright comparison involves materially different statutory language and supplies no decision about copyright perfection. The governing former California provisions, rather than revised Article 9, control this transaction.

### V. The stipulated perfection preserves priority and relief from the stay

**Holding and operative rule:** Because the creditors' nonownership security interest was properly prepared, executed and timely filed under California law before commencement, and neither federal recording nor either state withdrawal rule defeats that perfection, their interest has priority over the trustee's preserved section 544(a)(1) claim. The Ninth Circuit's judgment sustaining the BAP and bankruptcy court's priority determination and relief from the automatic stay is affirmed.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join the complete disposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the legal objections fail on the stipulated transaction and filing facts. There is no unresolved characterization or state-filing defect requiring a remand. The bankruptcy court's permission to pursue foreclosure remains effective; that permission is distinct from a completed foreclosure or transfer of ownership. The judgment establishes priority in this actual lien-creditor contest, not priority over an unpresented actual purchaser.

# Thomas v. Chicago Park District — 2002-01-15

[Authoritative Record](../records/thomas-v-chicago-park-district_merits_2002-01-15.md)

## Event

Thomas v. Chicago Park District, No. 00-1249; 534 U.S. 316. Merits decision, January 15, 2002; October Term 2001, chunk 2. Argued December 3, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Seventh Circuit. The ordinance requires permits for events involving more than fifty people and activities including amplified sound. Applicants seeking expressive events, including marijuana-policy advocacy, challenge its facial standards and procedures. The Seventh Circuit sustained the system. The questions concern the full censorship safeguards, constrained discretion, and timely action and review.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

## Public Action

The Seventh Circuit judgment rejecting the facial challenge is affirmed.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The Seventh Circuit's rejection of the facial challenge stands, and the permit ordinance remains operative. Properly presented challenges to discriminatory, delayed or content-based administration retain their ordinary availability; no particular permit denial is adjudicated here.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Scalia | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Affirms the facial judgment | All three holdings, thirteen qualified denial grounds, actual deadlines/review and limits on administration |

## Holdings

### I. Neutral park coordination does not require the entire censorship procedure

**Holding and operative rule:** A genuinely content-neutral permit system for allocating shared park space and coordinating activities need not adopt Freedman's entire procedure for censorship of expressive content, including a requirement that the government initiate judicial proceedings and bear the censorship burden after each denial. Such a system still requires real constraints on discretion, bounded administrative action and meaningful access to review; a logistical label alone does not establish validity.

**Authority:** Justice Scalia's opinion is joined throughout by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine directly adopt this and the following two holdings with their qualifications.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the safeguards must address the function officials may exercise. Freedman concerns official censorship of acceptable expressive content. Allocating park uses under definite scheduling, capacity and safety rules presents a different recurring risk. Cox recognizes neutral coordination of shared public space; Ward requires actual content independence rather than a benign label. The need to obtain a permit does not itself erase that distinction.

The challengers correctly stress that delay and favoritism can suppress speech even without an express message category. The answer is not to presume good administration, but to examine the authorized criteria and the concrete timing and review protections. Here those protections constrain park management rather than authorize approval of ideas. Requiring the District to commence a judicial case after every scheduling refusal would import a censorship procedure without its predicate. Freedman and FW/PBS remain in force in their own settings. Because the full censorship procedure does not apply, the Court need not decide whether Freedman's expeditious-review requirement there demands prompt commencement of proceedings or a prompt final judicial determination.

### II. The ordinance confines denial to objective management grounds

**Holding and operative rule:** The ordinance's two qualified prior-conduct predicates and eleven further grounds, set out below, sufficiently confine denial to neutral park-management subjects for this facial challenge. It permits denial on a listed ground but does not require denial whenever a ground occurs; permission to excuse a disqualifying circumstance authorizes neither an invented ground nor selective treatment by viewpoint.

**Authority:** The unanimous Court opinion adopts this standards determination and its limits.

**Denial grounds governed by this holding:**

The strongest objection concerns the judgment involved in assessing danger, applying cross-references or forgiving defects. But permission to decline denial does not confer permission to invent a disqualifying reason. Nor must every technical defect compel refusal to avoid facial invalidity. The criteria remain neutral management limits; they do not authorize message approval, viewpoint favoritism or an audience-hostility burden. Forsyth's separate rule against hostility-based fees continues to govern any covered fee. This case does not adjudicate an unpresented fee amount or validate every financial condition in every application. A materially different or discriminatory administration remains subject to challenge on its own facts, without a new numerical-pattern prerequisite to proving viewpoint discrimination.

### III. The actual deadlines, reasons and review support the facial judgment

**Holding and operative rule:** The District must decide within fourteen days unless written notice extends the period for fourteen additional days; a denial must state its grounds in writing, propose feasible cures, and suggest alternative times or places when based on an earlier competing application. An applicant has seven days to appeal in writing to the General Superintendent, who must act within seven days, followed by available state common-law certiorari review; together with the objective grounds, these protections sustain the ordinance against the facial challenge.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join this procedural-sufficiency holding and affirmance.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that these actual protections answer the presented structural objection without a universal rule for every neutral permit system. Receipt order governs processing; the bounded decision period prevents open-ended administrative withholding; written reasons identify the decision for challenge; feasible correction and alternative scheduling address curable defects; and administrative and judicial review provide avenues to contest an improper refusal. Content neutrality alone would not cure unrestricted discretion or ineffective review.

No automatic-issuance rule is established on this record, and no fixed court-adjudication deadline is supplied. The Court adds neither. It also does not resolve an actual late application or discriminatory denial absent a proper claim and record. Those are applications of continuing First Amendment constraints, not a license for indefinite delay. The Seventh Circuit's facial judgment is affirmed, leaving the ordinance operative while preserving properly presented claims about actual administration.

# United States v. Arvizu — 2002-01-15

[Authoritative Record](../records/united-states-v-arvizu_merits_2002-01-15.md)

## Event

United States v. Arvizu, No. 00-1519; 534 U.S. 266. Merits decision, January 15, 2002; October Term 2001, chunk 2. Argued November 27, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 232 F.3d 1241, as amended. The district court rejected the stop and voluntary-consent challenges; Arvizu's conditional plea preserved suppression review. The Ninth Circuit held the stop unlawful and treated the ensuing search as tainted. The questions are the proper totality method and whether the supported pre-stop combination establishes reasonable suspicion. The United States seeks restoration of suppression denial; Arvizu seeks exclusion.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

## Public Action

The Ninth Circuit judgment resting on an unlawful stop is reversed, and the case is remanded for the preserved independent consent issues.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The stop was supported by reasonable suspicion. The Ninth Circuit must address the remaining preserved consent-voluntariness and scope issues under their applicable review. The unlawful-stop taint ground falls, but the conviction and complete suppression denial are not finally restored without that review.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | O'Connor | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Reverses unlawful-stop ruling and returns preserved independent consent issues | All four holdings; Scalia joins the governing Ornelas application while separately retaining his review objection |
| Concurrence | Scalia | No other Justice | Joins opinion and judgment in full | Criticism of inferential deference within independent review; same result under traditional independent review |

## Holdings

### I. Supported observations must be considered in their actual relationship

**Holding and operative rule:** Reasonable suspicion for an investigative stop depends on an objective, particularized inference from the total circumstances known before the seizure; an observation cannot be excluded merely because it has an innocent explanation or would be insufficient alone. Courts must still test each proposed inference's factual basis and actual weight, so neither the number of observations nor an officer's unexplained intuition supplies the required justification.

**Authority:** Justice O'Connor's opinion is joined throughout by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine directly adopt all four holdings and their limits; Scalia separately qualifies the review discussion without withholding his application join.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that context may make several lawful observations informative together. Terry requires articulable suspicion of wrongdoing; Cortez requires the whole picture and an inference particular to the person or vehicle stopped. Wardlow likewise permits contextual consideration without making location or ambiguous behavior automatically sufficient. Innocence of each isolated act does not compel a conclusion about the connected pattern.

The amended Ninth Circuit opinion acknowledged totality and softened some earlier formulations. It nevertheless categorically discounted the children's posture and waving and separated observations before assessing their relationship. That method removed potentially probative context too early. Arvizu's profiling objection remains substantial: every response cannot become suspicious merely because an officer says experience makes it so. The court must identify supported connections and may assign little or no weight to an asserted factor. The totality rule is not a command that every fact count positively, and the later discovery of marijuana cannot justify the earlier seizure.

### II. Ultimate sufficiency receives independent appellate judgment

**Holding and operative rule:** Under Ornelas, appellate courts independently determine ultimate reasonable suspicion while reviewing historical facts, credibility and genuinely factual subsidiary inferences for clear error, and giving genuine due weight to grounded local and experiential inferences. That contextual weight does not create deference or a presumption of correctness for the officer's ultimate constitutional conclusion.

**Authority:** All nine join this application of the governing Ornelas allocation. Scalia's concurrence preserves his objection to that allocation's inferential formulation without withholding a present join.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that review must preserve both the trial court's factual role and the appellate court's responsibility for constitutional sufficiency. Ornelas supplies that division. Local knowledge and experience can explain the significance of an observed route or pattern; they cannot manufacture a fact or insulate the conclusion that the seizure was lawful. Describing what the children did and deciding what constitutional significance their conduct has are not the same task.

Scalia objects that deference to an inferential conclusion can compromise genuinely independent review. The controlling formulation protects genuinely factual subsidiary judgments and gives contextual understanding actual weight while reserving the ultimate sufficiency decision to the reviewing court. It adopts no intermediate standard or numerical formula. The supported pre-stop findings here permit that legal application now; another remand simply to redo the stop comparison is unnecessary.

### III. This connected pre-stop pattern supplied reasonable suspicion

**Holding and operative rule:** The sensor-linked checkpoint-bypass route, final bypass turn, timing context and particular occupant and apparent-cargo observations, assessed together with their ordinary explanations and the agent's actual experience, supplied a particularized basis for this brief investigative vehicle stop. The holding does not require a traffic offense, direct contraband observation or proof that criminal activity was more likely than an innocent explanation, and does not treat any one greeting, vehicle type or neighborhood as independently sufficient.

**Authority:** All nine join this record-specific sufficiency holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the connection among the observations distinguishes this case from a general profile. Sensors tracked travel along a route capable of avoiding an operating checkpoint, and the final turn took the last bypass road. The approaching staffing transition supplied relevant local context. The driver's slowing and stiffness, combined with the children's persistent forward-facing waving and raised knees, supported a limited inference of concealed cargo and an attempted appearance of normality. Neither a greeting nor an acknowledgment of an officer is legally required; the behavior matters only within that route-and-load pattern.

The opposing account has force. The road serves homes, recreation and tourists; a minivan is ordinary family transport; children may wave or rest their feet on luggage; and the shift change was still about forty-five minutes away. The agent had local patrol and alien-stop experience, not a personal history of drug stops. These qualifications reduce the weight but do not eliminate the supported relationship. A similar minivan's drug stop one month earlier does not taint all minivans. Registration to a relative in a broadly described smuggling neighborhood contributes little without a particular connection and is unnecessary to the sufficient combination. The Court presumes neither knowledge of the staffing schedule nor erratic or unlawful driving.

Wardlow's actual insufficiency holding involved a materially thinner departure-and-location record. Here the sensor-tracked bypass and passenger/cargo observations provide the additional individualized connection. The conclusion authorizes the brief stop, not a search of the vehicle or its bags, and rests exclusively on information preceding the seizure.

### IV. The lawful stop removes taint but does not resolve independent consent issues

**Holding and operative rule:** The Ninth Circuit's judgment resting on the stop's illegality is reversed, and the case is remanded for proper appellate treatment of the preserved independent consent-voluntariness and scope issues. A lawful stop removes that taint ground but neither establishes voluntary consent nor determines its extent; the claimed exterior-only permission and disputed gun-hand interaction remain subject to their applicable review and preservation limits.

**Authority:** All nine adopt this complete disposition and bounded remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that stop justification and search authorization remain distinct. The district court rejected both the stop and voluntary-consent challenges, and the conditional plea preserved suppression review. The Ninth Circuit's unlawful-stop analysis treated the ensuing search as tainted without deciding the independent consent grounds. Reversing the stop ruling therefore does not finally restore the conviction or conclusively sustain the whole suppression denial. The receiving court must give the trial court's findings their applicable review and address the consent questions actually preserved.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1:** Applied for the objective, particularized reasonable-suspicion threshold and limited investigative-stop authority.
- **United States v. Cortez, 449 U.S. 411:** Applied for assessment of the whole picture and rational, individualized connections among observations.
- **Ornelas v. United States, 517 U.S. 690:** Applied for independent ultimate review, clear-error protection of facts and genuine contextual weight without a police-correctness presumption; no new review standard is adopted.
- **Illinois v. Wardlow, January 12, 2000:** Its contextual method is applied; its actual insufficiency holding is distinguished by the additional sensor-linked bypass and vehicle-specific observations here. Its rejection of categorical location or flight permission remains intact.
- **Florida v. J. L., March 28, 2000:** Its requirement of sufficient grounds before intrusion and rejection of retrospective contraband justification remain effective.
- **United States v. Knights, December 10, 2001:** Distinguished because the probation status and search condition there are absent; its confined holding supplies no ordinary-driver exception.

## Law After Decision

Supported observations capable of innocent explanations retain their actual contextual force but gain no automatic positive weight by accumulation. Independent judicial review determines whether their relationships supply individualized suspicion. This pre-stop combination meets that standard without neighborhood stigma, proof of an observed offense or post-stop contraband. The lawful-stop conclusion leaves the independent authority for the ensuing search to the preserved consent questions.

## Separate Writings

**Justice Scalia, concurring, joined by no other Justice.** He joins the Court's full application of governing law and its reversal and bounded remand. He retains his Ornelas dissent's preference for deferential review and his objection that giving weight to an inferential conclusion can be conceptually inconsistent with independently deciding the ultimate issue. A factual description of the children's waving can receive factual protection; whether it is suspicious cannot be sheltered from constitutional judgment simply by calling it an officer inference. Reversal follows here even under traditional independent review. His join applies Ornelas as governing law without endorsing its original choice of standard, dispensing with innocent explanations or authorizing the subsequent search.

## Procedure After Action

The Ninth Circuit must give the trial court's findings their applicable review and resolve the preserved consent-voluntariness and scope questions. The reasonable-suspicion issue is settled in favor of the stop. The lawful-stop conclusion does not itself finally restore the conviction or resolve the remaining request for suppression.

United States v. Arvizu, 232 F.3d 1241, amended from 217 F.3d 1224; Terry, 392 U.S. 1; Cortez, 449 U.S. 411; Ornelas, 517 U.S. 690. The record distinguishes pre-stop observations from the later search and contraband discovery, and preserves disputed consent voluntariness and scope. Local patrol and alien-stop experience do not establish prior personal drug-stop experience. Registration neighborhood alone supplies no finding that the relative or address was tied to crime.

# National Cable & Telecommunications Assn., Inc. v. Gulf Power Co. — 2002-01-16

[Authoritative Record](../records/national-cable-telecommunications-assn-inc-v-gulf-power-co_merits_2002-01-16.md)

## Event

National Cable & Telecommunications Assn., Inc. v. Gulf Power Co.; Nos. 00-832 and 00-843. Merits decision, January 16, 2002; argued October 2, 2001. Review of the Eleventh Circuit's judgment setting aside the FCC's authority over mixed cable-Internet and wireless telecommunications pole attachments. The questions are whether section 224's category-specific rate formulas restrict its general coverage, and whether qualifying wireless equipment falls outside the statute.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the challenged categorical exclusions from the FCC's pole-attachment authority and remands.

## Judgment & Remedy

| Component | Disposition | Supporting | Opposing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mixed cable-Internet attachments | Reverse the categorical jurisdictional exclusion and remand, 7-2 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Ginsburg, Breyer | Souter, Thomas; they would vacate for agency classification and explanation |
| Wireless telecommunications attachments | Reverse the categorical exclusion and remand, 9-0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | No opposing Justice |

Further proceedings must recognize coverage while preserving every applicable statutory formula and any properly remaining classification or particular-rate objection. The judgment does not approve a particular attachment charge.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Kennedy | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Ginsburg, Breyer throughout; Souter and Thomas as to Part II only | Supports both reversals | Seven join Part I, mixed-service coverage; all nine join Part II, wireless coverage |
| Concurrence in part and dissent in part | Thomas | Souter | Concurs in wireless reversal; would vacate the mixed-service component for agency explanation | Mandatory formula classification and reasoned statutory basis |

## Holdings

### I. Adding Internet service does not remove a qualifying cable-system attachment from the statute

**Holding and operative rule.** An attachment by a cable television system that satisfies 47 U.S.C. §224(a)(4) remains within the FCC's authority under §224(b) when it carries Internet service together with cable television. Sections 224(d) and (e) prescribe mandatory rates within their respective terms; they do not exhaust the broader attachment definition or permit the agency to evade an applicable formula.

**Authority.** Part I of Kennedy's opinion has the full support of Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplying seven votes.

**Controlling explanation.** Congress defined the regulated attachment before prescribing methods for setting its rate. An attachment by a cable system does not cease to meet that definition when the system adds a service. The limitation to attachments used solely for cable service controls the cable formula's application; it does not rewrite the general definition. Section 224(b)'s direction to ensure just and reasonable rates, terms and conditions and its rulemaking grant therefore continue to govern covered attachments. Iowa Utilities Board supplies the distinction between an actual congressional assignment and the limits governing its exercise. The utilities' method objection deserves examination when a particular rate method is challenged, but does not establish a categorical absence of authority. Nor must the Commission resolve Internet classification to answer this coverage question: either possible classification leaves an attachment by a cable system within the general definition. Mandatory formulas remain mandatory when their conditions are met. Mead requires an actual delegation before an agency interpretation receives controlling force; the enacted text itself resolves this question, so no new deference rule is needed. The Eleventh Circuit's categorical exclusion cannot stand. Classification of Internet service and validity of a particular rate are outside the questions decided.

### II. Qualifying wireless equipment is a covered attachment

**Holding and operative rule.** Section 224 covers an attachment by a provider offering telecommunications to the public for a fee, without excluding the provider's equipment because it serves wireless transmission. Both the attachment and its owner must satisfy the statutory definitions; the utility's wire-use condition does not impose a separate wire-only restriction on the attached provider's service.

**Authority.** All nine Justices join Part II of Kennedy's opinion.

**Controlling explanation.** The telecommunications-service definition addresses service offered for a fee regardless of the facilities used. The reference to wire communications in the utility definition identifies the covered owner and structure; it does not alter the separately defined service or exclude physical wireless equipment attached to that structure. Reading the definitions together covers both wireline attachments used by wireless providers and qualifying wireless equipment. The contrary construction relocates an owner-related condition into a service limitation Congress did not enact. Iowa Utilities Board's distinction between statutory eligibility and the duties governing eligible facilities supports preserving those separate functions. The statute's exclusions for railroads, cooperatively organized persons and federal or state ownership remain, as do the incumbent-local-exchange-carrier limitation in §224(a)(5) and §224(c)'s allocation to qualifying state regulation. The holding reaches qualifying poles, ducts, conduits and rights-of-way; it does not extend authority to every independent wireless tower or property outside those definitions. The wireless categorical exclusion is reversed, with lawful rates, terms and conditions governed by the applicable statutory provisions.

## Precedent Treatment

- AT&T Corp. v. Iowa Utilities Board (January 25, 1999): applies its separation of congressionally conferred FCC authority from the independent limits on particular implementing rules; its local-competition holdings remain intact.
- United States v. Mead Corp. (June 18, 2001): preserves its actual-delegation requirement; the Court resolves section 224's coverage from its text without adding a deference holding.
- Permian Basin Area Rate Cases, 390 U.S. 747: the separate writing invokes reasoned ratemaking to require an agency explanation; that objection does not become a controlling ground for additional remand.

## Law After Decision

Section 224's general coverage encompasses qualifying mixed cable-Internet and wireless telecommunications attachments. A gap or dispute about an applicable rate formula does not itself create a jurisdictional exclusion. The statutory formulas, ownership and provider limits, and qualifying state-regulation allocation retain their force. These are new coverage holdings; no Internet-service classification or particular rate is approved.

## Separate Writings

Thomas, joined by Souter, concurs in the wireless holding but would vacate the mixed-service portion for a reasoned agency determination. If cable-modem access is cable service, subsection (d)'s formula controls; if it is telecommunications service, subsection (e)'s formula controls. Only after resolving those possibilities can residual authority under subsection (b) support the particular regulatory choice. The service supplied by an Internet service provider and the transmission carrying customers to that provider are distinct classification questions. The Commission's treatment of one does not necessarily resolve the other. Permian Basin's demand for a reasoned rate method supports returning that choice to the agency. Probable ultimate jurisdiction and policy favoring broadband deployment cannot supply the missing statutory explanation. This position rejects categorical noncoverage but withholds approval of the existing unexplained agency determination.

The case returns through the Eleventh Circuit for proceedings consistent with both coverage holdings, preserving properly remaining classification and rate-method objections under the applicable statutory commands.

# Kansas v. Crane — 2002-01-22

[Authoritative Record](../records/kansas-v-crane_merits_2002-01-22.md)

## Event

Kansas v. Crane; No. 00-957. Merits decision, January 22, 2002; argued October 30, 2001. Review of the Kansas Supreme Court's judgment setting aside a sexually violent predator commitment under a complete-inability-to-control construction. The question is what impairment of control due process requires for this form of civil commitment.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The Court vacates the judgment and remands for application of the serious-difficulty standard under Kansas's prescribed proof burden.

## Judgment & Remedy

Vacated and remanded, 7-2. **Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. **Opposing:** Scalia and Thomas, who would reverse the complete-incapacity construction without requiring an additional independent control finding. All nine reject a complete-incapacity requirement.

The Kansas courts must decide whether the verdict and evidence lawfully establish serious difficulty controlling the dangerous conduct, substantially connected to the qualifying present condition, beyond a reasonable doubt. The trial commitment is not automatically restored. If that requirement is not lawfully established, the existing order cannot stand; any new commitment proceeding requires independent lawful authority. A properly preserved punitive-operation claim remains subject to its distinct predicates under Hendricks and Seling.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Breyer | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg | Vacates and remands | Entire functional-control rule, statutory burden and application remand |
| Dissent | Scalia | Thomas | Would reverse and remand under the statute's genuine condition-and-danger predicates | Rejects complete incapacity and an additional independent control finding |

## Holdings

### Serious functional difficulty, rather than total incapacity, limits this civil commitment

**Holding and operative rule.** Due process permits this form of civil commitment only upon proof of a serious present functional impairment producing serious difficulty controlling the dangerous conduct and substantially connected to the qualifying mental condition and demonstrated danger. Complete inability to control every act is unnecessary, but diagnosis and predicted recidivism alone do not distinguish an eligible person from a dangerous ordinary recidivist; Kansas must establish the required functional connection under its beyond-a-reasonable-doubt burden.

**Authority.** Breyer's opinion speaks for Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplying seven votes for the full rule and its application remand.

**Controlling explanation.** Civil confinement needs a substantive basis different from a prediction that a person will commit another crime. Foucha supplies that requirement of lawful present predicates rather than danger alone. A genuine disorder must produce sufficiently serious difficulty controlling the conduct that makes this custody permissible. The dissent's disorder-and-danger formulation does not adequately express that functional boundary when diagnosis and prediction could include ordinary repeated crime. At the other extreme, complete incapacity would exclude serious impairment merely because some capacity to choose remains. Neither liberty's protection nor psychiatric uncertainty requires that unrealistic line. The condition's nature and severity and evidence of actual impairment must be assessed together; clinical categories inform the legal finding without deciding it. Addington recognizes uncertainty in psychiatric judgments but supplies no reduction in Kansas's more protective burden. No mathematical measure, compulsory clinical label, involuntariness of every act or universal jury-instruction formula is required. Personality disorder is neither automatically sufficient nor automatically disqualifying. Hendricks's controlling punishment judgment concerned its own application; its non-dispositive due-process discussion did not settle today's rule. Seling likewise keeps actual punitive operation distinct from substantive civil eligibility. On remand the Kansas courts must evaluate the verdict and evidence under the stated rule. A verdict framed only in statutory diagnosis and likely repetition does not necessarily establish the necessary impairment.

**Limits and questions not reached.** The functional showing remains required for this form of commitment regardless of whether the condition is described as emotional, cognitive or volitional. The Court classifies no particular emotional diagnosis as sufficient or insufficient; clinical terminology does not replace the required functional assessment. A new punishment adjudication, facial redesign of commitment, periodic-review procedures and treatment or alternative-placement requirements lie outside the grant. A preserved operational punishment objection requires Crane-specific facts and the independent Hendricks-Seling inquiry.

## Precedent Treatment

- Foucha v. Louisiana (May 18, 1992): applies its requirement of lawful present substantive custody predicates and its rejection of dangerousness alone after the psychiatric predicate has ended; narrower genuinely justified civil regimes remain possible.
- Addington v. Texas, 441 U.S. 418: preserves its ordinary civil-commitment proof principles and recognition of clinical uncertainty; it does not lower Kansas's reasonable-doubt burden.
- Kansas v. Hendricks / Hendricks v. Kansas (June 23, 1997): retains its as-applied retrospective-punishment holding. Its noncontrolling diagnostic discussion neither establishes today's control rule nor transfers findings from Hendricks to Crane.
- Seling v. Young (January 17, 2001): preserves the cumulative civil-intent and punitive-operation inquiry and its distinct remedial predicates; it supplies no categorical invalidation or approval of Crane's commitment.

## Law After Decision

The serious-difficulty requirement is a new controlling due-process rule for the civil-commitment question presented. Courts must examine actual functional impairment connected to the qualifying condition and dangerous conduct, while retaining the applicable statutory burden. The functional rule governs application on remand; whether the existing verdict and evidence satisfy it is factual and remedial work, not unresolved law. Separate retrospective-punishment claims retain their own demanding proof and temporal requirements.

## Separate Writings

Scalia, joined by Thomas, agrees that due process does not require complete inability to control conduct. He would hold that a genuine present mental abnormality or disorder causally making repeated sexual violence likely supplies the substantive limitation, when proved under Kansas's burden. An additional independent control finding is not constitutionally required. The new serious-difficulty formulation, in his view, gives uncertain guidance and turns descriptions of an individual's condition into a universal extra element. This position neither treats a label or danger alone as sufficient nor allows a high burden to create a missing substantive predicate. It preserves Foucha's ended-predicate rule and Hendricks and Seling's separate review of actual punitive operation. He would reverse the complete-incapacity rule and remand under the statute's genuine condition-and-causal-danger requirements.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns to the Kansas courts for the required functional-impairment determination under Kansas's reasonable-doubt burden and for the resulting lawful disposition of the commitment order. Independently preserved objections remain in their proper channels.

U.S. Const., Amdt. 14; Kansas sexually violent predator commitment provisions, Kan. Stat. Ann. §§59-29a01 et seq. The diagnoses and predicted danger require evaluation with the evidence of functional control; another detainee's program findings do not establish Crane's circumstances.

# Lee v. Kemna — 2002-01-22

## Event

Lee v. Kemna; No. 00-6933. Merits decision, January 22, 2002; argued October 29, 2001. Review of the Eighth Circuit's affirmance of denial of federal habeas relief after Missouri courts rejected the continuance claim. The question is whether the state's written-motion and absent-witness requirements supply an adequate procedural ground when subpoenaed alibi witnesses unexpectedly disappear during trial and counsel orally requests a short delay.

## Participation

The Court vacates the judgment resting on this procedural-default ground and remands for the remaining habeas inquiry.

## Judgment & Remedy

Vacated and remanded, 6-3. **Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. **Opposing:** Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

The federal courts must consider the due-process continuance claim under the remaining applicable habeas rules, with this inadequate state-ground barrier removed. The conviction remains effective pending lawful further proceedings. The separate cause and innocence gateway rulings are not affirmatively reversed by this judgment.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |

## Holdings

### I. This exceptional application of the continuance rules is inadequate to bar federal review

**Holding and operative rule.** A normally valid state procedural rule does not bar federal review when its application is exorbitant in the particular circumstances. Here the combined unexpected disappearance of subpoenaed witnesses who had attended trial, substantial prior presentation of the alibi and anticipated return, brief requested delay, and the trial judge's stated scheduling impediments make the additional written and repeated showings inadequate as a federal-review bar; the holding creates no general oral-motion or emergency exemption.

**Authority.** Souter's opinion speaks for Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, six Justices, on the complete rule and application.

**The distinct state requirements.** Missouri Rule 24.09 requires a written motion and affidavit unless the adverse party consents to an oral application. Rule 24.10 separately requires materiality and diligence; the witness's identity and residence if known, or diligence to learn them; reasonable grounds to expect attendance or testimony within a reasonable time; the particular expected facts and absence of another available person who could prove them as fully; and absence not procured, consented to or contrived by the applicant, with good faith rather than vexation or delay. If an affidavit is considered insufficient, the court must permit amendment. The oral-application exception does not erase these information requirements, and opponent consent is not inferred from silence.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that adequacy depends on the rule's operation as well as its ordinary legitimacy. Coleman preserves genuinely independent and adequate state grounds. Osborne distinguishes useful presentation from pointless repetition after a matter has been adequately brought before the judge. Ford protects fair notice and regular application, but neither regularity nor the absence of an identical earlier case conclusively answers this exceptional application. The Court respects Missouri's construction of its rules and decides the separate federal question.

The State has legitimate interests in current information about diligence, materiality and likely availability, as the dissent stresses. Here subpoenas, earlier attendance, prior alibi descriptions and counsel's explanation of the expected local presence supplied the substantial practical basis for the short request. The emergency made a contemporaneous sworn explanation of the unexplained disappearance unavailable. The trial judge's stated scheduling impediments would not have been cured by a perfectly written motion. Those facts work together: earlier testimony descriptions alone, a scheduling remark alone or an unexpected absence alone would not establish the same result. The holding neither declares literal compliance nor requires trial judges to announce every procedural defect. It holds that the additional formal demand, in this particular setting, does not adequately justify withholding federal consideration.

### II. Removal of the default barrier returns the distinct constitutional claim

**Holding and operative rule.** With this state ground inadequate, Lee need not additionally establish cause and prejudice to overcome that ground; the federal court must adjudicate his continuance claim under due process and any independently applicable habeas restriction. The state appellate court's procedural ruling does not become an adjudication of the federal claim's merits under 28 U.S.C. §2254(d) merely because it resolved the appeal.

**Authority.** The same six Justices join the bounded remand and its separation of adequacy from entitlement to relief.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that access to federal consideration and a right to relief are different inquiries. Coleman concerns excuses for an adequate default; it does not require such an excuse after the ground has been found inadequate. Trest confirms that default is a defense with actual predicates rather than a subject-matter-jurisdiction defect. The underlying due-process inquiry asks whether denial of the delay arbitrarily deprived Lee of a meaningful opportunity to present his defense in the actual trial circumstances. It requires examination of the missing testimony's disputed significance under the applicable habeas law. The relatives' account that an officer directed them to leave remains an allegation, and their anticipated alibi is not a finding. The separate gateway rulings are not reversed by eliminating this inadequate ground. The constitutional merits and entitlement to relief remain for adjudication because the federal courts did not reach them.

## Precedent Treatment

- Coleman v. Thompson, 501 U.S. 722: applies its requirement of an independent and adequate state ground and preserves exceptions for genuinely adequate defaults; the present inadequacy ruling precedes those excuses.
- Osborne v. Ohio, 495 U.S. 103: applies its distinction between useful presentation and purposeless repetition in the exceptional circumstances here; it supplies no general waiver of continuance requirements.
- Ford v. Georgia, 498 U.S. 411: preserves fair notice and regular application as adequacy safeguards without making them conclusive in every application.
- Trest v. Cain (December 9, 1997): applies its distinction between procedural default and jurisdiction; it supplies neither an automatic hearing entitlement nor the underlying due-process violation.
- O'Sullivan v. Boerckel (June 7, 1999): preserves ordinary exhaustion through a state's normal review opportunities; the present adequacy holding does not dispense with otherwise applicable habeas requirements.

## Law After Decision

The adequate-state-ground doctrine now expressly reaches this exceptional application of Missouri's continuance requirements. Useful, regularly applied procedural requirements remain enforceable, but their ordinary legitimacy does not make an exorbitant application adequate. Removal of this particular default barrier leaves the due-process merits, remaining lawful habeas restrictions and entitlement to relief for their own adjudication.

## Separate Writings

Kennedy, joined by Scalia and Thomas, would affirm because Rule 24.10 retained a substantive function apart from the written-motion form. Earlier alibi descriptions and earlier attendance did not necessarily establish what the witnesses would then say, whether they could be obtained within a reasonable time, or the current diligence and other facts needed to evaluate the delay. A focused contemporaneous presentation also creates a reviewable record. A known procedural ground may support appellate affirmance even when the trial judge relied on scheduling; an uncited requirement is not thereby useless. Osborne involved repetition of a legal objection already foreclosed, not the initial support for a factual request. The dissent objects that searching earlier portions of the record for substantial compliance can displace the State's actual presentation rule. It does not hold that every formally regular rule is adequate in every application, and relies on no finding about why the witnesses left or whether their alibi was true.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns through the Eighth Circuit for adjudication of the continuance claim and remaining applicable habeas questions with this default ground removed. The conviction remains in force pending lawful further proceedings.

## Source Notes

Mo. Rules Crim. Proc. 24.09 and 24.10; Lee v. Kemna, 213 F.3d 1037; 28 U.S.C. §2254. The reason for the witnesses' departure, the truth of the proposed alibi and the constitutional effect of refusing the delay remain disputed rather than established by the adequacy judgment.

## Holdings

### I. The presented platform combination requires an actual competitive inquiry

**Holding and operative rule.** The challenged combination of a principal platform for third-party applications and complementary browser functionality, including its contractual and removal restrictions, must be evaluated under the rule of reason because the existing per se tying category does not reliably resolve the material integration and competitive questions presented. Courts must examine separate demand, conditioning, power, foreclosure and supported efficiencies for the actual restrictions; integration supplies neither immunity nor a presumption of justification, and an adequately supported inquiry may be abbreviated rather than invariably requiring a comprehensive market study.

**Authority.** Part I of Stone-Zsela's opinion has eight votes: Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Stevens does not join the uniform category direction.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the legal method must fit the combination's actual competitive mechanism. Jefferson Parish and Kodak preserve independent consumer demand and the need to prove tying elements; functional connection alone does not merge separate products. But evidence of separate demand and past industry practice may incompletely measure a platform integration that changes the product. California Dental requires enough inquiry to test a substantial competing explanation, permitting abbreviated analysis when experience and the mechanism make the effects sufficiently clear. Khan and NYNEX likewise require a restraint to fit the category invoked. None supplies general software immunity.

Stevens would retain ordinary per se analysis for separable contractual coercion. That concern identifies conduct the actual inquiry must examine, but the relationship among product definition, interface distribution and each restriction warrants the bounded competitive inquiry here. Distributing useful application interfaces may explain less than compulsory retention of the whole browser. Microsoft must substantiate benefits material to its defense, and an explanation that does not justify a particular restriction cannot shelter it. Plaintiffs must establish net anticompetitive effect in browser competition, considering benefits actually supported for that restriction. Operating-system injury sustaining section 2 does not itself establish browser-market injury under section 1. The original undifferentiated judgment did not undertake that inquiry; its vacatur stands.

**Application and mandate limits.** OEM nonremoval restrictions and omission from the removal utility remain possible tying theories, with any additional relief separately justified. Plaintiffs may test the default-browser override's distinct browser-market effect despite rejection of its section 2 ground on the earlier record. Price bundling requires resolution of a positive browser-attributable price increment, including conflicting competitor-discount evidence, before weighing effects and benefits. Commingled code is not added as an independent trial tying basis. The attempted-monopolization reversal remains: plaintiffs may not renew tying theories dependent on a precise browser-market definition or independent entry barriers they failed to establish for that claim, apart from matters implicit in the tying arrangement itself. That restriction preserves this case's mandate rather than creating a universal antitrust rule.

### II. The undisclosed contacts require disqualification and a fresh remedial proceeding

**Holding and operative rule.** Undisclosed press contacts concerning pending merits, credibility and remedy require disqualification under 28 U.S.C. §455(a) when the circumstances cumulatively cause an informed, detached observer reasonably to question impartiality, without proof of actual bias. The established violation requires reassignment here; the entire June 7, 2000 structural-and-conduct decree remains vacated, and relief must be reconsidered on lawfully established liability and an adequate remedial record.

**Authority.** All nine Justices support Part II of Stone-Zsela's opinion, including the common remedial principles and the complete decree disposition.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that publication delayed until judgment did not remove the appearance created by secret outside discussions while adjudication continued. Liteky supplies the cumulative objective standard; judicial origin is context rather than immunity, and outside origin is neither necessary nor sufficient by itself. These contacts concerned the dispute's live subjects and denied the parties an opportunity to raise the issue while the conduct continued. The reported accounts remain assumed true for the disqualification inquiry on the appellate posture, rather than new trial findings about every reported detail.

Liljeberg separately requires attention to injustice to these parties, injustice in other cases and public confidence when determining relief from earlier civil adjudication. Its finality-specific Rule 60(b)(6) prerequisites do not become conditions of this direct appeal. The three interests reject both automatic total vacatur and automatic preservation. All nine agree that the existing decree cannot stand. Apart from disqualification, unresolved remedy-specific disputes without an appropriate hearing, inadequate explanation of the remedy and materially altered liability foundations independently require new remedial proceedings. Neither mandatory divestiture nor immunity from divestiture follows; disputed predictions about division require adjudication at the remedial stage.

### III. Ordinary appellate review does not conclusively resolve the need to revisit compromised liability foundations

**Holding and operative rule.** On the established appearance violation, the new judge must apply Liljeberg's three remedial interests to determine which material factual or credibility foundations cannot meaningfully be separated from the concealed contacts and require fresh determination, and must reconsider the findings and dependent conclusions accordingly. Independently sustainable determinations may remain at their established scope, with appropriate reuse of the record; neither actual-bias proof as to a particular finding nor an automatic all-issues retrial is required.

**Authority.** Part III of Stone-Zsela's opinion has five votes: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg. O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas and Breyer reject this additional-remedy application while accepting the common standard.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the appellate allocation left a material part of Liljeberg's inquiry unanswered. The interviews began before the findings and concerned integration, credibility and the dispute's merits. Rule 52 clear-error review preserves permissible factual choices; it does not independently choose among competing credible accounts or permissible expert inferences. The careful rejection of some liability bases therefore cannot alone establish that every retained foundation may remain binding despite the undisclosed relationship. Anderson and Pullman-Standard preserve this distinction between appellate review and factfinding.

The opposing Justices properly emphasize the innocent plaintiffs' burden, differentiated appellate corrections, limited additional deterrence and confidence restored by replacement of the judge and remedy. Those considerations weigh against indiscriminate repetition. They do not sufficiently justify conclusive insulation of every disputed material foundation reached after the contacts began. Public confidence and the risk of injustice in other cases require the new judge to decide which foundations need neutral reconsideration, without demanding proof of actual bias that section 455(a) does not require. Undisputed matters and independently sustainable legal determinations may remain; prior legal rulings remain pertinent to facts lawfully determined. The remand establishes a tailored inquiry and resulting reconsideration, not presumptive universal taint. Particular findings' binding force is application work under that rule.

# Barnhart v. Sigmon Coal Co. — 2002-02-19

Barnhart v. Sigmon Coal Co., No. 00-1307. Argued November 7, 2001; decided February 19, 2002. On writ of certiorari to the Fourth Circuit's judgment rejecting the challenged Coal Act assignments. ## Holdings

### Asset succession alone does not create a new assignment category

**Holding and operative rule:** Sections 9701(c)(2) and 9706 do not authorize assigning a signatory operator's beneficiaries to an asset purchaser solely because it directly succeeded to that operator's business. A purchaser independently within an enumerated related-person class remains subject to the statute; the holding does not determine an independent contractual obligation or every contested meaning of successor.

**Authority:** Thomas's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg, controls the complete statutory rule, its limits and the affirmance.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that financial responsibility must arise from the relationships Congress enacted. Section 9701(c)(2)(A) identifies a member of the signatory's controlled group under section 52(a), including the signatory; a trade or business under common control under section 52(b); and a person with a partnership interest or joint venture with a signatory in a coal-industry business employing eligible beneficiaries, except an interest held solely as a limited partner. The successor sentence refers to those listed classes rather than adding all direct successors of an original signatory. Subsection (c)(2)(B) measures the relationship on July 20, 1992, or immediately before an earlier cessation of business. The assignment priorities in section 9706(a) do not enlarge these classes.

Section 9706(b)(2) separately permits a post-enactment assignment transfer to a successor under its conditions, retaining the transferor and related persons as guarantors and requiring notice to the trustees. That provision does not automatically reach this earlier asset purchase. Russello gives effect to Congress's differing language; Ron Pair requires enforcement of the resulting text. The strongest objection is that reaching a related person's successor while excluding the direct successor creates an anomalous financing gap. But an imperfect allocation of statutory liability is not an absurdity authorizing a new liable-person class. Jericol's continuation of the business and agreement assumption do not establish the claimed statutory relationship. The contextual reading required by Chickasaw and J.E.M. does not replace the specific classes with general successor policy.

**Precedent treatment:** Russello and Ron Pair are applied to the differentiated categories; Chickasaw and J.E.M. retain contextual construction without supplying an omitted category.

### Clear statutory limits resolve this assignment theory

**Holding and operative rule:** The definite assignment classes cannot be enlarged by an administrative preference for preserving program revenue. Because that statutory limit resolves these assignments, the Court does not reach the unnecessary constitutional objections or decide an unestablished independent contractual ground.

**Authority:** The same six-Justice Court opinion controls.

**Controlling explanation:** Mead distinguishes an agency's interpretive authority from the persuasive force of its reasoning; neither permits liability contrary to clear statutory categories. Eastern Enterprises invalidated particular tier-three assessments without a controlling affirmative constitutional rationale. It did not invalidate the Coal Act generally and does not supply a constitutional shortcut here. The Court's statutory judgment resolves the challenged theory while leaving any genuinely independent obligation to its own legal predicates.

**Precedent treatment:** Mead's statutory boundary is applied; Eastern Enterprises is preserved at the scope of its judgment and independently majority-supported property proposition.

## Precedent Treatment

- Russello v. United States, 464 U.S. 16: different language in related provisions supports the enacted distinction.
- United States v. Ron Pair Enterprises, Inc., 489 U.S. 235: the statutory text governs despite a claimed policy anomaly.

- United States v. Mead Corp., June 18, 2001: interpretive authority and persuasion remain distinct, and neither authorizes departure from clear liability conditions.
- Eastern Enterprises v. Apfel, June 25, 1998: the limited assessment judgment and absence of a controlling affirmative constitutional rationale remain; the Coal Act is not invalidated as a whole.

## Law After Decision

Direct asset succession to an original signatory is insufficient by itself for the challenged statutory assignments. Independently qualifying related persons, the separate prospective assignment-transfer mechanism and enforceable contractual obligations remain governed by their own conditions. This is a statutory coverage rule, not a general immunity for asset purchasers or a constitutional invalidation of the program.

## Separate Writings

**Stevens, joined by O'Connor and Breyer, dissenting.** The dissent would reverse and remand for application of an inclusive succession rule. Congress allocated existing health-benefit obligations across continuing business relationships. Reading the successor sentence in that integrated structure avoids the anomaly of reaching a related person's successor while exempting the closer direct successor that continued the coal business and assumed its agreement. The contextual treatment of successors in 1 U.S.C. sections 1 and 5, together with the labor-law succession principles illustrated by Wiley, Golden State Bottling and Fall River Dyeing, supports that reading; those authorities do not establish liability for every solvent purchaser.

The dissent recognizes that a differentiated list and a separate prospective transfer provision could reflect compromise. Its answer is that the entire succession structure supplies the context for that list, and the Court's separation defeats the allocation Congress made. Legislative explanations corroborate that construction rather than replacing the text. The dissent does not treat a bargaining discount as a proved fact, impose unrestricted federal common-law liability, or decide the constitutional question. Preserved successor-status and other application issues remain for the proper tribunal on remand.

The Fourth Circuit's judgment against these assignments stands. The statutory basis asserted in this review cannot support reassignment; any independent obligation must be established on its own terms.

# Igartua De La Rosa v. United States — 2002-02-19

[Authoritative Record](../records/igartua-de-la-rosa-v-united-states_merits_2002-02-19.md)

## Event

Igartua De La Rosa v. United States. Decided February 19, 2002. On review of the First Circuit's October 13, 2000 judgment, 229 F.3d 80, vacating the district court's territorial presidential-voting order and directing dismissal. The question is whether a federal court may require Puerto Rico to appoint presidential electors and require their votes to be counted when the Constitution supplies no territorial allocation.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated.

## Public Action

The Court affirms the First Circuit's vacatur and its direction to dismiss the preserved territorial-elector claim with prejudice.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 9–0; the territorial-elector order remains vacated and the preserved claim is dismissed with prejudice.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The district court's appointment-and-counting order remains vacated, and the action is dismissed with prejudice within the preserved territorial-elector claim. This disposition does not prohibit a locally authorized nonbinding vote or adjudicate a distinct preserved absentee claim through an existing State allocation.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stevens | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports the judgment | Entire opinion; every controlling proposition and its stated limits |

## Holdings

### Judicial relief must rest on a constitutional electoral allocation

**Holding and operative rule:** Article II allocates presidential electors to States, and the Twenty-Third Amendment separately allocates electors to the District of Columbia. Citizenship alone supplies Puerto Rico no additional electoral unit, number of electors or appointment-and-counting authority; a federal court therefore cannot order the territorial appointment and count sought here.

**Authority:** Stevens's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies nine direct votes for the rule and disposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the requested remedy exceeds the constitutional electoral allocation. McPherson identifies the State-based appointment structure. The Twelfth Amendment regulates that machinery, while the Twenty-Third Amendment expressly adds the District of Columbia. These are constitutive rules about who appoints and how the electoral vote is formed, not merely administrative qualifications that a court may equalize by adding an individual voter. The serious exclusion asserted by citizens residing in Puerto Rico does not itself provide the missing allocation.

Rice protects against racial exclusion within an existing state electorate; Thornton preserves the qualifications of existing constitutional offices; Saenz protects equal citizenship upon taking residence in a State; and Bush addresses administration of an existing presidential count. None supplies a new territorial appointing unit. The Court resolves the concrete constitutional entitlement on its merits, rather than treating all territorial-rights litigation as a political question. No premise of racial or cultural inferiority is accepted. The Constitution's allocation is sufficient; no general territorial immunity or reconsideration of unrelated Insular Cases holdings is necessary.

**Precedent treatment:** McPherson's structural rule is applied. Rice, Thornton, Saenz and Bush retain their protections within the governmental arrangements those cases addressed and do not authorize this allocation.

**Preserved scope:** The rejected treaty and UOCAVA grounds were not appealed and are not revived. Congressional representation, territorial statehood and the local-law validity of Law 403 are outside the question reviewed. Participation through a former State's already allocated electors is a different claim.

## Precedent Treatment

- McPherson v. Blacker, 146 U.S. 1: applied for Article II's allocation of elector appointment to States.
- Rice v. Cayetano, February 23, 2000: its prohibition on racial exclusion from a public state electorate remains; it does not create another electoral unit.
- U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton / Bryant v. Hill, May 22, 1995: fixed congressional qualifications remain distinct from creating an office or appointing unit.

The judgment resolves the preserved claim and leaves the unappealed and outside-scope questions in their existing posture.

# Owasso Independent School District No. I-011 v. Falvo — 2002-02-19

[Authoritative Record](../records/owasso-independent-school-district-no-i-011-v-falvo_merits_2002-02-19.md)

## Event

Owasso Independent School District No. I-011 v. Falvo, No. 00-1073. Argued November 27, 2001; decided February 19, 2002. On writ of certiorari to the Tenth Circuit, which treated peer-graded papers as education records under FERPA. The question is whether the temporary classroom exchange, grading and reporting process satisfies the statutory maintenance requirement.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the Tenth Circuit's coverage judgment and remands for proceedings consistent with the limited statutory holding.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0; temporary peer grading does not establish institutional maintenance under FERPA.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The judgment treating the temporary peer-graded papers as institutionally maintained education records is reversed. The case returns for proceedings consistent with that coverage holding; any genuinely distinct surviving claim must be addressed on its own record.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |

## Holdings

### Temporary instructional handling is not institutional maintenance

**Holding and operative rule:** Under 20 U.S.C. §1232g(a)(4)(A), student-related information must also be maintained by the institution or someone acting for it in the relevant recordkeeping capacity to constitute an education record. Classmates' temporary exchange and grading of papers, followed by immediate reporting of marks during this instructional exercise, does not itself satisfy that condition; teacher direction alone does not make the peer grader an institutional recordkeeper.

**Authority:** Ginsburg's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer, supplies nine direct votes for this coverage rule and its limits.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that identifying a student's performance and maintaining an institutional record are separate statutory requirements. Falvo's privacy concern is real, but disclosure of educational information does not eliminate the maintenance condition. The opposing agency theory relies on the teacher's direction and the student's production of a score. Those facts describe instructional participation; they do not establish institutional record custody. Immediate reporting of the mark does not turn the preceding temporary handling into maintenance.

FERPA's access and custody provisions illuminate recordkeeping without requiring a central repository, permanent storage, one custodian or a particular location. The sole-possession exclusion in section 1232g(a)(4)(B)(i) retains its separate work and is not erased by an invented central-file limitation. Nor does student possession invariably defeat coverage: a student may perform an actual institutional recordkeeping function in a different setting. The maintained-record definition, rather than a general preference for peer grading, decides this practice. A teacher's retained gradebook and papers collected for institutional retention present different conduct outside this coverage question.

**Precedent treatment:** The provision-specific approach in Blessing is preserved; TRW's treatment of independent statutory limitations is consistent with giving maintenance its own function.

### Coverage can resolve the assumed claim without a general enforcement ruling

**Holding and operative rule:** The Court assumes, without deciding, that the asserted section 1983 enforcement route is available and rejects this claim because the challenged temporary process falls outside FERPA's education-record definition. That assumption creates no holding that FERPA generally confers a private action.

**Authority:** The same unanimous Court opinion controls the scope of decision and remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The federal claim supports jurisdiction, and private statutory enforceability is a separate, nonjurisdictional inquiry. No affirmative relief requires recognition of the disputed action when the pleaded practice fails the coverage condition. Blessing requires a right-specific enforcement inquiry when that inquiry matters to relief. Sandoval's authorization of its particular prospective Title VI action supplies neither a general FERPA remedy nor a categorical bar. This complete adverse coverage disposition leaves the separate enforcement question undecided because its answer is unnecessary to the presented coverage question. Later institutional retention must be assessed separately rather than resolved by extending this holding.

**Precedent treatment:** Blessing and Sandoval retain their distinct statute-specific holdings; neither is enlarged or displaced.

## Precedent Treatment

- Blessing v. Freestone, April 21, 1997: the particular statutory right and enforcement vehicle remain separate inquiries; assuming an action here establishes neither.
- Alexander v. Sandoval, April 24, 2001: its integrated Title VI prospective action is preserved and does not decide FERPA enforcement.
- TRW Inc. v. Andrews, November 13, 2001: independent statutory requirements and exclusions retain their actual limiting work.

## Law After Decision

Temporary peer grading and immediate reporting in the described instructional process do not constitute institutional maintenance of education records. FERPA coverage requires both relation to the student and the statutory maintenance function. Record location is not a categorical condition; later institutional retention and the private-enforcement question remain distinct.

# Romeu v. Cohen — 2002-02-19

[Authoritative Record](../records/romeu-v-cohen_merits_2002-02-19.md)

## Event

Romeu v. Cohen. Decided February 19, 2002. On review of the Second Circuit's October 31, 2000 affirmance, explained September 6, 2001, at 265 F.3d 118; lower-court Nos. 00-6287 and 00-6303. The judgment on the pleadings dismissed the challenge to federal and New York presidential absentee eligibility. The question is whether those laws may preserve the former-state presidential franchise for otherwise qualified former residents abroad while completely excluding a comparable former resident who moves to Puerto Rico. Romeu seeks participation through New York's existing electors; Igartua De La Rosa presents the distinct question of territorial electoral allocation.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

## Public Action

The Court reverses dismissal of the federal and state equality claims and remands for prospective relief. The independent entitlement theories supply no additional ground for relief.

## Judgment & Remedy

| Component | Disposition and vote | Supporting | Opposing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Federal equality challenge to UOCAVA | Dismissal reversed; remanded for prospective declaratory relief, 5–4 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg | O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas, Breyer |
| State equality challenge to New York Election Law §11-200(1) | Dismissal reversed; remanded for prospective injunctive relief, 5–4 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg | O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas, Breyer |

The federal declaration, directed to the responsible federal officer, must state that UOCAVA's overseas-voter definitions in §1973ff-6(5)(B) and (C) may not be applied to deny an otherwise comparably qualified Romeu the presidential absentee eligibility preserved for former residents abroad solely because he resides in Puerto Rico. That direction includes the departure-linked former-domicile condition insofar as it recreates the same exclusion despite his last relevant state domicile in New York before moving to Puerto Rico. The declaration operates as applied; it does not deem Puerto Rico foreign or redefine the United States throughout the Act.

The state injunction must prevent the responsible New York election official from denying an otherwise qualified Romeu access to the existing presidential absentee program because of Puerto Rico residence, abode or domicile, registration, or voting in local elections. It must also disapply the departure-from-the-United-States formulation insofar as that formulation would independently exclude him despite his last relevant state domicile in New York. These qualifications are unenforceable in this application; the decree does not delete them throughout every federal-election provision.

The lower court must determine the remaining ordinary former-residence, age, registration and authentication facts and implement prospective equal access. A restriction against a second presidential vote remains permissible; Puerto Rico's local vote is not such a vote. New York may not equalize by withdrawing ballots required by UOCAVA's continuing overseas minimum. Relief operates through New York's existing electors, creates no territorial electors, orders no change to another State's program and reopens no completed election.

The eight Associates reject the asserted independent travel, Article IV and general due-process entitlements. Stone-Zsela does not reach those theories. Their rejection is not a separate partial affirmance of either equality dismissal.

## Holdings

### The prospective federal and state challenges remain distinct and reviewable

**Rule and authority.** Completion of the 2000 election does not extinguish Romeu's recurring claim to prospective presidential absentee eligibility. UOCAVA sets a minimum for its covered overseas voters and does not prohibit broader state eligibility, but that floor does not itself dispose of the independently pleaded federal equality challenge. Part I, joined by all nine Justices, controls.

**Controlling explanation.** Romeu seeks relief from continuing eligibility classifications, not alteration of the completed count. His alleged exclusion is personal, and prospective relief through the officials responsible for the existing absentee mechanism can redress it. The complaint separately challenges federal protection and state eligibility. Giving relief against the state rule does not make the federal merits dismissal an adjudication of a different claim or eliminate the need to decide that challenged classification here.

### Equal protection reaches complete exclusion within the retained state presidential franchise

**Rule and authority.** An applicant who establishes otherwise comparable qualification for New York's retained former-resident presidential electorate and complete exclusion from presidential participation because of relocation to Puerto Rico suffers a severe electoral burden. The State must show that its exclusion is narrowly drawn to advance a compelling electoral interest; the asserted membership, administrative and incremental-accommodation interests do not justify this exclusion. Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg, controls the state holding and its as-applied relief.

**Controlling explanation.** The State's authority to require residence does not answer unequal treatment within the former-resident franchise it has preserved. Prior New York qualification provides the relevant connection; the existing absentee mechanism and lack of another presidential ballot define the burden. Anderson supplies the inquiry into actual injury, precise interests and necessity. Burdick requires examination of the complete electoral system and demanding justification for severe burdens. Its reasonable-burden application does not govern a complete denial. McDonald concerned a record that did not establish exclusion from voting itself; this claim concerns presidential participation, not merely a preferred absentee method.

Harper and Reynolds protect equality in the franchise government makes available. Domestic political membership is the dissent's strongest distinction, but local elections and selection of a Resident Commissioner supply no presidential opportunity. Preventing a second presidential vote can be addressed directly. Ordinary identification and former-residence checks remain available, so administrative concerns do not require this geographic denial. Incremental assistance to citizens abroad is legitimate, but it does not justify the complete exclusion under the applicable severe-burden standard. Concern about unequal opportunities among Puerto Rico residents does not cure unequal treatment within this existing former-state mechanism.

The trigger is comparable prior qualification plus complete presidential exclusion, not territorial residence alone. The rule creates no permanent franchise for every emigrant and does not subject every residence or absentee requirement to strict scrutiny. McPherson preserves the state-based electoral allocation; the injunction operates within it.

### The federal minimum must satisfy the same electoral equality requirement

**Rule and authority.** Through the Fifth Amendment's equality protection, UOCAVA's preservation of presidential absentee eligibility for comparable former residents abroad cannot completely exclude Romeu solely because he resides in Puerto Rico unless the exclusion satisfies the same compelling-interest and narrow-tailoring requirement. It does not; a prospective as-applied declaration against the responsible federal officer is required. Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg, controls.

**Controlling explanation.** The federal law distributes mandatory protection of the same preserved presidential opportunity. Calling that protection a floor explains why New York may go further; it does not answer whether Congress has distributed the protection equally. The comparison and complete-exclusion trigger are the same as in the state claim. The lack of a second presidential ballot, continuing availability of qualification checks, and limited force of the domestic-membership and incremental-accommodation interests therefore have the same constitutional significance.

Harris v. Rosario concerns Congress's territorial benefits distinction. Its deference in that setting does not supply the scrutiny for complete exclusion from this preserved presidential franchise. Territorial residence acquires no suspect status, and the Court does not adopt an alternative holding that the distinction fails ordinary rational-basis review. The holding concerns UOCAVA's application to this comparable former resident; the prospective declaration leaves the federal overseas minimum operative and preserves the existing allocation of electors.

### The independent theories create no permanent right to the former State's ballot

**Rule and authority.** The asserted Article IV, travel and general due-process theories do not independently entitle Romeu to carry New York electoral membership permanently after leaving the State. They supply no additional remedy on these facts; the Fifth Amendment equality claim is separately sustained. Part IV, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, controls this rejection. Stone-Zsela does not reach it.

**Controlling explanation.** Saenz protects equal citizenship upon becoming a bona fide resident of a new State; it does not transfer a former State's franchise to a new domicile. Article IV's protection of interstate equality likewise supplies no general entitlement to remain in a former State's political electorate. The right to travel does not by itself preserve every electoral incident of the former residence. A general due-process entitlement adds no independent basis for the requested ballot. These limits do not defeat the separate equality claim: unequal treatment within a franchise that government actually preserves differs from an unconditional right to retain it. The Court rejects the independent theories without affirming either equality dismissal on that ground.

- **Anderson v. Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780:** Its actual-burden, precise-interest and necessity inquiry is applied to the retained former-resident presidential opportunity.
- **Burdick v. Takushi, 504 U.S. 428:** Its calibrated framework is extended to this selective former-resident franchise; its reasonable-burden application remains controlling in its complete-system setting.
- **McDonald v. Board of Election Commissioners, 394 U.S. 802:** Distinguished because its record did not establish exclusion from voting itself; it remains authority for the absentee distinction it reviewed.

## Law After Decision

New York's existing former-resident presidential absentee program and UOCAVA's corresponding federal protection are subject to electoral equality when an otherwise comparably qualified former resident is completely excluded because of relocation to Puerto Rico. Once that comparison and exclusion are established, the government must satisfy severe-burden scrutiny. The state and federal exclusions fail at that scope, with different prospective remedies against their responsible officials. Ordinary eligibility and authentication conditions and prevention of duplicate presidential voting remain enforceable.

The independent travel, Article IV and general due-process theories establish no permanent former-state franchise. The judgment changes neither Article II's electoral allocation nor the federal minimum owed to covered overseas voters. Territorial statehood, congressional representation, a territorial allocation of electors and the terms of a future amended absentee program are outside the questions decided. Every decided component has controlling majority reasoning.

## Separate Writings

**O'Connor, joined by Scalia, Thomas and Breyer, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** The four would affirm both equality dismissals. Residence ordinarily identifies membership in a State's electorate. Congress and New York may accommodate former residents outside the domestic political system without extending the accommodation to people who establish membership in another domestic polity. Burdick, Timmons and Schrader permit meaningful electoral categories and require identification of the relevant electorate before the severity of a lost opportunity determines scrutiny. Saenz concerns equal citizenship in the new State, not a continuing former-state vote.

The dissent acknowledges that Puerto Rico's local elections and Resident Commissioner do not replace presidential participation. That absence makes the exclusion consequential but does not establish continuing membership in New York's electorate. Domestic political membership and incremental overseas accommodation provide a rational basis for the distinction, without making territorial power unreviewable or every residence rule valid. UOCAVA is a federal floor, so New York could extend eligibility; that permission does not establish a constitutional duty to do so. The proposed decree's precision does not cure the majority's antecedent membership error. The four join the prospective-review and federal-floor conclusions and the rejection of independent permanent-membership, travel, Article IV and general due-process entitlements.

The federal and state equality dismissals are reversed. Implementation must preserve the continuing overseas minimum and distinguish a prohibited second presidential vote from participation in local territorial elections. The completed election remains intact.

## Event

Winn-Dixie Stores, Inc. v. Commissioner. Decided February 20, 2002. On merits review of the Eleventh Circuit's June 28, 2001 judgment, 254 F.3d 1313, affirming the Tax Court's 1993 deficiency determination. The question is whether satisfying the four-of-seven exception to section 264's interest limitation requires deductions for a borrowing arrangement accepted to lack both a nontax business purpose and any prospect of pretax profit.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Kennedy | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports the judgment | Entire opinion; every controlling proposition and its stated limits |

## Holdings

### The exception removes one disallowance without guaranteeing a deduction

**Holding and operative rule:** Compliance with the four-of-seven exception in the governing section 264(c)(1) removes the relevant section 264(a)(3) restriction on systematic premium-financing borrowing; it does not affirmatively confer a section 163 deduction or immunize the borrowing from the substantive-sham inquiry. Where accepted findings establish both no nontax business purpose and no prospect of pretax profit, that exception does not require the claimed interest and related fee deductions. The accepted four-of-seven compliance means that no portion of the annual premium was financed by policy borrowing in four of the first seven years.

**Authority:** Kennedy's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies nine direct votes for the statutory rule, narrow application and affirmance.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an exception to one restriction does not dispense with the substantive requirements of the claimed deduction. Knetsch examines whether the transaction supplies genuine substantive indebtedness notwithstanding the absence of a particular prohibition; Gregory likewise distinguishes statutory substance from a tax form that lacks it. Real policies, payments and contractual obligations do not alone establish the borrowing arrangement's qualifying substance.

Winn-Dixie's strongest argument is that Congress deliberately regulated insurance borrowing in detail and authorized the four-of-seven pattern. The exception has real work: qualifying timing avoids that particular statutory bar. But avoiding the bar is not an affirmative grant covering every transaction. Gitlitz preserves tax consequences Congress actually confers while retaining independent deduction requirements; it does not protect a transaction that fails those requirements. This reading therefore does not permit general revenue policy to override a legislatively conferred benefit.

Here compliance with the exception and both adverse economic findings are accepted. Interest and fees exceeded relevant benefits, contemporaneous memoranda and the accepted absence of a business use support the substantive-sham conclusion, and no factual retrial is called for. Tax motivation alone is insufficient, and the Court does not require every intentionally tax-favored investment to be profitable without its incentive. Because both relevant substance findings are negative here, no universal conjunctive or disjunctive test is chosen for arrangements with a substantial nontax purpose or meaningful pretax returns. That different factual question is outside this review.

**Precedent treatment:** Knetsch and Gregory are applied to the accepted transaction; Gitlitz's express statutory benefits and independent qualification requirements remain intact.

## Precedent Treatment

- Gregory v. Helvering, 293 U.S. 465: applied for statutory substance rather than a general prohibition on arranging affairs to reduce tax.
- Gitlitz v. Commissioner, January 9, 2001: its protection of enacted tax consequences is preserved alongside the independent requirements of the actual deduction; it supplies no immunity for this arrangement.

The four-of-seven exception is not a statutory safe harbor from substantive-sham scrutiny. This arrangement's accepted lack of both nontax business purpose and pretax profit potential supports the disallowance. Transactions with meaningful economic substance, mixed findings or an affirmative statutory incentive remain governed by their own legal and factual predicates.

# Wisconsin Department of Health and Family Services v. Blumer — 2002-02-20

[Authoritative Record](../records/wisconsin-department-of-health-and-family-services-v-blumer_merits_2002-02-20.md)

## Event

Wisconsin Department of Health and Family Services v. Blumer, No. 00-952. Argued December 3, 2001; decided February 20, 2002. On writ of certiorari to the Wisconsin Court of Appeals, which required a resources-first Medicaid calculation after rejecting the administrative determination and circuit-court judgment; the Wisconsin Supreme Court declined review. The question is whether section 1396r-5 permits considering anticipated support from the institutionalized spouse before increasing the community spouse resource allowance.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the state judgment requiring resources first and remands for proceedings consistent with the permissible income-first construction.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 5–4; the statute permits the income-first method within its stated limits.**

**Supporting:** Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor and Scalia.

The Wisconsin Court of Appeals' federal-law restriction on the income-first method is reversed. The matter returns for proceedings and any remaining calculation under the permissible method, using supported income and resource figures and the statute's distinct actual-payment requirements.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Breyer | Supports the judgment | Entire opinion; every controlling proposition and its stated limits |
| Dissent | Stevens | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia | Opposes the judgment | Entire statutory disagreement and contrary remedy |

## Holdings

### A State may consider prospective support before increasing protected resources

**Holding and operative rule:** At the preeligibility hearing under 42 U.S.C. §1396r-5(e)(2)(C), a State may consider the community spouse's expected posteligibility income, including support that may be transferred under subsection (d)(1)(B), before increasing the community spouse resource allowance. The statute permits income first; it does not require nationwide adoption or independently adjudicate every resources-first program.

**Authority:** Ginsburg's opinion of the Court, joined by Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer, supplies five direct votes for this construction, its statutory qualifications and the reversal.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this hearing anticipates the circumstances in which eligibility and the maintenance allowance operate. Both methods require forecasts. Congress protected a maintenance-income floor while preserving resources for care; it did not guarantee the largest possible resource reserve. Subsection (a)(1) supersedes inconsistent Medicaid provisions, and (a)(3) preserves ordinary methodology except where the section otherwise provides. Neither clause eliminates the need to read the specific provisions together.

Subsection (b)(1) prevents deeming the community spouse's income available to the institutionalized spouse. It does not state the reciprocal prohibition asserted against considering prospective support. Subsection (b)(2) governs posteligibility attribution according to its ownership rules, not the antecedent assessment's exclusive list of potential support. Subsection (c) separately governs resource assessment and later separation. Under (d)(1)(B), a deduction for the community spouse allowance is available only to the extent income is actually made available to or for the benefit of that spouse; transfer remains optional. A forecast neither establishes an actual payment nor commands one.

The contrary reading treats (e)(2)(C)'s resource-increase command as requiring present ownership of every income source considered. But that hearing assesses future maintenance under the statutory arrangement. It may include the contemplated support allowance without deeming ownership or payment already accomplished. Once inadequacy is established under the lawful calculation, the statute requires the resource adjustment. The Court imposes no additional consent, escrow or payment-guarantee prerequisite and makes no finding that a later transfer occurred. Good Samaritan supports considered administration within the statute, not displacement of these specific protections.

**Operative qualifications:** For the separate posteligibility attribution under (b)(2), a governing nontrust instrument may specify the allocation; otherwise income solely payable to one spouse is available to that spouse, jointly payable income is divided equally, and income shared with others follows the spouse's interest or half of the spouses' unspecified joint interest. Trust income follows the governing Medicaid trust provisions and the trust's allocation, or the parallel payment rules when no specific allocation appears. Nontrust income without an ownership instrument is divided equally. The institutionalized spouse may rebut the nontrust payment and no-instrument presumptions by a preponderance of evidence of different ownership. These rules do not turn a forecast into payment. Under (e)(2)(B), if either spouse establishes that exceptional circumstances causing significant financial duress require community-spouse income above the ordinary minimum monthly maintenance needs allowance, an amount sufficient to supply the necessary additional income must replace that allowance. Under (d)(5), when a court orders the institutionalized spouse to provide monthly income for the community spouse's support, the community spouse monthly income allowance must be at least the ordered amount. Those income-allowance adjustments remain distinct from (e)(2)(C)'s resource-increase command; none substitutes another provision's trigger or establishes that its conditions are met here.

**Precedent treatment:** Good Samaritan's permission for considered administration remains bounded by the specific enactment; its holding supplies no universal asset-preservation or administrative-discretion rule.

### Persuasive agency reasoning supports independent statutory construction

**Holding and operative rule:** The agency materials here receive the weight their reasoning earns rather than automatic controlling force. The Court independently concludes that income first is permissible; no new exception to, or abolition of, lawful delegated interpretive authority is adopted.

**Authority:** The same five-Justice Court opinion controls.

**Controlling explanation:** The November 1993 instruction described income first as required. The March 3, 1994 clarification instead permitted income first or another reasonable interpretation pending a final rule. The September 7, 2001 publication remained a proposed rule at this decision. Those different legal forms and dates cannot be collapsed into a binding final mandate. Mead and Christensen distinguish delegated legal force from persuasive reasons, and Skidmore supplies the latter inquiry. ## Holdings

### I. Limited account entrustment does not categorically extinguish subscriber privacy

**Holding and operative rule:** A user's limited entrustment of nonpublic subscriber information to a bulletin-board operator for account administration does not by itself extinguish a reasonable expectation of privacy. Confidential credentials and identifying records of a restricted account remain protected when the user actually expects confidentiality and the account's terms, functions and practices make that expectation reasonable; each claimant must establish a personal protected interest and an actual governmental intrusion.

**Authority:** Part I of Breyer's opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg, giving five direct votes for the complete rule. O'Connor also joins its application to confidential access credentials, giving that proposition six direct votes, but does not join its broader identifying-record scope. No aggregation under Marks is necessary.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that access for a limited operational purpose is not automatically surrender to unrestricted governmental examination. Katz protects a reasonable personal expectation of confidentiality. Carter's retained shared-privacy principle and Ferguson's distinction between a limited information relationship and a different governmental use support examining what the account actually protects. A password's function is to restrict access, not invite public inspection.

Miller and Smith remain binding for bank business records and dialed telephone numbers. Both involved information conveyed to another, and confidentiality alone therefore cannot distinguish them. Here the question concerns the user's own restricted account information, its exclusion and identification functions, the terms on which it is maintained, and the intrusion actually proved. The mere fact of an operator's possession cannot replace that inquiry. Nor does an operator's notice concerning access to messages conclusively establish that account credentials or identifying records were made public.

This rule does not immunize records from a lawful evidence search. Zurcher rejects a requirement that every person whose records are searched be a criminal suspect. The equipment could be seized for lawful off-site examination, but that authorization does not by itself resolve whether examination of particular subscriber information fell within the constitutionally sufficient, offense-limited warrant. The lower court used its categorical privacy conclusion to avoid that subscriber-specific inquiry. The affected institutional claims return for that application and their independent defendant, attribution and causation requirements. Publicly exposed information does not obtain protection through this holding.

### II. The personal defendants retain conduct-date qualified immunity

**Holding and operative rule:** An individual official is protected from personal constitutional damages when the unlawfulness of the challenged conduct was not sufficiently clear under the law existing when the official acted, assessed on the supported facts material to that conduct. The 1995 subscriber-information searches at issue fall within that protection: the later account-privacy rule does not supply prior notice, and the officials need not prove actual reliance on authority expressly permitting each search.

**Authority:** Part II of Breyer's opinion is joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg. Those eight direct joins sustain the personal judgment; Stone-Zsela dissents from this portion.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the constitutional question and the personal defense have different temporal inquiries. Harlow and Anderson require an objective assessment of conduct-date legal clarity, with the right stated at the specificity the actual conduct requires. Wilson and Elder preserve consideration of all relevant law rather than an identical-case requirement. Saucier left that defense in place, and Denius distinguished later recognition of privacy from sufficient earlier notice.

The challenged searches occurred in June and August 1995. The later Carter, Ferguson, Kyllo and Denius decisions cannot establish notice at those earlier dates. Miller and Smith supplied substantial support for treating voluntarily conveyed operational information as outside the subscriber's protected sphere; no then-operative rule made the newly recognized account-specific distinction sufficiently clear to defeat the personal defense on the reviewed claim.

The proposed actual-reliance requirement would replace the settled defense, not apply it. Harlow protects objectively reasonable official action amid legal uncertainty without requiring an inquiry into the official's actual legal sources or subjective consultation. Section 1983's remedial purpose does not by itself remove that established protection. The ordinary merits rule's allowances for reasonable factual perceptions do not eliminate the distinct problem of uncertainty about legal obligations. The Court therefore retains the objective defense. This immunity protects the personal defendants, not a governmental entity, and does not decide whether the disputed searches satisfied the substantive constitutional rule.

## Holdings

### Supplemental tolling does not cover this nonconsenting State's immune state-law claims

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1367(d) does not toll the limitations period for a state-law claim against a nonconsenting State that falls outside subsection (a)'s supplemental adjudicative grant because of sovereign immunity. A valid federal claim within original jurisdiction does not itself authorize the separate state-law claim or bring that immune claim within the linked tolling provision.

**Authority:** Thomas's opinion is joined throughout by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Ginsburg. Those five direct joins control the statutory rule and affirmance.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the statutory reference to subsection (a) limits which asserted claims receive subsection (d)'s protection. Pennhurst preserves a State's immunity from federal adjudication of its state-law duties without consent. Vermont Agency distinguishes constitutional power from affirmative statutory authorization to proceed against a sovereign. Subsection (a)'s general supplemental grant contains no unmistakable authorization for this nonconsenting State-defendant claim, and subsection (d) supplies no independent affirmative indication that Congress included it despite that exclusion.

The employees correctly distinguish tolling in a competent state forum from a federal merits judgment. But that difference does not eliminate the cross-reference Congress enacted. The word asserted does not make every related filing a claim selected by subsection (a). The ordinary protection lasts while a covered claim is pending and for thirty days after dismissal, unless state law provides longer tolling; those periods do not enlarge the class covered. The additional provision for other same-action claims voluntarily dismissed at or after dismissal of a covered claim does not govern these involuntary dismissals.

Kimel authorizes the federal ADEA action. Schacht accordingly prevents immunity on the supplemental claim from erasing the independent federal jurisdiction. Neither decision supplies consent to adjudicate the state claim. The Regents asserted immunity in their answers, and subsequent discovery, mediation and scheduling did not relinquish that express defense. College Savings Bank likewise rejects consent inferred merely from ordinary activity.

This statutory ground sustains the state limitations judgment without treating the federal ADEA claim as unauthorized or reopening its final dismissal. The Court need not decide Congress's constitutional power to enact a differently specified tolling rule: statutory coverage resolves the presented claim. The holding supplies no general rule excluding every dismissal outside subsection (c), and no general presumption against federal laws affecting States.

### II. The assumed deprivation satisfies binding Farmer

## Holdings

### I. The transmission grant includes unbundled interstate service for retail purchasers

**Holding and operative rule:** The Federal Power Act's separate grant over transmission of electric energy in interstate commerce includes independently supplied interstate transmission serving retail purchases. The wholesale limitation governs the separate sales grant; it does not narrow the transmission grant, and FERC may regulate the covered transmission's rates, terms and conditions without ordering a utility to begin retail wheeling.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join Part I of Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court. That direct unanimous coalition controls the category and the following limits.

**Operative statutory limits:** Section 201(b)(1) retains state retail-sale authority and, except as the Act specifically provides otherwise, excludes facilities used for generation, local distribution, intrastate-only transmission or transmission of energy consumed wholly by the transmitter. Its preservation of lawful state authority over hydroelectric exports remains. Section 201(b)(2) applies sections 210, 211 and 212 and their enforcement to the entities those provisions cover; compliance with a section 210 or 211 order does not otherwise subject an entity to general FPA jurisdiction. Section 201(f)'s exclusion of federal, state and local governments, their agencies, authorities and instrumentalities, corporations wholly owned directly or indirectly by one or more of them, and their officials acting in duty remains unless a provision specifically refers to them.

Section 212(g) prohibits orders inconsistent with state retail-marketing-area law. Section 212(h) prohibits an order conditioned on, or requiring, direct transmission to an ultimate consumer. Its separate rule for transmission to or for a resale entity permits the covered exception only when both conditions hold: the entity is a federal power marketing agency, TVA, a State or political subdivision or its agency, a corporation or association that has ever received a Rural Electrification Administration loan to provide electric service under the Rural Electrification Act of 1936, a person with a state- or local-law public electric-service obligation arising independently of contract alone, or a corporation or association wholly owned directly or indirectly by one or more of those listed entities; and it either served that consumer on October 24, 1992 or would deliver all the energy through transmission or distribution facilities it owns or controls. State and local authority over direct-to-consumer transmission remains preserved. Section 212(e) makes use of sections 210, 211, 213 or 212 unnecessary as a substitute for other lawful authority and, except as those sections provide, prevents them from limiting other Commission authority; it does not eliminate the retail-wheeling restrictions.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a service does not become a retail sale merely because it transmits energy that the customer purchases at retail. The statute grants authority over interstate transmission separately from wholesale interstate sales. Reading the wholesale limitation into both clauses would insert a restriction Congress did not enact. Section 201(a)'s general policy statement must be read with those operative grants and specific exclusions, not made a repeal of them.

New York's strongest objection is that federal regulation of retail-associated transmission shifts an activity traditionally administered by States. The answer lies in the actual service regulated. The order reaches independent interstate transmission already provided voluntarily or under state direction; it does not require new retail wheeling or transfer retail-rate and local-distribution authority wholesale. State siting, local reliability and retail-service functions remain, and no particular facility classification is decided. The connected grid identifies the interstate facts; it creates no power apart from the text.

Iowa Utilities Board supplies the distinction between an enacted federal assignment and independent limits governing its exercise. Gulf Power likewise separates coverage from a particular regulatory method. Mead requires a real delegation before agency interpretation acquires controlling force; the enacted categories resolve this question independently, without a new deference rule. The statutory boundaries thus answer the allocation objection while sustaining the order's limited transmission coverage.

### II. This proceeding did not compel Enron's additional bundled-service remedy

**Holding and operative rule:** FERC could remedy the wholesale transmission-access discrimination established in this proceeding through the adopted open-access requirements while declining compulsory unbundling of bundled retail service on its independently sufficient determination that this additional remedy was unnecessary. Section 206 does require a lawful prospective determination and order after a hearing and finding that a jurisdictional rate, charge, classification or affecting rule, regulation, practice or contract is unjust, unreasonable, unduly discriminatory or preferential; the burden to prove that unlawfulness lies on the Commission or complainant, and the agency may not leave established discrimination uncorrected.

**Authority:** Part II of Stone-Zsela's opinion has the direct support of Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Those six votes control. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would remand this component for corrected agency analysis.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that authority, the trigger for a duty to act and the necessary extent of correction are different questions. Sections 205 and 206 preserve lawful rates, nondiscrimination, filed schedules and mandatory correction when the required finding is made. They do not make every proposed structural extension obligatory merely because the Commission may possess authority to consider it.

The agency did characterize bundled delivered energy as a state-regulated retail sale. That ground cannot be erased by calling the order purely discretionary. But the agency also separately determined that compulsory retail unbundling was unnecessary to eliminate the discrimination it found. The Court sustains that actual, record-linked remedial determination without adopting a categorical jurisdictional exclusion. The functional-unbundling requirements address the established wholesale-access discrimination; Enron's demand does not show why the findings require the additional restructuring in this proceeding. Counsel's claim of inadequate record support is not substituted for the agency's own explanation.

The opposing Justices contend that the jurisdictional characterization undermines the agency's understanding of the corrective duty. That concern would require remand if the refusal depended on legal powerlessness. Here the independent unnecessary-remedy determination adequately supports the refusal of the particular additional remedy sought. Good Samaritan permits considered administration within a mandatory statutory structure but rejects unsupported litigation rationalizations. MCI forbids dispensing with an enacted obligation, and Blumer distinguishes a mandatory trigger from a permissible means; none requires the Court to prescribe Enron's preferred remedy. Gulf Power's demand to preserve applicable classifications and formulas does not remove the actual independent ground present here.

The holding establishes no permanent bundled-service exemption. A universal allocation of bundled-service jurisdiction beyond the authority needed for this order falls outside the particular remedial demand and is not decided. The demand for compulsory additional unbundling in this proceeding is fully resolved.

## Holdings

### I. Forfeited Rule 11 error is governed by Rule 52(b)

**Holding and operative rule:** A defendant who forfeits a Rule 11 objection must satisfy Rule 52(b): there must be error that is plain and affects substantial rights, ordinarily with the defendant bearing the effect burden, and correction remains discretionary only when the error seriously affects the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings. Rule 11(h) does not exempt this plea-taking omission from ordinary forfeiture review; intentional waiver remains distinct.

**Authority:** Part I of O'Connor's opinion has the direct support of Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Those eight votes control; Stevens dissents from this rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the plea rule and the general review rule operate together. Rule 11(h) requires disregarding variances that do not affect substantial rights, but does not say that a failure to object has no consequence. Olano supplies the separate requirements and usual defendant burden. Johnson applies that framework to an important constitutional omission without making its importance an automatic exception.

The contrary argument correctly identifies the judge's affirmative personal duty and a defendant's possible inability to recognize an omitted warning. Those concerns establish why the colloquy is mandatory; they do not delete Rule 52(b) for the unpreserved claim. McCarthy enforced an earlier Rule 11 regime, and Rule 11(h) displaced automatic reversal without expressly abolishing plain-error review. McCarthy did not decide the present choice of review standards. The trial-counsel omission therefore remains error despite Vonn's representation and the prosecutor's intervention. His different withdrawal argument did not preserve it. The ordinary effect inquiry and independent discretion requirement apply without requiring actual innocence or treating substantial-rights impact as sufficient by itself.

### II. Relevant portions of the existing record inform the error's effect

**Holding and operative rule:** In assessing the effect of the Rule 11 omission on substantial rights, a reviewing court may consider relevant information throughout the existing record, including earlier judicial rights advisements. Such information bears on actual understanding and effect; it neither cures the judge's omitted personal warning nor conclusively establishes understanding from representation or a signed form alone.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join Part II of O'Connor's opinion, giving direct unanimous authority independent of the division over burdens.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that actual earlier court advisements cannot be excluded merely because they occurred outside the plea colloquy. United States v. Young permits review of the whole record in assessing error, and Olano separates a procedural breach from its effect. McCarthy's personal-colloquy requirement remains mandatory; admitting relevant evidence about consequences does not excuse noncompliance.

The magistrate twice advised Vonn of counsel rights and obtained acknowledgments of understanding. Those events may inform what he understood when pleading, but their timing, content and demonstrated comprehension must be evaluated. The Court supplies no off-record explanation and treats neither counsel's presence nor a form as an irrebuttable cure. A transcript boundary that excludes actual recorded advice would obstruct the inquiry into effect without improving enforcement of the duty itself.

### III. The proper framework must first be applied below

**Holding and operative rule:** The Ninth Circuit's use of the wrong burden and its exclusion of relevant existing-record material require vacatur and remand. That court must apply all four Rule 52(b) conditions, including record-specific effect and the independent basis for discretionary correction, before deciding the appropriate appellate relief.

**Authority:** Part III of O'Connor's opinion has the same eight-Justice coalition as Part I. Stevens agrees with vacatur and remand for whole-record consideration but would apply the Government's harmlessness burden.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court of Appeals did not assess this record under the governing framework. Earlier warnings are relevant, not conclusive, so their presence alone cannot support the Government's requested immediate reinstatement. Olano requires the effect inquiry and the distinct judgment about correction to retain separate work. The case returns for that application and any other properly remaining appellate issues. The perjury-warning contention remains undecided because it was not resolved below and falls outside the review questions decided here. The case concerns direct review of this forfeited federal-rule omission, not collateral relief or the consequences of every constitutional plea defect.

# Young v. United States — 2002-03-04

## Holdings

### The three-year tax lookback excludes a prior legally disabling stay

**Holding and operative rule:** The three-year income-tax priority lookback in the applicable 11 U.S.C. section 507(a)(8)(A)(i), carried into dischargeability by section 523(a)(1)(A), is suspended for the interval during which a prior bankruptcy stay legally prevented collection of that same liability. This traditional suspension requires no bad-faith finding and does not extend the period for ordinary collection difficulty, every successive petition or every deadline in the Bankruptcy Code.

**Authority:** Breyer's Opinion of the Court is joined in full by all eight other Justices. Their direct unanimous agreement controls the rule, its statutory limits and the affirmance.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Code's temporary prohibition cannot consume the collection interval that its priority and discharge rules protect. The lookback is backward-looking, but its finite protection performs a limitations function; other remedies surviving the interval do not eliminate that function. Irwin supplies a rebuttable traditional tolling background, not a power to disregard statutory terms. The present suspension removes only time during which the Code legally disabled collection.

The taxpayers emphasize the absence of an express tolling clause and Congress's extensions elsewhere. Those provisions do different work. Section 523(b) addresses debts actually excepted from an earlier discharge, not this dismissed Chapter 13 proceeding. Section 108(c) addresses covered proceedings outside bankruptcy; its distinct setting does not forbid this suspension. The different 240-day rule's express treatment of offers in compromise does not make a collection stay usable time under the three-year rule. A broader construction of section 108(c)(1)'s suspension language is unnecessary to resolve this interaction.

Brockamp's detailed tax-refund filing and amount structure rebutted the incapacity extension sought there; it does not make every tax-related period untollable. Noland forbids changing statutory priority categories by general fairness, but this rule preserves the legislated interval and category. Raleigh keeps the substantive tax burden distinct from federal dischargeability. TRW's refusal to add a general discovery extension to the FCRA does not govern a later statutory prohibition on collection. No fraud, concealment or abuse premise supplies the rule.

The return was due October 15, 1993. The May 1, 1996 Chapter 13 filing imposed the stay that continued when the Chapter 7 petition followed on March 12, 1997. Excluding that interval places this debt within the three-year lookback. Because the legal disability operated regardless of motive, affirmance requires no finding that the taxpayers abused bankruptcy.


## Precedent Treatment

- Irwin v. Department of Veterans Affairs: its rebuttable traditional background is applied to the particular stay and lookback interaction; no universal deadline extension follows.
- United States v. Brockamp: distinguished by the integrated tax-refund filing, allowance and payment-window structure and the incapacity extension there; both of its statutory bars retain their force.
- United States v. Noland: applied to preserve Congress's priority categories while allowing lawful equity within the Code; no creditor class receives a new rank.
- Raleigh v. Illinois Department of Revenue: its preservation of the substantive tax burden remains distinct from this federal dischargeability calculation.
- TRW Inc. v. Andrews: its rejection of a general discovery extension under the governing FCRA remains; accrual or discovery and a later legal disability to collect are different questions.


## Law After Decision

The stated three-year lookback excludes a prior bankruptcy stay that actually prohibited collection of the covered tax debt. Motive is immaterial to that legal disability. The tax's substantive burden and statutory priority categories remain unchanged, and other deadlines retain their own text and structure.
