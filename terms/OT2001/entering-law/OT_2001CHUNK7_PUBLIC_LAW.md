## Event

Balderas v. Texas, No. 01-1196. Summary disposition on direct appeal under 28 U.S.C. § 1253; decided June 17, 2002. The Court considers the papers without oral argument or plenary review. The three-judge Eastern District of Texas court adopted Plan 1151C for thirty-two congressional seats on November 14, 2001, after trial from October 22 through November 1 and argument November 2. The jurisdictional statement was filed February 12, 2002; motions to affirm followed March 21 and replies April 2.

The congressional appeal challenges rejection of additional Latino-opportunity districts, the capacity and compactness standards used, and the adequacy of the findings. The relief sought is correction of the congressional replacement arrangement for asserted vote dilution. This decision concerns that congressional judgment; the separate Texas House and Senate judgments are outside its scope.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated in consideration of the papers and the decision. There was no oral argument. No Justice is recused.

## Public Action

The congressional judgment is summarily affirmed. The Court issues an unsigned order accompanied by separate opinions; no rationale for affirmance commands a majority.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Disposition:** Affirmed, 5–4, as to the congressional judgment adopting Plan 1151C and denying the appealed request for additional Latino-opportunity districts.

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

**Opposing:** Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, who would order limited vacatur and remand of the rejection of the appealed additional Latino-opportunity arrangement.

Plan 1151C remains operative unless lawfully modified. The 2002 election under that plan is left undisturbed. The congressional judgment receives no remand; the separate legislative judgments are unaffected.

## Holdings

### Existing capacity rule retained

**Holding and operative rule:** For a preserved district-formation claim by one protected group, the first Gingles condition may be met through a numerical majority in a lawful, geographically compact district or through rigorous proof of dependable crossover support sufficient to elect the group's candidates of choice. Compactness, cohesion, relevant majority-bloc defeat, causation and unequal opportunity under the independent totality inquiry remain necessary; speculation, one unusual electoral victory, population arithmetic or mere influence does not establish dependable capacity, and a sufficient lawful ground independent of numerical exclusivity remains effective.

**Authority:** Valdespino v. Alamo Heights Independent School District, June 17, 1999, remains controlling. Stone-Zsela's concurrence, Part I, and Souter's dissent, Part I, joined by Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer, expressly accept that same rule and its limits. This is direct agreement by five Justices preserving existing law, not a new test or a majority rationale for today's affirmance. The four dissenters do not join the affirmance or its factual application.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 2 protects actual opportunity to elect the protected group's preferred representatives. Valdespino permits a single group to establish that opportunity through dependable support from other voters while preserving a lawful district, the other voting conditions and the ultimate inquiry into unequal opportunity. Gingles supplies the causal requirements; Growe carries them into districting claims; Voinovich requires a proved violation before a federal court compels a district; De Grandy makes the necessary conditions distinct from the ultimate statutory judgment and rejects a duty to maximize districts. A population percentage neither completes that inquiry nor forecloses the functional route. The same precedent also preserves independent factual and procedural rulings, so citing an obsolete numerical rule does not by itself establish reversible error. The Justices who accept this rule disagree here over whether the actual findings sufficiently resolve the claims carried into this congressional appeal. Their agreement on existing law supplies no common answer to that application dispute.

**Precedent treatment:** Valdespino is maintained within its single-group scope; the first Gingles condition remains modified as that decision held, while the remaining statutory conditions and independently sufficient lawful grounds retain their force.

### Congressional judgment affirmed without a majority rationale

**Judgment without controlling rationale:** The November 14, 2001 congressional judgment adopting Plan 1151C and rejecting the appealed request for additional Latino-opportunity districts is affirmed, 5–4. Stone-Zsela, O'Connor and Kennedy find the practical-effectiveness and geography grounds independently sufficient, with the qualifications in their separate writings; Scalia and Thomas reject the asserted statutory district-allocation entitlement. Neither ground commands five votes. The judgment binds the parties, but it does not adopt every premise of the lower opinion.

**Authority and limit:** The Court's unsigned summary order controls the disposition. The application rationale assumes and applies the statutory dilution framework; the noncoverage rationale denies that framework's application to the requested allocation. The latter supplies no finding that the former's elements were not met. These incompatible grounds do not yield a controlling narrower rationale under Marks v. United States, 430 U.S. 188 (1977). The Court establishes neither a numerical-majority safe harbor nor a rule that equivalent compactness scores, turnout differences, incumbent success or district shape alone resolve a Section 2 claim. The separate legislative judgments remain outside this congressional decision.

## Law After Decision

The congressional judgment and Plan 1151C stand. Existing single-group capacity law continues unchanged: dependable functional capacity may satisfy the first condition, but a claimant must establish the remaining statutory conditions and unequal opportunity. The action establishes no new capacity, compactness, totality or preservation test. The sufficiency of these findings has no majority explanation that can be extended into a general rule. Distinct-group coalition entitlement, influence-only entitlement and a universal population or statistical formula remain outside the question resolved here. No later New Jersey judgment is decided or predicted by this disposition.

## Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in the judgment.** Part I maintains Valdespino's two capacity routes and the surviving statutory conditions. Rigorous proof means reliable proof of dependability under the ordinary preponderance burden, not a higher burden, compulsory study, fixed winning percentage or permanent coalition. A single group's outside electoral support differs from aggregation of separate protected groups.

Part II finds the appeal's majority-registration, compactness and findings objections insufficient to require vacatur. Appellants offered election-performance evidence, including reaggregated contests and cross-examination; their case was not devoid of functional proof. But they sought seven registered-voter-majority districts and argued that those proposals exceeded the existing majority threshold. Evidence offered to prove those districts effective does not itself establish that a preserved claim was rejected solely because a protected group lacked a numerical majority. No particular terminology is required to preserve an issue; the material legal objection must actually be presented.

The lower court's findings about practical effectiveness and geography, read with the trial, independently sustain the judgment. Competing testimony addressed marginal districts, turnout and the proposed electorate; selected victories in Plan 1099C did not conclusively establish performance of every district in Plan 1123C. The geographic dispute included corridors, community relationships and subdivision boundaries, not shape alone. Comparative scores were relevant but did not compel a finding of workable geography. The findings are brief, yet identify grounds sufficient for review without adopting every appellate characterization of the testimony. The separate multigroup-cohesion finding does not answer a single-group crossover claim. Neither partisan convenience nor statewide gains could excuse proved local deprivation; the required violation was not established on the independent grounds here. Plan 1151C and the 2002 election under it remain undisturbed.

**O'Connor, concurring in the judgment; Kennedy joins Part I only.** Part I regards the trial court's practical-effectiveness and geography findings as sufficient when read with the record. The competing testimony concerns whether the additional arrangement would yield actual electoral opportunity, including marginality, turnout and the proposed electorate. Registration majorities do not answer those questions, and a geometric comparison does not by itself establish lawful compactness. Rule 52(a) requires an intelligible factual basis; it does not require discussion of every exhibit. The opinion's brevity presents a serious objection, but these findings permit review and sustain the judgment. This conclusion requires neither a numerical safe harbor nor rejection of Valdespino. Voinovich supplies the distinction between a State's choices and a federally compelled district; De Grandy supplies the need for proof beyond the necessary conditions.

In Part II, O'Connor also accepts the lower court's contextual totality determination as a sufficient basis for rejecting the requested additional district. That is an assessment of the actual opportunity shown in this record, not a conclusive defense based on statewide proportionality. Kennedy does not join this further account; his agreement is confined to the independent practical-effectiveness and geographic grounds in Part I.

**Thomas, concurring in the judgment, joined by Scalia.** Consistent with their positions in Holder v. Hall and De Grandy, they conclude that Section 2 does not authorize the asserted judicial allocation of districts to remedy this form of vote dilution. That antecedent statutory ground is sufficient to affirm. They accept the court's authority to replace an unconstitutionally malapportioned plan, but they do not join either capacity route or a finding that these plaintiffs failed the governing dilution framework. The actual electoral-performance evidence does not answer their statutory objection.

**Souter, dissenting, joined by Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer.** Part I expressly accepts the existing capacity rule and independent-ground limits stated in Stone-Zsela's Part I. Part II would vacate only the congressional rejection of the appealed additional Latino-opportunity arrangement and remand for application of that rule with sufficiently identified findings. The appeal affirmatively presents performance evidence, comparative geography and an objection to the adequacy of the findings. Its majority-registration framing does not eliminate the need to determine what the court decided about that evidence under current law.

The lower opinion links its conclusions to a majority-citizenship requirement and does not sufficiently explain why the proposed arrangement fails independently. The appellees' account of marginality and geography could support a lawful judgment, but cannot substitute for identifiable trial findings. Four selected victories do not prove dependable capacity, and a compactness score does not prove legal compactness; neither can be treated as absent. Plan 1099C testimony and Plan 1123C data must remain distinct. De Grandy requires an independent totality judgment, not repetition of a disputed capacity conclusion. The separate finding about cohesion between protected groups does not dispose of single-group outside support. The proposed remand would preserve supported findings and plaintiffs' burden, require explanation of any sufficient independent ground, and permit supplementation only under ordinary procedural rules. It would neither mandate an additional district nor reopen the entire trial or disturb the 2002 election under the operative plan.

The November 14, 2001 replacement-map judgment remains in force; Plan 1151C governs unless lawfully modified, and the 2002 election under it remains undisturbed. There is no congressional remand or release condition. Separate state legislative judgments remain outside this action.

## Event

Carey v. Saffold, No. 01-301. Argued February 27, 2002; decided June 17, 2002. On certiorari to the Ninth Circuit's reversal of a federal habeas limitations dismissal. The questions concern intervals between state collateral-review filings, California's original-petition procedure, and whether alternative merits language establishes timely filing. Saffold pursued successive California court levels; roughly four and one-half months separated the Court of Appeal denial from his state supreme court filing. The latter denial referred to both merits and lack of diligence. The warden seeks enforcement of the federal limitation; Saffold seeks the qualifying interval exclusion.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated in submission and decision. No Justice is recused.

## Public Action

The Ninth Circuit judgment is vacated and the case remanded for the state-timing determination and federal calculation under the timely-continuation rule.

**Disposition:** Vacated and remanded, 6–3.

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, who would reverse the California interval-exclusion ruling and remand without that exclusion.

## Holdings

### Timely ordinary state review includes its intervals

**Holding and operative rule:** Under 28 U.S.C. §2244(d)(2), a properly filed state collateral application remains pending during timely continuation through the State's ordinary review process, including the interval before a timely filing at the next available level. California's successive-level original petitions qualify when they perform that review function; an abandoned or completed process, an unreasonable gap or an unrelated later attack does not receive interval exclusion merely because another collateral petition is possible.

**Authority:** Ginsburg's opinion of the Court, joined by the other five members of the six-Justice coalition identified in Opinion Topology; all six adopt both holdings.

**Controlling explanation:** Pending describes a review process awaiting completion, not only a paper physically awaiting one judge's order. A contrary construction would consume the federal period during steps ordinarily needed to complete state review and encourage premature federal filings. California's original-petition terminology does not change that practical function. The dissent correctly insists that a properly filed application, not a merely possible future claim, is required. The Court's rule retains that requirement and confines interval protection to timely continuation of the ordinary course; extraordinary or unrelated filings do not qualify automatically. Artuz distinguishes filing conditions from claim-relief bars and leaves pendency separate. Duncan requires state rather than federal review. Boerckel explains the ordinary state-review round, without making every extraordinary remedy part of it.

**Precedent treatment:** Artuz and Duncan are applied without alteration; Boerckel supplies ordinary-review context. The previously open interval question is resolved at this functional, timely-continuation scope.

### State timeliness and the actual federal calculation remain necessary

**Holding and operative rule:** An alternative merits discussion does not establish timely state filing or override a valid independent state determination that the relevant interval was unreasonable. The Ninth Circuit must determine the California order's actual meaning, the applicable state timing standard, the supported reasons for this interval and the exact elapsed time, then recalculate the federal limitation without excluding any interval that fails the governing requirements.

**Authority:** The same six-Justice Court opinion adopts this rule and bounded remand; Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas also reject a conclusive merits-language inference but do not join the Court's functional-pendency rule or resulting mandate.

**Controlling explanation:** The California Supreme Court referred both to the merits and lack of diligence. Its merits phrase therefore cannot alone resolve whether the four-and-one-half-month interval complied with California law. A clear state determination of untimeliness defeats interval exclusion even if merits were also considered. Where the order's meaning is uncertain, the federal court must identify the actual state ground and relevant timing circumstances; certification or another lawful clarification may be used if needed, but is not automatically required. Neither a fixed nationwide deadline nor an assumption that this interval is necessarily reasonable or necessarily late supplies the answer. Proper filing, pendency and reasonable state timing remain distinct. Artuz does not turn an actual filing deadline into a relief bar. Duncan forecloses counting federal habeas pendency as state review and preserves independently available commencement or equitable grounds only on their own requirements. Unpresented equitable tolling and the conviction's constitutional merits are outside this decision.

**Precedent treatment:** Artuz's filing-deadline-exception reservation remains; Duncan's independent timing rules remain. Lee's adequate-state-ground inquiry and Young's bankruptcy tolling do not determine statutory pendency here.

## Separate Writings

**Kennedy, dissenting, joined by Scalia and Thomas.** Section 2244(d)(2) protects a pending application. Once a California original application is finally denied, a later petition initiating another original proceeding has not yet been filed during the gap. Artuz's application-level distinction and Duncan's attention to statutory categories support that reading. An actual timely appeal may continue disposition of the original application; California's later original petition is different. Functional resemblance and the policy favoring exhaustion cannot make a future application already pending. The dissent accepts that the State's selected procedure may consequently affect the federal calculation but regards that consequence as the enacted distinction. It agrees that an alternative merits ruling does not establish timely filing. It would reverse the interval-exclusion rule and return for the actual calculation, preserving independently available timing grounds on their own predicates.

## Holdings

### A preserved nonretroactivity defense must be resolved before granting relief

**Holding and operative rule:** A federal habeas court may not grant a writ while leaving unresolved a properly preserved, applicable Teague defense capable of defeating relief. Section 2254(d) adds a condition to relief; satisfaction of that condition does not remove nonretroactivity review merely because the state court did not discuss it, and the requirement to justify a grant does not impose an inflexible sequence before every denial.

**Authority:** The Court's per curiam opinion, joined by all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Caspari requires adjudication of the preserved defense. Williams gives §2254(d) its independent inquiry into the state merits decision; Teague instead compares the precise rule sought with law dictated when the conviction became final. A later ruling may articulate an old rule, but that determination must actually be made. Pennsylvania raised the defense in both federal courts. The Third Circuit therefore could not treat its favorable §2254(d) conclusion as a complete basis for sentence relief.

On remand the court must fix finality, identify the rule claimed and determine whether prefinality precedent dictated it. A later decision's date, different facts or judicial disagreement alone does not establish newness. If the rule is new, the court must consider any applicable exception: rules withdrawing conduct or a class of persons or punishments from the State's substantive power, and watershed procedures essential to accuracy and fundamental fairness. Both exceptions remain. The Court decides neither Mills's newness nor either exception's application because the reviewed error is the omission of that analysis.

Schiro preserves the nonjurisdictional character and ordinary preservation limits of the defense; no forfeited defense is raised here. Lambrix permits denial on another independently sufficient ground without an inflexible priority between nonretroactivity and default. Tyler's distinct successive-application requirement is not imported into this case. The instructional merits and other genuinely remaining habeas requirements must be addressed only as necessary after the preserved defense receives its proper legal effect.

**Precedent treatment:** Caspari and Williams are applied to distinct conditions for relief; Schiro and Lambrix retain their preservation and sequencing limits; Tyler's separate statutory gate is distinguished.

## Event

Leventhal v. Knapek; review of Second Circuit No. 00-9306, 266 F.3d 64. Merits decision, June 17, 2002, on the developed summary-judgment record. Independent demotion and salary claims are outside the review.

## Holdings

### I. Limited administrative access does not erase workplace privacy

**Holding and operative rule:** A public employee retains a reasonable expectation of privacy in an individually used office computer where actual access practices do not expose its contents generally. Government ownership, occasional maintenance or document access, and a rule against personal business use do not alone waive that privacy or authorize unrestricted contents searches.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's Part I, joined by all eight other Justices; nine direct votes at this record-specific scope.

**Controlling explanation:** Leventhal used a private office and an individually assigned computer. Occasional technical access and the prohibition on private business did not establish a general practice of inspecting stored contents. Bond distinguishes limited exposure from consent to additional information acquisition; Guest likewise separates retained information privacy from a lawfully justified intrusion. Neither case answers the employer-search question merely by recognizing coverage. The Court does not adopt an absolute privacy rule for every workplace computer or treat government ownership as consent.

**Precedent treatment:** Bond and Guest are applied to the distinction between privacy coverage and justified acquisition, without extending their separate factual holdings to every workplace.

### II. A work-related investigation requires justification at inception and proportionate scope

**Holding and operative rule:** A public employer investigating work-related misconduct may inspect a protected workplace area without a warrant when reasonable grounds support the inquiry at inception and its measures remain reasonably related to that employment objective and not excessively intrusive in light of the suspected misconduct. Each follow-up examination requires its own justification from information lawfully obtained and the continuing work purpose; later discoveries cannot retroactively justify the first intrusion.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's Part II, joined by the other six full-opinion Justices; seven direct votes. Scalia does not join this general framework and Stevens does not approve the searches on the merits.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court directly adopts Ortega's inception-and-scope framework for the presented internal misconduct inquiry; it does not describe the divided Ortega opinions as a preexisting unified rationale. The standard accommodates legitimate supervision while preserving information privacy. An anonymous complaint is neither automatically sufficient nor automatically irrelevant. Its actual specificity, connection to work and the proposed intrusion matter. Robinette forbids using later discoveries to create earlier authority, while Arvizu requires genuine contextual force rather than automatic weight for every observation. Ordinary police collection, general monitoring and unrelated personal contents are not authorized by this employer-specific holding.

**Precedent treatment:** Ortega's inception-and-scope approach receives seven direct votes here; Robinette and Arvizu retain their independent-justification requirements. Knights supplies no employee-consent fiction.

### III. These four inspections were reasonable

**Holding and operative rule:** The October 25, 1996 file-name inventory and the three limited February and April 1997 examinations of selected program directories and files were reasonably initiated and confined to the actual employment-misuse inquiry. The reviewed individual-capacity Fourth Amendment dismissal is therefore affirmed; the established search record does not include examination of personal tax-return contents.

## Event

United States v. Drayton, No. 01-631. Argued April 16, 2002; merits decision June 17, 2002, on certiorari to the Eleventh Circuit's reversal of suppression denials. The accepted question is whether the February 4, 1999 coordinated drug-and-weapons encounter aboard the bus effected a seizure before the searches. Three officers boarded after the driver checked tickets and left: one observed from the driver's area while two proceeded from the rear. Brown agreed to a bag search and patdown and was arrested; Drayton then underwent a patdown. Brown entered a conditional plea; Drayton was convicted after trial. Independent consent voluntariness and a Terry alternative are outside the question reviewed.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated in submission and decision. No Justice is recused.

## Public Action

The Eleventh Circuit's seizure-based suppression judgment is reversed and the case remanded for lawful further proceedings consistent with the no-seizure determination.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Disposition:** Reversed and remanded, 5–4, for both respondents on the seizure-based suppression issue.

**Supporting:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg, who would preserve suppression as the product of an unsupported seizure.

The taint-based suppression direction is set aside. Further proceedings must respect Drayton's trial conviction, Brown's conditional plea and any separately preserved issue outside the accepted seizure question. ## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | O'Connor | Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Breyer join throughout; Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg join Part I | Reverses and remands | I: objective contextual seizure framework; II: no-seizure application and bounded mandate |
| Dissent | Souter | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Ginsburg | Would affirm seizure-based suppression | Joins governing framework in Part I; rejects application to both respondents and preserves immediate-taint consequence |

## Holdings

### I. Objective freedom to decline governs the bus encounter

**Holding and operative rule:** Police questioning aboard a bus is a seizure only when, considering the whole objectively communicated encounter, a reasonable passenger would not feel free to decline the requests or terminate the interaction. Constraints arising from the passenger's decision to travel are not themselves police restraint; absence of a refusal warning is relevant but neither necessary nor sufficient to establish seizure.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Part I, joined by all eight other Justices; nine direct votes for the governing framework, with a divided application.

**Controlling explanation:** Bostick adjusts the ordinary freedom-to-leave inquiry to a passenger whose own travel choice already limits movement. The inquiry concerns police-created restraint, not whether a person could conveniently abandon the trip. Robinette rejects an indispensable warning while retaining examination of actual communicated authority. Physical obstruction is not necessary to a seizure, and quiet requests do not prove its absence. Number, position, words and conduct must be considered together. An officer's undisclosed willingness to accept refusal is not an objective assurance, just as an undisclosed coercive purpose would not establish detention. Neither the bus setting nor one isolated circumstance controls.

**Precedent treatment:** Bostick and Robinette are applied without creating a mandatory warning or categorical bus rule; Bond's protection against additional physical intrusion remains distinct.

### II. Neither respondent was seized before the search

**Holding and operative rule:** The coordinated encounter did not objectively compel either Brown or Drayton to cooperate before the challenged searches: the officers used quiet requests, concealed their weapons, left the aisle passable, gave no command to remain and exercised no control over the passengers' tickets. Brown's arrest was relevant to the later request to Drayton but did not, in the circumstances of that request, itself detain him; the seizure-based suppression judgment is reversed.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Part II, joined by the other four Court-application Justices identified in Opinion Topology; five direct votes. Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg dissent from this application and disposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The officer observing from the driver's area and the colleagues approaching from the rear presented coordinated authority. The Court considers that combined arrangement rather than tallying isolated absences of coercion. It nevertheless conveyed requests capable of refusal: the observer did not block passage, the questioning officer spoke conversationally, weapons remained concealed and the officers did not direct passengers to stay or surrender tickets. The driver, not the police, handled the tickets. An announced drug-and-weapons purpose did not transform those requests into commands.

Brown's arrest after his patdown changed the setting for Drayton, but neither the arrest nor the other officers' positions communicated that Drayton was detained. Cooperation remains a factual circumstance, not a substitute for the objective legal judgment. The Court does not infer freedom from an officer's uncommunicated intent or use the discovered drugs to justify earlier conduct.

The accepted question concerns seizure. Finding no seizure removes the antecedent unlawful-detention ground for suppression but does not decide independent consent voluntariness or an unpresented Terry justification. Arvizu preserves that separation of stop and search questions. The respondents' distinct conviction postures and any independently preserved issues remain for lawful further proceedings.

**Precedent treatment:** Bostick's contextual rule is applied to each respondent; Arvizu preserves the separate consent inquiry. Knights supplies no advance passenger authorization or suspicion for this encounter.

## Separate Writings

**Souter, dissenting, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens and Ginsburg.** They accept Bostick's objective totality inquiry and reject any indispensable refusal warning. They disagree about the combined message. An officer facing passengers from the front, two colleagues moving from the rear, close requests to seated passengers and announcement of an interdiction operation communicated expected submission before travel resumed. ## Event

The district court dismissed the remaining Section 922(g)(8) possession count on Second and Fifth Amendment grounds; the Fifth Circuit reversed and returned the prosecution. The review concerns individual arms coverage, the absence of an express credible-threat finding, the constitutional application of the complete possession disability, and the independent fair-warning ground.

The charged predicate is the September 14, 1998 injunction following a September 4 evidentiary hearing, not the August 28 ex parte order. The sole remaining count alleges possession of a Beretta pistol on November 16 while the injunction remained operative. The four other counts had been dismissed on the Government's motion. ## Participation

## Public Action

The Fifth Circuit judgment reversing dismissal of the remaining count is affirmed, with the constitutional and statutory limits stated in the Court's opinion. The prosecution returns for ordinary proceedings.

## Holdings

### I. The right belongs to the individual

**Holding and operative rule:** The Second Amendment protects an individual's keeping of an ordinary pistol independently of enrollment in an organized militia. That coverage remains subject to a restriction justified under the governing burden-sensitive framework; a civil protective order does not itself remove the individual from the guarantee.

**Authority:** Souter's Part I, joined by all eight other Justices; nine direct votes.

**Controlling explanation:** Love establishes the individual holder and Hale separates coverage from justified regulation. Wright directs courts to examine the practical burden, and Napier applies that framework to a civil protective-order disability. Those decisions resolve coverage here. The Court therefore applies the existing personal right without adopting a new collective-right account or treating recognition of coverage as a complete defense to regulation.

**Precedent treatment:** Love, Hale, Wright and Napier are applied to personal coverage; none is displaced.

### II. The statute preserves alternative order predicates

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 922(g)(8) requires an order issued after a hearing of which the person received actual notice and at which the person had an opportunity to participate; the order must restrain harassment, stalking or threats against that person's intimate partner or a child of either person, or other conduct placing the partner in reasonable fear of bodily injury to the partner or child. The order must also either find a credible threat to the partner's or child's physical safety, or expressly prohibit using, attempting or threatening physical force against the partner or child reasonably expected to cause bodily injury. The express finding in subsection (C)(i) is not an element of the alternative in (C)(ii); statutory satisfaction remains distinct from the constitutionality of the resulting federal disability.

**Authority:** Souter's Part II, joined by all eight other Justices; nine direct votes.

**Controlling explanation:** Congress made (A) and (B) cumulative conditions and (C)(i) and (C)(ii) alternatives. An interpretation requiring both alternatives would alter that structure. The September 14 order followed notice and an opportunity to participate and prohibited bodily injury and threats of imminent bodily injury. The August ex parte order is not the charged predicate. Napier rejects an indispensable criminal-conviction condition but does not authorize every civil-order application. The absence of the statutory danger formula thus supplies neither a statutory defense under (C)(ii) nor categorical constitutional invalidity. The federal commerce element and the knowledge requirement retain their separate operation.

**Precedent treatment:** Napier is applied to the alternative statutory predicates and absence of a categorical criminal-conviction requirement.

### III. This protective adjudication supports the possession disability

**Holding and operative rule:** A force-prohibiting domestic-relations order entered after actual notice and meaningful opportunity to contest specific violent-threat evidence may supply a constitutionally sufficient civil danger predicate when the applicable law requires a real, nonspeculative protective necessity, the order addresses that threatened injury, and the federal disability depends on the operative order. On this record the restriction is a substantial regulation under Wright and satisfies intermediate scrutiny: it serves the important objective of preventing armed domestic injury, substantially relates to that objective, and does not unnecessarily or effectively eliminate protected activity beyond the justified individual disqualification.

**Authority:** Souter's Part III, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer; these six Justices adopt the complete rule, application and affirmance. Stone-Zsela, Scalia and Thomas do not join.

**Controlling explanation:** Napier upheld a civil violence-and-recurrence predicate. Its principle extends here to an actual protective adjudication of threatened injury; completed assault and a verbal danger formula are unnecessary. The hearing included testimony that Emerson threatened to kill his wife's friend and communicated that threat to her. It did not establish that he threatened to kill her. The resulting injury restraints were entered under Texas law requiring actual protective necessity. That combined legal and procedural basis supplies the predicate, without inventing an express finding or treating unrebutted testimony as a criminal admission.

Preventing armed escalation is an important objective. A command not to injure leaves access to firearms intact, while a public-carry restriction would not address private domestic encounters. Intermediate scrutiny requires neither inevitable violence nor earlier gun use. The specific protective adjudication and continuing order dependence limit the disability; a safety label, temporary duration or the availability of guns to other people would not suffice. Modification and mandamus review were available, although direct interlocutory appeal was not. The order lasted until the divorce decree or further order; no fixed short duration or guaranteed restoration is assumed.

The strongest objection identifies the difference between authority to enjoin threats and justification for every-possession disarmament. The actual threat proceeding, protective legal standard and operative bodily-injury restraints provide that link here. Constitutional review of the additional federal burden remains distinct from routine collateral retrial of the divorce case. Later unadjudicated allegations supply no justification. Wright's prohibition on general abolition of meaningful ordinary defensive home possession remains; it does not forbid this justified individual protective disqualification.

**Precedent treatment:** Napier is extended from adjudicated violence and recurrence to the defined threat-order predicate; Wright's intermediate-scrutiny requirements and categorical home boundary remain intact. Fraternal Order of Police's official-use holding is not made a rule for every private-possession case.

### IV. The asserted ignorance-of-law ground does not support dismissal

**Holding and operative rule:** The Fifth Amendment does not require dismissal of this possession charge for lack of fair warning where the defendant previously signed a federal form specifically identifying the restraining-order disability, later knew of the operative order and its contents, and allegedly continued possessing the firearm more than sixty days after the order. The earlier warning is a concrete circumstance prompting inquiry, not conclusive proof of later recollection or a substitute for the Government's proof of every enacted offense element.

**Authority:** Souter's Part IV is joined by all seven other Associate Justices; eight direct votes. Stone-Zsela agrees with reversal of this dismissal ground in the judgment only and adopts no additional ground.

**Controlling explanation:** Lambert addressed wholly passive conduct without knowledge or circumstances prompting inquiry. The signed firearm-purchase form supplied a particular warning here, although it preceded the injunction and Emerson's answer was correct when made. His later knowledge of the order and its contents distinguishes the asserted blanket ignorance-of-law theory from Lambert. Staples separates knowledge of regulated facts from general awareness of illegality. Bryan construes a distinct willfulness requirement and does not rewrite this possession offense. The divorce judge's failure to provide an additional warning is not constitutionally dispositive on these facts. This pretrial ruling does not establish guilt or dispense with factual knowledge required at trial.

**Precedent treatment:** Lambert is distinguished by the specific warning and known operative order; Staples and Bryan retain their offense-specific mental-state rules.

## Law After Decision

Section 922(g)(8)'s force-prohibition alternative can rest on the defined threat-order adjudication without an express credible-threat sentence or completed assault. The six-Justice holding extends Napier at that scope; it does not validate every divorce injunction or replace Wright with general reasonableness. The operative hearing, actual protective legal standard, targeted injury restraint, federal burden and continuing order dependence remain material. Ordinary collateral relitigation and constitutional review of the additional federal restriction remain distinct. Statutory satisfaction does not conclusively establish constitutionality, and rejection of the present pretrial defenses does not establish criminal guilt.

## Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** The Chief Justice joins individual coverage and the statutory alternatives. An actual civil danger adjudication may suffice without a criminal conviction or a particular formula, but complete private-possession disarmament burdens the core of the right. He would overrule Napier's intermediate-scrutiny classification to the extent it avoids strict scrutiny for that complete deprivation. Wright, in his view, requires a compelling protective interest, narrow tailoring and no comparably effective, materially less restrictive alternative.

Preventing lethal domestic violence is compelling. The hearing, specific threat, bodily-injury restraint and Texas protective standard matter, but do not conclusively establish the fit of every-possession prohibition. He would return the constitutional application for assessment of actual individualized risk, duration, review mechanisms and supported narrower conduct or access restrictions. Such alternatives may be inadequate if continued firearm access preserves the danger; that must be assessed rather than assumed. Existing materials may suffice, with additional proceedings only if needed. If the Government makes the required showing the prosecution may proceed subject to all elements; otherwise the unconstitutional application must be dismissed. The remand would not automatically retry the divorce dispute. He agrees in the judgment rejecting the independent Fifth Amendment dismissal ground without joining its reasoning.

**Scalia, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by Thomas.** They join coverage, the statutory alternatives and the fair-warning holding. Their Napier position requires actual justification of the full possession burden even where a civil order lawfully restrains violence. The hearing and Texas protective standard support a serious affirmance argument, but do not themselves complete that federal constitutional inquiry. They would vacate the arms application for restriction-specific review under Wright and Napier, considering the actual protective basis and operation of the entire disability. They do not require completed violence, a criminal conviction or the (C)(i) words, and do not adopt a rule that every individual restriction requires strict scrutiny. Nor do they find the hearing defective, disbelieve the wife or require automatic additional evidence. Ordinary further proceedings are permitted as needed; their proposed return is not immediate dismissal, acquittal or firearm restoration.

The four dismissed counts remain dismissed. No new civil hearing or automatic retrial of the divorce proceeding is required. ## Event

United States v. Fior D'Italia, Inc., No. 01-463. Argued April 22, 2002; decided June 17, 2002. On certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, which affirmed a restaurant's refund judgment against an aggregate assessment of employer FICA tax on unreported tips for 1991 and 1992. The IRS extrapolated from reported credit-card tips, applied the rate to receipts and deducted reported tips. The restaurant disputed statutory authority while expressly leaving the factual assumptions and estimates undisputed for this litigation.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision. No Justice is recused.

## Public Action

Reversed and remanded, 6–3, for judgment consistent with aggregate-assessment authority and the parties' actual stipulation.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Disposition:** Reversed and remanded, 6–3, for judgment consistent with aggregate-assessment authority and the parties' actual stipulation.

## Holdings

### Reasonable aggregate estimation may assess the employer's independent tax

**Holding and operative rule:** Sections 3111, 3121(q) and 6201 permit the IRS to assess an employer's own FICA liability through a reasonable aggregate estimate of taxable tip wages without first determining or assessing every employee's liability. Estimation implements the existing tax; it does not enlarge the statutory wage base.

**Authority:** Breyer's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg, controls for the coalition stated in Opinion Topology.

**Controlling explanation:** The employer owes tax because qualifying tips count as its remuneration, not because the government first assesses or collects an employee's tax. Section 6201's general assessment power includes reasonable estimation of that obligation. The references to an employee in the wage definition retain their substantive work but do not add a sequence of individual assessments before assessment of the employer. The dissent rightly insists that recipient-specific limits matter; it does not follow that those limits forbid every aggregate method of determining the lawful total. Section 446's particular accounting authority does not negate general assessment power elsewhere. Section 6205's regulated interest-free correction mechanism is distinct and does not require an authorizing regulation before this assessment. For subtitle F, Section 3121(q) treats tips omitted from an employee's statement, including an inaccurate or incomplete statement, as paid when the Secretary makes notice and demand on the employer. Penalties and interest therefore do not arise merely from the earlier underreporting; the employer must subsequently fail to pay the demanded amount timely. Initial employer reporting based on employee statements does not immunize qualifying omitted tips from later lawful assessment. Administrative convenience confirms the statutory construction but cannot supply a new taxable category.

**Precedent treatment:** Cleveland Indians separates tax computation, timing and benefit accounting; United Dominion applies the computation actually supplied by the governing scheme. Neither adds an employee-assessment prerequisite here.

### The estimate remains bounded by taxable wages and the actual litigation stipulation

**Holding and operative rule:** Reasonable aggregate estimation must retain the exclusion of noncash tips, the exclusion of cash tips below $20 for an employee in a calendar month, and the annual wage bases applicable to each tax component and employee for 1991 and 1992. A taxpayer may ordinarily challenge an estimate's reasonable factual foundation, but this restaurant's express stipulation forecloses an accuracy challenge in this case while preserving its unsuccessful statutory-authority argument.

**Authority:** Breyer's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg, controls for the coalition stated in Opinion Topology.

**Controlling explanation:** A tax estimate must approximate the liability Congress imposed, not simply treat all receipts or tips as taxable wages. The later removal of Medicare's wage ceiling and the later-enacted tip credit do not govern these tax years. Supported differences between cash and credit-card tipping, below-threshold tips, annual ceilings, cash returned through credit-card transactions or deducted card charges can show a method likely to overstate liability. A taxpayer need not reconstruct every exact tip merely to expose an unreasonable foundation. None of those possible defects is a proved error here: the restaurant deliberately limited the litigation to legal power and conceded the underlying estimates and assumptions. That concession does not itself create statutory authority, but it prevents converting hypothetical inaccuracies into a factual recalculation. The Court therefore rejects the categorical legal bar and applies the actual stipulation governing the refund and counterclaim. General concern about reporting-program pressure establishes no improper motive in this assessment.

**Precedent treatment:** Brockamp's distinct refund time and amount limits remain unchanged; permission to estimate neither expands refund jurisdiction nor suspends those limits.

## Precedent Treatment

## Law After Decision

The IRS may reasonably estimate an employer's aggregate taxable tips without prior employee assessments. Each substantive exclusion, annual base and notice-and-demand timing rule remains enforceable. In a case presenting accuracy, the taxpayer may demonstrate that the method lacks a reasonable foundation. This case ends the categorical authority challenge on its stipulated factual submission.

## Separate Writings

**Souter, dissenting, joined by Scalia and Thomas.** Section 6201 authorizes assessment of the tax Congress imposed; it does not replace the employee-based taxable wages specified by Section 3121(q) with restaurant-wide receipts. The monthly exclusion, annual bases and notice-and-demand structure require determinations tied to the recipient before the employer's liability is computed from them. Estimates of particular employees' taxable wages remain possible. The dissent reserves whether a formal employee audit must precede the employer action; it does not adopt a collection or benefits-credit prerequisite. Accurate aggregate factual estimates do not resolve the disputed legal method, so the restaurant's stipulation does not concede authority. Cleveland Indians separates the applicable annual tax limits from other functions, while United Dominion's aggregation followed that different statutory scheme. The dissent would affirm the refund judgment on the employee-based determination ground, without finding numerical error or an abusive motive.

## Event

United States v. Hooper. Decided June 17, 2002, on full merits review of the Ninth Circuit judgment reported at 229 F.3d 818. After the husbands pleaded guilty to drug offenses, the spouses asserted community interests in property found to be criminal proceeds or purchased with those proceeds. The ancillary proceeding protected interests established to derive from legitimate funds. The question is whether the remaining after-arising community interests qualify under 21 U.S.C. § 853(n)(6)(A).

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at submission and decision. No Justice is recused.

## Public Action

Denial of the challenged third-party proceeds claims affirmed, 9–0; protection already granted to legitimate-property interests remains intact.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Disposition:** Denial of the challenged third-party proceeds claims affirmed, 9–0; protection already granted to legitimate-property interests remains intact.

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer.

The federal statutory ground sustains the judgment without endorsing the disputed state-law ownership ruling. The claimants' standing to assert their interests is preserved.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Thomas | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Complete statutory priority rule and the confined affirmance |

## Holdings

### The protected interest must exist at the statutory time

**Holding and operative rule:** Under Section 853(n)(6)(A), a third-party claimant must prove by a preponderance of the evidence a legal interest that invalidates forfeiture in whole or part because, at the time of the acts giving rise to forfeiture, it was vested in that claimant rather than the defendant or was superior to the defendant's interest. That timing requirement governs both alternatives; assuming the spouses' community ownership, interests arising only through receipt of the criminal proceeds satisfy neither here.

**Authority:** Thomas's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer, controls for the coalition stated in Opinion Topology.

**Controlling explanation:** State law identifies a claimed property interest; the federal forfeiture statute determines its protection and priority. Section 853(a)(1) expressly reaches directly or indirectly obtained criminal proceeds and property derived from them. Subsection (c) makes federal title relate back to commission of the acts giving rise to forfeiture once forfeiture is lawfully adjudicated, subject to the actual third-party protections; it does not bypass the ancillary proceeding. The spouses' asserted interests arise with the very proceeds the crimes produced. Immediate community vesting upon receipt cannot make those interests antecedent to the acts generating the property. The strongest contrary argument invokes innocent ownership, but innocence does not supply the statute's missing time or purchase predicate. Otherwise a later gratuitous transfer could defeat the carefully distinct purchaser protection. Bennis concerns a separately held lawful interest exposed to another's later misuse, not an interest generated with criminal proceeds. The court already separated legitimate assets, demonstrating why provenance matters item by item. The judgment can therefore stand on federal priority without deciding the disputed California title interpretation or treating marriage as guilt.

**Precedent treatment:** Bennis is distinguished by the lawful preexisting property; Buena Vista by its different civil owner defense; Lester by its untainted substitute property. Their protections retain their own predicates.

### The separate purchaser protection remains distinct and successful statutory proof requires relief

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 853(n)(6)(B) separately protects a claimant who proves by a preponderance of the evidence that the claimant was a bona fide purchaser for value of the relevant interest and, at purchase, was reasonably without cause to believe the property subject to forfeiture. Proof of either statutory route requires amendment of the forfeiture order to reflect the protected interest; innocence alone supplies neither route, and no purchaser-for-value claim is asserted here.

**Authority:** Thomas's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer, controls for the coalition stated in Opinion Topology.

**Controlling explanation:** The vested-or-superior route protects interests satisfying its temporal condition; the purchaser route protects a different class under its value and reasonable-notice conditions. Combining them into a general innocent-spouse exception would remove requirements Congress retained. Conversely, failure on these proceeds claims does not authorize taking every lawful asset a spouse owns. The protected legitimate-property interests remain intact. Buena Vista preserves meaningful adjudication of the defense available under the civil statute; it does not transfer that statute's donee protection into the criminal ancillary categories. Bollin requires authority for the actual property and order, which the proceeds provision supplies here. Farmer protects meaningful review of a supported legitimate-assets claim; these claimants used the statutory hearing and challenge the priority result. No procedural-due-process or purchaser theory is presented, so neither is adjudicated.

**Precedent treatment:** Buena Vista's civil eligibility and lawful-adjudication requirements are maintained. Bollin and Farmer preserve actual statutory authority and meaningful adjudication without creating a right to retain proved proceeds.

## Precedent Treatment

- United States v. 92 Buena Vista Avenue, February 24, 1993: its civil owner-defense eligibility and opportunity to prove title remain; the criminal statute has different third-party conditions.
- Bennis v. Michigan, March 4, 1996: protects the qualifying separately held lawful automobile interest when the owner proves nonparticipation, lack of knowledge and all reasonably expected prevention; the present proceeds are outside that category.
- United States v. Lester, 85 F.3d 1409 (9th Cir. 1996): its untainted substitute-property setting is distinguished from proceeds.
- United States v. Bollin, June 10, 2002: actual statutory authority remains necessary for the particular property and order; Section 853(a)(1) supplies proceeds authority here.
- United States v. Farmer, June 10, 2002: meaningful focused review of a supported legitimate-assets claim remains; it supplies no entitlement to proved proceeds.
- United States v. Craft, April 17, 2002: state property interests and federal consequences remain distinct; no general marital exemption follows.

## Law After Decision

An after-arising community interest in these proved proceeds fails the vested-or-superior protection at the statutory time. Antecedent qualifying interests and qualifying purchases for value retain their separate protections, and successful statutory proof requires amendment of forfeiture. Independently lawful spousal property remains outside this confined judgment.

No separate opinion is filed.

## Procedure After Action

The denial of the challenged proceeds claims stands. The relief already granted for property established to derive from legitimate funds remains in force. No further ancillary hearing is required by this disposition; the state-law ownership dispute is unnecessary to the federal ground.

The spouses asserted no purchaser-for-value or procedural-due-process claim and used the ancillary hearing procedure.

## Event

Atkins v. Virginia, No. 00-8452. Argued February 20, 2002; decided June 20, 2002. On certiorari to the Supreme Court of Virginia, which sustained a death sentence after a second penalty proceeding. The question is whether established intellectual disability categorically bars execution. The defense psychologist reported an IQ of 59 and diagnosed disability; the State expert disputed the diagnosis.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at submission and decision. No recusal or vacancy affects the case.

## Public Action

Death-sentence judgment reversed and remanded, 7–2; convictions remain intact.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Disposition:** Death-sentence judgment reversed and remanded, 7–2; convictions remain intact.

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer.

**Opposing:** Scalia, Thomas.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Breyer | Categorical restriction, limited overruling and remand |
| Dissent | Scalia | Thomas | Rejects categorical restriction and would affirm against this claim |

## Holdings

### Execution is unavailable for established intellectual disability

**Holding and operative rule:** The Eighth and Fourteenth Amendments prohibit execution of a person with established intellectual disability: significantly impaired intellectual functioning, substantial adaptive limitations and developmental onset. The State must provide a reliable procedure to determine that status; a score, diagnostic label or courtroom impression alone does not establish it.

**Authority:** Ginsburg's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Breyer: seven Justices adopt the entire rule, explanation and remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The most severe punishment requires culpability proportionate to its severity. Trop supplies the inquiry into developing standards; Coker and Enmund require attention to the punishment's relation to culpability and legitimate penal purposes. The sustained domestic legislative movement since Penry v. Lynaugh supplies objective support: eighteen of thirty-eight death-penalty States enacted restrictions, alongside twelve States without capital punishment. This evidence is directional, not eighteen identical statutes or a mechanical count. Eleven enactments have temporal limits; New York retains a correctional-custody murder exception; Kansas uses a distinct definition. A vetoed Texas bill is not an enactment, and reported low-IQ executions do not establish complete clinical diagnoses. Those qualifications do not erase the consistent movement or infrequency of executing the class. Clinical evidence explains diminished reasoning, judgment and ability to learn from experience, which weaken retributive and deterrent justifications. The same limitations impede assistance to counsel and reliable presentation and can make disability appear aggravating. Individual mitigation therefore does not fully answer the class-wide culpability and reliability problems. Polls, foreign practice and organizational views are unnecessary to this conclusion. Penry's earlier assessment of insufficient consensus no longer sustains categorical permission. Atkins's conflicting expert evidence requires application of the constitutional rule through reliable individual factfinding. The Court establishes no numerical cutoff, proof burden or universal hearing design; those details are not resolved on this disputed record.

**Precedent treatment:** Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), is overruled only insofar as it permits execution of persons with established intellectual disability. Penry v. Johnson retains its requirement of effective mitigation where death remains lawful.

## Precedent Treatment

- Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86 (1958): supplies the inquiry into evolving standards, informed by objective domestic evidence.
- Coker v. Georgia, 433 U.S. 584 (1977), and Enmund v. Florida, 458 U.S. 782 (1982): supply categorical proportionality and the relation between culpability and penal purposes.
- Ford v. Wainwright, 477 U.S. 399 (1986): supports implementation of a constitutional death restriction through lawful state procedures; its distinct incompetence rule is not equated with intellectual disability.
- Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302 (1989): its categorical permission is overruled to the stated extent; its mitigation protection survives.
- Penry v. Johnson, June 4, 2001: effective consideration of mitigation remains required; that decision did not already establish today's categorical bar.

## Law After Decision

Established intellectual disability removes death as a lawful punishment. Conviction and lawful noncapital punishment remain available. Reliable implementation must evaluate intellectual functioning, adaptive limitations and developmental onset together. Detailed proof allocation, diagnostic procedure, retroactivity in another collateral case and other disability categories are outside the present resolution.

## Separate Writings

**Scalia, dissenting, joined by Thomas.** The domestic record does not establish the objective constitutional rejection required to overturn Penry. Fewer than half of capital-punishment States adopted the asserted rule, several enactments are recent or prospective, and their exceptions and definitions matter. States without capital punishment do not independently decide which offenders may be executed. The majority's independent penal judgment replaces legislative choice and juries' individual assessment; serious crimes and variation within the clinical class weaken a categorical culpability conclusion. Polling, foreign practice and professional submissions neither establish the controlling domestic legal standard nor provide a tested substitute. The dissent would preserve individualized mitigation and affirm against this categorical challenge; it does not resolve the contested diagnosis.

The case returns to Virginia for reliable individualized determination under the categorical rule. If intellectual disability is established, a lawful noncapital disposition must follow. The convictions remain intact.

## Holdings

### A retrospective access claim must identify a concrete lost judicial opportunity

**Holding and operative rule:** A claim seeking relief for past obstruction of court access must identify a nonfrivolous underlying judicial action, intentional obstruction causing loss or material impairment of the opportunity to pursue it, and presently available relief for that loss that cannot be obtained through surviving litigation. Ordinary notice pleading requires identification of these substantive predicates, not proof of certain success or a futile prior filing.

**Controlling explanation:** Lewis v. Casey ties access to actual injury to a judicial opportunity; the same need applies when the alleged obstruction has already ended. Without an identifiable underlying action and causally lost relief, a court cannot distinguish a denied legal opportunity from an additional description of the underlying wrong. Harbury's complaint failed to identify the lost cause of action. Swierkiewicz preserves ordinary notice pleading and rejects a requirement to plead evidentiary proof; it does not remove the substantive elements of the claim. The grave allegations must receive their proper procedural assumption, but their gravity cannot identify a missing cause or remedy. The asserted constitutional category and a Bivens damages channel are assumed only to resolve these defects, so their source and availability need not be decided. The actual truth of the allegations, official immunity, foreign-affairs defenses and the surviving torts' elements likewise are not necessary to this disposition.

**Precedent treatment:** Lewis v. Casey is extended from actual hindrance of current litigation to the necessary injury and remedial connection for an asserted lost past opportunity; Swierkiewicz retains ordinary notice pleading.

### The clarified theory also fails to identify distinct available relief

**Holding and operative rule:** Even treating counsel's intentional-infliction theory and lost emergency injunction as part of the pleaded case, this access count identifies no presently attainable remedy unavailable through the surviving tort action. That independently sufficient defect requires dismissal of the revived count.

**Authority:** The same eight Justices join this alternative holding; Thomas's judgment agreement supplies no additional rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** The requested emergency intervention could no longer save Harbury's husband when this suit was brought; that relief is unavailable under either theory. The identified intentional-infliction claim against the CIA defendants remains pleaded in counts 18 and 19. If it can be maintained, relief for its demonstrated consequences remains potentially available through that claim, and Harbury identifies no additional presently obtainable access remedy. This is an examination of the actual clarified cause and requested relief, not a presumption that all torts with similar labels compensate identical harm. If the predicate fails for a reason independent of the alleged deception, that failure cannot establish a viable underlying action for access purposes. Intentional deception can impede legal action, but it does not automatically create an additional damages channel for the same alleged consequences. Merely naming the same tort on amendment does not answer the independently adjudicated defect. Ordinary amendment rules remain applicable to any properly sought amendment on its actual allegations; the mandate orders none.

**Precedent treatment:** Lewis requires injury-fitted relief, not a duplicative access label. Malesko supplies no automatic new federal damages channel from an allegation of serious injury.

City of Columbus v. Ours Garage & Wrecker Service, Inc., No. 01-419. Argued April 23, 2002; decided June 20, 2002. On certiorari to the Sixth Circuit, which held the City's towing regulations categorically outside the federal safety exception because the exception names a State without separately naming political subdivisions. The Court decides whether preserved state safety authority may be delegated locally.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at submission and decision. No recusal or vacancy affects the case.

## Public Action

Reversed and remanded, 7–2, for consideration of authorized safety regulation.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Disposition:** Reversed and remanded, 7–2, for consideration of authorized safety regulation.

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer.

**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Kennedy | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Delegated safety authority and limited remand |
| Dissent | Scalia | O'Connor | Would affirm the State-only construction of the exception |

## Holdings

### A State may delegate its preserved motor-vehicle safety authority

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 14501(c)(2)(A) preserves a State's ability to authorize political subdivisions to exercise its safety regulatory authority with respect to motor vehicles. A local rule must actually fall within that delegated safety authority; an economic restriction does not qualify merely because the municipality calls it safety regulation.

**Authority:** Kennedy's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer, controls for seven Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** The general preemption clause expressly covers state and local laws relating to motor-carrier prices, routes or services. The safety exception preserves an identified state power. Mortier explains that a State ordinarily exercises retained regulatory power through authorized subdivisions; naming the State does not alone forbid that internal allocation. The contrary inference from Congress's separate references to subdivisions elsewhere is substantial but not conclusive: the exceptions preserve different subjects, and a preserved state authority is not naturally restricted to personal exercise by state officials. The statute's separate protection for vehicle size or weight and hazardous-cargo route controls, and for minimum financial responsibility or insurance, retains its distinct scope. These provisions do not transform all towing regulation into safety regulation. Economic deregulation remains effective because the court must examine what each ordinance actually regulates; Section 31141's federal supervisory process likewise remains operative. The Sixth Circuit stopped at categorical institutional exclusion and did not decide the necessary ordinance-specific questions. It must examine Ohio's authorization and the actual safety character of each challenged requirement, together with any independent federal conflict.

**Precedent treatment:** Wisconsin Public Intervenor v. Mortier, June 21, 1991, supplies the ordinary state-delegation principle; the federal statute still controls the scope of saved regulation and actual preemption.

## Precedent Treatment

### Necessary reasonable access includes a supported exception to a driveway rule

## Public Action

## Holdings

### The constitutional inquiry concerns a protected interest and the operation impairing it

**Holding and operative rule:** Racial consideration or predominance in a common territorial electorate does not itself establish a constitutional deprivation. The constitutional claims presented here must identify the governmental operation impairing a protected political interest and satisfy that claim's own elements; voters of every race receive the same protection, and Section 2 claims proceed independently on their statutory elements.

**Authority:** Part I of the signed opinion of the Court is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: five direct votes. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas do not join this part.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court reaffirms the boundary drawn by Shaw, Miller and Easley. A common territorial electorate places residents in the same geographical constituency without different legal eligibility because of individual race. Racial motivation explains why boundaries were chosen; the relevant operation identifies what those boundaries did to the protected interest. Composition, shape and predominance may supply evidence, but cannot replace proof of an actual exclusion, intentional dilution, materially unequal participation or other independently recognized wrong. Conversely, a shared electorate does not excuse a proved denial of equal access. Intentional dilution requires purpose, material impairment of protected electoral opportunity and causation. A participation claim requires an identified protected activity and concrete material impairment attributable to the challenged racial treatment or prohibited purpose; it does not require an already lost election. A material impairment is a practical restriction on the protected ability to participate or elect; total exclusion is unnecessary, while a technical change or disappointment about political leverage alone is insufficient. Those distinctions require separate attention to the actual dilution and chilling claims rather than allowing the lower court's nonpredominance reasoning to answer them. This case applies the existing boundary to those claims; it adopts no comprehensive new test for unpresented partisan or regional-separation theories.

**Precedent treatment:** Shaw v. Reno, Johnson v. Miller and Easley v. Cromartie: reaffirmed at their established territorial-design boundary and protection of independent injuries. The actual statutory inquiry remains governed by Section 2 and its own precedents.

### The statutory dilution claim fails on the relevant opposing-bloc evidence

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 2 does not require retention of a numerical-majority district where, on supported findings concerning the affected electorate and relevant nomination and election stages, challengers fail to establish opposing-bloc voting ordinarily sufficient to defeat the protected group's preferred candidates after considering relevant crossover support and special circumstances. Failure of that necessary condition disposes of this claim without deciding the first capacity condition or the remaining totality inquiry.

**Authority:** Part II of the signed opinion of the Court is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: seven direct votes. Scalia and Thomas agree only in the statutory judgment on their noncoverage ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that actual electoral opportunity, rather than a fixed racial percentage, governs the asserted loss. Gingles supplies the necessary voting conditions, and Voinovich permits disposition when the relevant opposing-bloc showing fails. Valdespino preserves both the numerical-majority route and rigorous proof of dependable crossover capacity, but neither route eliminates this separate requirement. Plaintiffs retain the ordinary preponderance burden: the required proposition must be more likely true than not. Valdespino's rigorous-support requirement concerns reliability, not a heightened proof standard or mandatory research protocol. The trial court credited an analysis of more than 150 elections, distinguished relevant contests and candidate preferences, and examined projected performance in each affected district. It also considered the Democratic nomination stage and evidence of African-American primary participation. Those findings support opportunity in both Districts 27 and 34 even without Hispanic support. The contrary expert evidence was received and rejected for identified methodological and contextual reasons; clear-error review does not authorize a new appellate credibility trial. The reduction from 52.8% to 27.5% Black voting-age population in District 27 therefore does not itself prove loss of opportunity. Possible gains elsewhere or the race of successful candidates would not answer that question, but the judgment rests on local preference, crossover and nomination evidence. De Grandy rejects both maximization and conclusive proportionality; it supplies no statewide offset for a local deprivation. No error in the independently sufficient findings requires remand. Distinct-group aggregation and influence-only entitlement remain undecided because neither is necessary after this condition fails.

**Precedent treatment:** Gingles and Voinovich: applied to the independently necessary opposing-bloc condition. Valdespino: both capacity routes remain intact without a new capacity determination. De Grandy: preserved against maximization, numerical shortcuts and statewide-offset reasoning. Balderas: its separate record and fractured application supply no substitute for these identified findings.

### The intentional statutory claim also fails on this record

**Holding and operative rule:** The separately pleaded intentional Section 2 claim fails because supported findings establish neither purposeful diminution of minority voting rights nor the actual impairment asserted. This ground is separate from the results claim and does not make intent necessary for a Section 2 results violation.

**Authority:** Part II of the signed opinion of the Court is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: seven direct votes. Scalia and Thomas agree only in the statutory judgment on their noncoverage ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court addresses Count II on its own terms. The trial court rejected the asserted purpose to diminish minority electoral opportunity and separately rejected the alleged loss and participation impairment. Those findings remain supported under clear-error review. Racial awareness and changing population shares do not themselves establish the pleaded purposeful diminution. The Court therefore affirms this statutory count without treating the results count as an intent claim or using lack of racial predominance as a required element.

**Precedent treatment:** The existing Section 2 results framework remains independent. Voinovich preserves claim-specific proof and does not make all statutory protection depend on purpose.

### The actual dilution and participation theories fail on supported findings

**Holding and operative rule:** Intentional dilution and materially unequal political participation require proof of the unlawful purpose or treatment, material impairment and causal connection belonging to the actual claim. The supported findings rejecting both loss of electoral opportunity and the separately alleged chilling of participation defeat these claims; the absence of established discriminatory purpose provides an additional ground.

**Authority:** The constitutional application is joined by all nine Justices: Part III addresses the Fourteenth Amendment and Part IV the Fifteenth Amendment. This complete agreement on the confined applications does not enlarge the five-Justice coalition for Part I.

Easley preserves independently asserted intentional dilution and unequal-participation claims under their own elements. The trial addressed these independent theories. Supported local electoral findings defeat the dilution allegation, and the court separately rejected the predicted chilling of African-American and Hispanic participation. That second finding matters: a preferred candidate's ability to win does not universally answer an obstruction of political access. The court also found no purpose to diminish minority voting rights. Arlington Heights and Feeney distinguish awareness of racial consequences from a prohibited purpose; Brooks requires the government, once prohibited purpose is established as a substantial or motivating factor, to prove by a preponderance of the evidence that materially the same decision would have occurred without that purpose. ## Holdings

### Political alignment and common requested relief do not bind these nonparties

**Holding and operative rule:** A prior judgment cannot bind different plaintiffs merely because they share political interests, request the same relief, know of the earlier case or could have sought intervention. **Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Page judgment does not bind these plaintiffs on the reasons the district court gave. Richards requires lawful authority to represent an absent person, and Hansberry preserves the due-process prerequisite to genuine representative adjudication. Shared political association, vigorous advocacy, adequate incentives and the public importance of a controversy do not establish that authority. Nor does describing the second suit as tactical create a general duty to intervene. The parties agreed to the use of Page testimony as evidence; they did not thereby agree to its inferences, authorize representation or consent to binding adjudication. The public character of map relief does not, without an actual authorized arrangement, turn ordinary litigation into a proceeding representing all interested voters. Devlin concerns persons already bound as members of a mandatory class and does not make these strangers members of one. Valid representative and special remedial arrangements remain possible, but none is supplied by the stated rationale. The independent merits ground permits disposition without an exploratory remand to construct a new preclusion relationship.

**Precedent treatment:** Richards and Hansberry: applied to the necessary authority for binding absent claimants. Devlin: distinguished because its appellants were already bound mandatory-class members, not political allies outside the litigation.

### The territorial-design count identifies no independent constitutional deprivation

**Holding and operative rule:** Predominantly racial design of a common territorial electorate does not alone establish a Fourteenth Amendment deprivation. Even giving these plaintiffs the favorable reasonable inferences required on summary judgment, Count I identifies no independently cognizable denial or abridgment; the judgment denying relief on that count therefore stands.

**Authority:** Parts II and III of the signed opinion of the Court are joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: five direct votes. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas dissent from the merits disposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the disputed weight of racial considerations is not material to the design-only claim under governing law. Shaw, Miller and Easley distinguish the motive for common district boundaries from an actual exclusion, dilution, unequal participation or other prohibited treatment. The Court assumes favorable reasonable inferences from Bartels's minority-incumbent protection commitment and does not decide that race could not have predominated merely because other criteria were also considered or the map had ordinary shape. Anderson v. Liberty Lobby requires those favorable inferences, but an inference about motive cannot supply a missing legal deprivation. The dissent would reconsider the governing boundary and treat predominant racial assignment itself as unequal treatment. The Court retains the distinction: membership in the resulting common electorate imposes no racial qualification or legally unequal civic status by itself. That conclusion requires neither a second governmental act nor electoral defeat or financial loss. The actual territorial theory also supplies no independent denial or abridgment for the pleaded Fifteenth Amendment theory; its broader dilution coverage need not be decided.

**Precedent treatment:** Shaw, Miller and Easley: applied without changing the territorial-design boundary or protections for actual independent wrongs. Anderson v. Liberty Lobby: applied to favorable reasonable inferences, with materiality determined by the governing substantive law. Voinovich: its broader Fifteenth Amendment coverage reservation remains intact.

### The actual incumbent-prospect claim concerns the same territorial operation

**Holding and operative rule:** A claim that district boundaries favor minority incumbents' reelection prospects does not become a separate racial allocation of candidacy or civic rights merely by describing those prospects as an incumbent benefit. Count II identifies the composition of the common electorate and its expected political support, without an independent eligibility restriction or participation impairment, and fails under the same constitutional boundary.

**Authority:** Parts II and III of the signed opinion of the Court are joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: five direct votes. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas dissent from the merits disposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the actual governmental operation, not the beneficiary label, determines this claim. The evidence of a commitment to protect minority incumbents receives its favorable inference; the Court neither approves every such preference nor excuses established racial discrimination because no incumbent owns a future term. Here the challenged benefit consists of the reelection prospects resulting from district lines. The record supplies no separate racial ballot qualification, restricted candidacy, office entitlement, official racial ballot label or independent participation barrier in Counts I or II. Anderson v. Martin concerns official racial electoral labeling and therefore addresses a different operation. Adarand supplies justification requirements for an actual covered racial allocation; it does not itself supply the missing deprivation here. Easley preserves review of unequal eligibility and materially unequal participation without demanding a changed winner, but those are not the actual basis of this count. The separate residency controversy in Count III cannot be borrowed to enlarge this appeal. Because the asserted benefit remains the same territorial composition, the disputed priority of motives does not require a trial under the governing rule.

**Precedent treatment:** Easley: applied to the actual operation and preserved independent injuries. Anderson v. Martin: distinguished from common geographical composition. Adarand: preserved for an actual covered racial allocation, without using its justification rule to create one.

## Precedent Treatment

- Richards v. Jefferson County: applied to lawful authority to bind absent claimants; political affinity, common objectives and failure to intervene supply no substitute. Its allowance for actual authorized representative and special remedial arrangements remains intact.
- Hansberry v. Lee: genuine representation must meet due process.
- Devlin v. Scardelletti (June 10, 2002): distinguished because its mandatory-class members were actually bound; it creates no representative relationship among these political allies.
- Shaw v. Reno, Johnson v. Miller and Easley v. Cromartie: the territorial-design boundary is reaffirmed for both the direct design claim and the actual electorate-based incumbent-prospect theory. Their protection of independently cognizable wrongs remains intact.
- Anderson v. Liberty Lobby: favorable reasonable inferences are preserved; materiality depends on the substantive cause.
- Anderson v. Martin: official racial electoral labeling differs from the common geographical electorate challenged here.
- Adarand Constructors, Inc. v. Peña: justification rules for an actual covered racial allocation remain effective and do not supply a missing deprivation.
- Voinovich v. Quilter: the broader Fifteenth Amendment dilution-coverage question remains reserved.

## Law After Decision

The nonparty rule remains grounded in actual authority to bind a person's rights. The constitutional territorial-design rule covers the actual incumbent-prospect theory when the claimed benefit consists of the same district composition; it does not immunize a different racial allocation of eligibility, civic status or participation. Both claims can be rejected while assuming favorable racial-motive inferences, without resolving the disputed relative weight of motives or endorsing every rationale below.

## Separate Writings

**Kennedy, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas.** They join rejection of the stated nonparty-preclusion ground but would vacate summary judgment on Counts I and II for consideration under their proposed predominant-racial-assignment rule. Following their Easley position, they regard governmental racial assignment as unequal treatment when race actually predominates, without requiring an additional electoral loss. Bartels's express minority-incumbent protection commitment permits a reasonable inference about racial priority. A regular map and the presence of other criteria do not conclusively defeat that inference on summary judgment. The proposed remand would assess the relative role of race and any required justification under ordinary procedure. It would not find predominance or a violation established, order new boundaries, or guarantee a trial. This opinion seeks reconsideration of the Court's territorial-design boundary; it does not describe that proposed rule as existing law.

## Procedure After Action

The direct appeal concludes in affirmance on Counts I and II. The requested redistricting injunction is denied. The Court orders no replacement plan, election change or new preclusion hearing. The separate residency count and proceeding are unaffected.

The evidentiary agreement allowed Page testimony to be used; it did not establish agreement with each inference or authorize representative adjudication. The lower opinion expressly declined to find that the litigation tactics were improper. Bartels's minority-incumbent protection commitment and his other districting criteria are both evidence, not findings that race did or did not predominate.

## rush-prudential-hmo-inc-v-moran-merits-2002-06-20

## Event

Rush Prudential HMO, Inc. v. Moran, No. 00-1021. Merits decision, June 20, 2002. Argued January 16, 2002. On writ of certiorari to the Seventh Circuit. The question is whether ERISA preempts Illinois's requirement of independent physician review of a disputed medical-necessity determination and provision of a covered service found necessary. Rush denied the out-of-network procedure recommended for Moran; she obtained it at her expense, and the independent reviewer found it necessary. Rush refused reimbursement. Following removal, her reimbursement claim proceeded as an ERISA benefits action. The Seventh Circuit reversed the district court's preemption ruling and entered judgment for Moran.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The judgment of the Seventh Circuit is affirmed.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

The benefits judgment remains in force through ERISA's existing enforcement channel, with the reviewer's medical-necessity determination given its statutory effect.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joins and scope |
|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Breyer; all parts |
| Dissent | Thomas | Scalia and Kennedy; actual remedial-conflict ground |

## Holdings

### Independent medical review regulates this insured coverage

**Holding and operative rule:** Illinois HMO Act §4-10 is saved from ERISA preemption as applied to this risk-bearing HMO: when the primary care physician and HMO disagree about the necessity of a covered service the physician proposes, it requires timely independent physician review and provision of the service if found medically necessary. The rule supplies an enforceable insured-coverage condition; the three insurance guideposts are not indispensable conjunctive requirements.

**Authority:** The signed opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, controls with six votes.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that furnishing medical care does not erase the insurance function of an HMO that assumes its members' medical-cost risk. Metropolitan Life permits States to regulate insurance-contract terms, and UNUM v. Ward applies that principle to a mandatory condition governing payment. Section 4-10 directs its command to insured HMO coverage and makes independent medical assessment part of the policy relationship. Those industry and policy-relationship features confirm the common-sense classification as insurance regulation; the Court need not decide whether independent review also independently transfers risk. The law therefore cannot be dismissed as ordinary regulation of health care merely because the insurer arranges treatment as well as paying for it. This holding concerns the insured arrangement before the Court and leaves the deemer clause's protection of self-funded plans intact.

**Precedent treatment:** Metropolitan Life Insurance Co. v. Massachusetts: applied to substantive insurance-contract regulation. UNUM Life Insurance Co. of America v. Ward: extended from notice-prejudice to this limited medical-necessity term, preserving its nonconjunctive guideposts and separate conflict inquiry.

### The saved medical determination remains enforceable through ERISA

**Holding and operative rule:** The binding physician determination under §4-10 is compatible with ERISA because it supplies the medical-necessity predicate for existing covered benefits while judicial enforcement remains under §502(a)(1)(B). It neither creates an independent state cause of action nor authorizes consequential or punitive damages, and ERISA does not guarantee that an insurer may preserve contractual medical discretion against an otherwise saved insurance condition.

**Authority:** The signed opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, controls with six votes.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a mandatory medical assessment can define what the insurer owes without creating a competing remedy. Pilot Life bars an alternative state enforcement scheme; Moran instead seeks the benefits promised by her insured coverage through ERISA's own action. The unaffiliated reviewer holds the same class of professional license as the primary care physician and resolves the limited medical-necessity question, rather than awarding additional relief or exercising general arbitral jurisdiction. Firestone governs review of discretion a plan actually possesses; it does not entitle an insurer to retain discretion that valid insurance regulation removes. UNUM likewise allows a saved policy condition to inform a federal benefits action while separately rejecting an administrative reassignment rule on its own ground. Rush's strongest objection—that binding review necessarily substitutes an outside adjudication for the federal scheme—thus overlooks the limited determination and unchanged remedy here. Great-West construes the distinct §502(a)(3) equitable channel and does not restrict the benefits action Moran invokes. The reviewer's favorable medical-necessity decision supports the existing benefits judgment. The Court does not decide an unraised proper-defendant objection, the proper initial vehicle for compelling review, or systems resolving different coverage questions. The authority of this reviewer does not settle a separate experimental-treatment exclusion or a challenge to biased or inaccurate review, which are outside the presented dispute.

**Precedent treatment:** Pilot Life Insurance Co. v. Dedeaux: distinguished because no competing state remedy is supplied. Firestone Tire & Rubber Co. v. Bruch: applied to actual contractual discretion, without converting it into immunity from insurance regulation. UNUM: applied to compatible federal enforcement of saved coverage terms. Great-West Life & Annuity Insurance Co. v. Knudson: distinguished as addressing §502(a)(3), not §502(a)(1)(B).

## Precedent Treatment

- Metropolitan Life Insurance Co. v. Massachusetts: substantive insurance-contract regulation remains protected by the saving clause.
- UNUM Life Insurance Co. of America v. Ward, No. 97-1868 (Apr. 20, 1999): its saved-condition rule extends to this medical-necessity term; its separate administrative-connection holding and actual-conflict inquiry remain intact.
- Pilot Life Insurance Co. v. Dedeaux: its bar on competing state enforcement remains effective; the limited medical review does not supply such a remedy.
- Firestone Tire & Rubber Co. v. Bruch: its review rule concerns discretion lawfully retained by the plan and does not guarantee discretion against saved policy regulation.
- Great-West Life & Annuity Insurance Co. v. Knudson, No. 99-1786 (Jan. 8, 2002): its §502(a)(3) limits remain unchanged and do not alter this benefits action.

## Law After Decision

The saved-insurance-condition rule now includes this mandatory independent determination of medical necessity within insured coverage. The resulting benefit is enforceable under §502(a)(1)(B), with the federal remedial limits unchanged. Neither HMO status nor the label independent review creates a general exemption from ERISA; the operation of another system and any actual federal conflict require their own analysis.

## Separate Writings

**Thomas, dissenting, joined by Scalia and Kennedy.** The dissent would reverse the preemption ruling and return the benefits claim for determination through ERISA's process without treating the outside physician's decision as binding. It assumes that the State regulates insurance but concludes that the compulsory determination is an incompatible alternate remedial mechanism. Unlike the notice-prejudice condition sustained in UNUM, the reviewer determines the disputed obligation through an external adjudication that replaces the plan's decision. Pilot Life's remedial exclusivity and Great-West's insistence on the enacted enforcement boundaries remain decisive even when the subject is insured coverage. The dissent accepts ordinary saved insurance terms; its objection is the binding substitute process. It would neither decide medical necessity itself nor categorically deny Moran benefits.

## Procedure After Action

The Seventh Circuit's judgment for Moran stands. The affirmed relief enforces the insured benefit with the statutory physician determination; the Court orders no further merits proceedings.

No. 00-1021; judgment below, 230 F.3d 959; 29 U.S.C. §§1132(a)(1)(B), 1132(a)(3), 1133, 1144(a), (b)(2)(A)–(B); Illinois HMO Act §4-10, 215 Ill. Comp. Stat. 125/4-10. The medical review addresses necessity within covered services; the defendant-status question was not challenged.

## utah-v-evans-merits-2002-06-20

## Event

Utah v. Evans, No. 01-714. Merits decision, June 20, 2002. Argued March 27, 2002. On direct appeal from the three-judge District Court for the District of Utah. Utah challenges its loss of a marginal House seat from the 2000 census's hot-deck imputation. After mail and field efforts left some address, occupancy and household-size information unresolved, the Bureau used nearby comparable units to complete the missing information. Utah asserts standing and contests statutory permission and constitutional validity; North Carolina challenges redressability based on the completed certificate and defends the method. The district court sustained the method. No discriminatory manipulation was found.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The judgment of the District Court is affirmed.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas.

All nine find standing. Seven uphold statutory permission: the six supporting affirmance and Thomas; O'Connor and Scalia oppose. Six uphold constitutional validity: the affirming coalition; Thomas opposes, and O'Connor and Scalia do not reach that question. The challenged count and resulting allocation remain in place.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joins and scope |
|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Breyer | Part I: all other Justices. Part II: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg. Part III: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg. |
| Concurrence in part and dissent in part | O'Connor | Scalia; joins Part I, opposes Part II, reserves Part III |
| Concurrence in part and dissent in part | Thomas | No other Justice; joins Parts I and II, opposes Part III |

## Holdings

### Completed transmission does not defeat redress of the representation injury

**Holding and operative rule:** A State has standing to contest a census method that causes an identified loss of a House seat when authoritative relief concerning the responsible census determination would likely correct the allocation through the statutory sequence. Completed transmission and certification do not categorically bar correction of court-determined legal error in the count.

**Authority:** Part I of the opinion of the Court is joined by all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Utah's identified marginal-seat loss is a concrete injury caused by the challenged method. Franklin recognizes likely redress through correction of the responsible census determination; Wisconsin applies that principle to representational injury, and Department of Commerce requires concrete injury for the particular plaintiff. Utah meets those requirements. North Carolina's certificate objection would make a legally erroneous census input irreversible, but the reporting and allocation statutes contain no such categorical command. The Court reads their sequence to permit correction of the underlying determination and the resulting certificate for legal error. Likely redress follows from that official sequence, without a coercive presidential injunction or a general assumption that absent persons obey judicial advice. Whether Utah proves illegality is a merits question and does not erase its standing. The President's APA status and susceptibility to coercive relief are unnecessary to this holding and remain reserved.

**Precedent treatment:** Franklin v. Massachusetts: applied to likely redress through the allocation sequence. Wisconsin v. City of New York: applied to continuing representational injury. Department of Commerce v. United States House of Representatives: applied to plaintiff-specific injury rather than statutory authorization alone.

### The Census Act permits this residual completion method

**Holding and operative rule:** Sections 141 and 195 permit hot-deck imputation that completes particular unresolved units with nearby comparable information after genuine mail and field enumeration. This residual method does not select a representative sample to substitute for enumeration of a larger unenumerated population, and §195 does not prohibit it; its small percentage alone supplies no permission.

**Authority:** Part II of the opinion of the Court is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: seven direct votes.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the method's actual operation fits the enacted enumeration authority. The Bureau attempts to identify and count units individually, then fills remaining address, occupancy and household-size gaps from nearby units of the same type. Treating unresolved entries as zero would itself infer an answer rather than count established persons more literally. Section 141 permits this supported completion of the count, and §195 does not withdraw that authority for this method. The contrary argument equates every inference from observed information with a representative sample. But this process completes identified residual units after attempted enumeration; it does not select a fraction of the population to stand for a larger unenumerated whole. The distinction rests on function, not merely timing, randomness or scale. Department of Commerce permits two particular sampling designs; its general permission-versus-duty construction of §195 did not command a majority. The Court therefore neither elevates that construction into law nor decides materially different sampling designs. The statutory claim fails on the described method itself.

**Precedent treatment:** Department of Commerce v. United States House of Representatives: its permission for two identified designs remains intact; the four-Justice general construction does not control this independent method-specific analysis.

### The explained method reasonably serves enumeration and geographic representation

**Holding and operative rule:** The Enumeration Clause permits this residual imputation because it supplements a genuine population-counting effort and bears an explained reasonable relationship to enumeration and population-based representation. Locality and comparable housing type support the inference on this record without proof of quantified geographic superiority; a national-total improvement alone, a fixed percentage or unsupported geographic assumptions would not supply that relationship.

**Authority:** Part III of the opinion of the Court is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: six direct votes. Thomas opposes; O'Connor and Scalia do not reach this component.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Constitution does not require every counted fact to be personally observed. Department of Commerce rejects an exclusively physical-headcount rule, while Wisconsin requires a genuine enumeration and an explained relationship to population-based representation. The Bureau first attempted direct enumeration. For remaining gaps, it chose a geographically nearby donor of the same housing type and kept the unresolved unit at its own location. Those features connect the inference to geographic distribution rather than merely a larger national total. The Hogan declaration's numerical-accuracy priority and the absence of a study establishing improved distributive accuracy preclude a finding that this design was proved geographically superior. They do not establish that geography was ignored. The actual choice is between a supported local inference and treating unresolved entries as zero, which also carries geographically uneven error. Neither alternative guarantees perfection; the explained design satisfies reasonable-relationship review. The final-seat consequence does not change that standard. The small residual use is evidence of fit, not a constitutional safe harbor. Thomas's proposed count-based limit cannot supply the governing rule after Department of Commerce, though his disagreement remains stated separately. Discriminatory manipulation, unsupported geographic proxies and absence of a genuine enumeration effort remain outside this permission. No freestanding APA merits claim is before the Court.

**Precedent treatment:** Wisconsin v. City of New York: applied without changing its reasonable-relationship standard or requiring empirical certainty. Department of Commerce v. United States House of Representatives: applied for constitutional permission to supplement a genuine enumeration with supported inference. Franklin v. Massachusetts: its supported counting choices remain consistent with this application; its separate administrative-review rule is unchanged.

## Precedent Treatment

- Franklin v. Massachusetts, No. 91-1502 (June 26, 1992): applied to redress through correction of the responsible determination and allocation sequence; its independent finality and administrative-review rules remain unchanged.
- Wisconsin v. City of New York, Nos. 94-1614, 94-1631 and 94-1985 (Mar. 20, 1996): applied to continuing representational injury and explained methodological choice reasonably related to enumeration and geographic representation.
- Department of Commerce v. United States House of Representatives / Clinton v. Glavin, Nos. 98-404 and 98-564 (Jan. 25, 1999): applied to plaintiff-specific standing and constitutional permission for supported supplementation; its two-design statutory permission remains binding, while its general §195 construction remains a four-Justice position.

## Law After Decision

Completed apportionment certificates do not make the responsible census determination immune from correction of legal error. The Census Act and Enumeration Clause permit the described residual, geographically grounded completion of a genuine enumeration. The constitutional reasonable-relationship standard is applied without requiring proof of perfect or guaranteed improvement. The decision adds this method-specific statutory permission without settling the general construction of §195 or authorizing every inference method.

## Separate Writings

**O'Connor, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by Scalia.** They join the standing holding but would reverse under §195 and remand for lawful agency correction through the statutory sequence. In their view, donor information substitutes an inferred population for information never observed, and choosing donors late, nonrandomly or for only a small residual group does not remove the technique from the statutory sampling restriction. The prior permission for two other designs binds those designs but does not settle this different method. They reserve the constitutional question and would leave any corrected count and allocation to the lawful official process.

**Thomas, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** He joins standing and statutory permission, agreeing that this imputation falls outside the statutory sampling restriction. He would nevertheless reverse because the Constitution requires an actual count rather than adding persons through donor inference, especially where the existence or occupancy of the unit is unresolved. He disagrees with extending Department of Commerce's constitutional permission and with its rejection of a count-based limit to the extent necessary. His position is a dissent from that governing premise, not a description of the current rule. He would remand for lawful agency correction rather than judicially specify a replacement number.

## Procedure After Action

The direct appeal concludes in affirmance. The challenged 2000 count and allocation remain operative. The Court orders no seat transfer, replacement dataset, presidential injunction or new census. Review of materially different or unlawfully implemented methods remains governed by the applicable statutory and constitutional standards.
