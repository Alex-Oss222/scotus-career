# Effective public law from Brown ? November 13, 2001


## Event

Brown v. City of Oneonta. Decided November 13, 2001. On review of the Second Circuit's amended judgment, 221 F.3d 329. The question is whether deliberate investigative selection by race escapes racial-classification review when the racial feature comes from a victim's description and additional hostility is not shown, and what constitutional standard governs that selection.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the affected equal-protection dismissal and remands.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed in part and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The reversal reaches the rejection of the equal-protection claim insofar as it rests on a categorical victim-description exemption. The affected claim is restored for application of the governing racial-classification standard to the actual selection, scope and burdens. Independently resolved claims, existing Fourth Amendment dispositions and the earlier qualified-immunity judgment protecting university officials for release of the list remain undisturbed.

## Holdings

**Deliberate racial selection requires justification.** When officials intentionally select people for investigation by race, they use a racial classification even if a victim supplied that feature and no hostility is shown. The government must establish an important lawful investigative objective and a substantial, evidence-supported relationship between that objective and the actual selection, its coverage and burdens, including a supported response to concrete practicable alternatives offering comparable investigative value with materially less racial disadvantage.

**Authority:** O'Connor's opinion for all nine Justices controls the classification rule, its standard and the bounded remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the source of identifying information can justify an official racial choice; it does not erase that choice. Adarand Constructors, Inc. v. Peña supplies the common justification standard; Engineering Contractors and City of Dallas require examination of the evidence connecting the objective to the actual measure and burden. Solving the reported violent attack is an important lawful objective. A concrete description linking race to age, sex, an injured hand, location and timing may support appropriately limited measures. The Court therefore rejects the contention that classification review requires police to ignore useful descriptions. It also rejects a categorical permission to investigate a racial community without examining the description's other features and the practical scope of the operation. The wider sweep and alleged encounter with a woman require that examination. Testimony, investigative records and reasonable inferences can establish fit; no commissioned study or universal least-restrictive-means requirement follows. Consensual questioning and a Fourth Amendment seizure are distinct, but consent alone does not remove an intentionally racial burden from equal-protection review. Membership in a described group does not establish an individual encounter, injury or defendant's responsibility.

**Precedent treatment:** Peña's common standard is extended to purposeful investigative selection; its evidentiary and remedial limits remain.

**Remand rule.** The lower courts must examine the actual selection criteria, scope and burdens under that standard, and address personal responsibility, coercion where relevant, immunity and relief through their distinct governing rules. Immunity remains measured against the law at the September 1992 conduct; this decision does not retrospectively supply clearly established notice.

**Authority and explanation:** The same unanimous opinion controls. Lesage separates unequal-process injury from consequential loss and appropriate relief. The categorical exemption prevented the necessary examination; removing it restores the affected claim without reopening independently sustained dispositions.

## Precedent Treatment

- Adarand Constructors, Inc. v. Peña (June 12, 1995): extended to deliberate investigative selection; the common substantial-justification standard governs rather than strict scrutiny.
- Engineering Contractors (1998) and City of Dallas (1999): applied for evidence-supported fit, actual burdens and concrete practicable alternatives.
- Eisenberg (May 30, 2000): its acknowledgment of the binding common standard is preserved; its distinct educational component is not extended to criminal investigation.
- Lesage (November 29, 1999): applied to keep the classification inquiry separate from individual causation, immunity and consequential relief.

## Law After Decision

Purposeful investigative selection by race is subject to the common substantial-justification standard. Victim-supplied identifying information may justify fitted measures, but its source and the absence of hostility create no categorical exemption. The rule governs the classification and its justification; individual liability and remedies remain subject to their existing requirements.
