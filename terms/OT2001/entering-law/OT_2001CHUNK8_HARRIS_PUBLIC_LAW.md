# Harris v. United States: exact public-law reading copy

Mechanically copied bounded Public Projection. The Record controls. Effective June 24, 2002.

## Event

Harris v. United States, No. 00-10666. Argued March 25, 2002; decided June 24, 2002. On writ of certiorari to the Fourth Circuit, 243 F.3d 806, which sustained the seven-year firearm sentence. The granted question is whether brandishing that raises the mandatory minimum under 18 U.S.C. §924(c)(1)(A) must be charged and proved beyond a reasonable doubt; statutory construction is a necessary antecedent. Harris seeks sentence relief; the United States seeks affirmance.

The indictment charged carrying a firearm during and in relation to a drug transaction, without alleging brandishing. A lawful bench trial established the basic offense. Harris objected at sentencing that brandishing was an uncharged statutory element and added the constitutional argument on appeal. The sentencing judge treated brandishing as a close question, found it by a preponderance and imposed the seven-year minimum, which determined the guideline sentence under §2K2.4. Although the judge considered seven years appropriate, he declined to commit to that term if the enhancement were reversed. The basic conviction and separate evidentiary rulings are outside the granted challenge. Harris and Ring are separately decided matters; neither supplies a prior holding for the other. United States v. Angle was decided June 20, 2002.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision. No Justice is recused.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the judgment sustaining the firearm sentence, vacates the seven-year sentence and remands for lawful resentencing. The basic firearm conviction remains intact.

## Holdings

### I. The amended statute uses a mandatory sentencing condition

**Holding and operative rule:** Amended §924(c)(1)(A) defines the basic firearm offense and assigns graduated mandatory minimums; brandishing is a sentencing condition under that structure, a classification that does not dispense with constitutional element protections. The opening exception applies to the extent a greater minimum is otherwise provided by §924(c) or another provision of law; brandishing requires displaying all or part of the firearm, or otherwise making its presence known to another person, for the purpose of intimidating that person, whether or not the firearm is directly visible.

**Authority:** Part I of Scalia's opinion, joined by all eight other Justices, supplies nine direct votes for this construction.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress's completed offense clause and ensuing directions for how the offender shall be sentenced identify a basic offense followed by penalty conditions. The five-, seven- and ten-year clauses establish the structure; the present claim concerns the seven-year brandishing condition. Jones requires attention to text, structure, tradition and consequence. Its statutory analysis does not make the placement of a provision conclusive. Castillo construed the materially different former firearm-type provision and therefore does not decide this amendment.

Defined intimidating conduct and two additional mandatory years make the aggravated-element reading plausible. The enacted arrangement nevertheless supplies the better statutory account. Avoidance does not license rewriting an ascertainable penalty provision merely to avoid deciding its constitutional operation. The statute itself does not prescribe preponderance factfinding or dispense with charging protection. Applying the independent constitutional requirements thus gives effect to its words without treating the sentencing label as dispositive. The greater-minimum exception remains intact; no dispute concerning its application is presented. The distinct discharge condition is not independently adjudicated on this record.

**Precedent treatment:** Jones supplies the interpretive method and retains its carjacking construction. Castillo is distinguished because its former firearm-type text differs materially; its statutory-element holding remains intact.

### II. Compulsory additional punishment requires element protections

**Holding and operative rule:** A present-offense fact legally necessary to raise a mandatory minimum must receive the charging protections applicable to the prosecution and must be validly admitted as an element or proved beyond a reasonable doubt to the proper guilt factfinder. A lawful jury waiver permits the judge to determine the element under that same proof standard; it neither enlarges the indictment nor converts an ordinary sentencing finding into the required adjudication.

**Authority:** Part II of Scalia's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg, supplies six direct votes for the rule and its federal application.

**Controlling explanation:** Angle already holds that a present-offense fact changing either binding endpoint receives element protections and separately requires applicable federal grand-jury protection. The Court applies those holdings to brandishing. Before that additional finding, the law permits a sentence beginning at five years; afterward, it forbids a sentence below seven. The unchanged outer maximum does not erase that alteration of lawful punishment. Apprendi supplies the actual-authorization principle, and Gaudin and Winship supply the factfinder and proof protections once the aggravating fact is identified.

The Government and the dissent emphasize that seven years could have been selected within the outer range. That argument confuses discretionary selection with a new legal obligation. Judicial findings remain permissible for choosing punishment within a range already authorized; they cannot establish the fact that first changes the binding range. This is an allocation rule, not a policy judgment against mandatory sentences. Angle has already overruled McMillan's contrary unchanged-maximum distinction, and the Court adheres to that treatment. Legislative reliance does not sustain this pending sentence under a displaced rule; final judgments retain their own review doctrines.

Harris's indictment omitted brandishing, and his lawful jury waiver preserved the reasonable-doubt requirement. The contested preponderance finding supplies neither an element admission nor the missing aggravated adjudication. Prior-conviction facts, collateral retroactivity and a general redesign of sentencing are outside the present question; this decision creates no constitutional prior-conviction exception.

**Precedent treatment:** Angle is applied, including its prior overruling of McMillan to the stated extent. Apprendi supplies the punishment-authorization principle; Gaudin and Winship supply proper factfinding and reasonable doubt. Jones and Castillo retain their independent federal charging consequences.

### III. This record satisfies all four plain-error requirements

**Holding and operative rule:** Assuming without deciding that Harris forfeited the constitutional theory, Rule 52(b) requires error, clarity at review in this settled-law-then-changed setting, the defendant's ordinary showing of outcome-affecting prejudice, and a separate determination that the error seriously affects the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings before discretionary correction is warranted. Harris satisfies each condition on this record; the Court reverses the sentence-sustaining judgment, vacates the firearm sentence and remands for lawful resentencing while preserving the conviction.

**Authority:** Part III of Scalia's opinion has the same six direct joins as Part II, including its affirmative plainness application, independent prejudice finding and discretionary correction.

**Controlling explanation:** Olano separates forfeiture from intentional waiver and preserves the defendant's ordinary prejudice burden. The statutory objection and later constitutional argument need not be treated as identical: even the stricter forfeiture standard yields relief. Applying Angle, compulsory use of the uncharged, lower-standard aggravator is error. Johnson makes it plain now because settled contrary trial law changed during direct review; the Court decides no timing rule for initially unsettled law.

Harris demonstrates outcome-affecting prejudice. He contested brandishing; the judge found it a close question under a preponderance standard; and the resulting minimum supplied the sentence through §2K2.4. The carrying verdict did not decide the distinct intimidation purpose beyond a reasonable doubt. The judge's view that seven years was appropriate is contrary evidence, but his express refusal to select that term independently leaves a reasonable probability of a different lawful sentencing outcome. This is an affirmative inference from the actual record, not presumed injury from lost discretion or a burden shifted to the Government. Possible future departure authority is not an entered alternative judgment.

Correction is independently warranted because genuinely contested conduct, never adjudicated with the required protections, compelled additional imprisonment. That circumstance seriously impairs sentencing fairness and integrity. Substantial-rights injury alone would not suffice, and actual innocence is unnecessary. Johnson's clear-materiality setting and Cotton's overwhelming, essentially uncontroverted threshold record are materially different. Neder's preserved-error inquiry supplies neither automatic reversal nor a hypothetical brandishing verdict; clear-error affirmance cannot supply the missing reasonable-doubt adjudication.

Resentencing begins from the five-year minimum and its applicable guideline consequence. Any different sentence requires an independently lawful basis and reasons. A further aggravated proceeding requires independent charging, waiver, procedural and double-jeopardy authority. Neither an automatic enhancement trial nor a particular replacement term follows.

**Precedent treatment:** Olano's four conditions and ordinary burden are applied; Johnson supplies limited review-time plainness but its refusal of correction is distinguished. Cotton preserves nonjurisdictional treatment and record-specific discretion. Neder retains its distinct preserved-error burden; Vonn permits relevant existing-record consideration without a burden shift.

## Precedent Treatment

- Jones v. United States, No. 97-6203: supplies text-and-structure interpretation and preserves its carjacking element construction and federal charging consequences.
- Castillo v. United States: distinguished as construction of the former firearm-type provision; the amended brandishing text receives its own construction.
- United States v. Angle, June 20, 2002: applied to the binding statutory floor and applicable federal charging protections, without distinguishing a mandatory statutory constraint from a mandatory Guidelines constraint.
- McMillan v. Pennsylvania: its permission for ordinary judicial present-offense findings to raise a mandatory floor merely because the maximum is unchanged was overruled in Angle; that displaced proposition is not revived.
- Apprendi v. New Jersey: its actual-authorization principle supports the present application; its earlier minimum reservation was resolved by Angle.
- United States v. Gaudin and In re Winship: apply proper element factfinding and proof beyond a reasonable doubt; a lawful bench waiver changes the finder, not the burden.
- Witte v. United States and United States v. Watts / United States v. Putra: retain ordinary judicial assessment within independently authorized punishment, subject to Angle's binding-range rule.
- United States v. Olano: applies all four independent conditions, ordinary defendant prejudice and discretionary correction; no structural-error or automatic-relief rule replaces them.
- Johnson v. United States: applies review-time plainness where settled contrary trial law changes during direct review; distinguishes its particular fourth-condition denial of relief.
- United States v. Cotton: preserves the nonjurisdictional character of the charging defect and the independent correction inquiry; its essentially uncontroverted quantity threshold is not this disputed brandishing record.
- Neder v. United States: preserves its qualified actual-verdict rule and Government burden for preserved error; it supplies no automatic cure or burden transfer here.
- United States v. Vonn: permits relevant existing-record assessment under Rule 52(b), preserving the distinction between error and its effect.

## Law After Decision

The amended brandishing clause is a statutory sentencing condition subject to constitutional element protections because it raises the binding minimum. The federal charge and reasonable-doubt adjudication must support that aggravated condition, with lawful admission and jury-waiver requirements preserved. Angle's range rule and prior displacement of McMillan remain controlling; ordinary sentencing discretion within independently authorized limits continues.

The statutory construction and this complete Rule 52(b) application are new applications at the present scope. The review framework is unchanged: a range error does not presume prejudice, and outcome injury does not automatically compel correction. Harris's contested finding, mandatory sentence and absence of an independently selected alternative establish both actual effect and the separate reason for correction here.

## Separate Writings

**O'Connor, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by Kennedy.** They join the amended-text construction but would affirm by reconsidering Angle's floor extension. Their Apprendi and Angle positions distinguish the facts defining an offense's outer punishment limits from legislative directions governing judicial choice within those limits. McMillan's former rule and the continuing sentencing role recognized in Witte and Watts support their preferred allocation. They acknowledge that Angle now governs against that position; the statutory source of this floor does not distinguish it from a binding Guidelines constraint. O'Connor also maintains her objections to Jones and Apprendi. Neither Justice treats the bench waiver as permission to lower the burden for a genuine element, and neither rests affirmance on an independently imposed alternative sentence or a separate no-prejudice holding. Their merits disagreement does not eliminate the ordinary review conditions if the Court's element rule applies.

**Breyer, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** Breyer joins Part I and would affirm while continuing his objection to the Apprendi allocation and Angle's extension. He does not find a persuasive logical distinction between facts increasing minima and maxima. His objection instead concerns constitutional permission for legislatures to structure sentencing through judicial findings and the practical operation of that allocation. Opposition to mandatory minimums as policy does not establish a jury requirement: moving the necessary finding into the charged offense can shift control toward charging and stipulation decisions without removing the mandatory penalty. He would preserve legislative sentencing authority rather than extend the element rule. He seeks reconsideration of the controlling floor holding, supplies no vote for the Kennedy-O'Connor logical distinction, and does not invent an unaffected alternative sentence or dispense with ordinary error review.

## Procedure After Action

The Fourth Circuit's sentence-sustaining judgment is reversed and the seven-year firearm sentence is vacated. The case returns for lawful resentencing beginning from the five-year minimum and its applicable guideline consequence. Any different term requires an independently lawful basis and reasons. The basic firearm conviction remains intact. Any further aggravated proceeding must rest on independent charging, waiver, procedural and double-jeopardy authority; the mandate itself supplies none.
