## Standards and Tests selection: Partial withdrawal of school-desegregation supervision

### Partial withdrawal of school-desegregation supervision

**Governing question:** Release of completed portions of a school-desegregation decree while obligations elsewhere remain unfinished.

**Current rule:** A court may withdraw supervision from a completed component while retaining jurisdiction and supervision elsewhere only after the school district shows full and satisfactory compliance and elimination of the relevant vestiges to the extent practicable. The court must assess demonstrated good faith toward the whole decree and whether retaining control of that component is necessary or practicable to obtain compliance elsewhere; release may not obstruct the remaining remedy. The district bears the burden of showing that imbalance for which it disclaims responsibility is not traceable to the constitutional violation. Genuinely independent demographic changes do not require perpetual balancing after required remedial work is completed. Existing findings receive clear-error review, but a finding on one question does not decide unexamined causal or remedial relationships. Withdrawal orders must define obligations released, remaining duties and retained supervision.

**Authority by component:**
- Partial-release conditions, burden and integrated assessment: Freeman v. Pitts, March 31, 1992, holding 1, “Partial withdrawal under an unfinished decree.”
- Causation, clear-error review and defined retained obligations: the same record, holding 2, “Causation and the limits of the remand.” Stone for White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter controls; Thomas did not participate.

**Present operation effective:** Freeman, March 31, 1992.

**Exceptions and limits:** Racial imbalance alone establishes neither a present constitutional violation nor entitlement to release. Neither good intentions alone, an automatic demographic exemption nor categorical simultaneous compliance across all components replaces the specified showing. Rufo's consent-decree modification standard is distinct and imposes no additional changed-circumstances prerequisite for finding a school-decree obligation fulfilled. Separate opinions' specific factual applications and proposed changes to causation presumptions do not control.

**Holdings navigation:** Freeman v. Pitts, 503 U.S. 467 (1992) — School desegregation decrees.

## Standards and Tests selection: Intermediary contribution limits and meaningful association

### Intermediary contribution limits and meaningful association

**Governing question:** Fit of limits on contributions received by political committees and political funds.

**Current rule:** Contributions remain distinct from independent expenditures. A broadly restrictive ceiling on contributions received by political committees or funds must preserve meaningful collective political association and fit an identified corruption or circumvention mechanism. Realistic routes around valid candidate limits may be regulated; an insufficiently fitted ceiling cannot be sustained merely by invoking circumvention. The invalidation of the reviewed $100 aggregate annual ceiling establishes no permanent constitutional dollar minimum. Annual duration, actual recipient function, per-recipient versus aggregate operation, amount and demonstrated effect on collective advocacy must be considered together. Other available committees and aggregate political success do not alone cure a burden on the chosen association.

**Authority by component:**

- This $100 intermediary-contribution ceiling lacks sufficient fit: This $100 intermediary-contribution ceiling lacks sufficient fit, 1995-05-15. Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, Part III, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: nine direct votes, at this stated functional application.

- Russell v. Burris — Canonical Decision Record, Holdings, “The ordinary-PAC receipt limit survives on this operational record”, 1999-04-28; O’Connor Parts II–IV with Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Part III supplies the actual-channel and operational considerations.

**Present operation effective:** 1995-05-15; the component authorities identified above.

**Exceptions and limits:** The reviewed Day recipient class and its exclusions remain as originally stated. Russell supplies no general approval of a $200 intermediary receipt ceiling, no permanent dollar floor, no immunity for sham or materially different structures and no unlimited candidate-contribution authority. Genuine independence and actual candidate-conduit status retain their separate requirements.

**Holdings navigation:** This $100 intermediary-contribution ceiling lacks sufficient fit. [Russell v. Burris — The ordinary-PAC receipt limit survives on this operational record](HOLDINGS.md#the-ordinary-pac-receipt-limit-survives-on-this-operational-record).

**Current wording authority:** Russell, April 28, 1999, applies Day’s meaningful-pooling and actual-fit rule to a demonstrated per-recipient candidate-conduit setting without adopting a general dollar safe harbor.

**Related current rules:** Candidate contribution limits and anticorruption fit states the distinct direct-candidate and exact dispersed-donor classification rules.

## Standards and Tests selection: Candidate contribution limits and anticorruption fit

### Candidate contribution limits and anticorruption fit

**Governing question:** What justifies direct candidate-contribution limits and differentiated source allowances.

**Current rule:** Candidate contribution limits must be closely drawn to a sufficiently important interest in preventing corruption or its appearance while preserving meaningful political association. No completed bribe is necessary, and equality of political influence is not an independent justification. Amount and inflation comparisons establish no permanent constitutional boundary; actual campaign scale, fundraising burden and cumulative restrictions matter. Under the adjudicated Arkansas classification, a registered committee receiving contributions exclusively from individuals and no more than $25 cumulatively from any individual during a calendar year may receive a $2,500 per-candidate, per-election contribution allowance while ordinary sources face lower ceilings. The actual dispersed-donor structure and its anticorruption fit, rather than a small-donor label, support the distinction.

The same contribution standard permits the presented viewpoint-neutral prohibition of direct business-corporation treasury gifts to federal candidates when the government justifies the direct-recipient and commercial-source safeguard while preserving voluntary human political channels. Neither corporate form, concentrated wealth, ordinary access nor nominal availability of a separate fund alone supplies close fit. Materially different party-financing channels do not require permission for direct treasury transfers where the direct-control risk remains justified; underinclusion must still receive a real fit inquiry. Independent expenditures, materially different organizations and supported operational burdens retain their own analysis.

**Authority by component:**

- Direct-candidate interest and close fit: Russell v. Burris — Canonical Decision Record, Holdings, “Contribution limits require actual anticorruption fit; two candidate categories fail here”, April 28, 1999; O’Connor Part I, all nine Justices.
- Exact dispersed-donor classification: Russell — Holdings, “The exact small-donor qualification supports a different candidate allowance”, April 28, 1999; O’Connor Part IV with Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

- Business-treasury candidate restriction and distinct soft-money comparison: Canonical Decision Record `Mariani_v_United_States_merits_2000-12-12.md`, Public Projection / Holdings / II. The presented direct corporate-treasury restriction is constitutionally permissible; III. The demonstrated soft-money practices do not invalidate the direct-transfer rule, 2000-12-12; six direct joins.

**Present operation effective:** Mariani, December 12, 2000, for the business-treasury source component; Russell, April 28, 1999, retains the earlier general and dispersed-donor rules.

**Exceptions and limits:** Office-specific dollar applications establish no general floor or safe harbor. Every small-donor qualification remains necessary to the adjudicated distinction; no unlimited candidate-giving right, immunity for sham structures or automatic validity of materially different classifications follows. A failed categorical challenge does not resolve a genuinely different office-specific application.

**Related current rules:** Intermediary contribution limits and meaningful association separately governs committee receipt restrictions and actual candidate-conduit fit.

**Holdings navigation:** [Mariani v. United States — Corporate contributions and true-source attribution](HOLDINGS.md#mariani-v-united-states-1).

## Standards and Tests selection: Limited drug testing of interscholastic athletes

### Limited drug testing of interscholastic athletes

**Governing question:** Fourth Amendment treatment of a documented school-athletic urinalysis program.

**Current rule:** Collecting and chemically analyzing a student’s urine is a search; enrollment and voluntary athletic participation do not waive protection. In the documented interscholastic setting, objective seasonal and random testing may be reasonable without individualized suspicion where the protective safety need, athletic privacy expectations, limited collection and drug information, confidentiality and restricted counseling or athletic consequences together justify the intrusion. The holding does not authorize testing all students, unrelated medical investigation or ordinary police use.

**Authority by component:**

- I. Urine testing is a search; athletic participation is no waiver: I. Urine testing is a search; athletic participation is no waiver, 1995-06-26. Part I of Scalia's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; nine Justices adopt the search and no-waiver rule.
- II. This interscholastic-athlete testing program is reasonable: II. This interscholastic-athlete testing program is reasonable, 1995-06-26. Part II of Scalia's Opinion of the Court, joined by Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; five Justices adopt the rule and application.
- III. The facial prescription-verification objection does not invalidate the policy: III. The facial prescription-verification objection does not invalidate the policy, 1995-06-26. Part III of Scalia's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; eight Justices adopt this limited facial rule. Stone-Zsela does not join it. It independently answers this objection, not the entire testing challenge.

**Present operation effective:** 1995-06-26; the component authorities identified above.

**Exceptions and limits:** The upheld program used a concealed-identity student draw with adult witnesses, initial seasonal testing and weekly random selection of about ten percent of athletes, leaving no individualized official selection discretion. Boys remained clothed facing away from a monitor without genital viewing; girls used enclosed stalls with an outside monitor. Drug testing covered amphetamines, marijuana and cocaine and occasional LSD, not alcohol or steroids. Anonymous laboratory numbers, restricted school access, one-year retention and no police transmission, suspension, expulsion or general discipline limited use. Initial refusal excluded only the current season.

**Sequence or decisionmaker:** Prescription verification allows a prescription copy or doctor’s authorization, not compulsory personal delivery to teachers or coaches. Missing verification plus a positive result invokes positive-test procedures, not exclusion for missing verification alone. The written policy permits a second test and requires no action if it is negative; confirmation was supplied by testified operation, not a mandatory written retest rule. Confirmed positives lead to parental notification and a hearing. First offense allows counseling with six weekly tests or current-and-next-season athletic suspension. Written second-offense consequences are current-and-next-season suspension, with a counseling/testing alternative described in practice. Third offense excludes the current and next two seasons; testing precedes the next eligible season.

**Open question:** The facial prescription ruling does not approve every form, nonprescription disclosure, physician-name demand or actual disclosure practice. Materially different programs and the independent state constitutional question remain open. No universal numerical efficacy requirement or suspicion-impracticability prerequisite is adopted.

**Holdings navigation:** I. Urine testing is a search; athletic participation is no waiver; II. This interscholastic-athlete testing program is reasonable; III. The facial prescription-verification objection does not invalidate the policy.

## Standards and Tests selection: Substantial special need for suspicionless candidate drug testing

### Substantial special need for suspicionless candidate drug testing

**Governing question:** Fourth Amendment justification for compelled drug testing as a candidacy condition.

**Current rule:** Compelled urine collection and chemical analysis are searches, and candidacy does not waive bodily privacy. A suspicionless program justified by a need beyond ordinary law enforcement must be reasonable in light of the actual governmental need, program operation and affected privacy. General desires for drug-free officials and public confidence do not supply the substantial, concrete need required for broad advance-scheduled candidate testing; reduced intrusion or public prominence alone cannot compensate for that deficiency.

**Authority by component:**

- Chandler v. Miller — Candidacy does not waive bodily privacy, 1997-04-15; unanimous Opinion of the Court, holding 1, supplies the no-waiver boundary
- Chandler v. Miller — Georgia has not supplied a special need, 1997-04-15; unanimous holding 2 supplies the concrete-need requirement at this program scope

**Present operation effective:** Chandler, 1997-04-15.

**Exceptions and limits:** Materially different programs retain contextual special-needs review. No universal proof-of-catastrophe, numeric efficacy requirement or exhaustion of all alternatives is imposed.

**Holdings navigation:** Chandler v. Miller (1997-04-15) — Criminal Procedure / Suspicionless candidate drug testing.

## Standards and Tests selection: Public-hospital evidence programs and special needs

### Public-hospital evidence programs and special needs

**Governing question:** Whether a public hospital’s coordinated patient-testing and prosecution program may rely on special needs in the absence of adequate consent.

**Current rule:** Systematic patient-urine collection and testing designed for police disclosure, preservation of evidence and arrest requires a justification sufficient for that actual operation. Genuine clinical activity and a beneficial treatment objective do not alone establish the special-needs authority to conduct the prosecution program without ordinary Fourth Amendment protection. Attendance for medical care does not itself waive protection; the scope of any consent to the evidence operation requires its own fact-sensitive analysis.

**Authority by component:**

- Operational justification and distinction between clinical attendance and consent: Canonical Decision Record `Ferguson_v_City_of_Charleston_merits_2001-03-21.md`, Public Projection / Holdings / The coordinated prosecution program lacks an adequate special-needs justification, 2001-03-21; seven direct joins to Kennedy’s opinion.

**Present operation effective:** Ferguson v. City of Charleston, 2001-03-21.

**Exceptions and limits:** Police cooperation or later criminal use does not invariably defeat special needs, and no general immediate-versus-ultimate-purpose formula is adopted. The rule imposes no universal emergency, numerical-efficacy or exhaustion condition and no compulsory three-permission formula for collection, testing and disclosure. Disclosure alone is not classified as a separate search. Independent reporting duties, private hospitals, emergency treatment and materially different authorized clinical programs remain outside the holding.

**Related current rules:** Primary ordinary-crime purpose in suspicionless checkpoints addresses the separate antecedent authority for a suspicionless roadblock.

**Holdings navigation:** [Ferguson v. City of Charleston, 532 U.S. 67 — Hospital testing coordinated with criminal prosecution](HOLDINGS.md#ferguson-v-city-of-charleston-532-us-67).

## Standards and Tests selection: Relevant precedent in appellate qualified-immunity review

### Relevant precedent in appellate qualified-immunity review

**Governing question:** What legal sources an appellate court must consider on a preserved clearly-established-law issue.

**Current rule:** Whether a federal right was clearly established when officials acted is reviewed independently using all relevant precedent, including cases neither cited to nor discovered by the district court. This does not admit a new claim or factual record, make each considered precedent controlling or permit later decisions to supply notice retrospectively. The notice inquiry stays fixed to the conduct date. Ordinary claim and evidence preservation, particularized objective immunity and proper summary-judgment treatment remain distinct; no mandatory merits-first sequence follows.

**Authority by component:**

- Independent legal review, contemporaneous notice and preservation limits: Elder v. Holloway — Appellate review must consider all relevant law on the preserved immunity issue, 1994-02-23. Ginsburg's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. All nine adopt the legal-authority rule and the limited reversal and remand.

**Present operation effective:** Elder v. Holloway, 1994-02-23.

**Holdings navigation:** Appellate review must consider all relevant law on the preserved immunity issue.

## Verbatim opening Holdings selection: Agostini v. Felton; Chancellor, Board of Education of the City of New York v. Felton

#### Agostini v. Felton; Chancellor, Board of Education of the City of New York v. Felton

**Docket or dockets:** Nos. 96-552 and 96-553.
**Decided:** June 23, 1997.

**Material judgment:** Both judgments vacated and remanded, 5–4, for tailored prospective Rule 60(b)(5) relief; Lemon modified as the governing test; Aguilar's categorical rule overruled and Ball's corresponding Shared Time rationale displaced at the stated scope.

**Proposition-level authority:** All four holdings are delivered by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela in the Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. Each has five direct joins at its stated level of generality, including the modification of Lemon in Holding I. No aggregation of separate opinions supplies controlling authority.

##### I. Lemon governs through one inquiry into governmental religious authority and function

**Holding:** Lemon remains the governing Establishment Clause test, modified so that purpose, effects and entanglement are evidence within one inquiry: a specific prohibition or distinct doctrine controls when it resolves the conduct; an established permissible relationship defeats the claim on that ground; otherwise a legally sufficient unresolved allegation is decided through attribution and function under ordinary procedure. These are three routes within the updated Lemon test, not freestanding prongs, exemptions from review or an alternative test for uncertain cases.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part I of the Opinion of the Court; Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas join this modification in full, supplying five direct joins at this level of generality.

**Operative qualifications:** The court identifies the governmental act—an official message, organized event, exercise of supervisory power or expenditure—and the function it performs. For financing, the question is what government purchases or performs; all religious conduct of the recipient need not become government speech. Private-access authorization, religious identity and physical location are relevant in context but are not individually decisive.

Government may not organize worship as its own activity, prescribe religious profession or observance, adopt doctrine as governmental truth, solicit religious adherence through public authority, govern theology or confer civil authority on a religious basis. Denominational classifications retain their distinct strict-scrutiny rule. Independently identifiable secular work in a religious setting may be purchased with enforceable controls against devotional use and diversion. Restoration of private exercise restricted by custody or comparable governmental control remains subject to the applicable accommodation inquiry, not an automatic financing prohibition. Those distinct doctrines retain their own sources and burdens.

**Controlling explanation:** Lemon addresses governmental sponsorship of religion, financing of religious functions and the improper union of civil and religious authority. Its method must identify those acts rather than condemn an independently secular service because officials supervise it to remain secular. Aguilar exposes that defect: location and repeated contact become substitutes for examining what the public program actually does. The Court therefore modifies Lemon's operation while retaining its constitutional concerns.

Purpose and effects help identify the governmental function; institutional relationships show who directs it. Once prohibited conduct is established, actual conversion, religious success or a completed penalty is unnecessary. A secular objective cannot justify prohibited religious means. Conversely, a provider's religious motivation does not make its secular work devotional. Ordinary monitoring of secular performance is not itself establishment, even when repeated; civil control of doctrine and religious control of public authority remain forbidden.

This method requires real operational separation and reliable accountability for funded work, but not invariably separate institutional governance or wholly distinct personnel. Shared structures cannot excuse devotional financing, and they do not automatically defeat a genuinely severable public service. Here public selection, staffing, materials and supervision identify the remedial service to be examined. The school's entire religious mission is not the governmental act.

The dissent's institutional-support objection warrants scrutiny of the service, not a presumption that its secular and religious functions are inseparable. Existing specific holdings continue to govern their established relationships: Kiryas Joel restricts denominational allocation of civil power; Rosenberger permits its controlled publication benefit; Pinette and Chabad distinguish private expression and governmental attribution. The Court does not decide absent voucher, monument, legislative-prayer or accommodation programs, or declare every future application compatible with every preserved precedent.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court decides no tuition-voucher or provider-redemption scheme, monument, legislative-prayer program, general accommodation rule or all forms of direct aid. Parental selection does not itself answer what a particular expenditure purchases; no conclusion about an absent program displaces Witters or Mueller. Religious editorial viewpoint in Rosenberger is not automatically purchased devotional instruction. Private speech, free exercise, institutional religious autonomy, denominational scrutiny and accommodation retain their distinct sources and burdens. No controlling rationale is drawn from separate opinions under Marks.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Lemon v. Kurtzman remains governing, with its independent purpose, primary-effect and excessive-entanglement prongs modified into the three routes of this single inquiry. The actual holdings of Kiryas Joel, Rosenberger, Pinette, Chabad, Witters and Mueller remain in force; Zobrest supplies no Establishment Clause merits holding. Kiryas Joel's denominational-allocation and accommodation holdings remain intact; its preservation of Aguilar and Ball established the rule entering this decision, not a bar to the Court's express overruling. Zobrest's antecedent-law remand remains; it supplied no Establishment Clause merits holding and did not already undermine Aguilar by such a holding. Rosenberger's actual controlled vendor-funded printing benefit remains permissible on its program terms; it concerns financing, not merely facility access, and neither decided this public-teaching case nor authorized every devotional expenditure. Pinette's bounded private-expression and access holding remains; it supplies no universal forum immunity or automatic attribution from location. Chabad's contextual-attribution and access holding remains; location alone does not establish governmental sponsorship. Witters's actual benefit holding remains in force; no absent tuition-voucher or provider-redemption scheme is decided. Mueller's actual tax-benefit holding remains in force; the Court does not decide an absent funding program or silently enlarge or displace that holding.

##### II. Separately administered secular instruction is not forbidden because of its religious-school location

**Holding:** The Establishment Clause permits the described supplementary Title I service for children independently selected under neutral public eligibility criteria, delivered by public employees under public supervision with public secular materials, without religious instruction, team teaching or other cooperative instructional activity with religious-school teachers, or participation in worship, and with effective controls against diversion. Religious-school location, presumed inculcation arising from that location, incidental benefit to the school, and ordinary monitoring of that separately administered public service do not by themselves establish unconstitutional sponsorship, devotional financing or institutional control; Aguilar's categorical prohibition and Ball's corresponding Shared Time rationale are displaced at that scope.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part II of the Court's opinion; the five-Justice coalition stated above directly joins the rule, its secular-control qualifications and its precise treatment of precedent.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the constitutional inquiry must distinguish the public remedial service from the religious school's own instruction. Lemon, as updated in Part I, identifies the governmental act and its religious or secular function, using purpose, effects and institutional relationships as evidence within that inquiry. Here the remedial purpose, independent selection of eligible children, public staffing and supervision, secular materials and restrictions on religious participation identify a supplementary public service. Those safeguards establish this program's severable secular operation; operational separation and reliable accountability do not invariably require wholly distinct personnel or separate institutional governance. Ordinary consultation about a child's secular educational needs is distinct from joint teaching. Neutral eligibility or public employment alone would not excuse devotional instruction or diversion.

Aguilar treated the supervision needed to preserve secular instruction as a reason to prohibit the instruction on religious premises. That categorical inference is unsound: supervising public teachers' secular work is not, without more, governing the school's religious affairs. The absence of a finding of indoctrination does not prove every possible defect absent, but location cannot substitute for the connection between the challenged public service and an actual constitutional objection. Nor does an incidental institutional benefit alone turn this separately controlled service into religious teaching.

These safeguards existed when Aguilar was decided. The legal defect is the categorical inference that confuses secular supervision with religious sponsorship and control, not a newly discovered factual transformation. Continuing expense and impaired access make the consequence substantial; they are not the constitutional rule. Property, contractual, completed-transaction and educational-service interests receive their actual weight. No identified concrete property or completed-transaction entitlement requires retaining the categorical ban; established obligations and service continuity remain protected in implementation. Ball's Shared Time program implicated the corresponding inference about supplementary public teaching. Its distinct Community Education program predominantly used religious-school teachers and principals, and today's holding neither approves that arrangement nor reopens its judgment. The onsite rule applies Lemon as modified in Part I; the Court does not approve every program considered in Ball or reopen its completed judgment.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Independent prohibitions on governmental worship, devotional financing and improper institutional control remain; public employment and neutral eligibility do not immunize supported devotional conduct.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Aguilar v. Felton is overruled as authority for categorically excluding this safeguarded public Title I service from religious-school premises; no reopening of completed claims or authorization of religious instruction follows. School District of Grand Rapids v. Ball loses its contrary Shared Time rationale at the same scope, insofar as religious location, presumed inculcation, incidental school benefit or ordinary secular monitoring categorically condemns the separately administered public service; its Community Education judgment and unaddressed grounds remain undisturbed. Lemon v. Kurtzman governs through the updated inquiry stated in Holding I.

For the significant-change inquiry and injunction remedy in Holdings III–IV, see Federal Courts, “Modification of continuing injunctions,” Agostini, June 23, 1997, Nos. 96-552 and 96-553.

#### Jackson v. Benson

**Docket or dockets:** No. 98-376
**Decided:** 1999-03-16.

##### Parental routing does not justify the demonstrated purchase of integrated devotional instruction

**Holding:** Wisconsin may not justify public payment for the demonstrated devotional instructional function merely by neutral eligibility, parental selection and restrictive endorsement of its provider-cost payment. Where the financed educational work integrates devotional instruction without reliable separation, those features do not cure the expenditure; religious identity alone does not establish that defect, and genuinely independent private benefits and reliably separated secular services retain their distinct protection.

**Proposition-level authority:** O'Connor's Part I is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Six direct joins control the rule and its application; the remedy has the separate coalition stated below.

##### The corrective judgment must preserve the actual limits of the federal conflict

**Holding:** The federal approval and its dependent blanket injunction-dissolution direction are vacated, and the lower courts must determine appropriate provider/payment relief and state-law severability under the funded-function holding. Relief must prevent the established federal conflict while preserving lawful severable applications; the judgment neither rewrites state law nor commands an immediate ban on every payment or religious provider.

**Proposition-level authority:** O'Connor's Part II is joined by Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Those five directly support this disposition and remedy. Stone-Zsela seeks the different reversal and mandatory direction stated separately; the three affirming dissenters' conditional acceptance of remedial limits supplies no extra vacatur vote.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- **Agostini v. Felton:** Applied to the actual instructional function purchased and the need for reliable separation of secular work. Its modified Lemon inquiry remains governing; its reserved voucher question is answered only for this supported cost/instruction arrangement.
- **Lemon v. Kurtzman:** Continues through Agostini's unified inquiry. Its former independent prongs are not restored.
- **Witters v. Washington Department of Services for the Blind; Mueller v. Allen:** Preserved for their actual individual vocational-benefit and general tax-deduction relationships; neither supplies automatic approval of this provider-cost payment.
- **Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of the University of Virginia:** Preserved as an actual controlled printing benefit for independently attributed expression; it does not equate all spending with a forum or authorize every devotional expenditure.
- **Bronx Household of Faith; Pinette; Chabad:** Preserve genuinely private expression and contextual attribution. Private access does not itself resolve governmental purchase of instructional work.
- **Committee for Public Education v. Nyquist:** Its aid limitations are not categorically displaced by parental routing; the present application rests on the actual funded function under Agostini.
- **School District of Grand Rapids v. Ball:** Agostini's displacement of its corresponding Shared Time rationale remains confined; the distinct Community Education judgment is not reopened.
- **Zobrest v. Catalina Foothills:** Supplies no Establishment Clause merits holding; its antecedent-law remand does not validate this program.
- **Dalton v. Little Rock Family Planning Services; Leavitt v. Jane L.:** Applied to conflict-specific relief and preservation of independently supported protection while state-law severability is resolved.
- **American Manufacturers v. Sullivan:** Preserves review of government's own conduct despite private-carrier nonattribution. It supplies no Establishment Clause financing exemption.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The actual provider-cost payment cannot receive categorical federal approval solely because eligibility is neutral and a parent selects and endorses the payment. Demonstrated devotional instruction within the financed work must be addressed under the governing expenditure rule. Genuine private-benefit relationships, private forum access and reliably separated secular services remain distinct. The decision supplies no all-provider exclusion, new general facial-challenge formula, compulsory school participation or constitutional subsidy entitlement.

**Operative remedy or transition:** **Vacated and remanded, 5-4 on that disposition.**

**Supporting:** Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing the Court's disposition:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela would reverse the federal approval and remand with the payment-preventing direction described in his separate opinion. Justices Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would affirm the federal approval. Stone-Zsela joins the Court's substantive financing rule but not its vacatur or remedial disposition.

The lower courts must apply the actual funded-function holding to provider/payment scope, appropriate relief and state-law severability. They may use the existing record where sufficient; no new trial or submissions are automatically required. Lawful secular applications and the actual enrollment-status qualification remain protected. No blanket statewide funding cutoff, school closure, pupil transfer, recoupment or damages award follows from this judgment.

#### Strout v. Albanese

**Docket or dockets:** No Supreme Court docket supplied; review of First Circuit No. 98-1986, 178 F.3d 57.

**Decided:** February 28, 2000.

##### Maine may refuse the integrated devotional tuition presented here

**Holding:** The Religion Clauses permit Maine to refuse public payment for the integrated devotional instructional function presented by this ordinary-tuition application. Independent parental choice and neutral student eligibility do not themselves justify public purchase of demonstrated devotional instruction integrated into the financed educational work without reliable separation.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies six direct joins to this holding.

**Material application:** The parents' ordinary St. Dominic's tuition, approximately $3,850 against a $4,900 ceiling, pays for the integrated instructional package. The cap distinguishes the amount from Jackson's provider-cost formula but does not separate the devotional function.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Religious affiliation alone establishes no financing defect. The ruling neither excludes every religious provider nor decides every voucher system or creates an education-subsidy entitlement.

##### Free exercise receives its own inquiry

**Holding:** Refusing this public purchase of integrated devotional instruction does not, in this application, violate the Free Exercise Clause. The conclusion does not depend on whether attending the school is a central religious tenet and does not authorize religious hostility, exclusion from a neutral private-expression forum, or categorical rejection of genuinely severable secular work solely because its provider is religious.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies six direct joins to this holding.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court rejects the lower court's central-tenet premise and independent-prong Lemon approach. Reliably separated secular services and genuinely private expression retain their existing protections; no distinct service or forum application is decided.

##### The affirmance is limited to the granted claims and this application

**Holding:** The judgment sustaining the challenged refusal of integrated tuition is affirmed within the granted Establishment and Free Exercise questions. The decision does not reopen the ungranted speech, equal-protection or parental-liberty claims, the unchallenged dismissals of local defendants, or distinct applications for reliably separated secular services.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies six direct joins to this holding.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Statutory eligibility conditions remain distinct: basic approval; First-Amendment-compliant nonsectarian status; Maine or United States incorporation; required reporting, auditing and adopted requirements; statewide assessment at sixty-percent public enrollment measured by the previous October/April average; and requested transfer of records for transferring students. Section 5204(4) applies only when the resident unit neither maintains a secondary school nor contracts for secondary-school privileges. Receiving-school permission remains necessary, and payment is required up to the legal rate for the allowed approved private, adjoining public, or out-of-state or foreign schools. School self-identification is an administrative finding, not statutory text; the repealed fourth condition is not restored.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirmed as applied, 6-3, within the granted Religion Clause questions. The tuition refusal stands; no tuition, damages, recoupment, transfer, closure, statewide cutoff or new factfinding is ordered.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

## Event

**Case:** Brown v. Gilmore; reviewed judgment 258 F.3d 265 (4th Cir. 2001).
**Event:** Merits decision following full merits submission.
**Decided:** May 20, 2002.
**Route and posture:** Review of the Fourth Circuit's July 24, 2001 judgment affirming the Eastern District of Virginia's summary judgment rejecting the facial Establishment Clause challenge. The student and parent challengers seek invalidation and an injunction. Virginia had permitted a school minute of silence since 1976; the amendment effective July 1, 2000 made it mandatory statewide.
**Question:** Whether the daily classroom silence required by Virginia Code §22.1-203, allowing meditation, prayer or other silent activity, violates the Establishment Clause.
**Enacted arrangement:** The statute states the objectives of guaranteeing each pupil's free exercise and minimizing Commonwealth pressure to engage in or refrain from religious observation. Every school division must establish one minute of silence daily in each classroom. The responsible teacher must ensure that pupils remain seated and silent and make no distracting display. Each pupil may individually meditate, pray or undertake other silent activity that does not interfere with, distract or impede other pupils' corresponding choice. The Office of the Attorney General must intervene and provide legal defense of the law.
**Record:** Administrative directions prohibit teacher selection or suggestion of religious activity, audible devotion and student religious coercion. These directions describe implementation rather than enacted words. Legislative history and student affidavits concerning religious practices and feared pressure are part of the facial record; a future classroom violation is not an established fact.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at full merits submission and decision.

## Public Action

The Court affirms the Fourth Circuit's judgment rejecting the facial challenge.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Disposition:** Affirm rejection of the facial Establishment Clause challenge, 9–0.
**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Remedy:** The challenged daily minute of silence remains in force. The challengers receive no injunction against the statute. Claims based on a materially different coercive implementation remain governed by the ordinary official-worship and substantial-pressure rules.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Kennedy | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Affirms | Full opinion, including the statutory, contextual and independent-pressure limits. |

## Holdings

### Genuine private choice during the required silent interval

**Controlling proposition:** A daily public-school minute requiring seated silence and no distracting display, while leaving each student genuinely free to pray, reflect or pursue another silent activity without requiring religious expression or disclosure, does not establish religion when the activity does not interfere with others' corresponding choice and the State neither directs devotion nor imposes substantial religious pressure. Virginia's statute and the neutral implementation described here satisfy that rule; permission for private prayer and religious motivation among supporters do not by themselves turn the required silence into official worship.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Opinion of the Court is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine directly support this holding and its limits.

**Controlling explanation:** Under Lemon as modified by Agostini: specific prohibitions and distinct doctrines govern conduct they resolve, an established permissible relationship governs at its actual scope, and otherwise attribution and function determine whether government exercises religious authority. Purpose, effects and institutional relationships are evidence within that inquiry, not independent fallback prongs.

Chaudhuri permits genuine silence leaving religious thought to the participant. Its university setting does not eliminate scrutiny of compulsory classrooms and younger children. Lee forbids official worship and independently protects against substantial school-created religious pressure; Santa Fe rejects delegation of the school's religious choice even before a prayer is delivered. Good News Club distinguishes private expression from governmental adoption, but its voluntary after-school forum does not decide this compulsory-school question.

Here the teacher maintains silence without selecting a devotional activity, speaker or text. No child must pray, reveal a belief or visibly refuse an identified religious observance. Administrative instructions prohibit teacher recommendations to pray, audible devotion and student coercion; those directions describe operation, not additional statutory language. Affidavits concerning religions that use movement or sound do not establish a command to practice silent prayer or a denominational classification. The law leaves nonreligious thought equally private.

The prayer-related preamble, retained prayer reference, mandatory amendment, legislative statements, accompanying resolution and Attorney General defense duty receive consideration. They do not establish devotional government conduct in this arrangement. The defense duty directs litigation, not belief. Even considering the disputed press quotations, the result is unchanged, so their admissibility need not be decided. A secular objective could not excuse actual official worship. The objection that neutral words conceal religious solicitation therefore requires examination of operation and context; it does not justify treating all required silence as religious observance.

**Limits and application:** The facial challenge fails on this statutory and implementation record. Teacher-directed devotion, religious evaluation of students and substantial pressure to pray or refrain remain subject to review. A completed coercive episode is unnecessary when the official program itself prescribes worship. A particular coercive classroom practice requires facts absent from this facial record; no distinct free-exercise claim or categorical immunity for silence laws is decided.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Lemon v. Kurtzman, 403 U.S. 602 (1971), and Agostini v. Felton, June 23, 1997:** Apply the governing unified inquiry into governmental religious authority and function; the former independent purpose, effect and entanglement prongs do not return.
- **Chaudhuri v. Tennessee, April 27, 1998:** Applies its genuine-silence and private-choice rule while separately examining the compulsory school setting and younger audience.
- **Lee v. Weisman, 505 U.S. 577 (1992):** Preserves both the independent prohibition on official worship and protection against substantial school-created religious pressure; optionality or silence during an actual prayer would not automatically remove either defect.
- **Santa Fe Independent School District v. Doe, 530 U.S. 290 (2000):** Distinguishes an official representative religious-choice mechanism from this private-thought interval; a delivered prayer is not a prerequisite to finding an unlawful official program.
- **Good News Club v. Milford Central School, 533 U.S. 98 (2001):** Uses its distinction between private expression and governmental adoption without transferring its after-school forum result to compulsory classrooms.
- **Wallace v. Jaffree, 472 U.S. 38 (1985):** Retains its significance in identifying religious solicitation from statutory history and context; its independent-purpose method does not displace Agostini's current inquiry. This different statutory and operational record establishes genuine private silence.

## Law After Decision

Genuine school silence leaving thought private is constitutionally permissible under the stated conditions. Religious supporters, an express permission to pray and compulsory silence do not themselves establish official worship. Courts must still examine actual governmental function, attribution and substantial pressure in the school setting. Neither secular objectives nor neutral statutory wording protects actual directed devotion. The decision sustains this facial arrangement without restoring independent Lemon prongs or establishing immunity for later administration.

# Delaney v. DeTella; reviewed Seventh Circuit No. 00-4145; 256 F.3d 679 — 2002-03-04

[Authoritative Record](../records/delaney-v-detella_merits_2002-03-04.md)

## Event

Delaney v. DeTella; reviewed Seventh Circuit No. 00-4145, 256 F.3d 679. Merits decision, March 4, 2002; October Term 2001, chunk 3. Submitted on the full merits record. On review of the Seventh Circuit's July 9, 2001 judgment affirming denial of qualified immunity to the warden and six guards. The questions are whether a knowing, unjustified six-month near-total exercise deprivation satisfies the Eighth Amendment and whether the prohibition was sufficiently clear in 1996. The officials seek judgment ending the personal-capacity damages claims; Delaney seeks further proceedings. The legal review assumes the plaintiff-favorable account: prolonged confinement in a cell about 122 inches long and 43–56 inches wide, no meaningful exercise alternative, repeated complaints and substantial physical and psychological effects. ## Participation

All nine Justices participated at submission and decision: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

## Public Action

The legal denial of qualified immunity is affirmed. Disputes about what the evidence proves remain outside this interlocutory review.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed at the legal assumed-fact scope, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The case returns for ordinary proceedings under the stated constitutional and immunity rules. Each official retains individualized defenses concerning actual knowledge, authority, reasonable response, causation and the injury required for the relief sought. The assumed facts are not findings, and later summary judgment remains available on a proper record.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Souter | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports the judgment | Entire opinion: legal appellate scope, application of binding and unchallenged Farmer, particularized 1996 notice and ordinary further proceedings |

## Holdings

**Common authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court is joined by all eight other Justices. That direct unanimous coalition controls each proposition below. The constitutional section applies the binding, unchallenged Farmer framework; it does not independently enlarge what qualifies as punishment.

### I. Legal immunity review uses assumed facts

**Holding and operative rule:** An immediate qualified-immunity appeal may decide whether the conduct on properly assumed plaintiff-favorable facts violated clearly established law. It may not resolve genuine disputes about injury, awareness, causation or an official's participation or authority; those factual objections do not eliminate a separable legal issue.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the officials' legal immunity claim is reviewable at this limited scope. Johnson v. Jones excludes appeals asking only whether evidence creates a genuine dispute. Behrens preserves legal review on proper assumptions without permitting fact selection. The officials may argue that the assumed conduct was lawful or insufficiently clear, but may not obtain immunity by substituting their account of Delaney's condition or their powers. The Court therefore decides the legal question while leaving the evidentiary disputes to the proper forum.

### II. The assumed deprivation satisfies binding Farmer

**Holding and operative rule:** Under the binding and unchallenged Farmer framework, prolonged denial of meaningful exercise in confinement inadequate for it violates the Eighth Amendment when it creates a substantial risk of serious harm that the responsible official actually appreciates and unreasonably disregards. The assumed six-month deprivation, known serious effects and lack of a reasonable response satisfy that legal standard; negligence, collective knowledge and duration alone do not.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court applies Farmer's separate risk, knowledge and response requirements. Wilson supplies the distinction between serious conditions and culpable administration; Farmer permits ordinary circumstantial proof, including obviousness and repeated complaints, without compelling an inference of knowledge. The cramped cell and shackled trips, on the assumed account, offered no meaningful exercise alternative. A known substantial health danger need not mature into grave injury before it can violate the governing rule.

Prison security remains material. Short restrictions, a genuine unavoidable security necessity, an acute risk posed by the particular prisoner, or reasonable alternative exercise or protective measures require their own facts. But a general lockdown label does not explain why all meaningful exercise had to be withheld for six months despite the known danger. A reasonable response defeats deliberate-indifference liability even if harm occurs. The prison's five-hour directive is not itself the constitutional measure, and the Court imposes neither a nationwide hour quota nor a requirement that officials always choose the least restrictive regimen. Each defendant's actual awareness and responsibility remain necessary.

### III. The assumed violation was sufficiently clear in 1996

**Holding and operative rule:** On facts establishing actual awareness of a substantial health danger from six months without meaningful exercise and an unreasonable failure to respond, the law existing in 1996 gave sufficient notice to defeat qualified immunity. The conclusion depends on those concrete conditions and each official's knowledge and role; it does not remove protection for materially different conditions, reasonable security responses or absent personal responsibility.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the right was established with the particularity Anderson v. Creighton requires. Farmer supplied the actual-awareness and reasonable-response rule. Davenport addressed prolonged exercise deprivation among segregated prisoners at this institution; French and Jamison-Bey reinforced the serious danger. Anderson v. Romero preserved an acute-security-risk qualification, not a general authorization for the assumed six-month deprivation. Decisions involving shorter restrictions, meaningful alternatives or particular danger therefore do not justify this conduct on the assumed facts.

Elder requires consideration of all relevant preconduct law. It does not require an earlier judgment with identical cell dimensions and duration, and circuit authority can establish notice without becoming a nationwide constitutional quota. Harlow's objective protection remains a distinct inquiry from constitutional sufficiency. The officials' asserted lack of power may establish a personal defense on the actual record; it cannot supply a favorable fact on this appeal. Later decisions furnish no retrospective notice. The affirmed legal denial thus preserves ordinary proceedings and the individualized factual and remedial inquiries rather than compelling liability or trial.

- Farmer v. Brennan: applied as binding conditions law; actual awareness, permissible circumstantial proof and reasonable-response protection remain intact.
- Wilson v. Seiter: applied to keep seriousness and culpability distinct; harm alone does not establish a violation.
- Harlow v. Fitzgerald and Anderson v. Creighton: their objective, particularized personal defense is retained; no actual-authoritative-reliance substitute is adopted.
- Elder v. Holloway: applied to all relevant law existing when the officials acted; no identical-case or later-notice rule follows.
- Johnson v. Jones and Behrens v. Pelletier: applied to preserve assumed-fact legal review and exclude evidentiary-sufficiency review.

# Searles v. Van Bebber; 251 F.3d 869 (10th Cir. 2001) — 2002-01-08

[Authoritative Record](../records/searles-v-van-bebber_merits_2002-01-08.md)

## Event

Searles v. Van Bebber. Merits decision, January 8, 2002. Review of the Tenth Circuit judgment reported at 251 F.3d 869. The questions concern original 42 U.S.C. §1997e(e)'s restriction on mental or emotional compensation absent physical injury, the availability of nominal and punitive damages for the proved religious-diet violation, the consequence of a punitive instruction tied to invalid compensation; and the preserved court-access and due-process objection to the restriction at this remedial scope. The jury found liability, no physical injury and a separate culpable state of mind supporting punitive eligibility; it awarded $3,650 compensation and $42,500 punishment and returned verdicts for the other officials.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument or submission and decision.

## Public Action

Affirm the reviewed remedial judgment, 9–0.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 9–0, at the reviewed remedial scope.** Each component is supported by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

| Judgment component | Disposition | Operative remedy |
|---|---|---|
| Mental or emotional compensation | Affirm vacatur | The $3,650 award is vacated |
| Nominal damages | Affirm entitlement | Enter one dollar for the established violation |
| Punitive availability and culpability | Affirm | Punitive relief remains available; the separate culpability finding survives |
| Punitive amount | Affirm vacatur and remand | The $42,500 amount is vacated; determine punishment under instructions independent of the invalid compensation |
| Dependent attorney fees | Affirm reconsideration | Reconsider fees in light of lawful recovery |
| Preserved court-access and due-process objection | Reject | No additional constitutional relief; Stone-Zsela agrees in this component's disposition without joining its rationale |

Constitutional liability, the no-physical-injury finding, the punitive-culpability finding and the judgments for the other officials remain intact.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Scalia | Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer throughout; Stone-Zsela except the constitutional-objection holding | All nine support every judgment component | All nine join statutory compensation, nominal and punitive holdings and remedies; eight Associates join court-access and due-process rationale; Stone-Zsela agrees only in that component's disposition |

## Holdings

### The named injury restriction bars the compensatory award, not every remedy

**Holding and operative rule:** Original 42 U.S.C. §1997e(e) bars an action by a prisoner confined at filing for mental or emotional injury suffered in custody without the required prior showing of physical injury. That restriction bars the actual distress compensation awarded here even though the underlying wrong is constitutional; it does not erase the violation or compensation-independent remedies.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join this statutory holding in the Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress restricted recovery for a named injury category. Harris v. Garner establishes the confinement-at-filing trigger and the mental-or-emotional-injury limit; it does not convert that limit into immunity from every remedy. The jury found no physical injury, and the $3,650 award compensated mental or emotional harm. The religious source of the right does not exempt that recovery. Carey and Stachura also prevent compensation based merely on an abstract valuation of the constitutional right. The all-damages reading goes too far because it ignores the distinct functions of nominal recognition and punishment, while the contrary compensation argument cannot save a jury award for the expressly barred injury.

**Precedent treatment:** Harris is applied at its actual injury-and-filing scope; Carey and Stachura retain the distinction between a violation and compensable loss.

### Nominal damages recognize the established violation

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1997e(e) does not bar nominal damages recognizing an established constitutional violation without compensable injury. On the preserved violation and lack of compensable injury here, the plaintiff is entitled to one dollar.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join this holding in the Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** Nominal relief does not compensate the mental or emotional injury named in the statute. Carey permits recognition of an established constitutional wrong without inventing injury or valuing the right itself as compensatory loss. The preserved liability verdict therefore requires the one-dollar award even though the distress compensation cannot stand.

**Precedent treatment:** Carey v. Piphus supplies nominal recognition; Stachura's prohibition on abstract-right compensation remains.

### Punitive eligibility survives, but the amount requires a lawful redetermination

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1997e(e) does not categorically bar punitive damages for conduct satisfying Smith v. Wade's evil-motive-or-intent or reckless-or-callous-indifference standard. Punishment remains discretionary even when that culpability is established; an amount expressly tied to invalid compensation must be redetermined independently, preserving separate liability and culpability findings.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join this holding and remedy in the Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** Punitive damages punish culpable conduct and deter its repetition; they do not compensate distress. Smith supplies a distinct remedial predicate rather than a rule that punitive fault must invariably exceed every substantive violation standard. The jury separately made the required culpability finding, which survives the compensation defect. But the instruction expressly relied on the invalid compensatory award in setting the punitive amount. That linkage prevents preserving $42,500 as an independently sound calculation. The corrected proceeding concerns amount, without retrying established liability or punitive culpability. A positive punitive award is not compelled, punishment may not reproduce barred compensation, and no automatic punitive-to-nominal ratio applies. Fees must then be reconsidered in light of the lawful recovery.

**Precedent treatment:** Smith v. Wade's culpability requirement and discretionary punishment are applied; the distinct remedial functions recognized in Carey and Stachura prevent relabeling barred compensation.

### The preserved constitutional objection fails at this remedial scope

**Holding and operative rule:** Applying the physical-injury restriction to this distress award does not deny access to court or due process where the established right remains enforceable through nominal relief and punitive relief on its distinct predicates. This ruling resolves the preserved challenge in these circumstances, without validating every possible application of the statute.

**Authority:** Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer join this portion of the Opinion of the Court. Stone-Zsela agrees in its disposition only.

**Controlling explanation:** The statute restricts one injury-based recovery rather than withdrawing adjudication of the underlying right. Searles has established liability, receives nominal relief and retains punitive relief subject to its lawful standard. At this actual scope, the constitutional objection therefore supplies no reason to restore barred compensation or upset the preserved remedial distinctions.

**Precedent treatment:** Harris's bounded restriction and Carey and Smith's distinct remedies operate together; no remedy-wide immunity follows.

## Precedent Treatment

- Harris v. Garner: confinement at filing and the restriction to mental or emotional injury remain controlling; the nominal and punitive questions it left open are resolved here.
- Carey v. Piphus, 435 U.S. 247: nominal damages recognize a proved wrong without compensable injury.
- Memphis Community School District v. Stachura, 477 U.S. 299: abstract value of a constitutional right cannot substitute for actual compensatory injury.
- Smith v. Wade, 461 U.S. 30: evil motive or intent, or reckless or callous indifference, supplies punitive culpability; a qualifying award remains discretionary.

## Law After Decision

Original section 1997e(e) bars the named distress compensation absent physical injury while preserving nominal recognition and punitive relief under their own predicates. Established liability and punitive culpability survive a separate damages-calculation defect. The lawful punitive amount and consequential fee decision remain ordinary remedial work under those settled rules.

## Procedure After Action

# Thomas v. Chicago Park District; No. 00-1249; 534 U.S. 316. — 2002-01-15

[Authoritative Record](../records/thomas-v-chicago-park-district_merits_2002-01-15.md)

## Event

Thomas v. Chicago Park District, No. 00-1249; 534 U.S. 316. Merits decision, January 15, 2002; October Term 2001, chunk 2. Argued December 3, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Seventh Circuit. The ordinance requires permits for events involving more than fifty people and activities including amplified sound. Applicants seeking expressive events, including marijuana-policy advocacy, challenge its facial standards and procedures. The Seventh Circuit sustained the system. The questions concern the full censorship safeguards, constrained discretion, and timely action and review.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

## Public Action

The Seventh Circuit judgment rejecting the facial challenge is affirmed.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The Seventh Circuit's rejection of the facial challenge stands, and the permit ordinance remains operative. Properly presented challenges to discriminatory, delayed or content-based administration retain their ordinary availability; no particular permit denial is adjudicated here.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Scalia | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Affirms the facial judgment | All three holdings, thirteen qualified denial grounds, actual deadlines/review and limits on administration |

## Holdings

### I. Neutral park coordination does not require the entire censorship procedure

**Holding and operative rule:** A genuinely content-neutral permit system for allocating shared park space and coordinating activities need not adopt Freedman's entire procedure for censorship of expressive content, including a requirement that the government initiate judicial proceedings and bear the censorship burden after each denial. Such a system still requires real constraints on discretion, bounded administrative action and meaningful access to review; a logistical label alone does not establish validity.

**Authority:** Justice Scalia's opinion is joined throughout by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine directly adopt this and the following two holdings with their qualifications.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the safeguards must address the function officials may exercise. Freedman concerns official censorship of acceptable expressive content. Allocating park uses under definite scheduling, capacity and safety rules presents a different recurring risk. Cox recognizes neutral coordination of shared public space; Ward requires actual content independence rather than a benign label. The need to obtain a permit does not itself erase that distinction.

The challengers correctly stress that delay and favoritism can suppress speech even without an express message category. The answer is not to presume good administration, but to examine the authorized criteria and the concrete timing and review protections. Here those protections constrain park management rather than authorize approval of ideas. Requiring the District to commence a judicial case after every scheduling refusal would import a censorship procedure without its predicate. Freedman and FW/PBS remain in force in their own settings. Because the full censorship procedure does not apply, the Court need not decide whether Freedman's expeditious-review requirement there demands prompt commencement of proceedings or a prompt final judicial determination.

### II. The ordinance confines denial to objective management grounds

**Holding and operative rule:** The ordinance's two qualified prior-conduct predicates and eleven further grounds, set out below, sufficiently confine denial to neutral park-management subjects for this facial challenge. It permits denial on a listed ground but does not require denial whenever a ground occurs; permission to excuse a disqualifying circumstance authorizes neither an invented ground nor selective treatment by viewpoint.

**Authority:** The unanimous Court opinion adopts this standards determination and its limits.

**Denial grounds governed by this holding:**

| Ordinance basis | Permitted ground, with its condition |
|---|---|
| Prior conduct | To the extent permitted by law, prior material misrepresentation about the nature or scope of a permitted event, or violation of an earlier permit's terms, by the applicant or the person represented. |
| 1 | An application, including required attachments and submissions, is incompletely filled out or executed. |
| 2 | The application fee was not tendered with the application, or the user fee, indemnification agreement, insurance certificate or security deposit was not tendered within the General Superintendent's prescribed times. |
| 3 | The application contains a material falsehood or misrepresentation. |
| 4 | The applicant lacks legal capacity to contract or to sue and be sued. |
| 5 | The applicant or represented person has unpaid prior damage to District property or another outstanding unpaid District debt. |
| 6 | An earlier fully executed application for the same time and place has been received, and a permit has been or will be granted for uses that do not reasonably allow multiple occupancy. |
| 7 | The intended activity conflicts with a District-organized and conducted program already planned and scheduled for that time and place. |
| 8 | The proposed activity is prohibited by or inconsistent with the park's designated classification and uses under section C.1. |
| 9 | The intended activity presents an unreasonable health or safety danger to the applicant, other park users, District employees or the public. |
| 10 | The applicant has not complied or cannot comply with applicable licensing requirements, District ordinances or regulations governing sales or offers of goods or services. |
| 11 | Law, District code or ordinances, or the General Superintendent's regulations prohibit the intended activity. |

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the grounds must be understood with the receipt-order requirement, written reasons and review. Forsyth, Shuttlesworth and City of Lakewood require definite limits on expressive licensing rather than confidence in official goodwill. These provisions identify the matters officials may assess and permit a reviewing court to identify a refusal resting on some different reason.

The strongest objection concerns the judgment involved in assessing danger, applying cross-references or forgiving defects. But permission to decline denial does not confer permission to invent a disqualifying reason. Nor must every technical defect compel refusal to avoid facial invalidity. The criteria remain neutral management limits; they do not authorize message approval, viewpoint favoritism or an audience-hostility burden. Forsyth's separate rule against hostility-based fees continues to govern any covered fee. This case does not adjudicate an unpresented fee amount or validate every financial condition in every application. A materially different or discriminatory administration remains subject to challenge on its own facts, without a new numerical-pattern prerequisite to proving viewpoint discrimination.

### III. The actual deadlines, reasons and review support the facial judgment

**Holding and operative rule:** The District must decide within fourteen days unless written notice extends the period for fourteen additional days; a denial must state its grounds in writing, propose feasible cures, and suggest alternative times or places when based on an earlier competing application. An applicant has seven days to appeal in writing to the General Superintendent, who must act within seven days, followed by available state common-law certiorari review; together with the objective grounds, these protections sustain the ordinance against the facial challenge.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join this procedural-sufficiency holding and affirmance.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that these actual protections answer the presented structural objection without a universal rule for every neutral permit system. Receipt order governs processing; the bounded decision period prevents open-ended administrative withholding; written reasons identify the decision for challenge; feasible correction and alternative scheduling address curable defects; and administrative and judicial review provide avenues to contest an improper refusal. Content neutrality alone would not cure unrestricted discretion or ineffective review.

No automatic-issuance rule is established on this record, and no fixed court-adjudication deadline is supplied. The Court adds neither. It also does not resolve an actual late application or discriminatory denial absent a proper claim and record. Those are applications of continuing First Amendment constraints, not a license for indefinite delay. The Seventh Circuit's facial judgment is affirmed, leaving the ordinance operative while preserving properly presented claims about actual administration.

The same structure remains subject to meaningful review and to properly presented challenges to viewpoint discrimination, actual delay or other unauthorized administration. No automatic-issuance rule or universal court-decision deadline is established.

# United States v. Knights; No. 00-1260 — 2001-12-10

[Authoritative Record](../records/united-states-v-knights_merits_2001-12-10.md)

## Event

United States v. Knights, No. 00-1260. Merits decision, December 10, 2001. Argued November 6, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, which affirmed suppression because the residence search pursued ordinary criminal investigation. The question is whether a known probation search condition and individualized reasonable suspicion justify the search notwithstanding that purpose. The written condition permitted searches by probation or law-enforcement officers, and the detective had reasonable suspicion connecting the residence to criminal activity.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument or submission and decision.

## Public Action

Reverse and remand, 9–0.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.** Supporting: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. The categorical investigative-purpose ground for suppression is rejected. The lower courts apply the condition-and-reasonable-suspicion rule; evidence is admissible if no independently preserved defect remains. Any genuinely open scope or factual issue must be resolved on its own record.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Scalia | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports the complete judgment | Entire opinion |

## Holdings

### The known search condition and individualized suspicion justify this probation search

**Holding and operative rule:** A warrantless search of a probationer's residence within a known, lawful search condition is reasonable when supported by particularized reasonable suspicion of criminal activity in the circumstances presented. Ordinary criminal-investigative purpose does not independently invalidate that combination; neither the condition alone nor reasonable suspicion alone supplies this holding.

**Authority:** The unanimous Opinion of the Court; all nine Justices directly adopt this rule, its justification, and its limits.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the probation relationship and notice of the express condition reduce the privacy interest implicated by this residential search, while individualized suspicion supplies an objective justification for this intrusion. Griffin establishes that lawful probation supervision changes the constitutional setting; it does not make its special-needs rationale the exclusive ground for every probation search. Knights's proposed purpose restriction would invalidate a search supported by this combined justification merely because the officer investigates crime. The Fourth Amendment does not require that result here.

Whren supports examining the objective justification after authority for the intrusion has been established. Its ordinary probable-cause traffic rule does not itself confer residential search power or decide motive doctrine for every search based on reasonable suspicion. Edmond and Ferguson retain their purpose inquiries in their suspicionless and special-needs settings. Scott governs the consequence of an unconstitutional supervisory search, not the antecedent validity of this condition-and-suspicion search; its exclusion rule remains intact. Any ultimate suspicion question properly remaining is independently reviewed under Ornelas, with its protection for historical findings and due weight to grounded contextual inferences.

The holding rests on reasonableness, not complete contractual consent or forfeiture of Fourth Amendment protection. Suspicionless residential searches and blanket consent are outside the question necessarily resolved because individualized suspicion supplies the sufficient ground here.

**Precedent treatment:** Griffin's supervisory-relationship principle is applied without making its rationale exclusive; Whren's objective method is applied within its source-specific limits; Edmond and Ferguson are distinguished; Scott's exclusion rule and Ornelas's review allocation remain operative.

## Precedent Treatment

- Griffin v. Wisconsin, 483 U.S. 868: the lawful supervisory relationship informs diminished privacy; special needs is not the exclusive possible justification.
- Whren v. United States, 517 U.S. 806: objective assessment follows a demonstrated source of search authority; its traffic holding is not generally extended to every reasonable-suspicion search.
- City of Indianapolis v. Edmond, 531 U.S. 32, and Ferguson v. City of Charleston, 532 U.S. 67: purpose remains material in their distinct suspicionless and special-needs settings.
- Pennsylvania Board of Probation & Parole v. Scott, 524 U.S. 357: exclusion for the institution's unconstitutional supervisory search remains unchanged; this decision finds the bounded search reasonable.
- Ornelas v. United States, 517 U.S. 690: the division between factual and ultimate legal review is applied unchanged.

The Fourth Amendment permits the defined probation-condition and individualized-suspicion combination despite ordinary investigative purpose. Existing scope, execution and exclusion rules retain their separate operation; the decision creates no general suspicionless-search or consent-waiver rule.

# United States v. Arvizu; No. 00-1519; 534 U.S. 266. — 2002-01-15

[Authoritative Record](../records/united-states-v-arvizu_merits_2002-01-15.md)

## Event

United States v. Arvizu, No. 00-1519; 534 U.S. 266. Merits decision, January 15, 2002; October Term 2001, chunk 2. Argued November 27, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 232 F.3d 1241, as amended. The district court rejected the stop and voluntary-consent challenges; Arvizu's conditional plea preserved suppression review. The Ninth Circuit held the stop unlawful and treated the ensuing search as tainted. The questions are the proper totality method and whether the supported pre-stop combination establishes reasonable suspicion. The United States seeks restoration of suppression denial; Arvizu seeks exclusion.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

## Public Action

The Ninth Circuit judgment resting on an unlawful stop is reversed, and the case is remanded for the preserved independent consent issues.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The stop was supported by reasonable suspicion. The Ninth Circuit must address the remaining preserved consent-voluntariness and scope issues under their applicable review. The unlawful-stop taint ground falls, but the conviction and complete suppression denial are not finally restored without that review.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | O'Connor | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Reverses unlawful-stop ruling and returns preserved independent consent issues | All four holdings; Scalia joins the governing Ornelas application while separately retaining his review objection |
| Concurrence | Scalia | No other Justice | Joins opinion and judgment in full | Criticism of inferential deference within independent review; same result under traditional independent review |

## Holdings

### I. Supported observations must be considered in their actual relationship

**Holding and operative rule:** Reasonable suspicion for an investigative stop depends on an objective, particularized inference from the total circumstances known before the seizure; an observation cannot be excluded merely because it has an innocent explanation or would be insufficient alone. Courts must still test each proposed inference's factual basis and actual weight, so neither the number of observations nor an officer's unexplained intuition supplies the required justification.

**Authority:** Justice O'Connor's opinion is joined throughout by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine directly adopt all four holdings and their limits; Scalia separately qualifies the review discussion without withholding his application join.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that context may make several lawful observations informative together. Terry requires articulable suspicion of wrongdoing; Cortez requires the whole picture and an inference particular to the person or vehicle stopped. Wardlow likewise permits contextual consideration without making location or ambiguous behavior automatically sufficient. Innocence of each isolated act does not compel a conclusion about the connected pattern.

The amended Ninth Circuit opinion acknowledged totality and softened some earlier formulations. It nevertheless categorically discounted the children's posture and waving and separated observations before assessing their relationship. That method removed potentially probative context too early. Arvizu's profiling objection remains substantial: every response cannot become suspicious merely because an officer says experience makes it so. The court must identify supported connections and may assign little or no weight to an asserted factor. The totality rule is not a command that every fact count positively, and the later discovery of marijuana cannot justify the earlier seizure.

### II. Ultimate sufficiency receives independent appellate judgment

**Holding and operative rule:** Under Ornelas, appellate courts independently determine ultimate reasonable suspicion while reviewing historical facts, credibility and genuinely factual subsidiary inferences for clear error, and giving genuine due weight to grounded local and experiential inferences. That contextual weight does not create deference or a presumption of correctness for the officer's ultimate constitutional conclusion.

**Authority:** All nine join this application of the governing Ornelas allocation. Scalia's concurrence preserves his objection to that allocation's inferential formulation without withholding a present join.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that review must preserve both the trial court's factual role and the appellate court's responsibility for constitutional sufficiency. Ornelas supplies that division. Local knowledge and experience can explain the significance of an observed route or pattern; they cannot manufacture a fact or insulate the conclusion that the seizure was lawful. Describing what the children did and deciding what constitutional significance their conduct has are not the same task.

Scalia objects that deference to an inferential conclusion can compromise genuinely independent review. The controlling formulation protects genuinely factual subsidiary judgments and gives contextual understanding actual weight while reserving the ultimate sufficiency decision to the reviewing court. It adopts no intermediate standard or numerical formula. The supported pre-stop findings here permit that legal application now; another remand simply to redo the stop comparison is unnecessary.

### III. This connected pre-stop pattern supplied reasonable suspicion

**Holding and operative rule:** The sensor-linked checkpoint-bypass route, final bypass turn, timing context and particular occupant and apparent-cargo observations, assessed together with their ordinary explanations and the agent's actual experience, supplied a particularized basis for this brief investigative vehicle stop. The holding does not require a traffic offense, direct contraband observation or proof that criminal activity was more likely than an innocent explanation, and does not treat any one greeting, vehicle type or neighborhood as independently sufficient.

**Authority:** All nine join this record-specific sufficiency holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the connection among the observations distinguishes this case from a general profile. Sensors tracked travel along a route capable of avoiding an operating checkpoint, and the final turn took the last bypass road. The approaching staffing transition supplied relevant local context. The driver's slowing and stiffness, combined with the children's persistent forward-facing waving and raised knees, supported a limited inference of concealed cargo and an attempted appearance of normality. Neither a greeting nor an acknowledgment of an officer is legally required; the behavior matters only within that route-and-load pattern.

The opposing account has force. The road serves homes, recreation and tourists; a minivan is ordinary family transport; children may wave or rest their feet on luggage; and the shift change was still about forty-five minutes away. The agent had local patrol and alien-stop experience, not a personal history of drug stops. These qualifications reduce the weight but do not eliminate the supported relationship. A similar minivan's drug stop one month earlier does not taint all minivans. Registration to a relative in a broadly described smuggling neighborhood contributes little without a particular connection and is unnecessary to the sufficient combination. The Court presumes neither knowledge of the staffing schedule nor erratic or unlawful driving.

Wardlow's actual insufficiency holding involved a materially thinner departure-and-location record. Here the sensor-tracked bypass and passenger/cargo observations provide the additional individualized connection. The conclusion authorizes the brief stop, not a search of the vehicle or its bags, and rests exclusively on information preceding the seizure.

### IV. The lawful stop removes taint but does not resolve independent consent issues

**Holding and operative rule:** The Ninth Circuit's judgment resting on the stop's illegality is reversed, and the case is remanded for proper appellate treatment of the preserved independent consent-voluntariness and scope issues. A lawful stop removes that taint ground but neither establishes voluntary consent nor determines its extent; the claimed exterior-only permission and disputed gun-hand interaction remain subject to their applicable review and preservation limits.

**Authority:** All nine adopt this complete disposition and bounded remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that stop justification and search authorization remain distinct. The district court rejected both the stop and voluntary-consent challenges, and the conditional plea preserved suppression review. The Ninth Circuit's unlawful-stop analysis treated the ensuing search as tainted without deciding the independent consent grounds. Reversing the stop ruling therefore does not finally restore the conviction or conclusively sustain the whole suppression denial. The receiving court must give the trial court's findings their applicable review and address the consent questions actually preserved.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1:** Applied for the objective, particularized reasonable-suspicion threshold and limited investigative-stop authority.
- **United States v. Cortez, 449 U.S. 411:** Applied for assessment of the whole picture and rational, individualized connections among observations.
- **Ornelas v. United States, 517 U.S. 690:** Applied for independent ultimate review, clear-error protection of facts and genuine contextual weight without a police-correctness presumption; no new review standard is adopted.
- **Illinois v. Wardlow, January 12, 2000:** Its contextual method is applied; its actual insufficiency holding is distinguished by the additional sensor-linked bypass and vehicle-specific observations here. Its rejection of categorical location or flight permission remains intact.
- **Florida v. J. L., March 28, 2000:** Its requirement of sufficient grounds before intrusion and rejection of retrospective contraband justification remain effective.
- **United States v. Knights, December 10, 2001:** Distinguished because the probation status and search condition there are absent; its confined holding supplies no ordinary-driver exception.

## Law After Decision

Supported observations capable of innocent explanations retain their actual contextual force but gain no automatic positive weight by accumulation. Independent judicial review determines whether their relationships supply individualized suspicion. This pre-stop combination meets that standard without neighborhood stigma, proof of an observed offense or post-stop contraband. The lawful-stop conclusion leaves the independent authority for the ensuing search to the preserved consent questions.

## Separate Writings

**Justice Scalia, concurring, joined by no other Justice.** He joins the Court's full application of governing law and its reversal and bounded remand. He retains his Ornelas dissent's preference for deferential review and his objection that giving weight to an inferential conclusion can be conceptually inconsistent with independently deciding the ultimate issue. A factual description of the children's waving can receive factual protection; whether it is suspicious cannot be sheltered from constitutional judgment simply by calling it an officer inference. Reversal follows here even under traditional independent review. His join applies Ornelas as governing law without endorsing its original choice of standard, dispensing with innocent explanations or authorizing the subsequent search.

## Procedure After Action

The Ninth Circuit must give the trial court's findings their applicable review and resolve the preserved consent-voluntariness and scope questions. The reasonable-suspicion issue is settled in favor of the stop. The lawful-stop conclusion does not itself finally restore the conviction or resolve the remaining request for suppression.

## Holdings

### Congress may change the standards for future prison relief

**Holding and operative rule:** The PLRA may condition continued prospective prison relief on its termination and preservation criteria without reopening accrued damages or directing a finding that prison conditions are lawful. The State's consent to an earlier decree, and the prisoners' compromise in obtaining it, do not by themselves estop the State from invoking the later statute or create a vested right to unchanged future enforcement; courts must still afford a meaningful opportunity to present necessary proof and decide the statutory case.

**Authority:** Scalia's opinion, joined by all eight other Justices, controls this holding and the two holdings below at their stated scope.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an enforceable decree does not freeze the law governing its future operation. Plyler and Rouse distinguish prospective judicial duties from completed adjudication and accrued rights. Robertson permits Congress to change the governing legal criteria while leaving courts to apply them. Benjamin, Gavin and Hadix require that judicial application actually occur; they reject both perpetual enforcement of every negotiated term and termination without the required inquiry.

Texas's agreement to obey the decree does not establish a separate promise never to invoke a later statute. Consent decrees are among the instruments Congress subjected to termination. The negotiated exchange therefore supplies no exemption, and labeling future obligations contractual does not make them survive automatically. No distinct affirmative undertaking or independent accrued claim is established here.

The prisoners' general Article III and vested-right objections fail. That conclusion does not adopt the lower court's broader suggestion that prospective relief can never implicate due process. A concrete inability to offer necessary material proof would require review on its own facts. The reported fifty-hour-per-side hearing limit, without identification of a necessary excluded presentation and its consequence, establishes no such denial. Miller concerns an automatic stay under a different provision; it neither exempts this decree from termination law nor supplies a stay here.

### Retention requires actual findings connecting relief to a qualifying violation

**Holding and operative rule:** A movant must establish the applicable termination ground: section 3626(b)(1)(A)(iii) independently reaches pre-enactment relief after the statutory two-year period, while section 3626(b)(2) turns on the absence of substantive original findings of need, narrowness and least intrusiveness, not missing ritual words. Under amended subsection (b)(3), each retained obligation, or coherently explained group, requires written, record-based findings of a current and ongoing federal-right violation, necessity, narrowness and the least intrusive necessary correction; generalized injury findings alone do not sustain every old term, and deficient remedial findings alone do not establish entitlement to automatic wholesale termination.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the statutory conditions are mandatory and their application is a judicial task. Gavin and Hadix require a genuine connection between the violation and the relief retained. Rouse permits sufficient substantive findings without prescribed language. Adequate original findings can defeat subsection (b)(2)'s predicate without defeating an independently available elapsed-period motion under subsection (b)(1). Both routes remain subject to subsection (b)(3).

The amendment applicable to pending cases requires a violation that is both current and ongoing. Plyler's treatment of the original disjunctive text does not erase that change. This record establishes no violation excluded solely by the textual change, so the constitutional question about that particular operation is unnecessary to decision.

The 1999 findings identified constitutional injuries but did not adequately explain the fit of every retained decree duty. March's return for that explanation was proper. A court need not repeat identical reasoning under separate headings where one supported explanation covers a coherent group. It must nevertheless show why each obligation retained is necessary. A remedy may go beyond a minimum conduct rule, or operate throughout an institution, when the proved injury and practical remedial need support that scope; intrusion alone is no reason to leave a violation uncorrected when lesser measures cannot work.

Benjamin requires a genuine opportunity for necessary current proof when it has been refused. Rouse rejects an automatic demand for a new hearing following an actual assessment. Neither general injury findings nor the passage of time resolves that distinction. Retain or tailor duties meeting the test and terminate those that do not; the State's motion is not evidence of constitutional compliance.

### The March judgment is reviewed in light of completed implementation

**Holding and operative rule:** The March appellate judgment is affirmed at the reviewed legal scope, with a return for further proceedings only insofar as they remain necessary and lawfully available after the June 18, 2001 implementation. No duplicate findings inquiry, renewed ninety-day deadline, automatic revival of terminated duties, independent adjudication of June's correctness, or new medical-liability finding follows; any preserved medical issue still requiring disposition must be resolved through its actual procedural route under the governing liability elements.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that both grounds maintaining the injunction were reviewable under section 1292(a)(1). The alternative findings were not an unreviewable contingency: they independently sustained continuing obligations. June's implementation then terminated most old duties, retained narrowed force enforcement and left the class action open for potential new relief. The separate August order concerned a prisoner's appeal from a pro se filing-management order; it did not close this class action. These events do not establish that review of March's governing legal judgment lacks any effect, but they prevent treating its ordered inquiry as wholly unperformed.

The reviewed legal standard is complete. Force, safety, segregation and medical claims retain their own elements. For medical and conditions claims, Estelle distinguishes negligence from constitutional mistreatment, and the existing Farmer framework requires actual awareness and unreasonable disregard while allowing circumstantial proof and recognizing reasonable responses. Outsourcing medical care supplies no categorical defense; serious inadequacy alone supplies no finding of the required culpability. Extreme isolation may establish grave harm on proper evidence, especially for vulnerable prisoners, but its label does not establish every liability or remedial element.

The March opinion supplies no separate reasoned resolution of the medical cross-appeal's evidentiary merits. This decision neither resolves those facts nor treats the absence of a systemwide finding as proof of adequate care everywhere. The return preserves any actual remaining adjudicative task without inventing a pending appeal. New relief under subsection (a) remains distinct from retention under subsection (b); an invitation for proposals is not an entered remedy. Any interim protection requires applicable statutory or equitable authority and supported current danger, not suspension of the PLRA by decree.

## watchtower-bible-tract-society-v-village-of-stratton-merits-2002-06-17

## Event

Watchtower Bible & Tract Society of New York, Inc. v. Village of Stratton, No. 00-1737. Argued February 26, 2002; decided June 17, 2002. Certiorari under §1254(1) to the Sixth Circuit, 240 F.3d 553, limited to petition Question 2 concerning advance permission, identification, anonymity and permit display for door-to-door advocacy. Intended ministry and threatened enforcement support the pre-enforcement challenge. No independent free-exercise, hybrid-rights or vagueness component is before the Court.

Sections 116.01 and 116.03 prohibit covered uninvited entry onto private property or residences to advertise, promote, sell or explain a product, service, organization or cause without prior registration in the mayor's office and a permit. Invited visits are distinct. Registration requires the applicant's name and home address, residences during the preceding five years, a description of the activity and purpose, the employer's or affiliated organization's name and address with credentials showing relationship and authority, requested duration, intended residential addresses, and reasonably necessary additional information about the applicant and purpose.

Section 116.04 requires issuance upon completed registration, subject to §116.06's denial or revocation grounds. Issuance is free and described as routine. A permit holder may not enter an address absent from the registration, must carry the permit while acting under it, and must exhibit it on request by police or a solicited person. A valid permit removes §116.01's prohibition but does not override resident exclusion. The actual permit has no separate personal-name field; the identifying registration is publicly available. Section 116.06 permits denial or revocation for incomplete registration, registration fraud or misrepresentation, fraud, misrepresentation or false statements during activity, violation of the chapter or other local, state or federal law, trespass, or loss of required qualifications. Revocation is additional to applicable penalties. No application, actual denial or revocation by petitioners is established.

Section 116.07 lets an owner or lawful occupant prohibit covered uninvited entry by both registering the property and posting a reasonably visible no-solicitation sign. The registration identifies the owner or occupant and affected addresses, includes a signed exclusion and posting statement, and permits listed exceptions for persons or organizations. Permit holders remain subject to the exclusion. This resident-controlled section is unchallenged. Section 116.99 makes violations of §§116.01, 116.03, 116.07(c) and 116.07(d) fourth-degree misdemeanors; the carry/display rule is considered as dependent permit enforcement, not a separately adjudicated offense.

The district court altered the original hours restriction, required removal of the special Jehovah's Witness category from the resident form, accepted a Village-provided address list and treated the stated ministry as satisfying the additional-information provision. The discarded 5 p.m. cutoff supplies no ground for today's decision. The narrowed ordinance governs the challenge; no precise new hour is decided here.

The Village presented complaints about siding, meat and driveway sales, older residents' vulnerability, limited police resources and testimony about deterrence and investigation. That commercial-fraud record is real; its reach to the broad noncommercial advocacy category is disputed.

## Holdings

#### I. This broad advance-identification and permit prerequisite fails the required fit

**Holding and operative rule:** The Village may not apply §§116.01 and 116.03's advance identifying registration and permit prerequisite, or its dependent §116.04 carry/display enforcement, to the reviewed ordinary noncommercial religious and political canvassing and handbill distribution, because the objective burdens on anonymous and spontaneous advocacy lack adequate fit to the asserted fraud, crime and privacy interests even under the Village's less demanding standard. Remand must prevent that enforcement while preserving the unchallenged §116.07 resident-exclusion mechanism, actual owner refusal and ordinary fraud and trespass laws; materially different commercial, fundraising and financing-disclosure applications are outside the question decided.

**Authority:** Stevens's opinion of the Court is joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplying eight votes for the complete rule, explanation and remedy. Thomas agrees with the judgment on his distinct method and does not join the common rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that routine, free issuance does not eliminate compulsory public identification and advance official permission before ordinary advocacy. Talley and McIntyre protect anonymous advocacy; Buckley examines when, to whom and why identity is exposed. Seeing an advocate's face does not reveal her legal name and residential history. The registration requirement also prevents spontaneous speech. These objective burdens exist independently of any speaker's religious or patriotic unwillingness to obey an otherwise lawful rule.

Martin and Schneider preserve direct dissemination to willing listeners while permitting control of actual trespass and fraud. The Village offers real commercial-scam evidence, concern for older residents, limited police resources and investigative benefits. The Court does not require proof of complete uselessness or a prior local crime wave. Those considerations nevertheless fail to justify licensing every peaceful noncommercial advocate, including speakers seeking neither sales nor contributions. A permit does not assure genuine identity or prevent wrongdoing, and the possibility of some investigative assistance does not establish sufficient fit across this broad speech class.

Resident choice directly protects household privacy. Owners may decline contact or use the unchallenged registered-and-posted exclusion procedure. A permit does not make an unwanted visitor welcome, and barring an unlicensed advocate does not implement a willing listener's preference. Fraud and trespass controls target misconduct without this general prerequisite.

Thomas v. Chicago Park District sustains fitted management of competing uses of common public space, not every nondiscretionary permit. No comparable coordination function appears here. Buckley's distinct initiative-administration and financial-reporting questions likewise do not supply that function. City of Ladue reinforces the importance of a distinctive expressive channel; alternative means do not erase its burden.

Assuming content neutrality and the Village's less demanding means-end test, the prerequisite fails. No universal scrutiny tier or prohibition of every license is needed. The Question 2 grant does not call for independent free-exercise, hybrid-rights or vagueness holdings. Relief addresses the reviewed prerequisite as construed below, without enlarging willing-listener access into entry against an owner's objection.

**Precedent treatment:** Talley, McIntyre, Martin and Schneider protect anonymity and direct advocacy with their lawful-enforcement limits; Buckley's exposure inquiry is applied while its different administrative settings remain distinct; Thomas and City of Ladue retain their context-specific functions.

## Holdings

### I. Limited administrative access does not erase workplace privacy

**Holding and operative rule:** A public employee retains a reasonable expectation of privacy in an individually used office computer where actual access practices do not expose its contents generally. Government ownership, occasional maintenance or document access, and a rule against personal business use do not alone waive that privacy or authorize unrestricted contents searches.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's Part I, joined by all eight other Justices; nine direct votes at this record-specific scope.

**Controlling explanation:** Leventhal used a private office and an individually assigned computer. Occasional technical access and the prohibition on private business did not establish a general practice of inspecting stored contents. Bond distinguishes limited exposure from consent to additional information acquisition; Guest likewise separates retained information privacy from a lawfully justified intrusion. Neither case answers the employer-search question merely by recognizing coverage. The Court does not adopt an absolute privacy rule for every workplace computer or treat government ownership as consent.

**Precedent treatment:** Bond and Guest are applied to the distinction between privacy coverage and justified acquisition, without extending their separate factual holdings to every workplace.

### II. A work-related investigation requires justification at inception and proportionate scope

**Holding and operative rule:** A public employer investigating work-related misconduct may inspect a protected workplace area without a warrant when reasonable grounds support the inquiry at inception and its measures remain reasonably related to that employment objective and not excessively intrusive in light of the suspected misconduct. Each follow-up examination requires its own justification from information lawfully obtained and the continuing work purpose; later discoveries cannot retroactively justify the first intrusion.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's Part II, joined by the other six full-opinion Justices; seven direct votes. Scalia does not join this general framework and Stevens does not approve the searches on the merits.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court directly adopts Ortega's inception-and-scope framework for the presented internal misconduct inquiry; it does not describe the divided Ortega opinions as a preexisting unified rationale. The standard accommodates legitimate supervision while preserving information privacy. An anonymous complaint is neither automatically sufficient nor automatically irrelevant. Its actual specificity, connection to work and the proposed intrusion matter. Robinette forbids using later discoveries to create earlier authority, while Arvizu requires genuine contextual force rather than automatic weight for every observation. Ordinary police collection, general monitoring and unrelated personal contents are not authorized by this employer-specific holding.

**Precedent treatment:** Ortega's inception-and-scope approach receives seven direct votes here; Robinette and Arvizu retain their independent-justification requirements. Knights supplies no employee-consent fiction.

### III. These four inspections were reasonable

**Holding and operative rule:** The October 25, 1996 file-name inventory and the three limited February and April 1997 examinations of selected program directories and files were reasonably initiated and confined to the actual employment-misuse inquiry. The reviewed individual-capacity Fourth Amendment dismissal is therefore affirmed; the established search record does not include examination of personal tax-return contents.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's Part III has the seven full-opinion joins plus Scalia's express join in this confined application; eight direct votes. Stevens concurs in the judgment on personal immunity only.

**Controlling explanation:** The letter identified Leventhal by job grade and alleged neglect amid computer-related workplace behavior. It did not directly accuse him of tax work on the office machine. That indirect connection supports a modest file-name inventory, not unrestricted opening of personal documents. The initial inspection neither ran programs nor opened files. The program names lawfully found then justified targeted confirmation in the three later examinations. Permission for outside tax work did not grant permission to use departmental equipment for it; tolerance of nonstandard departmental software did not end inquiry into nondepartmental use.

Repeated inspection is a material concern, but the particular program evidence and confined employment purpose supplied the limit at each step. The Court does not rely on later admissions or an imagined search of client returns. The nonfinal disciplinary suppression ruling supplies no established preclusive adjudication. Because the searches adjudicated here were reasonable, the Court need not decide personal immunity to affirm the search judgment. The ungranted employment-benefit rulings and abandoned claims remain undisturbed.

## Event

United States v. Drayton, No. 01-631. Argued April 16, 2002; merits decision June 17, 2002, on certiorari to the Eleventh Circuit's reversal of suppression denials. The accepted question is whether the February 4, 1999 coordinated drug-and-weapons encounter aboard the bus effected a seizure before the searches. Three officers boarded after the driver checked tickets and left: one observed from the driver's area while two proceeded from the rear. Brown agreed to a bag search and patdown and was arrested; Drayton then underwent a patdown. Brown entered a conditional plea; Drayton was convicted after trial. Independent consent voluntariness and a Terry alternative are outside the question reviewed.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated in submission and decision. No Justice is recused.

## Public Action

The Eleventh Circuit's seizure-based suppression judgment is reversed and the case remanded for lawful further proceedings consistent with the no-seizure determination.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Disposition:** Reversed and remanded, 5–4, for both respondents on the seizure-based suppression issue.

**Supporting:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg, who would preserve suppression as the product of an unsupported seizure.

The taint-based suppression direction is set aside. Further proceedings must respect Drayton's trial conviction, Brown's conditional plea and any separately preserved issue outside the accepted seizure question. The judgment does not automatically reinstate both convictions or independently uphold consent.

## Holdings

### I. Objective freedom to decline governs the bus encounter

**Holding and operative rule:** Police questioning aboard a bus is a seizure only when, considering the whole objectively communicated encounter, a reasonable passenger would not feel free to decline the requests or terminate the interaction. Constraints arising from the passenger's decision to travel are not themselves police restraint; absence of a refusal warning is relevant but neither necessary nor sufficient to establish seizure.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Part I, joined by all eight other Justices; nine direct votes for the governing framework, with a divided application.

**Controlling explanation:** Bostick adjusts the ordinary freedom-to-leave inquiry to a passenger whose own travel choice already limits movement. The inquiry concerns police-created restraint, not whether a person could conveniently abandon the trip. Robinette rejects an indispensable warning while retaining examination of actual communicated authority. Physical obstruction is not necessary to a seizure, and quiet requests do not prove its absence. Number, position, words and conduct must be considered together. An officer's undisclosed willingness to accept refusal is not an objective assurance, just as an undisclosed coercive purpose would not establish detention. Neither the bus setting nor one isolated circumstance controls.

**Precedent treatment:** Bostick and Robinette are applied without creating a mandatory warning or categorical bus rule; Bond's protection against additional physical intrusion remains distinct.

### II. Neither respondent was seized before the search

**Holding and operative rule:** The coordinated encounter did not objectively compel either Brown or Drayton to cooperate before the challenged searches: the officers used quiet requests, concealed their weapons, left the aisle passable, gave no command to remain and exercised no control over the passengers' tickets. Brown's arrest was relevant to the later request to Drayton but did not, in the circumstances of that request, itself detain him; the seizure-based suppression judgment is reversed.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Part II, joined by the other four Court-application Justices identified in Opinion Topology; five direct votes. Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg dissent from this application and disposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The officer observing from the driver's area and the colleagues approaching from the rear presented coordinated authority. The Court considers that combined arrangement rather than tallying isolated absences of coercion. It nevertheless conveyed requests capable of refusal: the observer did not block passage, the questioning officer spoke conversationally, weapons remained concealed and the officers did not direct passengers to stay or surrender tickets. The driver, not the police, handled the tickets. An announced drug-and-weapons purpose did not transform those requests into commands.

Brown's arrest after his patdown changed the setting for Drayton, but neither the arrest nor the other officers' positions communicated that Drayton was detained. Cooperation remains a factual circumstance, not a substitute for the objective legal judgment. The Court does not infer freedom from an officer's uncommunicated intent or use the discovered drugs to justify earlier conduct.

The accepted question concerns seizure. Finding no seizure removes the antecedent unlawful-detention ground for suppression but does not decide independent consent voluntariness or an unpresented Terry justification. Arvizu preserves that separation of stop and search questions. The respondents' distinct conviction postures and any independently preserved issues remain for lawful further proceedings.

**Precedent treatment:** Bostick's contextual rule is applied to each respondent; Arvizu preserves the separate consent inquiry. Knights supplies no advance passenger authorization or suspicion for this encounter.

Police number and positioning remain relevant; neither a passable aisle nor absence of a warning is a categorical rule.

## Separate Writings

**Souter, dissenting, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens and Ginsburg.** They accept Bostick's objective totality inquiry and reject any indispensable refusal warning. They disagree about the combined message. An officer facing passengers from the front, two colleagues moving from the rear, close requests to seated passengers and announcement of an interdiction operation communicated expected submission before travel resumed. ## Holdings

### Capacity and independently sufficient grounds

**Holding and operative rule:** A preserved Section 2 district-formation claim by one protected group may satisfy the first Gingles condition in a lawful, geographically compact district either through a numerical majority or rigorous proof of dependable crossover support sufficient to elect the group's preferred candidates. Numerical exclusivity cannot defeat that supported alternative, but cohesion, majority bloc voting ordinarily sufficient to defeat the group's preferred candidates absent special circumstances such as an unopposed minority candidate, causation, and unequal opportunity under an independent totality inquiry remain necessary; an erroneous numerical screen warrants relief only when material to the disposition, and a genuinely independent sufficient lawful ground remains effective.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer. These five directly adopt the complete rule, its application, and the limited mandate. The Court applies Valdespino's existing rule without changing its scope or any statutory burden.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 2 protects actual electoral opportunity and operates on its own statutory elements. Valdespino permits one protected group to prove dependable ability to elect through support from other voters; population arithmetic, an isolated success, or influence alone does not establish that capacity. Gingles and Growe supply the remaining causal conditions, with attention to special circumstances in the voting evidence. De Grandy requires a distinct totality inquiry and rejects both maximization and a conclusive proportionality defense. Voinovich limits judicially compelled districts to relief for a proved violation. A timely lawful state plan has priority under Growe, but necessary federal replacement does not exempt the resulting map from Section 2.

The congressional appeal presents electoral-performance evidence, comparative geography, and an objection to the adequacy of the findings. The lower opinion links its rejection to a majority requirement without sufficiently identifying an independent resolution of that disputed material under the governing rule. Appellees identify substantial contrary evidence about marginal districts, turnout, possible loss of existing opportunity, and geographic corridors. That evidence could support a lawful judgment; their appellate explanation cannot substitute for sufficiently identifiable findings by the trial court. Rule 52(a) does not require discussion of every exhibit, but the explanation must permit review under the correct legal standard.

The selected electoral results do not conclusively establish dependable capacity, and geometric scores do not determine legal compactness. Testimony about Plan 1099C cannot establish the same facts about Plan 1123C without support. Nor does the separate finding against common cohesion of distinct minority groups resolve one group's dependable outside support. The limited remand requires the lower court to resolve the appealed claim under the existing inquiry and explain any independent sufficient ground, preserving supported findings and the plaintiffs' burden.

**Material limits:** The Court determines the adequacy of the stated grounds for appellate review, not whether the disputed proof establishes the statutory elements. Ordinary procedures govern supplementation; the remand does not require a new trial. Distinct-group coalition entitlement, influence-only entitlement, and a universal population or statistical formula lie outside the single-group question presented. The separate legislative judgments lie outside the appeal.

### Remedy for the deficient explanation

**Holding and operative rule:** Because the majority-linked explanation does not sufficiently identify a lawful ground independently disposing of the appealed additional Latino-opportunity arrangement, the congressional judgment is vacated only insofar as it rejects that arrangement. The remand requires application of both capacity routes and the remaining statutory conditions with sufficiently identified findings, while Plan 1151C remains operative unless lawfully modified and any 2002 election already conducted under Plan 1151C is left undisturbed.

**Authority:** The same five Justices join the Court's application and mandate. This is a direct majority holding, not an inference from agreement on a result.

**Controlling explanation:** Valdespino distinguishes a material error in the capacity rule from independent findings and procedural rulings that remain valid. The defect here requires a reviewable application to the actual congressional claim, not automatic rejection of every finding made after trial. Growe preserves priority for timely lawful state action and permits necessary federal replacement; Voinovich permits a federal court to compel districts only as relief for an established violation. Those principles leave the operative replacement map in force during the bounded remand and require any further remedy to rest on the violation proved and lawful election administration. The remand is confined to the appealed arrangement and the districts it concerns, preserves supported findings, and allows supplementation only through ordinary procedure. It mandates neither an additional district nor a general reopening of the trial; the separate House and Senate judgments are unaffected.

## Event

Page v. Bartels; district No. 01-1733. Summary disposition on direct appeal, June 20, 2002. On appeal from the three-judge District Court for the District of New Jersey. The Court considers the papers without oral argument. The reviewed judgment denied injunctive relief after an April 30–May 1, 2001 trial; judgment was entered May 2 and explained May 7. This appeal concerns the final judgment, not the earlier single-judge preliminary ruling. Count I alleges Section 2 results dilution; Count II alleges an intentional Section 2 violation; Count III alleges intentional constitutional dilution and impaired participation; Count IV invokes the Fifteenth Amendment. The material district disputes concern Districts 27 and 34. Black voting-age population fell from 52.8% to 27.5% in District 27 and rose from 3.9% to 35.3% in District 34. The court found continuing local opportunity and rejected the alleged participation chilling and discriminatory purpose. The question is whether those supported findings permit denial of the requested injunction under the distinct statutory and constitutional rules.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated in consideration of the papers and decision.

## Public Action

The final three-judge judgment denying injunctive relief is affirmed in a signed summary opinion.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 9–0, on Counts I–IV.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The denial of injunctive relief stands. The statutory judgment is unanimous, but Scalia and Thomas rely on statutory noncoverage and do not join the Court's applications. The distinct opinion coalitions appear below.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joins and scope |
|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stone-Zsela | Part I: Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer. Part II: Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer. Parts III and IV: all other Justices. |

O'Connor and Kennedy join Parts II–IV but not Part I. Scalia and Thomas join Parts III–IV but neither Parts I nor II.

## Holdings

### The constitutional inquiry concerns a protected interest and the operation impairing it

**Holding and operative rule:** Racial consideration or predominance in a common territorial electorate does not itself establish a constitutional deprivation. The constitutional claims presented here must identify the governmental operation impairing a protected political interest and satisfy that claim's own elements; voters of every race receive the same protection, and Section 2 claims proceed independently on their statutory elements.

**Authority:** Part I of the signed opinion of the Court is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: five direct votes. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas do not join this part.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court reaffirms the boundary drawn by Shaw, Miller and Easley. A common territorial electorate places residents in the same geographical constituency without different legal eligibility because of individual race. Racial motivation explains why boundaries were chosen; the relevant operation identifies what those boundaries did to the protected interest. Composition, shape and predominance may supply evidence, but cannot replace proof of an actual exclusion, intentional dilution, materially unequal participation or other independently recognized wrong. Conversely, a shared electorate does not excuse a proved denial of equal access. Intentional dilution requires purpose, material impairment of protected electoral opportunity and causation. A participation claim requires an identified protected activity and concrete material impairment attributable to the challenged racial treatment or prohibited purpose; it does not require an already lost election. A material impairment is a practical restriction on the protected ability to participate or elect; total exclusion is unnecessary, while a technical change or disappointment about political leverage alone is insufficient. Those distinctions require separate attention to the actual dilution and chilling claims rather than allowing the lower court's nonpredominance reasoning to answer them. This case applies the existing boundary to those claims; it adopts no comprehensive new test for unpresented partisan or regional-separation theories.

**Precedent treatment:** Shaw v. Reno, Johnson v. Miller and Easley v. Cromartie: reaffirmed at their established territorial-design boundary and protection of independent injuries. The actual statutory inquiry remains governed by Section 2 and its own precedents.

### The statutory dilution claim fails on the relevant opposing-bloc evidence

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 2 does not require retention of a numerical-majority district where, on supported findings concerning the affected electorate and relevant nomination and election stages, challengers fail to establish opposing-bloc voting ordinarily sufficient to defeat the protected group's preferred candidates after considering relevant crossover support and special circumstances. Failure of that necessary condition disposes of this claim without deciding the first capacity condition or the remaining totality inquiry.

**Authority:** Part II of the signed opinion of the Court is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: seven direct votes. Scalia and Thomas agree only in the statutory judgment on their noncoverage ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that actual electoral opportunity, rather than a fixed racial percentage, governs the asserted loss. Gingles supplies the necessary voting conditions, and Voinovich permits disposition when the relevant opposing-bloc showing fails. Valdespino preserves both the numerical-majority route and rigorous proof of dependable crossover capacity, but neither route eliminates this separate requirement. Plaintiffs retain the ordinary preponderance burden: the required proposition must be more likely true than not. Valdespino's rigorous-support requirement concerns reliability, not a heightened proof standard or mandatory research protocol. The trial court credited an analysis of more than 150 elections, distinguished relevant contests and candidate preferences, and examined projected performance in each affected district. It also considered the Democratic nomination stage and evidence of African-American primary participation. Those findings support opportunity in both Districts 27 and 34 even without Hispanic support. The contrary expert evidence was received and rejected for identified methodological and contextual reasons; clear-error review does not authorize a new appellate credibility trial. The reduction from 52.8% to 27.5% Black voting-age population in District 27 therefore does not itself prove loss of opportunity. Possible gains elsewhere or the race of successful candidates would not answer that question, but the judgment rests on local preference, crossover and nomination evidence. De Grandy rejects both maximization and conclusive proportionality; it supplies no statewide offset for a local deprivation. No error in the independently sufficient findings requires remand. Distinct-group aggregation and influence-only entitlement remain undecided because neither is necessary after this condition fails.

**Precedent treatment:** Gingles and Voinovich: applied to the independently necessary opposing-bloc condition. Valdespino: both capacity routes remain intact without a new capacity determination. De Grandy: preserved against maximization, numerical shortcuts and statewide-offset reasoning. Balderas: its limited-remand ruling does not displace the independently sufficient findings identified here.

### The intentional statutory claim also fails on this record

**Holding and operative rule:** The separately pleaded intentional Section 2 claim fails because supported findings establish neither purposeful diminution of minority voting rights nor the actual impairment asserted. This ground is separate from the results claim and does not make intent necessary for a Section 2 results violation.

**Authority:** Part II of the signed opinion of the Court is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: seven direct votes. Scalia and Thomas agree only in the statutory judgment on their noncoverage ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court addresses Count II on its own terms. The trial court rejected the asserted purpose to diminish minority electoral opportunity and separately rejected the alleged loss and participation impairment. Those findings remain supported under clear-error review. Racial awareness and changing population shares do not themselves establish the pleaded purposeful diminution. The Court therefore affirms this statutory count without treating the results count as an intent claim or using lack of racial predominance as a required element.

**Precedent treatment:** The existing Section 2 results framework remains independent. Voinovich preserves claim-specific proof and does not make all statutory protection depend on purpose.

### The actual dilution and participation theories fail on supported findings

**Holding and operative rule:** Intentional dilution and materially unequal political participation require proof of the unlawful purpose or treatment, material impairment and causal connection belonging to the actual claim. The supported findings rejecting both loss of electoral opportunity and the separately alleged chilling of participation defeat these claims; the absence of established discriminatory purpose provides an additional ground.

**Authority:** The constitutional application is joined by all nine Justices: Part III addresses the Fourteenth Amendment and Part IV the Fifteenth Amendment. This complete agreement on the confined applications does not enlarge the five-Justice coalition for Part I.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that neither demographic redistribution nor racial attention alone establishes the constitutional wrong alleged here. Easley preserves independently asserted intentional dilution and unequal-participation claims under their own elements. The trial addressed these independent theories. Supported local electoral findings defeat the dilution allegation, and the court separately rejected the predicted chilling of African-American and Hispanic participation. That second finding matters: a preferred candidate's ability to win does not universally answer an obstruction of political access. The court also found no purpose to diminish minority voting rights. Plaintiffs bear the ordinary preponderance burden on their elements. A prohibited purpose need not be the sole or predominant motive; action partly because it would impair equal electoral opportunity or participation is distinct from mere awareness of effects. Arlington Heights and Feeney distinguish awareness of racial consequences from a prohibited purpose; Brooks requires the government, once prohibited purpose is established as a substantial or motivating factor, to prove by a preponderance of the evidence that materially the same decision would have occurred without that purpose. The threshold predicate is not established here. The lower court's additional reliance on lack of racial predominance does not supply the governing test, but its independent findings sustain this judgment. No developed separate due-process theory is presented for decision.

**Precedent treatment:** Easley: its protection of independently asserted dilution and participation claims is applied; no reconsideration of the distinct territorial-design rule is necessary. Arlington Heights, Feeney and Brooks: their purpose and burden rules remain intact; no established prohibited-purpose predicate triggers the latter burden here.

### The Fifteenth Amendment claim fails without deciding dilution coverage

**Holding and operative rule:** Assuming the Fifteenth Amendment reaches the asserted dilution theory, the supported absence of purposeful racial abridgment defeats this claim. The Court leaves the broader coverage question open and affirms the denial of relief on this component.

**Authority:** The constitutional application is joined by all nine Justices: Part III addresses the Fourteenth Amendment and Part IV the Fifteenth Amendment. This complete agreement on the confined applications does not enlarge the five-Justice coalition for Part I.

**Controlling explanation:** Voinovich permits rejection of an assumed dilution claim for failed proof of prohibited purpose while reserving the Amendment's coverage. The completed trial supplies that independent ground here. Awareness of racial composition and a change in numerical concentration do not displace the supported finding that the plan was not designed to diminish minority voting rights. The Court therefore does not adopt the lower court's categorical suggestion that legislative dilution falls outside the Fifteenth Amendment. This constitutional purpose inquiry does not convert Section 2's separate enacted results standard into an intent requirement.

**Precedent treatment:** Voinovich: applied for the assumed-coverage, failed-purpose route; the Fifteenth Amendment coverage reservation remains effective.

- Valdespino v. Alamo Heights Independent School District (June 17, 1999): both the numerical-majority and proved dependable-crossover capacity routes remain available; the separate opposing-bloc condition resolves this results claim.

## Law After Decision

The governing statutory framework permits changes to racial population shares when the actual results claim fails a necessary electoral condition; it creates neither a numerical safe harbor nor a right to maximize seats. The separately pleaded intentional statutory theory and the constitutional claims remain distinct, each resolved on its own supported failure of proof. Constitutional protection extends equally to every race and preserves material participation injury independently of election success. The existing territorial-design boundary remains unchanged.

## Separate Writings

**Thomas, concurring in part and in the judgment, joined by Scalia.** They agree that the reviewed denial of relief should be affirmed and join the constitutional dispositions. They would reject the statutory district-allocation claim because Section 2 does not authorize that asserted entitlement. Their Valdespino and Balderas positions limit the statute to voting access, casting and counting rather than judicial allocation of districts. Conditional acceptance that ordinary factual review would sustain the judgment if the Gingles framework applied is not a direct join in the Court's statutory analysis or either capacity route. Their separate statutory ground does not establish a numerical-majority rule or displace the Court's governing framework.

## Procedure After Action

The direct appeal concludes in affirmance of the final denial of relief. Plaintiffs receive no redistricting injunction, replacement map, additional district, election postponement or new trial. The judgment resolves the reviewed claims rather than granting prospective immunity from another established wrong.

## robertson-v-bartels-summary-2002-06-20

## Event

Robertson v. Bartels, No. 01-721; district No. 01-2024. Summary disposition on direct appeal, June 20, 2002. On appeal from the three-judge District Court for the District of New Jersey. The Court considers the papers without oral argument. The challenged map was adopted April 11, 2001; this action began April 27; summary judgment was announced May 31 and explained June 18. The appeal concerns Count I's predominant-racial-design theory and Count II's alleged racial favoritism in incumbent protection through district lines. The court denied relief on nonparty-preclusion and alternative merits grounds. The plaintiffs were not parties to Page, although they agreed that its testimony could be admitted. The questions concern the authority to bind them and the legal sufficiency of their two constitutional claims. Count III's separate residency controversy is outside this appeal.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated in consideration of the papers and decision.

## Public Action

The judgment denying relief on Counts I and II is affirmed in a signed summary opinion on the independent merits ground. The stated nonparty-preclusion rationale is rejected.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 5–4, on Counts I and II.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

All nine reject the stated basis for binding these plaintiffs to Page. That agreement supplies no additional merits vote. The denial of the redistricting injunction stands on the independent merits ground; Count III and its separate residency proceeding remain outside the mandate.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joins and scope |
|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stone-Zsela | Part I: all other Justices. Parts II and III: Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer. |
| Concurrence in part and dissent in part | Kennedy | O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas; joins Part I, would vacate both merits counts under the proposed predominant-assignment rule |

Part I concerns nonparty preclusion; Part II concerns Count I; Part III separately examines Count II's claimed incumbent benefit.

## Holdings

### Political alignment and common requested relief do not bind these nonparties

**Holding and operative rule:** A prior judgment cannot bind different plaintiffs merely because they share political interests, request the same relief, know of the earlier case or could have sought intervention. The stated virtual-representation ground fails without constitutionally sufficient consent, control, genuine privity, authorized representation or another valid binding arrangement; agreement to admit testimony supplies none of those relationships by itself.

**Authority:** Part I of the signed opinion of the Court is joined by all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Page judgment does not bind these plaintiffs on the reasons the district court gave. Richards requires lawful authority to represent an absent person, and Hansberry preserves the due-process prerequisite to genuine representative adjudication. Shared political association, vigorous advocacy, adequate incentives and the public importance of a controversy do not establish that authority. Nor does describing the second suit as tactical create a general duty to intervene. The parties agreed to the use of Page testimony as evidence; they did not thereby agree to its inferences, authorize representation or consent to binding adjudication. The public character of map relief does not, without an actual authorized arrangement, turn ordinary litigation into a proceeding representing all interested voters. Devlin concerns persons already bound as members of a mandatory class and does not make these strangers members of one. Valid representative and special remedial arrangements remain possible, but none is supplied by the stated rationale. The independent merits ground permits disposition without an exploratory remand to construct a new preclusion relationship.

**Precedent treatment:** Richards and Hansberry: applied to the necessary authority for binding absent claimants. Devlin: distinguished because its appellants were already bound mandatory-class members, not political allies outside the litigation.

### The territorial-design count identifies no independent constitutional deprivation

**Holding and operative rule:** Predominantly racial design of a common territorial electorate does not alone establish a Fourteenth Amendment deprivation. Even giving these plaintiffs the favorable reasonable inferences required on summary judgment, Count I identifies no independently cognizable denial or abridgment; the judgment denying relief on that count therefore stands.

**Authority:** Parts II and III of the signed opinion of the Court are joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: five direct votes. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas dissent from the merits disposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the disputed weight of racial considerations is not material to the design-only claim under governing law. Shaw, Miller and Easley distinguish the motive for common district boundaries from an actual exclusion, dilution, unequal participation or other prohibited treatment. The Court assumes favorable reasonable inferences from Bartels's minority-incumbent protection commitment and does not decide that race could not have predominated merely because other criteria were also considered or the map had ordinary shape. Anderson v. Liberty Lobby requires those favorable inferences, but an inference about motive cannot supply a missing legal deprivation. The dissent would reconsider the governing boundary and treat predominant racial assignment itself as unequal treatment. The Court retains the distinction: membership in the resulting common electorate imposes no racial qualification or legally unequal civic status by itself. That conclusion requires neither a second governmental act nor electoral defeat or financial loss. The actual territorial theory also supplies no independent denial or abridgment for the pleaded Fifteenth Amendment theory; its broader dilution coverage need not be decided.

**Precedent treatment:** Shaw, Miller and Easley: applied without changing the territorial-design boundary or protections for actual independent wrongs. Anderson v. Liberty Lobby: applied to favorable reasonable inferences, with materiality determined by the governing substantive law. Voinovich: its broader Fifteenth Amendment coverage reservation remains intact.

### The actual incumbent-prospect claim concerns the same territorial operation

**Holding and operative rule:** A claim that district boundaries favor minority incumbents' reelection prospects does not become a separate racial allocation of candidacy or civic rights merely by describing those prospects as an incumbent benefit. Count II identifies the composition of the common electorate and its expected political support, without an independent eligibility restriction or participation impairment, and fails under the same constitutional boundary.

**Authority:** Parts II and III of the signed opinion of the Court are joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: five direct votes. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas dissent from the merits disposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the actual governmental operation, not the beneficiary label, determines this claim. The evidence of a commitment to protect minority incumbents receives its favorable inference; the Court neither approves every such preference nor excuses established racial discrimination because no incumbent owns a future term. Here the challenged benefit consists of the reelection prospects resulting from district lines. The record supplies no separate racial ballot qualification, restricted candidacy, office entitlement, official racial ballot label or independent participation barrier in Counts I or II. Anderson v. Martin concerns official racial electoral labeling and therefore addresses a different operation. Adarand supplies justification requirements for an actual covered racial allocation; it does not itself supply the missing deprivation here. Easley preserves review of unequal eligibility and materially unequal participation without demanding a changed winner, but those are not the actual basis of this count. The separate residency controversy in Count III cannot be borrowed to enlarge this appeal. Because the asserted benefit remains the same territorial composition, the disputed priority of motives does not require a trial under the governing rule.

**Precedent treatment:** Easley: applied to the actual operation and preserved independent injuries. Anderson v. Martin: distinguished from common geographical composition. Adarand: preserved for an actual covered racial allocation, without using its justification rule to create one.

## Precedent Treatment

- Richards v. Jefferson County: applied to lawful authority to bind absent claimants; political affinity, common objectives and failure to intervene supply no substitute. Its allowance for actual authorized representative and special remedial arrangements remains intact.
- Hansberry v. Lee: genuine representation must meet due process.
- Devlin v. Scardelletti (June 10, 2002): distinguished because its mandatory-class members were actually bound; it creates no representative relationship among these political allies.
- Shaw v. Reno, Johnson v. Miller and Easley v. Cromartie: the territorial-design boundary is reaffirmed for both the direct design claim and the actual electorate-based incumbent-prospect theory. Their protection of independently cognizable wrongs remains intact.
- Anderson v. Liberty Lobby: favorable reasonable inferences are preserved; materiality depends on the substantive cause.
- Anderson v. Martin: official racial electoral labeling differs from the common geographical electorate challenged here.
- Adarand Constructors, Inc. v. Peña: justification rules for an actual covered racial allocation remain effective and do not supply a missing deprivation.
- Voinovich v. Quilter: the broader Fifteenth Amendment dilution-coverage question remains reserved.

## Law After Decision

The nonparty rule remains grounded in actual authority to bind a person's rights. The constitutional territorial-design rule covers the actual incumbent-prospect theory when the claimed benefit consists of the same district composition; it does not immunize a different racial allocation of eligibility, civic status or participation. Both claims can be rejected while assuming favorable racial-motive inferences, without resolving the disputed relative weight of motives or endorsing every rationale below.

## Separate Writings

**Kennedy, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas.** They join rejection of the stated nonparty-preclusion ground but would vacate summary judgment on Counts I and II for consideration under their proposed predominant-racial-assignment rule. Following their Easley position, they regard governmental racial assignment as unequal treatment when race actually predominates, without requiring an additional electoral loss. Bartels's express minority-incumbent protection commitment permits a reasonable inference about racial priority. A regular map and the presence of other criteria do not conclusively defeat that inference on summary judgment. The proposed remand would assess the relative role of race and any required justification under ordinary procedure. It would not find predominance or a violation established, order new boundaries, or guarantee a trial. This opinion seeks reconsideration of the Court's territorial-design boundary; it does not describe that proposed rule as existing law.

## Procedure After Action

The direct appeal concludes in affirmance on Counts I and II. The requested redistricting injunction is denied. The Court orders no replacement plan, election change or new preclusion hearing. The separate residency count and proceeding are unaffected.
