### City of Burlington v. Dague, No. 91-810

Merits decision, June 24, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 21, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit, reviewing [935 F.2d 1343](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F2/935/935.F2d.1343.90-7544.415.html) (June 12, 1991). Review is limited to whether the Clean Water Act and Solid Waste Disposal Act permit contingency enhancement above the lodestar—the fee calculated from reasonable hours and a reasonable rate—and whether this 25% addition may stand. The City seeks removal of the addition. Procedural background source locator: [505 U.S. 557–576](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep505/usrep505557/usrep505557.pdf).

All nine Justices—Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas—participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The judgment is reversed insofar as it affirms the 25% contingency enhancement, by five votes to four. Blackmun and Stevens would affirm; Stone-Zsela and O'Connor would vacate and remand for a properly supported market assessment.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 25% contingency enhancement | Reverse in part; 5–4 | White, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor | Remove $49,506.87 enhancement; preserve $198,027.50 lodestar. Of the dissenters, Blackmun and Stevens would affirm; Stone-Zsela and O'Connor would vacate and remand for market-based reassessment without a predetermined percentage. |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Contingency does not justify an enhanced statutory fee

**Controlling proposition:** A reasonable attorney's fee under 42 U.S.C. §6972(e) and 33 U.S.C. §1365(d) may not be enhanced above a properly determined lodestar to compensate for the risk of nonpayment when the lawyer's compensation depends on success. The 25% contingency addition is removed; the unreviewed lodestar, fee eligibility and underlying environmental relief remain intact.

**Authority:** Scalia's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by White, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: five of nine. The fee-statute rule is an ordinary majority holding; the dissenters' alternative market approach supplies no controlling component.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that these statutes compensate reasonable legal services in successful litigation without adding insurance against losing. Hensley supplies the calculation of reasonable hours multiplied by a reasonable rate and keeps fee disputes from becoming a second major litigation. Blum and Delaware Valley I establish the strong presumption that this lodestar is sufficient and forbid counting difficulty and skill twice. White's opinion in Delaware Valley II identified the incompatibility between risk enhancement and the statutes' allocation of fees to prevailing parties; the Court now adopts that position. Dague answers that contingent payment differs from guaranteed payment. The statutory fee need not reproduce every feature of private compensation. Blanchard makes the lodestar independent of the private contingent bargain. A market-access exception would still require uncertain risk comparisons and would not reliably isolate an uncompensated service. The prohibition concerns contingency, not adjustment for delay or correction of an unreasonable underlying rate. Those questions and the environmental judgment remain outside this review.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Hensley v. Eckerhart, 461 U.S. 424 — applied for the lodestar, compensation for successful litigation and avoidance of extensive collateral fee litigation.
- Blum v. Stenson, 465 U.S. 886 — applied for the strong lodestar presumption, applicant's burden and prohibition on double counting; its reserved contingency question is answered here.
- Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens' Council for Clean Air, 478 U.S. 546 (Delaware Valley I) — applied for lodestar primacy; no new rule concerning delay or deficient rates follows.
- Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens' Council for Clean Air, 483 U.S. 711 (Delaware Valley II) — White's previously noncontrolling categorical position is adopted for these statutes; O'Connor's limited permission and Blackmun's broader permission remain noncontrolling.
- Blanchard v. Bergeron, 489 U.S. 87 — applied; the private contingent agreement is not the governing measure of a statutory fee.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Effective June 24, 1992, contingency risk cannot support an enhancement under the two reviewed fee provisions. The previously unresolved permission question is resolved categorically; the lodestar method, reasonable-hours/rates inquiry, applicant's ordinary burden and prohibition of double counting remain. The Court applies the same reasonable-fee principles used for similarly worded prevailing-party statutes but adjudicates this award under §6972(e) and §1365(d). It does not rule on a differently worded compensation scheme, delay, an incorrectly calculated lodestar or common-fund fees. No broader rule against all fee adjustments is announced.

Delaware Valley II's categorical position is adopted as a majority holding for these statutes; the earlier judgment is not rewritten and its other components remain. Ardestani's administrative-eligibility rule remains distinct, and Department of Energy v. Ohio supplies no federal-immunity premise for this municipal award.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Blackmun, joined by Stevens, would affirm: contingent payment differs from guaranteed hourly payment, and an otherwise supported adjustment can reflect that economic difference without awarding a losing party fees. O'Connor would permit a narrowly demonstrated class-wide market adjustment but vacate because this record does not support the 25% figure with market evidence. Stone-Zsela also would vacate and remand: the applicant must prove both the relevant market differential and substantial difficulty obtaining competent counsel without adjustment, account for mitigation of nonpayment risk, and avoid case-specific speculation or factors already counted in the lodestar. Neither market-based dissent fixes an amount or requires an enhancement.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court review of the enhancement is complete. Ordinary mandate implementation removes the $49,506.87 addition from the fee judgment and preserves the $198,027.50 lodestar. The environmental relief, fee eligibility and other unreviewed portions remain outside this action. No market-evidence remand is ordered by the Court, and no replacement multiplier is authorized. No retained Supreme Court proceeding, later filing or mandate-issuance date is established.

**End of entry: City of Burlington v. Dague, merits decision, June 24, 1992.**

### Baker v. General Motors Corp., No. 96-653

Merits decision, January 13, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

Baker v. General Motors Corp., No. 96-653. Argued October 15, 1997; decided January 13, 1998. The Court reviews the Eighth Circuit's treatment of a Michigan injunction as requiring exclusion of a former GM employee's testimony in the Bakers' separate Missouri wrongful-death action, removed to federal court on diversity grounds.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision. No Justice was absent.

#### Judgment

**The mandatory-exclusion ruling is reversed, and the case is remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The Michigan judgment retains its lawful effect between persons and conduct it binds. The Court does not automatically restore the entire $11.3 million verdict, order a new trial, compel blanket admission, require a Michigan proceeding or grant relief against Elwell.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Parts I and III: common recognition framework, instruments and mandate | Stevens | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supplies common recognition principles and the unanimous mandate | Parts I and III |
| Opinion of the Court, Part II: limited federal recognition and evidence-operation ground | Stevens | Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supplies the controlling federal ground for reversal | Part II |
| Concurrence in Parts I and III and the judgment | Kennedy | O'Connor, Thomas | Supports reversal on Michigan judgment-effect grounds | Parts I and III and judgment; Part II not joined |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. Judgment recognition begins with lawful rendering-state effect and has no general local-policy exception.

Section 1738 applies to state consent judgments in this federal forum. Consent, disagreement with a local evidentiary policy or a decree's mere modifiability does not permit withholding its lawful adjudicative effect. Part I is unanimous.

Matsushita requires the effect the rendering State supplies and preserves constitutional authority to bind represented persons. Richards prevents treating shared interests or an opportunity to intervene as sufficient by themselves to adjudicate strangers' own rights; consent, control, genuine privity, adequate representation or another sufficient relationship remains possible. Neither decision turns every effect on a nonparty into invalid preclusion. No Michigan modification is found, and the Court rejects a general public-policy exception rather than approving the trial court's policy rationale.

##### 2. This injunction does not itself require the separate forum to exclude Elwell's testimony.

Giving the GM–Elwell decree its lawful adjudicative effect does not require the Missouri federal court to execute its broad personal testimony restriction as an evidentiary rule in the Bakers' distinct action. The decree adjudicated relations between GM and Elwell, not the Bakers' wrongful-death claims or their entitlement to invoke ordinary evidence process. Part II commands six direct votes: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

Recognition and the requested evidentiary act are different operations. The asserted compulsory exclusion is not merely the preclusive effect of an adjudicated Baker claim or particular privilege dispute. The judgment can remain binding within its lawful sphere without becoming a roving command over another tribunal's evidence. GM's argument that a personal command may affect persons who are not precluded does not establish the additional duty it seeks from this forum.

This holding does not exclude injunctions from §1738, make all personal duties stop at state borders, empower another court to modify Michigan's decree or allow evidence policy to override valid preclusion. It creates no universal witness-access right or broad rule about land decrees, antisuit injunctions or every foreign evidence order. It gives Elwell no immunity from lawful personal enforcement. Nonparty status alone does not supply a universal exception; the actual adjudication, distinct proceeding and requested evidentiary execution define the holding.

##### 3. Neither the instruments nor orderly-review principles compel the Michigan modification proceeding asserted here.

The Bakers' request for evidence in their own case is not, on the asserted basis, a request to modify Michigan's injunction. Celotex retains its requirements of actual jurisdictional relationship, sufficient authority, covered restraint and lawful avenues of relief and review; it supplies no missing basis to bind these outsiders or turn their evidence request into disobedience. All nine adopt the bounded conclusion and preserved limits in Part III, through the reasons specified in their writings.

The injunction's exception concerns the specified then-pending Georgia case. A separate settlement agreement protects court-ordered testimony in other proceedings against an action for breach of the agreement or injunction. The two instruments and exceptions must not be merged. The Court decides no unquoted contractual term, general right to disregard an order, actual personal-enforcement claim or universal power of another forum to modify an injunction.

##### 4. The remand preserves ordinary evidentiary rules and other appellate grounds.

The Eighth Circuit must reconsider the case without treating §1738 as requiring this exclusion. Actual privilege, trade-secret, work-product, admissibility and legally supported preclusion questions retain their own predicates. Part III's mandate is unanimous.

The Bakers' reported, undisputed representation that GM raised no attorney-client, work-product or trade-secret objection to a particular question or answer does not establish that every conceivable item was unprotected or that every objection was waived. The appellate judgment rested on grounds including this testimony issue; removing that ground does not itself reinstate the whole verdict. The Michigan decree remains operative for persons and conduct it lawfully binds.

#### Precedent Treatment

Matsushita is applied to lawful rendering-state effect, consent judgments and the distinction between recognition and additional coercive control; its collateral-review reservation remains open. Richards retains the constitutional prerequisites for binding absent persons without creating a universal nonparty exception. Celotex is distinguished by its actual jurisdictional connection, authority, covered restraint and qualified relief/review mechanism; its orderly-review rule remains intact, including the preserved United Mine Workers and Walker qualifications. No precedent is overruled.

#### Law After Decision

The Court adds a bounded recognition/execution rule: §1738 does not make this GM–Elwell injunction a compulsory exclusion rule in the Bakers' separate action. Lawful adjudicative effects, genuine nonparty-binding relationships, personal injunction duties and ordinary evidence protections remain distinct. The three-Justice Michigan-specific rationale supplies no additional controlling rule; the six-Justice federal ground controls directly without combining incompatible rationales.

#### Separate Writings

Justice Kennedy, joined by Justices O'Connor and Thomas, reaches the same judgment through Michigan's actual judgment effect and does not join the independently sufficient federal ground. Nummer and Storey supply the reported same-party, full-opportunity and mutuality conditions; Lichon and Howell retain mutuality; Bacon and Detroit Railway provide concrete nonparty-injunction applications. Qualls and E & G Finance place persuasion on the party invoking preclusion. On the supported nonparty record, these rules establish no basis for the compulsory exclusion GM seeks, without inventing privity, control or an exhaustive list of exceptions.

The concurrence recognizes that a personal command to Elwell may have consequences without precluding the Bakers, but finds no legal bridge from that command to compulsory exclusion here. The reported Michigan Rule 2.613(B) direction to seek modification from the entering court does not make the Bakers' evidence request a modification request or impose a mandatory Michigan proceeding on them. The concurrence reserves genuine personal enforcement and every broader question about valid cross-border restraints. It regards the state-effect inquiry as sufficient and withholds a wider federal allocation rationale.

#### Source Notes

The reported record establishes the February 1990 collision, separate GM–Elwell litigation, November 1991 preliminary order, August 26, 1992 stipulated injunction, distinct settlement protection, Bakers' nonparty status, subpoenaed testimony and verdict. Original instruments, the complete transcript and all appellate issues are not reproduced. Published recitations support the Michigan authorities at their stated scope; the full opinions, omitted qualifications and complete historical Rule 2.613(B) text and amendment history are not supplied. Those limits establish no exhaustive exception rule, factual default or universal privilege waiver.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Eighth Circuit receives the case for properly remaining appellate proceedings under the corrected rule. Ordinary evidence, privilege, preclusion and protective-order questions remain available on their actual predicates. The Court neither dissolves the Michigan judgment nor decides its personal enforcement, restores the entire verdict, guarantees a new trial or requires another proceeding in Michigan.

**End of entry: Baker v. General Motors Corp., Merits decision, January 13, 1998.**

### Alden v. Maine, No. 98-436

Merits decision, June 23, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 31, 1999; decided June 23, 1999. Reported at 527 U.S. 706. On certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1257 to the Supreme Judicial Court of Maine, reviewing 715 A.2d 172 (1998). The question is whether Congress may require a nonconsenting State to answer this private FLSA damages action in its own competent courts under the Commerce Clause. The employees seek reversal; Maine seeks affirmance. The state action was separately filed in August 1996; the federal Mills proceeding is not the judgment under review.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and the other eight Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, who would affirm.

The Court reverses Maine’s immunity judgment; no wage violation or entitlement to an award is adjudicated.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joins and relationship to judgment |
|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I — review, distinct proceedings and express statutory authorization | Souter | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; unanimous. |
| Opinion of the Court, Parts II–III — constitutional state-forum rule and reversal/remand | Souter | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer; five Justices including the author. |
| Concurrence in Part I and dissent from Parts II–III and the judgment | Kennedy | O’Connor, Scalia and Thomas; all four would affirm on constitutional immunity. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Review concerns Maine’s separate state-court judgment

**Holding and operative rule:** Under 28 U.S.C. §1257, the Court may review the federal immunity question decided by Maine’s highest court in this independently filed state action. This review neither reopens nor adjudicates the separate federal proceeding in Mills.

**Authority:** Souter’s Part I, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** South Central Bell distinguishes review of a State’s own judgment from commencement of a new federal damages action. The employees separately filed in Maine Superior Court in August 1996 and obtained a final adverse state judgment on the federal question. That judgment and the unresolved wage controversy supply the present vehicle. The historical explanation of the federal dismissal does not establish that a categorical constitutional bar governed it, and a different account of federal immunity would not itself erase the independently entered state judgment. The Court decides the preserved state-forum issue without transferring the case, reviving the federal action or determining wage liability.

**Precedent treatment:** South Central Bell is applied to the §1257 review channel. Its distinction between appellate review and a new federal damages suit remains unchanged.

##### Congress expressly authorized this state-court FLSA action

**Holding and operative rule:** Sections 203 and 216(b) expressly authorize an employee’s action against a public agency for covered FLSA violations in a competent state court. That statutory authorization is distinct from the constitutional validity of the remedy and from proof that any overtime is owed.

**Authority:** Souter’s Part I, joined by all eight other Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** The Act includes public agencies in its employer definition and expressly permits the employee action against a public agency in any competent Federal or State court. Congress therefore supplied more than a general jurisdictional grant or a substantive duty from which a remedy might be inferred. Maine’s lack of consent, as determined under state law, does not make the federal text ambiguous. Garcia sustains generally applicable federal wage regulation of state employers; whether the Constitution permits this expressly chosen private remedy requires a separate answer. Coverage, exemptions, violations and statutory remedial conditions remain to be adjudicated.

**Precedent treatment:** Seminole’s distinction between unmistakable statutory authorization and constitutional amenability is applied without resolving federal-court amenability. Garcia remains effective on the substantive regulation of state employment.

##### Maine’s immunity cannot defeat the expressly enacted state-court remedy

**Holding and operative rule:** Congress, acting within its valid commerce authority, may displace Maine’s asserted nontextual immunity from this expressly authorized private FLSA action in a competent state court. Article VI requires the court to adjudicate that valid federal remedy despite Maine’s lack of consent.

**Authority:** Souter’s Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer; five Justices adopt this constitutional ground as well as reversal.

**Controlling explanation:** Clear congressional intent cannot overcome a constitutional restriction. But the immunity asserted here is not an unalterable constitutional limit on Congress’s power to provide this state-court remedy. The Eleventh Amendment limits federal judicial power; it does not establish the claimed state-forum bar. The Constitution’s division of sovereign authority and Article VI’s express obligation on state judges do not make Maine’s nontextual defense immune from deliberate displacement by valid federal legislation.

Garcia establishes the validity of the wage regulation. Congress separately and expressly chose an employee action against public agencies in competent state courts to enforce it. Testa and Howlett require those courts to honor valid federal law. Howlett did not itself decide the immunity of a State, so the Court independently rejects the asserted constitutional restriction here before applying that enforcement duty. Hilton preserved the specialized FELA action through statutory precedent and compensation reliance; it did not already decide every Article I remedy. Its enforcement principle extends to the expressly enacted FLSA remedy on the constitutional ground now decided.

Adjudicating this federal cause is distinct from compelling state legislation or executive administration. New York preserves state-activity regulation and separate judicial duties; Printz’s limited records-assistance holding creates no categorical insulation of state institutions. Hans’s federal-court judgment does not establish the asserted state-court immunity. This decision supplies no general rule for federal-court amenability, every statutory damages action or every public entity, and finds no constructive consent from state employment or commerce.

**Precedent treatment:** Garcia, Testa and Howlett are applied for their distinct regulatory and judicial-enforcement propositions. Hilton’s enforcement principle is extended beyond its retained FELA setting to this express FLSA remedy; its own holding and reasons remain intact. Hans, New York and Printz are distinguished within their actual decided reach. Seminole remains without a controlling general State-amenability rule, and Union Gas retains its actual judgment and five-vote constitutional conclusion without converting its plurality rationale into a general test.

##### The immunity dismissal is reversed without adjudicating wages

**Holding and operative rule:** Maine’s immunity judgment is reversed, and the case is remanded for ordinary adjudication of the federal overtime claims, including coverage, compensation, defenses and legally available relief. The reversal establishes no wage violation or present entitlement to an award.

**Authority:** Souter’s Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer; five Justices. O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would affirm on immunity.

**Controlling explanation:** The judgment on the pleadings rested on immunity and did not decide the employees’ overtime claims. Removing that defense requires the competent state court to adjudicate those claims under the FLSA and ordinary procedure. Applicable coverage, exemptions, proof, limitations and remedial conditions retain their force; the Court does not reinstate the State’s limitations argument rejected below. Section 216(b)’s written-consent requirement and specified termination of private rights upon a qualifying Secretary complaint retain their own predicates, none of which is newly found here. Wages, liquidated damages, fees and costs require the statutory conditions for their award. The separate retaliation provision supplies no remedy for an unpleaded retaliation claim. No new hearing, particular evidentiary procedure, officer suit, federal reopening or special mandate date is ordered.

**Precedent treatment:** Hilton’s distinction between opening the state forum and deciding compensation is followed. The existing limits on retrospective officer relief are unchanged; no substitute officer remedy is created.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority:** Applied to the validity of generally applicable federal wage regulation of state employers; it is not treated as independently deciding every remedy.
- **Testa v. Katt:** Applied to the obligation of competent state courts to enforce valid federal law.
- **Howlett v. Rose:** Applied after independently resolving the State’s immunity objection. Its governmental defendant was not the State itself, and its holding is not recast as a prior decision of this constitutional issue.
- **Hilton v. South Carolina Public Railways Commission:** Its state-court enforcement principle is extended to the express FLSA remedy. Its retained FELA coverage construction, unchanged text and specialized compensation reliance remain intact; no waiver finding replaces those grounds.
- **Seminole Tribe of Florida v. Florida:** Its statutory-authorization distinction is applied; the decision supplied no controlling direct-State constitutional amenability rule or general Article I prohibition. Its connected prospective officer remedy remains confined to its own statute and conditions.
- **Pennsylvania v. Union Gas Co.:** Its actual damages judgment, statutory construction and five-vote constitutional conclusion remain in force within their decided scope. No broader plurality rationale becomes binding here.
- **Hans v. Louisiana:** Its federal-court judgment is distinguished; it does not establish the asserted immunity against this state-court FLSA remedy. The Court does not overrule its actual result or decide federal-court amenability.
- **New York v. United States:** Its prohibition on compulsory state regulation or ownership of private obligations remains intact; generally applicable regulation and the distinct obligations of state courts remain separate.
- **Printz v. United States; Mack v. United States:** The confined permission for temporary executive records assistance and its unresolved fiscal questions remain unchanged. Neither determines this state-court treasury action.
- **South Central Bell Telephone Co. v. Alabama:** Applied to the authority to review a federal question in a State’s own judgment under §1257.
- **Fitzpatrick v. Bitzer and Ex parte Young:** Their distinct §5 and prospective connected-officer channels are not altered or newly established for these employees. No alternative suit or entitlement is found.

#### Law After Decision

A competent state court must hear the expressly authorized FLSA action against Maine notwithstanding its lack of consent. The new constitutional holding rejects an unalterable state-forum immunity defense to this validly enacted remedy; it does not rest on constructive waiver or on a previously settled general Article I rule. The FLSA’s substantive and remedial conditions still determine whether these employees recover anything.

The prior distinction between statutory authorization and constitutional power remains. This decision resolves that constitutional question for the state-court FLSA action while leaving general federal-court amenability, other statutes and other remedial arrangements undecided. The independent Mills proceeding is unchanged. No new officer or sovereign enforcement action, fiscal defense, burden shift, damages presumption or special procedure is created.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Kennedy, concurring in Part I and dissenting from Parts II–III and the judgment, joined by O’Connor, Scalia and Thomas.** The dissent accepts the review channel, express FLSA authorization, Maine’s lack of consent and continuing federal wage duties. It would nevertheless hold that retained constitutional sovereignty protects a nonconsenting State from this private retrospective treasury action in its own courts. Power to regulate state wages and a court’s duty to enforce valid federal law do not, in its view, resolve whether Congress may impose this particular remedy. The constitutional protection concerns the State’s public fisc and political responsibility, not merely a state common-law rule that Congress may displace.

The dissent acknowledges that Seminole supplied no controlling prohibition and that Union Gas remains precedent, while maintaining its disagreement with Union Gas’s constitutional conclusion. It preserves Hilton’s specialized statutory settlement and compensation reliance, rather than substituting a finding of consent; it distinguishes Howlett’s non-State defendant. New York supports separating a valid objective from permissible means, but neither that decision nor Printz negates Garcia or ordinary state-court duties. The dissent would affirm this immunity dismissal without extinguishing the wage obligation or deciding valid consent, United States enforcement, appropriate §5 legislation, established officer relief or suits against entities that are not arms of a State.

#### Source Notes

The state judgment, 715 A.2d 172 (1998), supplies the separate filing, immunity and no-waiver rulings and the absence of a wage-merits adjudication. Sections 203 and 216(b) of the FLSA supply the public-employer authorization and statutory remedial conditions. The question is stated by substance, without quotation of unavailable certified grant language. The earlier federal proceeding’s reported historical explanation does not determine the governing constitutional premise of this separate review. No missing original pleading or payroll document is treated as a finding of liability or a litigant default.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns to the Maine courts with the immunity dismissal reversed. The competent state court must adjudicate the federal overtime claims under ordinary FLSA requirements, including coverage, violation, compensation, defenses and legally available relief. No wage merits were decided by the judgment on the pleadings.

Statutory party-consent and Secretary-complaint conditions remain applicable on their own terms; no missing consent or qualifying complaint is found. No overtime, liquidated damages, fees or other award is ordered. The Court does not reinstate the State’s limitations argument rejected below, adjudicate all remaining defenses, reopen Mills, direct an officer or sovereign action, require a new hearing or set a special mandate date. The separate federal proceeding receives no disposition from this decision.

**End of entry: Alden v. Maine, merits decision, June 23, 1999.**

### College Savings Bank v. Florida Prepaid Postsecondary Education Expense Board, No. 98-149

Merits decision, June 23, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 20, 1999. On writ of certiorari to the Third Circuit, reviewing 131 F.3d 353 (1997), which affirmed dismissal of the federal Lanham Act false-advertising action on State-immunity grounds.

The questions concern Congress's Fourteenth Amendment §5 authority to remove State immunity for unfair-competition claims alleging false or misleading advertising of the State's own product, and whether continued interstate marketing after express statutory notice of federal suit supplies prospective Parden consent. The grant contains no reformulation. The separate patent action is not before the Court in this docket.

CSB, a New Jersey bank selling CollegeSure CDs, alleges that Florida Prepaid, an admitted arm of Florida offering competing tuition-prepayment products, misrepresented its own product in brochures and annual reports. This claim concerns neither appropriation of CSB's trademark nor false statements about CSB's product. The United States intervened to defend the statute. The alleged advertising violation and resulting loss remain unproved.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and the other eight Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

The reversal removes only the federal Lanham Act immunity dismissal. No majority adopts a common affirmative ground for State amenability.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | Stone-Zsela | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Common statutory proposition | Unmistakable federal-forum and remedy authorization; intent, power, consent, liability and relief remain distinct. |
| Opinion of the Court, Part II | Stone-Zsela | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Eight reject the asserted §5 justification | Own-product advertising/property distinction and Boerne's preventive-enforcement limits; Stevens does not join. |
| Opinion of the Court, Part III | Stone-Zsela | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Five reject constructive commercial consent | Clear congressional intent and optional ordinary commerce do not establish unequivocal State submission; actual consent and retained Hilton coverage remain distinct. |
| Opinion announcing the judgment, Part IV | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | His affirmative ground for reversal | Article I enforcement authority and fair inclusion of that argument; no majority rationale. |
| Opinion announcing the judgment, Part V | Stone-Zsela | Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer as to the judgment and remand only | Five support reversal | Common removal of the federal immunity dismissal and ordinary merits remand. |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Breyer | Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg | Four support reversal on a different ground | Prospective commercial-condition consent. All four join Part I and the Part V judgment; Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer also join Part II. They do not join Parts III–IV. |
| Further concurrence in part and in the judgment | Stevens | No other Justice | Additional ground for reversal | §5 prevention protecting an existing business and goodwill. |

The judgment supporters do not share an affirmative amenability rationale. No Associate joins Part IV's constitutional or review-scope ground.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Congress expressly authorized federal suit and otherwise available remedies

**Holding and operative rule:** Sections 1122(a)–(b) and 1125(a)(1)(B), (a)(2) unmistakably subject States and their instrumentalities to federal suit and otherwise available legal and equitable remedies for covered Lanham Act violations occurring on or after October 27, 1992. That enacted intent establishes neither constitutional power, State consent, a statutory violation nor entitlement to a particular award.

**Authority:** Part I, joined by all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 1122(a) expressly identifies State immunity and the federal forum. Subsection (b) separately provides remedies to the same extent as against nongovernmental defendants, preserving each remedy's conditions. Section 1125(a)(2) expressly includes States, while §1125(a)(1)(B) covers commercial misrepresentations about one's own or another's goods, services or commercial activities. The statutory clarity is not defeated by general coverage ambiguity. Seminole distinguishes Congress's unmistakable authorization from its power to impose the action; Lane, Blue Fox and West likewise keep substantive duties and actual remedial authority distinct in their own settings. Giving these provisions effect still requires a lawful basis for overcoming immunity and proof of the claim. The words do not find Florida's assent or turn an alleged violation into an award.

##### This own-product-advertising theory does not establish the required §5 basis

**Holding and operative rule:** The alleged loss of customers from Florida Prepaid's misdescription of its own product does not establish a constitutional entitlement to prospective customers or truthful competitive self-advertising, and the statutory tort and economic injury alone do not identify a constitutional deprivation to which this §5 remedy is fitted. Section 5 therefore does not sustain the asserted application on this theory; established business assets, goodwill and legal claims are not categorically excluded from constitutional property.

**Authority:** Part II, directly joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Stevens disagrees. The eight-Justice proposition is controlling despite the different judgment votes.

**Controlling explanation:** Roth and Paul require identification of the actual constitutional entitlement. Haddle permits statutory recovery for economic injury without equating that injury with deprivation of constitutional property. Sullivan preserves fair adjudication and established substantive entitlements while refusing to infer an unconditional payment right from a general statutory benefit. Those distinctions matter here: commercial injury and a federal remedy do not themselves establish the property deprivation that §5 enforces.

Boerne permits Congress to prevent constitutional injuries by reaching conduct that is not independently unconstitutional. The absence of a completed violation in each plaintiff's case is therefore not the objection. The defect is the absence of an identified constitutional deprivation to which the particular own-product-advertising remedy is fitted. Invoking a going business and goodwill does not make every lost sale caused by a competitor's self-description a deprivation of those assets without due process. Congress cannot supply the missing constitutional connection merely by creating the statutory tort.

The rule decides neither trademark appropriation, palming off, trade secrets, false statements about CSB's product nor every application of §1122. It creates no universal exclusion-right definition of property and demands neither formal legislative findings, a prescribed quantity of evidence, a violation in every jurisdiction, an individual constitutional violation for every prohibition, geographic limits nor a sunset. A properly supported preventive §5 remedy remains possible.

##### Continued ordinary commerce is not unequivocal consent to federal suit

**Holding and operative rule:** A State's continued operation of an otherwise lawful commercial undertaking does not unequivocally submit it to federal jurisdiction merely because Congress clearly and prospectively subjects that activity to private federal enforcement, even when the undertaking is realistically optional. Congress's legislative intent is distinct from the State's voluntary decision to consent.

**Authority:** Part III, directly joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer disagree.

**Controlling explanation:** Beers, Great Northern and Atascadero require the State's actual, unequivocal submission to the federal forum. Clear congressional language answers what Congress intended, not whether Florida chose to submit. A practical opportunity to leave ordinary commerce does not turn general regulation into acceptance of a specifically offered federal benefit or voluntary invocation of judicial authority. The relevant conduct remains operating the undertaking; the Court does not characterize the State as choosing to violate federal law.

The contrary prospective-condition argument gives proper weight to clear notice and realistic choice, but those features do not supply the missing act of assent in this commercial setting. Welch displaced the old inference from general FELA or Jones Act coverage. The express 1992 text cures a clarity problem without establishing a State waiver. The residual Parden inference necessary to this action therefore fails.

The Court does not adopt the Third Circuit's governmental-function classification. Nor does it disturb Hilton's independently retained FELA coverage and competent-state-court enforcement based on statutory continuity and reliance. Genuine voluntary waiver, properly conditioned federal benefits and interstate compacts remain distinct; no particular funding condition or coercion question is decided. Appearance and the dismissed counterclaim supply no holding here because the distinct litigation-waiver question is not reached.

##### The reversal has no common affirmative amenability rationale

**Judgment without controlling affirmative rationale:** Five Justices reverse the federal immunity dismissal. Stone-Zsela relies on Article I and an included-argument view of review scope; Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer rely on prospective commercial consent, and Stevens adds §5. These are distinct affirmative grounds, not five joins in any one of them.

**Authority and limit:** The Part V judgment binds the parties and permits the federal claim to return for ordinary adjudication. Parts I–III retain their direct majority support. No Marks rule makes the four-Justice consent position controlling: a participating majority expressly rejects that operative consent proposition, and consent and unilateral congressional displacement depend on different legal predicates. Stevens's §5 ground likewise cannot displace the eight-Justice rejection. No common constitutional or review-scope rule is extracted by combining unused alternatives.

The decision neither sustains nor rejects a general Article I abrogation power. It reaches no litigation-waiver question, particular accepted-benefit condition, new officer claim, underlying advertising merits or separate patent issue. A prospective officer action would require a connected officer, ongoing federal violation, proper claim and genuinely prospective relief; Edelman's retrospective-compensation boundary and Coeur d'Alene's actual limits remain.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Union Gas:** Its private damages judgment, statutory holding and five-vote constitutional conclusion remain effective within their decided scope. No majority here extends or overrules that conclusion, and Brennan's entire plurality does not become a majority rule.
- **Seminole Tribe:** Its clear-authorization distinction is applied. It established no controlling direct-State amenability rule or categorical Article I prohibition and did not overrule Union Gas.
- **Parden, Welch and Hilton:** The residual inference of federal-forum consent from ordinary commercial participation is rejected even under the new explicit enactment. Parden's independently retained substantive FELA coverage and Hilton's specialized state-court enforcement and reliance holding remain intact; no wholesale overruling of those retained propositions occurs.
- **Beers, Great Northern and Atascadero:** Their requirement of State consent supplies the distinction between unmistakable congressional intent and unequivocal State submission.
- **Boerne, South Carolina v. Katzenbach, Katzenbach v. Morgan and City of Rome:** The enforcement/redefinition boundary is applied while retaining real preventive legislation reaching nonviolations and each precedent's constitutional setting. No new numerical or formal legislative-record requirement is imposed.
- **Roth, Paul, Haddle and Sullivan:** Their distinctions between constitutional entitlement, statutory injury and fair adjudication are applied without denying established intangible property or converting every statutory tort into a constitutional deprivation. Logan's protection of a legal claim does not equate the underlying alleged commercial injury with a completed due-process violation.

- **Izumi, Caspari, Madsen, Lebron and X-Citement Video:** Their distinct question-presentation rules remain. There is no majority determination that this Article I argument is fairly included or exceptionally reviewable. Murphy, Kolstad and Olmstead retain their actual scope applications rather than creating an automatic rule for every alternative source of congressional power.
- **Hess, Lane, Blue Fox, Schacht, Cedar Rapids and West:** Their arm-status, remedy-specific consent, removal and accepted-funding distinctions remain confined to their actual settings. They establish neither Florida's commercial consent nor this claim's liability.
- **Ex parte Young, Edelman and Coeur d'Alene:** Their defendant, ongoing-violation and prospective-relief requirements remain; this judgment creates no officer defendant or substitute damages action.

#### Law After Decision

Effective June 23, 1999, the federal immunity dismissal is removed in this action, but no common affirmative rationale supplies a general rule permitting other direct-State Lanham suits. The express statutory authorization remains distinct from constitutional power, State consent and entitlement to relief.

The Court adds a controlling rejection of the proposed explicit prospective commercial-participation consent theory and a confined rejection of the asserted own-product-advertising §5 basis. It preserves actual voluntary consent, fitted preventive §5 legislation and Hilton's retained FELA state-court coverage. Union Gas remains precedent at its decided scope; neither a categorical Article I permission nor prohibition emerges here. Lower courts must respect the direct majority propositions and the mandate without treating the four-Justice consent theory, the one-Justice Article I theory or Stevens's separate §5 theory as a unified holding.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Stone-Zsela's nonmajority Part IV.** He would give effect to Congress's express federal remedy against a State selling competing investment products in interstate commerce. Union Gas supplies a retained Commerce-power conclusion; Seminole supplies no contrary categorical rule. On the federal-question account supported by Stevens's Union Gas concurrence, the Amendment's citizen-State diversity restriction does not prohibit this congressionally created federal action, and valid deliberate legislation may displace nontextual Hans immunity. That view does not depend on guessing Union Gas's corporate citizenship or adopting every statement of its plurality.

He regards the alternative source of congressional authority as an included legal argument for the same preserved federal immunity challenge, on the same established facts and for the same requested relief. He acknowledges the specifically framed §5 question but distinguishes the generic grant from a separate claim, new factual theory or expressly excluded question. Lebron supports the included-argument distinction; he does not rely on a prevailing-respondent defense or briefing alone. His position is confined to enforcement of this federal commercial obligation, not unlimited power to abolish every State immunity. He rejects constructive waiver and automatic constitutional property and finds no advertising violation or entitlement to relief. No other Justice joins these constitutional or scope grounds.

**Breyer, joined by Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg, concurring in part and in the judgment.** They find consent where Congress unmistakably and prospectively makes specified commercial conduct subject to private federal enforcement and the State continues an undertaking it can realistically choose not to operate. The relevant undertaking is competitive investment marketing, an optional way to further education, not education itself. The choice is whether to conduct that undertaking under the stated terms, not whether to violate the law. Clear advance notice, continuing covered activity and meaningful choice distinguish the condition from the displaced inference based on general statutory coverage.

They answer the objection that Congress alone imposed liability by treating this explicit prospective condition as a term accepted through continued optional activity. They do not assume a signed promise, federal subsidy, profit figure, exit-cost finding or withdrawal event. Nor do they revive original Parden, erase Florida Prepaid's arm status under Hess, or transfer Lane's federal consent and Cedar Rapids' accepted funding into an imaginary grant bargain. They would reverse and permit ordinary statutory adjudication without deciding direct Article I abrogation. Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer join the Court's confined §5 rejection; Stevens does not.

**Stevens, further concurring in part and in the judgment.** An existing going business, its economic activity and goodwill are property, and deliberate State destruction of that business can deprive its owner of property. Congress may protect against that category of constitutional injury through a defined unfair-competition remedy without requiring every covered advertisement itself to constitute a completed constitutional deprivation. He finds a reasonable preventive relationship, assessed under Boerne's congruence-and-proportionality rule and the presumption of legislative validity, between protection of the existing business interest and this private remedy.

He accepts the distinction between prevention and constitutional redefinition, rather than substituting general rational-basis review for Boerne. Haddle's statutory-injury distinction does not deny existing business property, and Sullivan's protection of substantive claims reinforces the need to identify and protect that interest. He would sustain the challenged §5 application as an independent ground for reversal. He finds neither the allegations true nor any remedy presently due. No other Justice joins this ground.

**O'Connor, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, dissenting.** They join the statutory clarity, §5 and consent holdings but would affirm within the two questions presented. Direct Article I abrogation identifies a different constitutional source; it answers neither whether Congress validly exercised §5 nor whether Florida actually consented. The same claim and the usefulness of an alternative do not themselves establish fair inclusion or an independently justified exception. Lebron concerns an included legal argument, while X-Citement Video permits a prevailing respondent to defend the same favorable judgment; CSB seeks reversal. Murphy and Olmstead reinforce attention to the actual grant; each leaves a presented but ungranted question undecided.

Their affirmance does not apply a categorical Seminole prohibition that the Court never adopted. Union Gas survives, and they leave the distinct Article I route undecided in this review. They preserve genuine voluntary waiver, valid §5 enforcement and properly available officer relief while finding neither of the presented routes sufficient for this claim. They would leave only the reviewed federal immunity dismissal in force and decide no underlying advertising violation, broader TRCA application or separate proceeding.

### Florida Prepaid Postsecondary Education Expense Board v. College Savings Bank, No. 98-531

Merits decision, June 23, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 20, 1999; decided June 23, 1999. On writ of certiorari to the Federal Circuit, reviewing 148 F.3d 1343, which affirmed the District of New Jersey's denial of Florida Prepaid's sovereign-immunity motion.

The question is whether Congress had power under §5 of the Fourteenth Amendment to abrogate State immunity and authorize federal patent-infringement suits through 35 U.S.C. §§271(h),296(a). CSB, the respondent, also defends the existing immunity denial on alternative grounds. The generic grant does not itself present every distinct constitutional source. The immunity denial is immediately reviewable before final adjudication of infringement.

CSB, a New Jersey bank, owns Patent No.4,722,055 and alleges that Florida Prepaid's competing tuition-prepayment program infringes it. Florida Prepaid is an admitted arm of Florida. Knowing and willful infringement and the requested declaratory, injunctive, and monetary relief remain allegations and requests. The Federal Circuit sustained §5 authority without deciding the preserved commercial- or litigation-consent theories.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and the other eight Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer.
**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas.

The Federal Circuit's judgment sustaining denial of immunity remains in place. The case returns for ordinary adjudication of patent validity, infringement, causation, defenses, and lawful remedies. No liability, damages, trebling, fees, compulsory license, or injunction is awarded.

The four dissenters would vacate the immunity-denial judgment and remand for ordinary treatment of the preserved actual-litigation-consent contentions and any other properly available ground under retained law. They would not direct unconditional dismissal, find consent, order additional proof or a hearing, or decide the patent merits. Their limited vacatur/remand is distinct from the Court's affirmance and return to patent proceedings.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Congress unmistakably authorized this federal patent action against States

**Operative proposition:** Sections 271(h) and 296 expressly subject States and their instrumentalities to private patent actions in federal court for covered violations on or after October 28, 1992. Section 296(b) makes the otherwise available patent remedies available on their own statutory terms; that authorization does not itself establish constitutional power, State consent, infringement, or entitlement to a particular award.

**Authority:** Stevens's Part I, joined by all nine Justices, directly adopts this statutory proposition and its limits.

**Controlling explanation:** Congress did not leave State coverage to inference from a general reference to persons or to federal-question jurisdiction. Section 271(h) identifies States and instrumentalities as persons subject to the patent obligations. Section 296(a) expressly addresses immunity, private claimants, and the federal forum; subsection (b) separately identifies available remedies. Seminole distinguishes this enacted authorization from the constitutional source of amenability, and Welch does not make general statutory coverage sufficient consent to federal suit. These provisions satisfy the distinct clarity inquiry. They still require a valid basis for proceeding against the State and proof of every element and remedial condition. No infringement, compensable injury, enhancement, fee award, or injunction follows from the text alone.

##### Patent property and unconstitutional deprivation remain distinct

**Operative proposition:** A patent is an established exclusive property interest; federal creation does not disqualify it from Fourteenth Amendment protection. Recognizing that property and alleging infringement do not alone establish deprivation without due process, compensable injury, or entitlement to an award; process, remedies, alleged conduct, and any applicable culpability requirement require separate treatment.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join this precise distinction in Part I. No additional patent-negligence rule or §5 fit conclusion receives that coalition's support.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 261 and the established patent-property authorities recognize an exclusive interest, rather than a mere expectation of continued competitive success. But a statutory infringement claim and a constitutional deprivation claim have different requirements. Available process and remedies cannot be assumed adequate or inadequate from the property's existence. Neither the allegation of knowing or willful infringement nor the request for damages adjudicates those additional matters. The Court does not decide whether Daniels's prison-negligence rule transfers wholesale to patent deprivation: Part II expressly reserves that extension. This common distinction supplies neither a judgment that §296(a) is valid §5 enforcement nor one that it fails substantive fit.

##### Appropriate respondent alternatives are legally available within their limits

**Operative proposition:** A respondent may defend an unchanged favorable judgment on an appropriately supported alternative that neither enlarges its rights nor reduces the adversary's rights under that judgment. CSB's posture permits consideration of its preserved defenses of the immunity denial, but preservation and briefing alone do not expand the §5 question or establish an alternative's merits.

**Authority:** Part I has all nine Justices' joins at this precise level. Their actual decisions on the alternative grounds remain distinct.

**Controlling explanation:** X-Citement Video permits an appropriately supported defense of an unchanged favorable judgment, while Izumi, Madsen, and Lebron preserve the boundary between included legal grounds and independent questions. Murphy leaves its two presented but ungranted questions undecided, while preserving the respondent-defense route for an unchanged judgment. Kolstad's inclusion of attribution within punitive relief and Olmstead's inclusion of the statutory duty's fundamental-alteration limit illustrate why the actual question and legal relationship matter; neither automatically includes a different constitutional source. CSB seeks to retain the denial of immunity, not obtain expanded relief. That posture permits consideration of properly supported alternatives without compelling every Justice to decide each one or establishing factual predicates by assumption. No general Article I reach rule, automatic consent finding, or exception for any preserved issue follows.

##### Section 5 independently sustains the authorized patent action

**Operative proposition:** Section 5 permits Congress to prevent unconstitutional property deprivations through congruent and proportional enforcement legislation reaching some conduct that is not independently unconstitutional. Sections 271(h) and 296 validly authorize this patent action on that basis; the plaintiff need not establish a separate constitutional violation to proceed under the Act.

**Authority:** Stevens's Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer, states an independently sufficient holding of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** Fitzpatrick establishes Congress's authority to subject States to suit when enforcing the Fourteenth Amendment. Boerne requires congruence and proportionality between the threatened constitutional injury and the means of prevention, while permitting some reach beyond independently unconstitutional conduct. Patents are existing exclusive property interests protected by that Amendment. Congress enforced those obligations rather than redefining the constitutional right.

Exclusive federal infringement jurisdiction and uncertain alternative State remedies support Congress's judgment that immunity could leave unconstitutional deprivations without adequate redress. State use can cause completed loss that prospective relief cannot repair. Proof difficulties and uncertainty about effective compensation reinforce the need for prevention in this defined field. Ordinary infringement can occur without a constitutional violation, but that does not defeat a remedy fitted to the risk of unconstitutional deprivation. National uniformity and equal competition alone would not supply Section 5 power; the protected property and the remedial relationship do.

The Court does not find every State remedy inadequate or equate alleged willfulness with a constitutional wrong. Whether Daniels's negligence rule transfers to patent deprivations remains reserved. The Act's validity does not require each plaintiff to prove a constitutional tort, and allowing this action preapproves no particular award. Compensation, enhanced damages, fees, and injunctions retain their own conditions and properly presented constitutional limits.

##### Article I independently sustains the expressly authorized action

**Operative proposition:** Congress may authorize private enforcement against States when the underlying obligation and enforcement measure fall within its Article I authority and Congress unmistakably authorizes the State's exposure to suit. The Patent Clause, together with the Necessary and Proper Clause, supports this federal patent remedy as an independent ground for sustaining the immunity denial.

**Authority:** Stevens's Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer, states an independently sufficient holding of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** Congress created a national patent system, concentrated infringement adjudication in federal court, and expressly included States in §§271(h) and 296. Uniform patent rights lose important content if a State may exploit a patented invention while withholding the ordinary federal remedy. The Patent Clause and Necessary and Proper Clause authorize enforcement of this valid federal obligation through the expressly enacted action.

Union Gas's retained five-vote constitutional conclusion supplies the Article I foundation. The Court extends that conclusion into the enforcement principle stated here without treating every proposition in its plurality rationale as binding. Seminole distinguished unmistakable statutory authorization from constitutional amenability and set aside the State-dismissal direction without a controlling constitutional rationale. It established neither a categorical Article I prohibition nor an overruling of Union Gas, and required no abrogation-before-officer sequence. The present holding resolves the constitutional question it left open.

The Eleventh Amendment limits the citizen-State diversity grants, not jurisdiction over this federal question. CSB's out-of-State citizenship does not change the claim's federal source. Hans does not establish an immunity that Congress cannot displace in this setting. The structural objection therefore cannot defeat an enforcement measure within Congress's enumerated authority and an unmistakable choice to permit suit.

X-Citement Video permits consideration of this preserved alternative source of power because CSB defends the same immunity-denial judgment without enlarging its relief. The holding is claim-specific: it removes immunity from this authorized action, finds no infringement or damages entitlement, and awards no relief. Neither this ground nor Section 5 depends on commercial participation constituting consent.

##### Continued commercial participation does not supply consent to this suit

**Operative proposition:** A State's continued commercial participation after Congress authorizes suit does not constitute consent: Florida Prepaid did not consent to federal patent suit by continuing its prepaid-tuition program after §§271(h) and 296 took effect. Actual consent through a distinct State choice, such as an express waiver or voluntary invocation of federal jurisdiction, remains available. Whether Florida Prepaid's litigation conduct waived immunity need not be decided, and no waiver finding is made.

**Authority:** Stevens's Part IV, joined by all nine Justices, applies College Savings Part III and states these limits.

**Controlling explanation:** College Savings Part III, announced earlier the same day, holds that a State's continued operation of an otherwise lawful commercial undertaking does not unequivocally submit it to federal jurisdiction merely because Congress clearly and prospectively subjects that activity to private federal enforcement, even when the undertaking is realistically optional. CSB's commercial-condition alternative rests on that inference: enacted notice, covered post-enactment activity, and Florida Prepaid's practical choice to keep marketing its competing tuition-prepayment program. Those circumstances establish Congress's intent, not Florida's voluntary submission. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer did not join College Savings Part III, but they accept it as controlling; no changed premise or special justification supports departing from it here.

Express waiver and voluntary invocation of federal jurisdiction remain distinct State choices that can supply actual consent; properly conditioned federal benefits and interstate compacts keep their own rules. CSB separately contends that Florida Prepaid waived immunity by appearing, seeking transfer under §1404(a), and requesting declarations of invalidity, noninfringement, and unenforceability. No Justice decides whether any of that conduct waived immunity, and no waiver finding is made.

##### Limits and questions not reached

The Court makes no finding of patent validity, infringement, willfulness, Florida-remedy adequacy, constitutional injury, or entitlement to relief. Section 296(b) retains its own conditions and any properly presented award-specific constitutional limits. Neither constitutional holding nor the judgment preapproves every compensation, injunction, fee, or enhanced award. Kolstad's Title VII punishment rules do not determine patent enhancement.

The appearance, transfer request under §1404(a), and request for affirmative declarations of invalidity, noninfringement, and unenforceability remain distinct litigation-consent contentions. The Court finds no waiver from any of them. Schacht does not settle their effect. No actual-violation exception, willfulness exception, State-remedy exhaustion, new constitutional damages cause, or Just Compensation Clause justification is adopted.

The action is not converted into an officer suit. Young and Coeur d'Alene require a connected officer, ongoing federal violation, proper cause, and genuinely prospective relief; Edelman and Pennhurst preserve retrospective-compensation and state-law boundaries. No Florida claims-bill or takings remedy is adjudged adequate or inadequate, and State Street supplies no invalidity finding about this different patent.

### Powell v. Ridge

Merits decision, April 3, 2000

#### Chronology and Posture

Certiorari granted December 13, 1999; submitted February 28, 2000. On review of the Third Circuit's judgment at 189 F.3d 387, reversing dismissal of two claims challenging alleged racial inequality in Pennsylvania's public-school funding. Count I seeks direct enforcement of 34 C.F.R. §100.3(b)(2); Count II invokes 42 U.S.C. §1983. Both seek declaratory and prospective injunctive relief, not damages.

The questions are whether Title VI's disparate-impact regulation is privately enforceable through either channel, and whether the regulatory theory permits relief without the intentional discrimination required by §601 itself. Parents and schoolchildren allege that districts with more nonwhite pupils receive less state funding at comparable poverty levels and that the resulting resource limits impair educational opportunities. These remain pleading-stage allegations, not findings of discrimination, causation or a funding entitlement.

All nine Justices participated at submission and decision.

#### Judgment

| Component | Disposition and vote | Supporting | Opposing | Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Count I: directly implied action to enforce the disparate-impact regulation | Reverse, 5–4 | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Reject the direct impact-only theory; remand for proceedings consistent with that holding. |
| Count II: §1983 enforcement of the asserted impact-only right | Reverse, 5–4 | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Reject the asserted statutory effects right; preserve only genuinely remaining ordinary procedural possibilities. |

The complaint contains no separate statutory §601 count. A genuinely preserved intentional-discrimination theory or an ordinary amendment request retains its actual procedural footing; the Court creates no claim and guarantees no amendment. The judgment does not invalidate the regulation, restrict authorized agency enforcement or funding remedies, find nondiscrimination, or order a funding cutoff.

#### Opinion Topology

Justice Thomas delivered the opinion of the Court, joined throughout by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Scalia and Kennedy. All five join the distinct direct-action and section 1983 holdings, the remedy and every stated limit. Justice Stevens dissented, joined by Justices Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, supporting both prospective enforcement channels and remand. Section 1983 is independently sufficient for the dissent; Stevens would also accept leaving the direct-action alternative unresolved if section 1983 were sustained, but does not join its rejection.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The regulation does not supply the directly implied action asserted in Count I

**Holding:** Title VI does not create a directly implied private action to enforce this §602 regulation's disparate-impact prohibition merely because the regulation validly implements the statutory program. Section 601's established private protection against intentional discrimination and §602's authority for agency implementation do not show congressional creation of the broader impact-only private remedy asserted here.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that regulatory authority and a private judicial remedy answer different questions. Section 601 protects persons against racial, color or national-origin discrimination in federally assisted programs; its established statutory action reaches intentional discrimination. Section 602 directs agencies to effectuate that protection through general rules consistent with the funding statute's objectives and supplies public compliance machinery. It contains no express private action for every condition those rules impose. Cannon's established beneficiary action does not automatically extend to an additional effects obligation. Guardians and Choate recognize the distinction between statutory intentional discrimination and regulatory effects, but their discussion does not decide the private remedy now asserted. The contrary reading draws force from the identified beneficiaries and express implementation authority. Those features support regulatory administration; they do not themselves demonstrate congressional creation of this broader private action. The Court neither invalidates §100.3(b)(2) nor holds that every implementing regulation lacks private significance. It resolves this claim's asserted remedy and preserves §601's established intentional-discrimination action.

##### Section 1983 does not create the missing individual effects right

**Holding:** Count II fails independently because §601's intentional-discrimination protection and §602's instructions to agencies do not confer the broader individual statutory right against disparate impact asserted here. A regulation may elaborate an existing statutory entitlement, but this regulation cannot by itself create the missing broader right for §1983 to enforce; rejection rests on right creation, not congressional foreclosure of an established right.

**Controlling explanation:** Blessing first asks for a particular provision intended to benefit the claimant, imposing a binding duty and defining a sufficiently definite individual entitlement. Only after that right exists does §1983's presumptive enforcement and congressional-foreclosure inquiry arise. Suter likewise keeps the right and directly implied action distinct, while the CAPTA decision shows that a binding agency obligation need not confer the personal entitlement asserted. Wright and Wilder permit enforceable statutory rights whose concrete content includes authorized regulatory elaboration; they do not allow a regulation to enlarge the substantive personal right beyond its statutory foundation. Here §601 identifies protected individuals but supplies an intentional-discrimination right; §602 addresses agency implementation rather than conferring the additional effects entitlement. Bruneau preserves §1983 enforcement of an identified Title IX right despite administrative funding controls and an implied action, but does not enlarge that right. The Court therefore does not treat failure of Count I as dispositive of Count II, rely on funding sanctions as comprehensive foreclosure, or categorically reject qualitative rights and regulatory elaboration. A useful enforcement vehicle cannot itself supply the missing substantive right.

##### Scope and unresolved matters

The children's alleged comparative treatment injury presents the controversy; the Court does not dismiss for lack of Article III standing. Prospective state officers are not categorically excluded as nonpersons, but the judgment establishes no defendant's liability or entitlement to relief. The municipal plaintiffs' separate standing is unnecessary to the decision.

Section 100.3(b)(2) concerns a covered recipient's criteria or methods of administering services, aid, benefits or facilities that cause the prohibited discrimination or defeat or substantially impair program objectives for protected individuals. It is not a prohibition of every statistical disparity. The Court decides no regulatory validity, recipient coverage, discriminatory purpose, causal funding effect, trial burden framework or statewide allocation requirement. Gebser and Davis govern attribution of intentional discrimination in their own settings and do not create this effects right. Properly presented intentional-discrimination claims remain subject to their own requirements.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Cannon v. University of Chicago preserves the established private-action setting for the statutory protection; it does not automatically supply a private action for the broader effects condition.
- Guardians Association v. Civil Service Commission and Alexander v. Choate preserve the statutory/regulatory distinction; their divided positions and remedial assumptions are not converted into an express majority holding on the private channels resolved here.
- Blessing v. Freestone supplies the particular-right inquiry and separate foreclosure analysis. Its allowance for qualitative entitlements and regulatory elaboration remains.
- Suter v. Artist M. preserves provision-specific rights and distinct direct-action analysis; it supplies no categorical immunity for funding statutes.
- Wright v. Roanoke and Wilder v. Virginia Hospital Association retain enforceable individual statutory entitlements and authorized elaboration, distinguished from creation of the additional effects right here.
- Doe v. District of Columbia supplies the distinction between a binding CAPTA agency command and the personal right asserted under §1983.
- Bruneau v. South Kortright Central School District preserves enforcement of an identified Title IX right without enlarging its substantive duty; administrative funding oversight alone is not automatic foreclosure.
- Gebser and Davis retain their intentional-discrimination attribution holdings; their recipient-responsibility rules do not decide this impact-only theory.
- NCAA v. Smith preserves the requirement of actual federal-assistance receipt separately from economic connection, without resolving coverage here.
- Will and Ex parte Young preserve the distinction between prospective relief against state officers and a categorical nonperson objection; they establish no merits liability.

#### Law After Decision

This Title VI impact regulation supplies neither the directly implied action nor the individual statutory right asserted through §1983. The two routes fail for separate reasons. Section 601 intentional-discrimination enforcement, genuine regulatory elaboration of existing rights, and authorized agency enforcement remain intact. Section 602's administrative conditions do not become private exhaustion requirements.

Agency termination or refusal of assistance remains subject to an express finding after a hearing opportunity, confined to the recipient and program or part found noncompliant; advice of noncompliance and inability to secure voluntary compliance; and a report to the appropriate congressional committees followed by thirty days before termination or refusal becomes effective. Other means authorized by law remain distinct. General rules remain subject to §602's statutory consistency and presidential-approval requirements. This judgment orders no agency enforcement action.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Stevens, dissenting and joined by Justices Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, would permit both prospective channels. Section 601 identifies the individual beneficiaries, §602 expressly authorizes implementation of their protection, and §100.3(b)(2) gives concrete content to nondiscriminatory administration of covered benefits. Cannon supports the established private-action setting; Guardians and Choate support the effects regulation's substantive account, without automatically deciding the remedy. The dissent reads that particular combination as authorized elaboration of individual protection, not a general rule that any valid agency regulation creates a private suit.

Section 1983 independently supplies an enacted enforcement vehicle once that right is identified under Blessing. Wright supports reading statutory protection with its authorized regulatory content. Section 602's general funding machinery offers no comparable personal remedy or demonstrated incompatibility, and Bruneau rejects automatic foreclosure from funding oversight or an existing implied action. Failure to imply a direct action would therefore not alone defeat §1983. The dissent rejects an additional §601-intent element for proof of this effects regulation, while retaining intent for the distinct statutory claim.

The dissent would affirm the reversal of dismissal and allow ordinary adjudication. It finds no discriminatory purpose, violation, funding amount, coverage or causation, and would require a covered recipient, prohibited administrative practice, causal injury and fitted relief. It orders no appropriation, equalization, statistical presumption, automatic discovery, damages or new statewide formula. Stevens regards §1983 as sufficient to leave the direct-action alternative unresolved if necessary, but does not accept rejection of that alternative.

### Board of Trustees of the University of Alabama v. Garrett, No. 99-1240

Merits decision, February 21, 2001

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 11, 2000. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit, 193 F.3d 1214. The court of appeals reversed the district court's immunity disposition in the consolidated employment actions. The question is whether Congress validly authorized private federal damages actions against state employers under Title I of the Americans with Disabilities Act. Garrett alleges disability-related adverse treatment at a state hospital; Ash alleges employment discrimination and denied accommodation associated with asthma and sleep apnea. Those allegations establish no statutory violation. This review concerns Title I only.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer.

**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stone-Zsela | Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Affirmance, 5–4 | Entire opinion; statutory authorization and independent Article I and Section 5 grounds |
| Dissent | O'Connor | Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Would reverse the Title I immunity rejection | Structural State-suit objection and insufficient Section 5 fit; statutory clarity accepted |
| Supplementary dissent | Kennedy | O'Connor | Same proposed reversal | Official prejudice may arise from indifference; opposition rests on the particular remedy's fit, not presumed State compliance |

#### Holdings

##### I. Article I sustains this expressly authorized employment action

**Holding and operative rule:** Congress may subject a State to the expressly authorized private Title I federal action when the covered employment obligation and its enforcement measure fall within the Commerce and Necessary and Proper Clauses. Those conditions are satisfied here, so the State defendants have no categorical immunity from these actions; authorization and amenability establish neither liability nor entitlement to a particular award.

**Authority:** The five-Justice opinion of the Court identified above directly adopts this independently sufficient ground and the Section 5 ground below.

**Controlling explanation:** Florida Prepaid permits expressly authorized private enforcement of a valid Article I duty against a State; Kimel applies that principle to employment. Title I regulates hiring, retention and other terms of covered employment, an economic relationship participating in actual labor markets. That connection satisfies Lopez before aggregate effects are considered; generalized productivity or social costs do not supply the constitutional basis. Garcia and EEOC v. Wyoming sustain direct federal regulation of covered state employment. An ordinary action enforcing the valid duty is an appropriate enforcement measure, not compelled State administration of a federal program.

Section 12202 expressly removes State immunity for violations of the chapter and makes legal and equitable remedies available on the same terms as against other public or private entities. Section 12117 supplies the employment-enforcement channels. These provisions resolve authorization without establishing consent. The States' distinction between valid employment regulation and private suit seeks reconsideration of Florida Prepaid and Kimel; the Court adheres to their enforcement rule. Requiring an additional individual constitutional violation would remove the statutory protection that Congress validly enacted. The merits and award-specific limits remain for ordinary adjudication.

##### II. Section 5 independently sustains the presented Title I enforcement

**Holding and operative rule:** Title I's protection of qualified workers against disability discrimination, including reasonable accommodation subject to its enacted qualifications and defenses, is congruent and proportional preventive and remedial enforcement of equal protection against state employers. Section 5 therefore independently sustains the expressly authorized actions without requiring each employee to prove a separate constitutional violation.

**Authority:** The same five-Justice opinion directly adopts this alternative sufficient holding; no combination of separate opinions is needed.

**Controlling explanation:** Boerne permits prevention extending beyond conduct that a court would independently hold unconstitutional, while requiring a fitted relationship to the constitutional injury. Cleburne forbids irrational disability prejudice. Heller and Romer require a rational relationship to the actual disadvantage while preserving relevant generalizations and conceivable legitimate explanations; they create neither heightened disability scrutiny nor a constitutional entitlement to every accommodation.

Congress received accounts of categorical exclusions, stereotyped assumptions, harassment and rejection in State departments and universities, alongside evidence concerning local employers, private conduct and other public services. The Court does not treat every reported incident as an adjudicated constitutional wrong or the approximately 600 state-actor submissions as a count of State-employment violations. The differentiated employment evidence and history nevertheless support the preventive inference that official exclusion can conceal or perpetuate irrational prejudice. Statutes prohibiting discrimination do not by their existence establish effective protection or compliance.

The States correctly observe that accommodation and screening duties can exceed constitutional rationality. That difference is permitted prevention, not sufficient proof of invalidity. Qualification, essential-function requirements, known-limitations rules, job-related screening and the separate hardship and accommodation defenses connect the means to the identified risk. Florida Prepaid confirms that fitted preventive enforcement need not demand an individual constitutional tort from every plaintiff. No incident quota, formal-findings requirement, geographic limit or sunset is imposed. The Court sustains the bounded employment scheme; it does not redefine every statutory breach as unconstitutional.

**Statutory limits and work remaining:** Covered-employer status requires an industry affecting commerce and ordinarily at least 15 employees for each working day in at least 20 calendar weeks of the current or preceding year; the original two-year transition used 25 employees. Agents are included; the United States, wholly owned federal corporations, Indian tribes and bona fide private membership clubs other than labor organizations that are exempt from taxation under 26 U.S.C. section 501(c) are excluded from this employer definition. Actual coverage remains to be proved.

Disability retains impairment substantially limiting a major life activity, record-of-impairment and regarded-as branches. A qualified worker must perform essential functions with or without reasonable accommodation. Employer judgment receives consideration; a job description prepared before advertising or interviews is evidence, not a conclusive veto. Accommodation examples are permissive and include reassignment to a vacant position. Section 12112(b)(5)(A) concerns known limitations and allows the employer to demonstrate undue hardship: significant difficulty or expense assessed by cost, facility and entity resources, workforce, operational and organizational factors. Section 12112(b)(6) separately addresses screening criteria shown job-related for the position and consistent with business necessity; section 12113(a) separately requires that performance cannot be accomplished by reasonable accommodation. Direct threat concerns a significant risk to the health or safety of others that reasonable accommodation cannot eliminate. No general reasonableness exception or Title II fundamental-alteration defense replaces those provisions.

No medical-inquiry, foreign-workplace, religious-entity, food-handling or drug/alcohol defense is established or adjudicated here. The Court creates no exception or holding about those distinct provisions. Title II, Rehabilitation Act waiver, individual liability and award-specific limitations lie outside this Title I abrogation question; no Title II dismissal or abandonment is ordered. Qualification, discrimination, causation and relief are ordinary remaining merits issues, not unresolved aspects of the constitutional enforcement holdings.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Florida Prepaid, June 23, 1999: applies both its express Article I enforcement rule and its independent congruent-and-proportional prevention principle; neither requires consent or an individual constitutional tort.
- Kimel, January 11, 2000: applies its Article I employment-enforcement holding; its reservation of the ADEA's Section 5 validity remains untouched.
- Lopez: applies the identifiable commerce connection before aggregation; no general social-cost theory or automatic jurisdictional-word test is adopted.
- Garcia and EEOC v. Wyoming: apply their support for valid federal regulation of state employment.
- Boerne: applies congruence and proportionality, preserving preventive authority and the prohibition on constitutional redefinition.
- Cleburne, Heller and Romer: apply actual rationality review without heightened disability scrutiny or a constitutional guarantee of accommodation.
- Olmstead: distinguishes its statutory integration holding and express nondecision of immunity and Section 5; its Title II defense is not imported.
- Union Gas and Seminole Tribe: preserve the constitutional conclusion extended in Florida Prepaid and the distinction between authorization and power; no categorical Article I prohibition displaces current law.
- College Savings Bank: preserves the rejection of consent inferred from ordinary State activity; these actions rest on legislative authority.

#### Law After Decision

Effective February 21, 2001, private federal Title I actions against covered state employers may proceed under the express enforcement provisions on either of two independent grounds: valid Article I employment enforcement and congruent and proportional Section 5 prevention. The existing general frameworks remain; the decision adds their controlling Title I application. Courts must apply the statutory coverage, qualification, defenses, proof and remedy conditions in each case. The ADA's broader duties do not become the constitutional minimum, and ordinary adjudication does not require proof of an individual constitutional tort as an added entry condition.

#### Separate Writings

**Justice O'Connor, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, dissenting.** The dissent accepts section 12202's express authorization and Congress's authority to regulate employment, but distinguishes that authority from power to compel a nonconsenting State to defend a private damages action. It would reconsider Florida Prepaid and Kimel's Article I enforcement rule and the retained Union Gas conclusion to the necessary extent. Independently, it finds Title I's accommodation, screening, justification and private-remedy reach insufficiently connected to the identified unconstitutional State-employment conduct under Boerne. It recognizes preventive authority and irrational official prejudice; it requires no numerical incident threshold, formal findings, violation in every State, sunset or constitutional tort in every preventive action. The statutory safeguards narrow the gap but do not, in its view, establish the required fit. It would reverse only the presented Title I immunity rejection.

**Justice Kennedy, joined by O'Connor, additionally dissenting.** Disability exclusion can result from official indifference and persistent stereotyping as well as express hostility. The existence of state antidiscrimination laws does not prove compliance, and lack of reported constitutional litigation does not settle Congress's enforcement authority. The disagreement concerns whether the employment evidence and safeguards adequately connect this private damages regime to the constitutional injury. The writing preserves ordinary rationality review and lawful prevention while joining the proposed scoped reversal.

### Alexander v. Sandoval, No. 99-1908

Merits decision, April 24, 2001

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 16, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit, reviewing its judgment affirming an injunction requiring Alabama's Department of Public Safety to accommodate non-English speakers in driver's-license examinations.

The Department receives federal assistance and adopted an English-only examination policy. The district court found prohibited national-origin effects; the court of appeals affirmed. The question is whether private individuals may enforce the Title VI section 602 disparate-impact regulations through the asserted direct action. Review accepts regulatory validity and the unchallenged impact finding, and does not reach a separate section 1983 claim.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

The affirmance recognizes the asserted direct prospective enforcement route and sustains the reviewed injunction on the accepted findings. The injunction remains operative within the scope reviewed. No damages, regulatory invalidation, new section 1983 claim or funding cutoff is ordered.

#### Opinion Topology

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela delivered the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Their agreement covers the direct-action holding, partial overruling, explanations, application, remedy and limits. Thomas dissented, joined throughout by O'Connor, Scalia and Kennedy, on the Title VI authorization and statutory stare-decisis grounds, preserving the stated boundaries; they would reverse the direct-action basis and remand.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Prospective enforcement of Title VI's valid implementing requirements

**Controlling proposition:** Title VI's established private action permits statutory beneficiaries to obtain prospective relief requiring a covered recipient to comply with valid, sufficiently definite section 602 regulations implementing the statutory protection against discrimination, including the effects regulation assumed valid here. Congress supplies the action through Title VI read as a whole; a regulation cannot independently create an action under an otherwise remediless statute, and plaintiffs must prove the regulation's actual elements and obtain relief fitted to the violation rather than rely on statistical imbalance alone.

**Authority:** The five-Justice Opinion of the Court controls this rule, its explanation, the application and remedy, and the treatment of Powell below, at the stated level of generality.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress's protection and its authorized implementation operate together. Section 601 identifies the persons protected from discrimination in federally assisted programs; section 602 expressly directs implementation of that protection. Cannon supplies the established private-action setting and a contextual inquiry into congressional authorization. Congress's 1986 recognition of legal and equitable remedies in Title VI suits and the Civil Rights Restoration Act's preservation of the enforcement structure provide affirmative statutory context, not an inference from silence alone. Read together, these provisions support prospective enforcement of valid, definite administration requirements protecting those beneficiaries.

Petitioners correctly distinguish an agency's authority to impose a duty from Congress's authorization of a judicial action. But section 602's agency-facing language does not sever its implementing standards from the private action accepted within this particular statutory scheme. Administrative funding enforcement is not made the exclusive means of securing compliance. The additional effects obligation does not become an intentional-discrimination claim under section 601; prospective enforcement rests on the integrated statute. Guardians and Choate explain that substantive distinction without themselves conclusively deciding this action.

McClellan requires contextual authorization and rejects an unsupported Cable Act damages action; it does not impose an express-action-clause prerequisite or erase Title VI's established enforcement background. Central Bank preserves a confined received securities action and confirms the need to examine the particular scheme, without deciding this Title VI question. Franklin separates an action's existence from available relief; an injunction request does not supply its own authority. The practical loss of an effective individual remedy confirms the consequences of the competing readings, but does not create the action.

The Department receives federal assistance. The regulations' validity and the finding of unjustified discriminatory effects are accepted premises of this review. Sandoval may maintain this prospective action, and the reviewed injunction remains supported by that route.

##### Powell's direct-action restriction is overruled to the stated extent

**Controlling proposition:** Powell v. Ridge is overruled insofar as its direct-action holding forecloses the prospective Title VI enforcement just described. Its distinct section 1983 holding is not decided or abrogated, and today's judgment neither authorizes retrospective damages for unintentional discrimination nor disturbs a completed final judgment.

**Authority:** The same five Justices join this express partial overruling and its complete statutory stare-decisis explanation in the Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court does not distinguish Powell on the ground that Sandoval seeks an injunction. Powell itself rejected prospective enforcement of the additional effects obligation. Nor do a different state policy or a finding instead of an allegation of effects change its action-creation rule. Reconsideration is necessary.

Statutory stare decisis deserves substantial weight because Congress can correct an interpretation and courts ordinarily preserve settled constructions. Powell nevertheless gave insufficient weight to the integrated source of Title VI's accepted beneficiary action. It treated the difference between statutory intentional-discrimination liability and valid preventive implementation as dispositive of prospective enforcement, although that difference does not answer the statute-specific authorization question. The Court corrects that identified error in the enforcement arrangement, rather than making disagreement alone a reason to overrule precedent.

Powell is recent, and no concrete reliance arrangement has been identified here. Neither consideration independently permits overruling a statutory decision. Their significance is that covered recipients already owed the regulatory duties whose prospective enforcement was barred. Restoring that enforcement channel does not prescribe a new substantive language policy, impose retrospective damages, or reopen a final judgment. Reliance on the absence of a beneficiary's action does not outweigh the better-supported statutory arrangement in these circumstances. The Chief Justice acknowledges that he joined Powell's contrary holding.

The Court preserves Powell's distinction between regulatory validity and the source of a private remedy, while rejecting its conclusion about this prospective Title VI action. Powell's separate section 1983 analysis concerns a distinct enforcement vehicle and individual statutory-right inquiry. That question is outside this grant and remains untouched.

##### Application, remedy and limits

The Eleventh Circuit's judgment is affirmed insofar as it recognizes this private prospective action and sustains the injunction requiring accommodation of non-English speakers in driver's-license examinations. The injunction remains operative on the accepted findings and within the scope reviewed. No damages are awarded and no regulation is invalidated.

Regulatory validity, the correctness of the disparate-impact merits determination, damages availability for unintentional discrimination, and a separate section 1983 action are outside the granted question. The accepted section 601 action for intentional discrimination remains, with its own intent requirement; this judgment finds no intentional discrimination. Ordinary application of the announced rule requires proof of the actual regulatory elements, coverage and fitted relief. Those applications are work under the governing rule, not a reservation about whether this category of prospective enforcement exists. The Court creates no new claim, funding cutoff, private administrative-exhaustion requirement or statistical presumption.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Powell v. Ridge, April 3, 2000:** Its direct-action prohibition is overruled only to the extent it bars prospective enforcement of valid, sufficiently definite Title VI implementing requirements protecting statutory beneficiaries. Its separate section 1983 holding, the distinction between regulatory validity and remedy authorization, and its completed judgment remain undisturbed.
- **Cannon v. University of Chicago:** Its contextual inquiry and established Title VI/Title IX private-action setting supply the foundation for the integrated statutory interpretation; Cannon is not treated as having already decided this regulatory action.
- **Guardians Association v. Civil Service Commission:** Its distinction between intentional-discrimination liability and regulatory effects protection is preserved. Its separate positions and remedial discussion are not converted into an earlier controlling resolution of the present action.
- **Alexander v. Choate:** Its account of the statutory/regulatory distinction remains; that distinction does not by itself answer prospective enforcement under Title VI as a whole.
- **McClellan v. Cablevision of Connecticut, Inc., January 7, 1999:** Its requirement of contextual congressional authorization is applied. Its rejection of the particular Cable Act damages action remains, distinguished by that statute's different remedial background rather than by a rule that requests for injunctions create actions.
- **Central Bank of Denver, N.A. v. First Interstate Bank of Denver, N.A., April 19, 1994:** Its confined received securities action remains intact; its contextual received-action rationale is consistent with, but does not itself establish, the Title VI rule adopted here.
- **Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools, February 26, 1992:** Its separation of an existing action from appropriate relief is applied. Its intentional-discrimination damages holding does not decide damages for unintentional effects here.
- **Blessing v. Freestone, April 21, 1997:** Its separate individual-right and section 1983 enforcement inquiries remain. The present Title VI direct-action holding supplies no new section 1983 ruling.

#### Law After Decision

Effective April 24, 2001, Title VI's established private action permits statutory beneficiaries to enforce prospectively valid, sufficiently definite section 602 requirements implementing their protection against discrimination. This includes valid effects regulations of the kind presented here. Plaintiffs must establish the regulation's actual elements and obtain relief fitted to the violation; statistical imbalance alone is not automatically enough.

Powell's contrary direct-action restriction no longer governs that category of prospective claims. Its separate section 1983 holding remains, as do section 601's intentional-discrimination requirement, valid administrative enforcement and the distinction between a regulatory duty and the congressional source of a private action. This decision neither creates private actions under otherwise remediless statutes nor resolves damages for unintentional effects. Its rule governs covered claims; it does not reopen completed final judgments.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Thomas dissents, joined throughout by O'Connor, Scalia and Kennedy. They would reverse insofar as the injunction rests on this direct regulatory-effects action and remand for ordinary proceedings on genuinely preserved claims and defenses, without creating a claim or guaranteeing amendment.

The dissent applies Powell's directly controlling prospective-relief rule. Section 601 supplies the accepted private protection against intentional discrimination; section 602 directs agency implementation and public compliance machinery. The distinction between those statutory functions matters because a valid additional effects obligation does not itself demonstrate congressional authorization of the broader private action. Cannon permits contextual implication but does not establish every regulatory action, and Guardians and Choate do not conclusively settle this enforcement route. The beneficiary-and-implementation argument is substantial, but Powell already rejected it in this precise prospective setting. A request for an injunction cannot cure the absence of an authorized action; Franklin concerns relief after that antecedent question is answered.

The dissent preserves McClellan's contextual inquiry and Central Bank's actual received securities action. It adopts neither a universal express-action-clause requirement nor a categorical denial of every regulation's private significance. Its sufficient ground is Title VI-specific. Regulatory validity, intentional-discrimination enforcement and authorized agency remedies remain intact.

Statutory stare decisis adds a separate objection to the Court's reconsideration. Congress can alter the rule, and a different policy, a proved rather than alleged effect, the decision's youth and continued disagreement do not change the authorization question. The dissent does not find that a recipient actually arranged its affairs in reliance on Powell. It considers the Court's account of the existing duty insufficient to overcome the distinction between a duty and a private cause of action. It would leave the accepted regulations in force while withdrawing this action as the injunction's legal foundation. It reaches no separate funding-notice ground, finds no discriminatory intent and supplies no damages, funding sanction or section 1983 claim.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Eleventh Circuit's judgment stands insofar as it recognizes the prospective action and sustains the reviewed injunction. The accommodation injunction remains operative on the accepted findings and within its reviewed scope. The Court retains no return condition and directs no new merits determination, damages proceeding or agency enforcement action.
