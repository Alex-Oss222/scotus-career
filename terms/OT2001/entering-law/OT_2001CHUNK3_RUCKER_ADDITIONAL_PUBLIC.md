### Austin v. United States, No. 92-6073

Merits disposition, 1993-06-28

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued 1993-04-20; decided 1993-06-28. On writ of certiorari to Eighth Circuit, 964 F.2d 814 (1992).

**Question:** Whether the Excessive Fines Clause applies to federal civil in rem forfeiture under 21 U.S.C. §§ 881(a)(4) and (a)(7), and the proper consequence if constitutional coverage exists.

**Material record:** Austin pleaded guilty to a South Dakota cocaine-possession-with-intent-to-distribute charge and received a state prison sentence. The United States separately sought the mobile home and auto-body shop under the federal forfeiture provisions. The account links a cocaine transaction to the two properties. The lower court did not undertake an Excessive Fines determination, and the Supreme Court record does not supply findings sufficient to fix their value, the constitutionally relevant proportionality, or the final forfeitable extent.

**Relief sought:** Austin seeks reversal of the ruling that the Clause does not reach the forfeitures and an opportunity to contest excessiveness. The United States seeks affirmance on the theory that these civil in rem forfeitures fall outside the Clause.

All nine Justices—Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas—participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded, 9–0, for an Excessive Fines inquiry. No order returning property or fixing its value.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### These statutory forfeitures are covered fines

**Controlling proposition:** Forfeiture to the United States under 21 U.S.C.§§ 881(a)(4) and(a)(7) constitutes payment in kind to the sovereign at least partly as punishment and is subject to the Excessive Fines Clause. Its civil designation, proceeding against property, and concurrent remedial aims do not remove coverage; coverage does not establish excessiveness or import all criminal-procedure safeguards.

**Authority:** Blackmun’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that these statutory takings cannot be characterized as solely remedial. Browning-Ferris distinguishes private awards from governmental extraction; here the United States prosecutes the action and receives the property. The statutory connection to drug offenses and the separate owner defenses make culpability relevant, while deterrence and punishment are evident in the provisions’ operation. Halper recognizes that civil proceedings can serve punitive purposes, but its distinct double-jeopardy accounting analysis is not the test adopted here. The Government’s instrumentality argument does not make ordinary lawful property equivalent to contraband: Plymouth Sedan rejects that equivalence for a vehicle. Nor do widely varying property values function simply as calibrated reimbursement of enforcement expenses. The Court therefore rejects a categorical civil-forfeiture immunity from the Clause. It does not decide every historical forfeiture, a constitutional innocent-owner defense, taxation or compensatory recovery, state incorporation, or the ultimate amount that may lawfully be taken from Austin.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Browning-Ferris v. Kelco Disposal, 492 U.S.257: government prosecution/receipt distinguishes this fine from private punitive award.
- United States v. Halper, 490 U.S.435: partly punitive function recognized by analogy; separate Double Jeopardy analysis not imported.
- One 1958 Plymouth Sedan v. Pennsylvania, 380 U.S.693: lawful property is not contraband merely because used in crime.
- One Lot Emerald Cut Stones v. United States, 409 U.S.232, and United States v. One Assortment of 89 Firearms, 465 U.S.354: remedial customs/contraband characterizations distinguished, not abolished.
- Buena Vista, February 24, 1993: statutory owner defenses remain provision-specific; no § 881(a)(7) or constitutional innocent owner holding imported.

##### Historical in rem form does not negate punishment

**Controlling proposition:** The historical treatment of statutory in rem forfeiture includes punishment and does not establish categorical exemption from the Excessive Fines Clause for these forfeitures. The traditionally limited common-law innocent-owner defense does not prove that forfeiture was solely remedial; the constitutionality of forfeiting a truly innocent owner’s property remains unresolved.

**Authority:** Blackmun’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter (5 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that historical forfeiture practice supplies no categorical civil-property exception. Peisch described forfeiture as punishment of an owner and declined to treat unauthorized removal by strangers as the owner’s forfeiting act. The Palmyra allowed condemnation without a prior personal conviction, but did not thereby establish a purely remedial purpose. Goldsmith-Grant and Calero-Toledo connect traditional forfeiture to negligence, care in entrusting property, and deterrence, while reserving serious questions involving a truly innocent owner. These authorities answer the argument that the property’s formal status as defendant necessarily excludes punishment of its owner. The history permits concurrent remedial purposes; it does not require their absence before a sanction can be punitive in part. The actual drug forfeiture provisions reinforce that understanding through their offense predicates and express owner protections. The Court leaves the reserved constitutional innocence question unresolved and does not turn every historical statement into a rule that all governmental acquisition of property is a fine.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Peisch v. Ware, 4 Cranch 347: punitive understanding and stranger-removal qualification applied.
- The Palmyra, 12 Wheat 1: no prior personal conviction required, not an absence of punishment holding.
- J.W. Goldsmith, Jr.-Grant Co. v. United States, 254 U.S.505, and Calero-Toledo v. Pearson Yacht, 416 U.S.663: punitive/deterrent features recognized; truly innocent owner question reserved.

##### Remand leaves excessiveness distinct from statutory connection

**Controlling proposition:** The lower court must evaluate whether Austin’s actual forfeiture is excessive; statutory facilitation and coverage as punishment do not themselves decide that question. The Court adopts no comprehensive excessiveness test and does not limit the remand to instrumentality alone: the property’s connection to the offense may matter, and other relevant considerations may also be examined.

**Authority:** Blackmun’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter (6 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that correcting the coverage error requires further consideration of the particular forfeiture. The court below expressed concern about the penalty but believed itself barred from applying the Excessive Fines Clause, so its comments are not a completed constitutional determination on developed findings. Browning-Ferris identifies governmental punishment as the coverage concern without supplying a comprehensive measure for this forfeiture. Calero-Toledo’s account of property connected to wrongdoing likewise does not decide the separate excessiveness inquiry. The proposed exclusive focus on instrumentality may identify a relevant consideration, but the Court leaves other considerations available rather than treating any sufficient statutory connection as conclusive. First-instance examination is appropriate because neither established values nor an applied constitutional standard are before this Court. No automatic return of the properties, numerical ratio, fixed monetary cap or ruling that the forfeiture necessarily survives follows. The Government retains its opportunity to establish lawful forfeiture within the statute and the constitutional limit.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Browning-Ferris, 492 U.S.257: fine coverage principle applied without inventing a comprehensive proportionality formula.
- Calero-Toledo, 416 U.S.663: statutory/historical connection not converted into a final excessiveness answer.

#### Current-Law Effect

Effective June 28, § 881(a)(4)/(a)(7) forfeitures are covered fines unanimously. Five adopt the historical partly punitive explanation; six expressly leave other factors available beyond instrumentality. No comprehensive excessiveness formula, mandatory ratio, automatic return, state incorporation or Double Jeopardy holding enters. Stone’s factor proposal and Scalia’s exclusive test remain noncontrolling.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia agrees that taking lawful property under these provisions is partly punitive and constitutes a fine. He rejects the need to establish owner blameworthiness across all historical forfeitures and would measure excessiveness through the property’s traditional closeness to the offense, treating value as irrelevant to that particular instrumentality inquiry. His exclusive test is not the Court’s rule. Kennedy, joined by Thomas, accepts the actual statutory conclusion but cautions against anachronistically imposing one personal-punishment theory on every historical forfeiture. He reserves whether such forfeitures invariably punish their owners and the constitutional question involving a truly innocent owner; he does not adopt Scalia’s exclusive remand test. Stone accepts coverage from the actual culpability-sensitive and deterrent statutory operation. He would examine the interest and supported value taken, seriousness and extent of wrongdoing, relevant owner culpability and the property’s actual role as nonexclusive considerations. He adopts no mandatory ratio, exhaustive checklist or later gross-disproportionality formula. The common remand leaves that choice of test open rather than converting Stone’s proposals into law.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court review of this merits event is complete. Federal § 881(a)(4)/(a)(7) forfeitures are covered; no lower court final excessiveness finding adopted. Determine excessiveness on a developed record; no automatic return or cap. Scalia would confine analysis to instrumentality; the Court leaves other factors available. Ordinary mandate procedure applies; no special issuance date, later filing or retained Supreme Court proceeding is established.

### Hurley v. Irish-American Gay, Lesbian and Bisexual Group of Boston, No. 94-749

Merits decision, June 19, 1995

Argued April 25, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court, which sustained application of the public-accommodations law requiring the private organizers to include GLIB. A continuing injunction against the traditional parade and the organizers’ desire to resume it keep the controversy live despite a differently organized 1995 event.

The Court reverses and remands, 9–0. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer support the judgment requiring dissolution of the compelled-inclusion injunction. Justice Souter delivers the single Opinion of the Court, joined in full by the other eight Justices. No separate writing is published.

Applying a public-accommodations law to compel private parade organizers to include an identified contingent conveying a message they do not choose violates the First Amendment. A parade remains expression despite broad admissions, multiple themes and the absence of a single precise message; the rule does not exempt ordinary nonexpressive goods or services from nondiscrimination law.

All nine Justices adopt this protection for private expression in Justice Souter’s Opinion of the Court.

The Court holds that the right to speak includes control over the composition of a private expressive presentation. Barnette prohibits compulsory affirmation, and Tornillo protects editorial choice against compelled inclusion. Turner preserves the importance of a speaker’s selection within a particular medium; its separate cable context is not mechanically imported here. A parade presents expression through the groups and messages its organizers choose, even if those selections are permissive or varied. GLIB’s bannered unit has its own protected message, but that protection creates no entitlement to insert it into another private speaker’s presentation. Use of public streets under a neutral permit does not convert this parade into a governmental forum. Roberts and PruneYard concern access without the same compelled alteration of expression. Independent review of intertwined expression characterizations, with credibility deference, rejects the conclusion that absence of a single theme eliminates protection. The Court invalidates only this compelled expressive inclusion, without finding every customer identity a message or requiring proof that spectators endorse each contingent.

West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette supplies protection against compelled expression.

Miami Herald Publishing Co. v. Tornillo supplies protection for editorial selection.

The Court applies the medium-specific editorial protection recognized in Turner Broadcasting System, Inc. v. FCC without importing its unrelated cable results.

Roberts v. United States Jaycees and PruneYard Shopping Center v. Robins are distinguished because they concern access that does not materially alter a private expressive presentation.

Bose Corp. v. Consumers Union and Fiske v. Kansas supply independent review of characterizations intertwined with the First Amendment issue, while retaining deference to credibility determinations.

The respondents’ state-action/public-forum theory against the City was dismissed and not cross-appealed; it is not revived here. The importance of message misattribution in another setting remains open. No general antidiscrimination exemption, facial invalidation or judgment about the correct political message is announced.

Ordinary nondiscrimination applications remain distinct and enforceable.

Private parade composition receives protection against compelled insertion of an identified expressive unit. Other expressive-association disputes require a comparably material expressive burden; ordinary customer identity alone is not a message.

**Source notes:** Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court, 418 Mass. 238, 636 N.E.2d 1293; respondent’s predecision mootness suggestion and petitioner’s response, Records and Briefs collection, No. 94-749. The continuing injunction supplies the live controversy; no later Court action or fee amount is determined.

The Supreme Judicial Court judgment is reversed and the case remanded. The injunction compelling GLIB’s inclusion shall be dissolved. The public-accommodations law remains enforceable where its application does not compel private expression. No fee amount, fine or sanction is decided.

**End of entry: Hurley v. Irish-American Gay, Lesbian and Bisexual Group of Boston, merits decision, June 19, 1995.**

### United States v. Bajakajian, No. 96-1487

Decision, June 22, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 4, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit. The question is whether forfeiture of the entire $357,144 transported out of the country without the required report violates the Excessive Fines Clause.

Bajakajian pleaded guilty to willfully failing to report currency under 31 U.S.C. §§5316 and 5322. A separate false-statement count was dismissed by agreement, not decided by acquittal. After a forfeiture trial, the District Court found a lawful source, intended payment of a lawful debt and no connection to another crime. It refused full forfeiture under 18 U.S.C. §982(a)(1), ordered $15,000 forfeited and imposed three years’ probation and a $5,000 fine. Section 5322(a) authorized up to five years and $250,000; the applicable Guidelines maxima were six months and $5,000.

The Ninth Circuit affirmed on the ground that reporting currency was not an instrumentality and thus could not be forfeited, without reaching its proportionality prong. The Government seeks the full fund. Bajakajian did not cross-appeal the $15,000 forfeiture.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia and Kennedy, who would reverse the refusal of full forfeiture.

The existing judgment remains in place. The Court does not decide the constitutional validity of the unappealed $15,000 forfeiture or the statutory authority to substitute that amount. The conviction, probation and ordinary fine are not reopened; no new forfeiture amount is fixed.

#### Opinions and Joins

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | Thomas | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Governing fines framework | Punitive coverage, gross disproportionality and rejection of a categorical instrumentality prerequisite. |
| Opinion of the Court, Part II | Thomas | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Appellate-review rule | Independent review of legal proportionality; clear-error review of supported factual findings. Stone-Zsela does not join. |
| Opinion of the Court, Part III | Thomas | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Affirmance | Actual excessiveness of full forfeiture on these findings and bounded affirmance. |
| Dissent in part | Kennedy | O'Connor, Scalia | Would reverse the refusal of full forfeiture | Joins Parts I–II of the Court's opinion; rejects Part III's application and would permit the full statutory forfeiture. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. A punitive criminal forfeiture must not be grossly disproportionate to its offense

**Controlling proposition:** Section 982(a)(1)’s forfeiture imposed on this convicted offender as punishment is a fine subject to the Excessive Fines Clause. A punitive forfeiture violates that Clause when grossly disproportionate to the gravity of the offense for which it is imposed; statutory property connection alone does not answer excessiveness, and instrumentality is not a categorical prerequisite barring every currency-reporting forfeiture.

**Authority:** Part I commands all nine votes. Parts II and III have the distinct coalitions shown above. Each proposition below has direct majority support at its stated scope.

**Controlling explanation:** Austin recognizes partly punitive forfeitures as fines and leaves excessiveness separate from statutory connection. Alexander requires that independent inquiry for criminal forfeiture. The Government’s exaction here is imposed as punishment for the reporting conviction; it is not exempt because the currency is the property involved in the offense.

Substantial respect for legislative punishment judgments and the imprecision of gravity assessments make gross disproportionality, rather than strict equivalence, the governing boundary. Solem supplies that respect and proportionality analogy without deciding a new imprisonment question. The nature of the offense, culpability, harm, property’s relation to wrongdoing and authorized sanctions inform the comparison where relevant. They are not a compulsory exhaustive checklist, fixed ratio or government-cost formula. Congress may punish a reporting violation through property forfeiture, but its statutory authorization does not foreclose the constitutional inquiry. The Court rejects the circuit’s conclusion that such currency can never be forfeited for want of an instrumentality.

##### II. The legal proportionality judgment receives independent appellate review

**Controlling proposition:** Appellate courts independently review the constitutional determination whether this punitive forfeiture is grossly disproportionate, while reviewing supported underlying factual findings for clear error.

**Authority:** Part II commands the votes of all eight Associate Justices; Chief Justice Stone-Zsela does not join this proposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The facts found and the constitutional evaluation perform different work. Deference to the trial court’s supported findings does not transfer the legal boundary of the Excessive Fines Clause to unrestricted sentencing discretion. Independent review supplies no authority to invent an additional offense or substitute adverse facts for the findings. The Court announces no universal review standard for every mixed question.

##### III. Full forfeiture is grossly disproportionate on these findings

**Controlling proposition:** Forfeiture of the full $357,144 is grossly disproportionate to the willful reporting offense established here, considering the lawful funds and purpose, absence of another-crime connection, limited reporting harm and particular sentencing assessment together. The refusal of full forfeiture is affirmed without reviewing the existing $15,000 amount or setting a constitutional dollar ceiling.

**Authority:** The six Justices joining Part III directly adopt this application and bounded affirmance.

**Controlling explanation:** The reporting duty is serious, and willful failure remains culpable even when transporting the funds would otherwise be lawful. But the proved offense withheld information; the findings establish neither criminal proceeds nor laundering, trafficking, fraud or a separate revenue loss against which the whole fund may be measured. Dismissal of the false-statement count does not establish that no false statement occurred.

The applicable six-month and $5,000 Guidelines maxima reflect the particular offense’s assessed severity. Congress’s broader five-year and $250,000 maxima and the forfeiture authorization deserve weight but do not conclusively make this full exaction proportionate. Treating them as conclusive would deprive independent excessiveness review of work. The lawful source, lawful debt and lack of another-crime connection are relevant together; lawful property is not automatically immune from a fine.

The reporting system’s preventive function and the large unreported transaction supply the strongest contrary argument. They do not overcome the extraordinary mismatch on these supported findings. The Court substitutes no new amount. Because Bajakajian did not cross-appeal, neither $15,000’s validity nor authority to select it is decided; no rule identifies $15,001 as excessive. Wealth, income, livelihood effects, other materially different crime-connected forfeitures and a comprehensive rule for innocent owners or forfeitures directed against property remain unresolved.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Austin v. United States:** Partly punitive coverage and an excessiveness inquiry beyond statutory connection are applied; its holdings are not converted into an instrumentality-only test or a universal historical forfeiture theory.
- **Alexander v. United States:** Its independent offense-related Excessive Fines inquiry for criminal forfeiture is applied and given the gross-disproportionality formulation here.
- **Browning-Ferris Industries v. Kelco Disposal:** Its distinction between governmental punishment and private awards supports fine coverage.
- **Solem v. Helm:** Its legislative-deference and proportionality principles inform this fines rule without a new imprisonment holding.
- **Department of Revenue v. Kurth Ranch, United States v. Ursery and Hudson v. United States:** Their distinct Double Jeopardy classification rules remain intact and supply no automatic Excessive Fines result or universal cost ceiling.
- **One Lot Emerald Cut Stones and One Assortment of 89 Firearms:** Their distinct customs and forfeiture classifications establish no exemption or proportionality answer for this direct criminal reporting fine.

#### Law After Decision

Punitive criminal forfeiture is subject to real but deferential gross-disproportionality review. A statutory connection to the offense is not enough, but currency-reporting forfeiture is not categorically forbidden. The full exaction fails on these findings; the judgment establishes neither a ratio nor a constitutional or statutory approval of the existing partial amount.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Justice Kennedy, dissenting in part, joined by Justices O’Connor and Scalia.** They join punitive coverage, the gross-disproportionality standard, independent legal review and rejection of the circuit’s instrumentality prerequisite. They disagree with the application and would reverse the refusal of full forfeiture.

The amount concealed measures the scale of a deliberate failure to report. Congress may punish that wrong seriously without establishing that the funds were criminal proceeds or proving a second offense. Reporting helps detect financial crime that otherwise may remain hidden; treating the absence of a proved additional crime as decisive undervalues that preventive function. The five-year and $250,000 statutory maxima and the expressly mandated forfeiture demonstrate substantial legislative seriousness. The $5,000 Guidelines fine operates alongside, rather than silently replacing, forfeiture.

In their view the full amount remains within the demanding constitutional boundary despite lawful source and intended use. Their ground adds no laundering or trafficking finding and does not treat dismissal of the false-statement count as a conviction. They reject neither fines coverage nor the need for an individualized constitutional judgment, and they do not reopen the conviction, probation or ordinary fine. The disagreement supplies no ruling on the unappealed $15,000 amount.

#### Source Notes

The reported findings and appellate grounds appear at [524 U.S. 321](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-524/pdf/USREPORTS-524-321.pdf); the [original record for No. 96-1487](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385014_0060) identifies the underlying filings. Sections 5316, 5322(a) and 982(a)(1) supply reporting, statutory punishment and forfeiture provisions. The $250,000 statutory and $5,000 Guidelines maxima are distinct; the false-statement count was dismissed rather than resolved by acquittal.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The judgment refusing the Government’s demand for full forfeiture remains effective. No new calculation or further forfeiture hearing is directed. The unappealed partial forfeiture and other sentence components remain undisturbed without affirmative adjudication of their independent validity.

**End of entry: United States v. Bajakajian, Decision, June 22, 1998.**

### Troxel v. Granville, No. 99-138

Merits decision, June 5, 2000

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 12, 2000. On writ of certiorari to the Washington Supreme Court in In re Custody of Smith, 137 Wash. 2d 1, 969 P.2d 21. Only the Troxel/Granville visitation dispute is before the Court. The question is whether former RCW 26.10.160(3), as applied over a fit parent's objection, violated parental liberty in directing the children's care and upbringing. The state court rejected a pending-custody-action limitation but sustained dismissal on broader federal constitutional grounds.

The former subsection permits any person to petition for visitation at any time, including but not limited to custody proceedings, and permits an order when visitation may serve the child's best interest whether or not circumstances have changed. After the children's father died, Granville continued contact with the paternal grandparents and proposed one daytime visit monthly and family holidays. The grandparents sought two overnight weekends monthly and two summer weeks; the trial order required one monthly weekend, one summer week and four hours on each grandparent's birthday. The dispute concerns the amount and conditions of continuing contact, not complete exclusion. The published account identifies no allegation or finding of parental unfitness and does not establish concrete harm from Granville's proposal.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Stevens, Scalia and Kennedy.

Stevens and Kennedy would vacate the constitutional dismissal and remand for further state consideration with proper parental protection. Scalia would reverse that dismissal without adopting the asserted new federal visitation rule. None of the three directs automatic reinstatement of the trial order.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion announcing the judgment; Opinion of the Court in Part I | Breyer | O'Connor and Ginsburg throughout; Stone-Zsela, Souter and Thomas in Part I only | Announces the six-Justice affirmance | I: protected parental-care liberty and prohibition of mere judicial-preference substitution; II: special weight and particular record application, three Justices only |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Affirms invalidation as applied; joins Part I | Serious-harm or comparable concrete legal-need trigger, strong presumption and narrowly fitted relief |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Souter | No other Justice | Affirms dismissal; joins Part I | Unrestricted statutory authorization as authoritatively construed |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Thomas | No other Justice | Affirms dismissal; joins Part I | Fundamental parental right and strict scrutiny |
| Dissent | Stevens | No other Justice | Would vacate and remand | Individualized assessment including the child's relational interests |
| Dissent | Scalia | No other Justice | Would reverse and remand | Refusal to extend established parental domains to this new substantive visitation rule |
| Dissent | Kennedy | No other Justice | Would vacate and remand | Contextual parental protection, actual relationships and extent of intrusion |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Parental liberty forbids displacement based only on a judge's preferred arrangement

**Holding and operative rule:** The protected liberty of a fit parent includes directing the child's care and upbringing. A court may not override that parent's visitation decision solely because the judge considers a different arrangement better for the child; ordinary judicial preference is not a sufficient constitutional justification.

**Authority:** Part I of Breyer's opinion has six direct joins: Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Their agreement is at precisely this level; their distinct additional scrutiny, trigger and statutory-scope rules are not combined.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the parent's constitutional interest cannot disappear into an unrestricted comparison of preferred arrangements. Meyer and Pierce establish protected parental direction, while Brown and Curtis preserve parental instruction and lawful educational choices even as they reject particular public-school vetoes. The State's ordinary family-law authority does not make the parent's decision equivalent to a stranger's proposed schedule.

The grandparents' continuing relationship and the benefits of contact are significant context, but a preference for more contact is not itself a sufficient basis for judicial displacement. Recognizing this limit does not establish an absolute parental veto or deny legitimate child protection. Baby Richard recognizes a bounded interest in avoiding serious harm from compulsory custody implementation while preserving the fit parent's entitlement against a preferred-home comparison. A. St. P. C. addresses a distinct complete-contact cutoff. Neither automatically supplies the threshold, proof quantum or remedy for this visitation dispute.

The six Justices sharing this proposition affirm dismissal, but differ about the further constitutional rule and how broadly the statutory authorization must be rejected. Their agreement establishes the prohibition of mere judicial-preference substitution, not a complete governing test for every nonparent-visitation order.

##### The judgment does not adopt a comprehensive visitation standard

**Authority and limits:** The judgment binds the parties and leaves this order unenforceable. Part II's full special-weight and record-application analysis has three votes, O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer. Stone-Zsela's serious-harm or comparable-need rule, Souter's statutory-breadth rationale and Thomas's strict-scrutiny ground remain separate. No common scrutiny level, universal harm requirement, parental-unfitness prerequisite, proof burden or general facial invalidation receives a controlling adoption. No Marks synthesis supplies one. Other statutes and materially different nonparent relationships remain open; the Smith and Wolcott disputes and collateral fees are not adjudicated here.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Meyer v. Nebraska** and **Pierce v. Society of Sisters:** Apply the established protection of parental care and direction; neither supplies the entire ordinary-visitation test.
- **Brown v. Hot, Sexy & Safer Productions, Inc.** and **Curtis v. School Committee of Falmouth:** Preserve protected family choice and instruction while distinguishing particular public-school veto claims from this intrusion.
- **Baby Richard v. Kirchner:** Preserves its bounded child-transition interest and protection of lawful parental custody against preferred-home substitution; it creates no permanent grandparent-contact entitlement.
- **A. St. P. C. v. B. C.:** Its distinct clear-and-convincing rule remains intact. That decision concerned a complete cutoff of contact and supplies no universal visitation burden.
- **Washington v. Glucksberg:** Remains a decision concerning the asserted physician-assistance entitlement, without an exclusive most-specific-history method for all liberties.
- **Parham v. J.R., Stanley v. Illinois, Prince v. Massachusetts** and **Santosky v. Kramer:** Retain their distinct parental, child-protection, fitness-process and termination principles; their use in the separate analyses creates no new common visitation threshold.

#### Law After Decision

Effective June 5, 2000, six Justices directly establish parental-care protection against displacement solely by judicial preference for another arrangement, and six affirm dismissal of this petition. No complete special-weight framework or general serious-harm, strict-scrutiny, facial-invalidation or proof-burden rule is adopted. Existing child-protection, educational-choice, custody-transition and severe-contact-restriction holdings retain their distinct force.

#### Separate Writings

**Part II of the lead opinion, Breyer with O'Connor and Ginsburg.** These three require special weight for a fit parent's judgment, drawing on Parham's presumption that parents ordinarily act in their children's interests. The trial court instead presumed grandparent contact beneficial unless contrary harm appeared, failed to credit Granville's continuing-contact offer, and relied in part on its own favorable grandparent experiences. The written findings establish benefits, not unfitness or concrete injury from her proposal. These circumstances make the order unconstitutional without deciding a universal harm or unfitness requirement. Limited visitation differs from custody termination, but that difference does not permit ordinary better-schedule substitution. Other statutes, relationships and justified protective interventions remain open.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and in the judgment.** Meyer, Pierce, Prince and Santosky support his proposed extension: a fit parent's reasonable visitation choice receives a strong presumption, and the nonparent must establish particularized serious harm to the child or a comparably concrete legally recognized need before the State may override it. Any order must be narrowly fitted to that need. The order fails because an unbounded best-interests preference supplies neither predicate nor fit. He treats that specification as an additional rule to be adopted, not as a threshold already established by the earlier cases. His rule preserves emergency protection, adjudication of unfitness and tailored relief on the required showing, and creates no absolute veto. No other Justice joins this proposed complete standard.

**Souter, concurring in part and in the judgment.** The authoritatively construed authorization permits any person to seek visitation at any time and leaves protected parental choice exposed to an unrestricted judicial preference for a better arrangement. That breadth supplies his sufficient ground without reaching Part II's particular record assessment. Possible justified applications cannot add restrictions the state court declined to find in the statute. Brown, Curtis and his Glucksberg approach support protection of the actual interest and assessment of the restraint's fit; he does not hold that harm is invariably the sole sufficient justification.

**Thomas, concurring in part and in the judgment.** Established parental care is a fundamental right, so its infringement requires strict scrutiny and compelling justification. Benefit-only second-guessing of this fit parent's decision lacks that justification. He joins Part I's exact liberty and anti-substitution rules, but not an unspecified special-weight standard that leaves the required scrutiny unstated. His ground does not transfer the termination proof burden to every visitation case or establish an absolute veto.

**Stevens, dissenting.** Parental protection must coexist with the child's supported interests in important relationships. His Parham position and the bounded interests recognized in Baby Richard and A. St. P. C. support individualized assessment, not a permanent grandparent right. Because a universal harm condition or broad facial invalidation exceeds the established rules, he would vacate and return the matter for state consideration with a presumptive parental role and attention to the child's actual interests. He neither finds the order valid nor commands new evidence or visitation.

**Scalia, dissenting.** He preserves the judicially protected parental domains recognized in Meyer, Pierce, Brown and Curtis but declines to infer this new substantive visitation boundary from those distinct decisions. His Baby Richard and A. St. P. C. positions require an established interest before transferring rules across materially different family-law settings. He would reverse the constitutional dismissal and allow ordinary state disposition without a new federal weighting or harm formula. That position does not deny every enforceable parental liberty, overrule existing family protections or command visitation.

**Kennedy, dissenting.** A categorical harm condition is too broad where an established caregiving relationship and limited intrusion may matter. Brown, Curtis and his Glucksberg opinion require attention to the actual liberty and restraint; the family-law cases do not erase those distinctions. He would vacate and remand for assessment of the order with meaningful parental protection and attention to the actual relationships. His proposed remand does not presume harm, validate the existing order, demand supplementation or reinstate visitation.

#### Source Notes

The [official report, 530 U.S. 57](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-530/pdf/USREPORTS-530-57.pdf), and the [Washington opinion, 969 P.2d 21](https://openjurist.org/969/p2d/21/in-re-custody-of-smith-1405279), supply the former subsection, schedules, trial findings and lower-court history. Published recitations establish continued offered contact and the trial court's benefit-based reasons; they do not establish unfitness or concrete harm from the offered schedule. Benefit findings are not findings of necessity, and the absence of a reported fitness allegation is not an adjudication of every conceivable parental concern.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Troxel petition remains dismissed and the visitation order unenforceable. The affirmance permits no renewed contest based merely on a preferred visitation schedule. No new best-interests proceeding, custody transfer, evidentiary hearing, fee award or disposition of the separate Smith and Wolcott controversies is ordered. Materially different visitation laws and relationships remain for cases presenting them.

**End of entry: Troxel v. Granville, merits decision, June 5, 2000.**

### Boy Scouts of America v. Dale, No. 99-699

Plenary merits decision, June 28, 2000

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 26, 2000; decided June 28, 2000. On writ of certiorari to the Supreme Court of New Jersey, reviewing 160 N.J. 562, 734 A.2d 1196 (1999), which affirmed and remanded.

The question is whether New Jersey's Law Against Discrimination may require retention of James Dale as an adult assistant scoutmaster consistently with the freedoms of speech and association. Dale was an Eagle Scout and became an assistant scoutmaster in March 1989. His adult membership was revoked after a July 1990 article identified his Rutgers Lesbian/Gay Alliance role and participation in a seminar about gay teenagers. The article did not identify him as a Scout leader or announce contrary instruction within Scouting.

New Jersey construed its statute to cover the organization and adult leadership as a privilege or advantage despite the absence of a single fixed location. It rejected the distinctly-private exemption on actual selectivity, the exemption for educational facilities operated or maintained by bona fide religious or sectarian institutions on the organization's nonsectarian character, and the reservation for a natural parent or person in loco parentis to direct a child's education and upbringing because temporary supervision was not maintaining, rearing and educating the child. Those state-law constructions do not decide the federal constitutional defense. The separate duplicative common-law claim remains dismissed.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 5-4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

The Law Against Discrimination may operate in the adult-leadership application presented. The Court awards no damages amount, prescribes no new trial and substitutes no membership-only compromise for the actual dispute. The rejection of the intimate-association defense remains undisturbed, but only four Justices adopt the separate reasoning in Part IV; Stone-Zsela joins the disposition on that defense without adding a ground. The four reversing Justices do not reach that defense.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Parts I-III | Souter | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer | Affirms | Actual message, adult role, material attributed impairment, direct-speech application and bounded remedy; Holdings 1-3 |
| Same lead opinion, Part IV | Souter | Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer | Additional grounds supporting affirmance | Stronger actual-advocacy requirement and intimate-association analysis; neither is a controlling rule |
| Dissent | O'Connor | Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas | Would reverse and remand | Protected moral education through the chosen adult example, material impairment and insufficient justification |

Stone-Zsela joins the judgment on intimate association without joining Part IV. The reversing Justices leave that question undecided. Parts I-III have five direct joins; no votes are combined under a narrowest-grounds rule.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. An access-law defense requires an actual message, a relevant role and material expressive impairment

**Holding and operative rule:** A generally applicable public-accommodations law may govern an open civic organization's admission or retention of an otherwise qualified participant unless the organization establishes an actual message, shows that the particular role communicates it, and demonstrates that inclusion materially alters expression attributable to the group. An exclusion policy alone does not prove that connection; establishing a material burden would present further questions the Court need not decide here.

**Authority:** Parts I-III of Souter's opinion control all three holdings through the five-Justice coalition shown above. Part IV supplies no controlling rule.

**Controlling explanation:** Roberts, Rotary and New York State Club Association require examination of real expressive impairment, rather than exemption for every activity of an association that engages in some expression. Moral instruction, including quiet persuasion and teaching by adult example, is protected. The inquiry therefore respects the organization's chosen values while independently examining whether this role and inclusion affect its actual expression. Hurley protects control of a particular private expressive presentation without demanding a single theme or unanimous beliefs. It does not make every asserted exclusion an expressive presentation. The Court requires neither a narrow founding purpose nor uniformity on all issues. Genuine instruction can establish a message; silence itself can be protected, and a bona fide direct-speech claim remains distinct. What is insufficient is treating a status-based exclusion as self-proving impairment of a message the organization has not actually shown to be its own expressive endeavor. Constitutional protection does not depend on whether the Court or society approves that message.

##### 2. This record does not establish the necessary message-role-impairment connection

**Holding and operative rule:** On the record presented, retaining Dale as an assistant scoutmaster has not been shown materially to alter a defined message attributable to the Boy Scouts or to compel a contrary expressive presentation. The organization's moral instruction and authority over genuine role-related conduct remain protected, but neither Dale's status nor his external advocacy alone establishes the missing connection here.

**Controlling explanation:** The oath, law, adult declaration and teaching by example establish an expressive program. They do not by themselves establish the claimed position about homosexuality or how this adult's inclusion contradicts instruction actually conveyed. Ordinary youth materials do not express that position, and sexual instruction is referred to parents and spiritual advisers. The 1978 exclusion statement and later 1991-1993 statements and testimony are relevant but must be considered in their sequence and institutional use; they are not all litigation inventions, and the 1991 statement predates suit. Neither those later materials nor the other evidence establishes the requisite connection for the completed expulsion or prospective application here. The Court independently reviews the intertwined expression and attribution questions while respecting credibility findings. It accepts that a chosen adult teacher can communicate by example; it rejects automatic attribution from this person's identity. Dale proposed no contrary Scouting lesson and gained no right to disregard lawful program instructions. Hurley's identified bannered contingent altered the organizer's particular presentation; the same predicate is not established here. Jones concerned an established transfer of official-nominee selection to outsiders, not an unproved inference about this leader's message. A leader who publicly epitomizes a position demonstrably incompatible with the group's actual advocacy could present a different case.

##### 3. Neutral access enforcement may proceed without balancing away an established expressive burden

**Holding and operative rule:** Because the necessary material expressive impairment is not established, the State may enforce its ordinary nondiscrimination requirement in this adult-leadership application without a further balance against a proved serious expressive burden. The judgment leaves genuine spokespeople, materially expressive leadership roles, direct expressive presentations, intimate relationships and neutral discipline for role-related conduct subject to their own constitutional protections.

**Controlling explanation:** Roberts and Rotary recognize the State's concrete access interest without making equality an automatic answer to every serious expressive injury. The Court does not reach a compelling-interest override of such an injury here, because the necessary premise is absent. Nor does it impose a duty to host contrary teaching or a debate the organization has not chosen. The state court's statutory coverage is accepted as state law; its public-accommodation label does not alone defeat a federal defense. Public assistance and a federal charter likewise do not establish state action by themselves. The judgment concerns the actual adult appointment, not all youth members, customers, employees or future applicants. A membership-only remedy would require resolution of the organization's contention that adult membership is leadership service; the Court does not invent that compromise. The state judgment and existing remand stand, with no new damages award, automatic trial or revival of the dismissed common-law claim.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Roberts v. United States Jaycees supplies the actual expressive-burden inquiry and preserves both protected association and ordinary access enforcement; the Court does not decide that an antidiscrimination interest overrides a proved severe burden here.
- Board of Directors of Rotary International v. Rotary Club of Duarte supplies attention to the association's actual activities, relationships and claimed impairment; a statutory label alone does not resolve the federal issue.
- New York State Club Association v. City of New York supplies the distinction between real expressive injury and a general exemption from access regulation; its application-specific limits remain.
- Hurley v. Irish-American Gay, Lesbian and Bisexual Group of Boston remains controlling for private expressive composition, independent review and credibility deference. Its rule does not demand a single parade theme, and its direct expressive predicate is not established by mere status inclusion here.
- California Democratic Party v. Jones retains its severe-burden holding for compelled outsider selection of official party nominees. The present record has not established the analogous material control of expression; Jones does not automatically decide every leadership or admission dispute.

#### Law After Decision

The presented adult-leadership application of New Jersey's law stands. An expressive-association defense requires the actual message, role and material attributable impairment identified in the Court's rule. General moral education is protected but does not make an exclusion policy self-validating. The decision preserves direct-speech protection, genuine message-bearing roles and role-related discipline. It creates no new controlling intimate-association test and no rule that equality defeats an established serious expressive burden.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Souter's Part IV, joined by Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer.** These four additionally require the particular claimed position to have been unequivocally adopted and actually advocated over the relevant period through customary organizational channels. That is their stronger proof requirement for this expressive-association defense, not a rule joined by Stone-Zsela or a demand for a single founding theme, unanimity or constant public campaigning. They distinguish Hurley's direct expressive presentation and preserve a genuine claim to remain silent. The common message-role-impairment requirement and this record's failure to establish it are sufficient for the Court's decision; their additional requirement is noncontrolling. These four also reject the intimate-association defense based on the organization's actual size, broad recruitment, openness, selectivity and relationships. They distinguish genuine personal intimacy from the mere existence of adult supervision or smaller constituent groups and do not rest on the state's public-accommodation label alone. This is a four-Justice rationale, not a new rule of the Court. Stone-Zsela supplies no additional reason on this defense.

**O'Connor, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, dissenting.** They would reverse the compelled adult-leadership application. The organization teaches chosen moral values through the example of adults entrusted to instruct children, a protected expressive activity under Roberts and Hurley. Its official position, adult commitments and Dale's publicly identified contrary position establish material impairment for them. Courts should respect the association's account of its values and the consequences of forced leadership while independently ensuring that more than a bare exclusion assertion is present. They would require neither an explicit lesson in every handbook, unanimity among members nor empirical proof that particular spoken words changed. Referring sexual education to parents does not eliminate an adult example's communicative role.

They distinguish ordinary nondiscriminatory access from direct interference with that choice. O'Brien's incidental-regulation approach cannot dispose of the direct burden they find, and the asserted access interests do not justify it under the compelling-interest and less-restrictive-means framework. Kennedy's concern for voluntary institutional development is reflected in the distinction between a group's own change and state-compelled alteration; the dissent endorses no particular moral belief and grants no universal exemption. Dale remains free to advocate his own views, but those freedoms do not establish a right to this expressive role. They would reverse and remand within this adult-leadership dispute, without deciding intimate association, other classes of participants or a final damages amount.

#### Source Notes

The [New Jersey Supreme Court opinion, 160 N.J. 562](https://static.case.law/nj/160/html/0562-01.html), reports the record, state-law coverage and exceptions, procedural judgment and competing constitutional characterizations. The [record and briefs for No. 99-699](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385014_0345) preserve the petition, merits submissions and joint appendix. The cited statements have different dates and uses; later statements are not collectively treated as contemporaneous explanations or as litigation inventions. The sources establish no deposition concession, exact damages amount or feasible membership-only settlement on which this decision rests.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The New Jersey judgment is affirmed, and its existing remand proceeds within the adjudicated adult-leadership application. The Court fixes no final damages amount and orders no new trial or membership-only relief. The duplicative common-law claim remains dismissed. No other membership, employment or service application is decided.

**End of entry: Boy Scouts of America v. Dale, Plenary merits decision, June 28, 2000.**
