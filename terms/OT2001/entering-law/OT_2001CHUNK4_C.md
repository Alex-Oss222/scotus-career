## Standards and Tests selection: Commercial-speech advancement and fit in selective restrictions

### Commercial-speech advancement and fit in selective restrictions

**Governing question:** What instructions govern commercial-speech advancement and fit in selective restrictions?

**Current rule:** For truthful, nonmisleading commercial speech about lawful activity, the government must establish real harms, material advancement and a reasonable fit between its chosen restriction and its interests. Where commercial and newspaper racks cause the same physical harms, the commercial category requires justification tied to those harms; benign objectives alone do not make content-selected regulation content neutral. Incremental regulation is not invalid merely because its reduction is small, but alternatives bear on fit without a least-restrictive-means or all-sources-at-once requirement. In professional solicitation the government must demonstrate advancement in the relevant professional setting, without an invariable empirical-study requirement. Deceptive or coercive solicitation remains regulable. Central Hudson and Fox remain the framework; no general equality of commercial and noncommercial speech or categorical vice exception follows. When the State otherwise makes records available, a condition denying access and prohibiting use because the requester intends truthful, lawful commercial solicitation receives this same review. Absence of a general right to every government record does not remove a speech-selected condition from First Amendment scrutiny; neutral access, retrieval-fee and privacy-redaction rules retain their lawful scope.

Stevens's Court opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter: seven. This rejects the City's independent time, place and manner defense.
- Real advancement must be established in the relevant professional setting: Edenfield v. Fane — Real advancement must be established in the relevant professional setting, 1993-04-26. ## Standards and Tests selection: Alcohol regulation and freedom of speech

### Alcohol regulation and freedom of speech

**Governing question:** Whether the Twenty-first Amendment reduces the First Amendment protection owed to speech concerning alcohol.

**Current rule:** The Twenty-first Amendment does not qualify the First Amendment's protection of speech or supply an additional presumption of validity for liquor-related speech restraints. LaRue's reliance on that Amendment to enhance deference no longer governs; its licensed-premises conduct holding remains supported by ordinary police powers, and Bellanca's conduct result remains intact. The rule neither invalidates direct alcohol regulation nor changes the ordinary Central Hudson and Fox standard for protected commercial speech.

**Authority by component:**

- Amendment interaction and the displaced rationale: III-B. The Twenty-first Amendment does not qualify freedom of speech; LaRue's contrary rationale is disavowed, 1996-05-13; Kennedy Part III-B, joined by Stevens, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg (six). Part III-A separately has all nine votes for rejecting an added presumption for the truthful-price restraint; no broader commercial-speech tier commands a majority.

**Present operation effective:** 44 Liquormart, 1996-05-13.

**Related current rules:** “Commercial-speech advancement and fit in selective restrictions” supplies the unchanged review of truthful, nonmisleading promotion of lawful transactions.

**Holdings navigation:** 44 Liquormart, Inc. v. Rhode Island, 517 U.S. 484.

## Standards and Tests selection: Cable-carriage rules and review of legislative predictions

### Cable-carriage rules and review of legislative predictions

**Governing question:** How the First Amendment reviews content-neutral cable-carriage requirements.

**Current rule:** Cable operators’ editorial choices receive ordinary First Amendment protection, without the broadcast-scarcity rationale. Content classification examines what the law selects and why. The general full-power must-carry provisions at issue are content neutral and receive intermediate scrutiny: they must advance substantial interests unrelated to suppressing expression and burden no substantially greater amount of speech than necessary. The least restrictive alternative is not required. Preserving broadcast availability, diverse information sources and fair competition are substantial interests, but identifying them does not prove the law satisfies the test. Legislative predictive judgments receive substantial deference while courts independently determine whether reasonable inferences rest on substantial evidence and support the claimed harms and response.

## Standards and Tests selection: Communicative targeting in public-nudity regulation

### Communicative targeting in public-nudity regulation

**Governing question:** When an asserted public-nudity regulation qualifies for incidental-burden analysis.

**Current rule:** An ordinance’s general public-nudity label does not permit incidental-burden treatment when its actual justification and operation principally target the communicative adult-entertainment setting. The government must satisfy the scrutiny applicable to that expressive targeting; a modest clothing requirement alone does not cure the defect. Genuinely general nudity laws and supported regulation of nonexpressive harms retain their own operation.

**Authority by component:**

- Nonsuppression boundary and the precise shared merits rule: City of Erie v. Pap’s A. M. — 2. Communicative targeting cannot receive incidental-burden treatment, March 29, 2000; direct agreement of Stevens, Scalia, Thomas and Ginsburg in Part II and Stone-Zsela in his concurrence.

**Present operation effective:** City of Erie, March 29, 2000.

**Exceptions and limits:** The controlling proposition does not adopt every step of the four-Justice strict-scrutiny account or the three-Justice independent intermediate-scrutiny alternative. It creates no blanket ban on nudity laws, universal theater exception or new evidentiary-hearing duty.

## Standards and Tests selection: Content-selected restrictions on protected adult cable programming

### Content-selected restrictions on protected adult cable programming

**Governing question:** The justification required for government restrictions directed at the sexual content of protected adult cable speech.

**Current rule:** Strict scrutiny requires a narrowly tailored restriction serving a compelling interest; the Government bears the ultimate justification burden. Once a concrete, plausibly effective arrangement imposing less restraint on speech is presented, the Government must show why its comparative inadequacy warrants the broader adult burden. Child protection and parental assistance do not by themselves establish necessity. Assessment concerns the actual medium, speech burden and supported implementation problems, with serious consideration but not conclusive deference to legislative predictions.

**Authority by component:**

- United States v. Playboy Entertainment Group, Inc.; No. 98-1682. — Canonical Decision Record, Holdings, I. Congress's content-selected cable restriction requires strict scrutiny, 2000-05-22; Ginsburg with all eight other Justices.

**Present operation effective:** 2000-05-22; the component authority identified above.

**Exceptions and limits:** Neither scientific certainty nor a perfect alternative is required. A paper alternative alone does not establish practical effectiveness. The rule creates no statutory publicity duty, automatic restoration of an invalid restriction, universal blocking-device rule or prohibition of supported narrower regulation; obscenity remains distinct.

## Standards and Tests selection: Temporal reach of ordinary AEDPA habeas amendments

### Temporal reach of ordinary AEDPA habeas amendments

**Governing question:** Which merits and factual-finding law governs a federal habeas application pending when AEDPA took effect.

**Current rule:** AEDPA's chapter 153 amendments at issue, including amended §2254(d), do not govern a noncapital federal habeas application filed before April 24, 1996. The governing pre-AEDPA law remains applicable to that application. This construction preserves separately directed provisions, chapter 154's express pending-case reach and Felker's treatment of the successive-petition provisions; it is not a blanket exemption of all pending proceedings from every AEDPA amendment.

The same application-date construction excludes amended §2254(d) and §2254(e)(1)’s replacement factual presumption from a capital application already pending at enactment when chapter 154 qualification has not been established. Former §2254(d), including its paragraph (8) whole-state-record qualification, governs that factual inquiry. Capital status alone establishes no chapter 154 qualification.

**Authority by component:**
- Application-date construction and preserved exceptions: Lindh, Lindh v. Murphy — The chapter 153 amendments at issue do not govern this pre-enactment noncapital application, Ginsburg with Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter and Breyer (six), 1997-06-23.

- Pending-capital extension for the identified merits and factual-presumption provisions: Carriger v. Stewart — Holding 1. Former-law treatment of the pending capital application, 1998-04-16.

**Present operation effective:** Carriger v. Stewart, 1998-04-16, for the specified capital-application extension; Lindh’s noncapital rule remains effective from 1997-06-23.

**Exceptions and limits:** Carriger does not decide §2254(e)(2)’s temporal application or require an evidentiary hearing.  The rule creates no constitutional entitlement to preserve every filing-date judicial doctrine and decides no capital State qualification, exhaustion, merits or ultimate entitlement to relief.

**Holdings navigation:** Lindh v. Murphy (1997-06-23) — The chapter 153 amendments at issue do not govern this pre-enactment noncapital application. Carriger v. ## Standards and Tests selection: Sequencing nonretroactivity and procedural default on habeas review

### Sequencing nonretroactivity and procedural default on habeas review

**Governing question:** The order of two nonjurisdictional habeas defenses.

**Current rule:** Teague nonretroactivity and state procedural default are distinct nonjurisdictional limits. No inflexible priority requires either always to precede the other; an accepted Teague question may be resolved while disputed default predicates remain reserved. This does not excuse default, permit bypass of an established jurisdictional defect or decide constitutional merits. An appellate court has no mandatory duty to raise an omitted state procedural-default defense; the defense is not a subject-matter-jurisdiction defect.

**Authority by component:**

- Lambrix v. Singletary — I. The Court may decide the accepted nonretroactivity issue before the unresolved default defense, 1997-05-12; Stevens with Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; seven

- An appellate court has no mandatory duty to raise an omitted state procedural-default defense: Trest v. Cain, 1997-12-09; Stevens for all nine Justices.

**Present operation effective:** Trest, 1997-12-09, for the no-duty rule; Lambrix, 1997-05-12, retains the sequencing rule.

**Exceptions and limits:** The specific old-rule conclusion for the influential capital recommendation at November 24, 1986 finality is an application, not a universal exception to Teague. Preserved adequate, independent and unexcused state grounds remain enforceable.

**Holdings navigation:** Lambrix v. Singletary (1997-05-12) — The Court may decide the accepted nonretroactivity issue before the unresolved default defense. Trest v. Cain — Habeas procedural default and appellate initiative.

**Open question:** Trest leaves judicial authority to raise an omitted default, the proper exercise of any discretion, intentional waiver or forfeiture, actual default and applicable exceptions unresolved. Any lawful later consideration must address the actual ground with an appropriate opportunity to respond; no universal extra-briefing requirement is imposed.

## Standards and Tests selection: Cross-appeals and enlargement of the decree

### Cross-appeals and enlargement of the decree

**Governing question:** When an appellee needs a cross-appeal rather than an alternative ground for affirmance.

**Current rule:** An appellee may defend the existing decree on an alternative preserved ground but may not, without a cross-appeal, enlarge its own rights or reduce the appellant’s rights under that decree. Invoking comity does not turn a defense of the judgment into an appeal for a more favorable one.

**Authority by component:**

- El Paso Natural Gas Co. v. Neztsosie — Canonical Decision Record, Holdings, “Nonappealing appellees may defend a decree but may not enlarge it”, 1999-05-03; Thomas, all nine Justices.

**Present operation effective:** El Paso Natural Gas, May 3, 1999.

**Open question:** The Court does not classify every omission as jurisdictional or decide every possible exception, including an independent jurisdictional defect requiring a different disposition.

**Holdings navigation:** [El Paso Natural Gas Co. v. Neztsosie — Nonappealing appellees may defend a decree but may not enlarge it](HOLDINGS.md#nonappealing-appellees-may-defend-a-decree-but-may-not-enlarge-it-1).

## Standards and Tests selection: Mitigating measures in actual ADA disability

### Mitigating measures in actual ADA disability

**Governing question:** Whether actual-disability coverage under original §12102(2)(A) is assessed with measures the individual uses.

**Current rule:** Successful mitigating measures do not erase the underlying impairment. Whether it substantially limits a major life activity is assessed without treating the beneficial effects of correction or medication as eliminating that limitation. The individual still must prove a substantial limitation of a major life activity; a diagnosis or the use of lenses or a prescription alone does not establish disability, and no corrected or treated condition is automatically covered. Actual functioning with treatment remains relevant to qualification, reasonable accommodation and legitimate safety standards, which are resolved on their own elements. No duty to stop treatment follows.

**Authority by component:**

- Murphy v. United Parcel Service, Inc. — Canonical Decision Record, Holdings, “Actual disability is assessed without treating successful mitigation as eliminating the underlying limitation”, 1999-06-22; Ginsburg Part I with Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Breyer.

United Air Lines, Inc. — Canonical Decision Record, Holdings, “Holding 1— Beneficial mitigation does not eliminate underlying-impairment coverage”, 1999-06-22; Stone-Zsela Part I with Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. The same-day decisions independently resolve Bragdon’s general mitigation reservation.

**Present operation effective:** Murphy and Sutton, June 22, 1999.

**Exceptions and limits:** Record-of and regarded-as branches remain separate. No general invalidation of EEOC authority or every regulation, new episodic-impairment threshold, qualification ruling or safety defense follows.

**Related current rules:** Actual disability and substantial limitation of reproduction retains Bragdon’s individual reproductive-risk application; Objective assessment of an ADA direct threat remains distinct from coverage.

## Standards and Tests selection: Religious accommodation and demonstrated operational hardship

### Religious accommodation and demonstrated operational hardship

**Governing question:** An unresolved work conflict with religious observance under Title VII.

**Current rule:** Under §2000e(j), the employer must reasonably accommodate religious practice unless it demonstrates inability without undue hardship. The governing Hardison threshold permits refusal of more than de minimis cost, including concrete lost efficiency, impaired training, safety burdens and protected seniority interests. Neutral scheduling and absence of hostility do not discharge this separate duty. If an effective reasonable accommodation resolves the conflict, the employer need not supply the employee’s preferred alternative or disprove every other possibility. If the conflict remains, hardship must address supported, materially distinct proposals and actual operations; a concession about one proposal does not dispose of materially different ones. Applicants, including applicants for public employment, receive the same accommodation protection as employees for a known sincere religious practice. The claimant must establish the sincere practice, employment conflict, employer knowledge and adverse treatment implicated by the claim. Good-faith discussion alone does not provide an effective accommodation; absent such accommodation, supported combinations of materially distinct proposals must be examined in actual operations. Public employment neither exempts the employer nor imposes an enhanced duty, and prior temporary service is unnecessary. Notice concerns the actual practice and conflict: direct or indirect knowledge can suffice without formal words, but general familiarity with faith does not identify every manifestation. Lack of a precise request before the first manifestation is not conclusive when the employer understands the practice before discharge and an effective prospective response remains materially possible; timing matters where harm is irreversible or no prospective response addresses it. Concrete noncash costs, supported predictions, supervisory pressure and third-party burdens may prove hardship, but offense, supervisory status or a possible lawsuit alone does not. No independent least-severe-sanction, compulsory warning, every-alternative or negotiation requirement follows.

**Authority by component:**

- Beadle — both accommodation holdings, 1996-02-05; Stevens and all seven other Associates (eight); Stone-Zsela dissents.

- Brown v. Polk County — Part II — Accommodation turns on the actual practice and supported burden, 1997-03-17; the Court's Part II, all nine, supplies the actual-practice predicates while preserving Beadle's predictive hardship rule
- Opuku-Boateng v. California — Applicants receive the same statutory protection, 1997-04-07; unanimous Opinion of the Court, holding 1, extends the rule to applicants
- Opuku-Boateng v. California — Supported predictions may establish hardship, 1997-04-07; the same all-nine coalition, holding 2, preserves supported predictive burdens and distinguishes discussions from effective accommodation
- Chalmers v. Tulon Co. of Richmond — Holdings, 1997-06-12; Stevens with Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; five direct joins to holding 1, Notice must be assessed in relation to the religious conflict and the available response, and holding 2, Concrete burdens may establish hardship, but this judgment cannot rest on a categorical inability to accommodate

**Present operation effective:** Beadle, February 5, 1996, supplies the carried accommodation/hardship mechanics; Brown v. Polk County, March 17, 1997, retains claim-specific notice and accommodation; Opuku-Boateng, April 7, 1997, addresses applicants; Chalmers, June 12, 1997, refines practice-specific notice and concrete hardship.

**Exceptions and limits:** Professional evidence and reasonable operational inferences may establish hardship without waiting for an accident or monetizing every burden. No independent negotiation duty arises when effective accommodation or adequate hardship already exists. General safety concerns create no occupational exemption, and this rule establishes no particular proposal’s feasibility or entitlement.

**Holdings navigation:** Beadle v. City of Tampa, judgment below 42 F.3d 633. Brown v. Polk County (1997-03-17) — Labor and Employment / Religious accommodation and completed mixed-motive proof; Opuku-Boateng v. California (1997-04-07) — Labor and Employment / Religious accommodation; Chalmers v. Tulon Co. of Richmond (1997-06-12) — Labor and Employment / Religious accommodation and notice before discipline.



**Remedial limit:** Liability does not itself guarantee appointment, a vacancy, a preferred shift or a continuing injunction. The employer may choose an effective accommodation; a permanent court-prescribed schedule requires lawful necessity after that opportunity.

#### Edenfield v. Fane, 507 U.S. 761 (1993)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 91-1594.
**Decided:** April 26, 1993.

##### Real advancement must be established in the relevant professional setting

**Holding:** When truthful, nonmisleading solicitation concerns lawful CPA services, the State must demonstrate that the harms supporting its restriction are real and that the restriction alleviates them to a material degree. Florida’s unsupported predictions about direct solicitation of business clients do not satisfy that burden; the ruling neither requires empirical studies in every case nor immunizes deceptive or coercive solicitation.

**Proposition-level authority:** Kennedy’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (8 Justices). Direct majority agreement at this level of generality; no Marks inference.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No strict scrutiny, universal empirical-studies requirement, or least-restrictive-means rule is adopted. Fraud, coercion and materially different professional settings remain distinct.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm the judgment and injunction against the challenged CPA business-solicitation prohibition; no damages or new licensing rule.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Central Hudson Gas & Electric Corp. v. Public Service Commission, 447 U.S. 557: applies the real-advancement burden.
- Board of Trustees v. Fox, 492 U.S. 469: retains reasonable fit without least restrictive means.
- Ohralik v. Ohio State Bar Assn., 436 U.S. 447: distinguished by professional setting, vulnerability and pressure.
- Ward v. Rock Against Racism, 491 U.S. 781: alternative time/place/manner characterization cannot cure deficient advancement.
- Cincinnati v. Discovery Network, March 24, 1993: consistent application; no expansion to strict scrutiny.

## Focused Holdings selection: Rubin v. Coors Brewing Co.

#### Rubin v. Coors Brewing Co.

**Docket or dockets:** No. 93-1631.
**Decided:** April 19, 1995.

##### The challenged scheme does not materially advance its asserted health interest

**Holding:** The Government may not suppress truthful, nonmisleading numerical alcohol-content statements on lawful malt-beverage labels unless the restriction directly and materially advances a substantial interest. Preventing strength competition and its health harms is substantial, but the challenged scheme fails that requirement on this record; predictive judgment is permissible, while abstract concern and unexplained inconsistencies do not establish the necessary connection.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Kennedy), eight Justices; Stevens concurs in the judgment only.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Central Hudson Gas & Electric Corp. v. Public Service Commission, 447 U.S. 557: applies its substantial-interest and direct-advancement requirements.
- Edenfield v. Fane, April 26, 1993: applies the requirement of responsive justification for the actual commercial-speech restriction.
- Cincinnati v. Discovery Network, March 24, 1993, and Lanphere & Urbaniak v. Colorado, April 18, 1995: apply the requirement that the selected speech restriction address the demonstrated harm, without an all-sources-at-once rule.
- United States v. Edge Broadcasting Co., June 25, 1993: distinguishes its supported state-based scheme; its holding creates no categorical vice-speech exemption.

**Independent alternative holding:** Independently of the failure of material advancement, the categorical suppression of accurate numerical beer-label information lacks a reasonable fit to the asserted strength-competition harm in light of its unexplained breadth and workable measures directed more closely at that harm. Reasonable fit requires proportionality, not the least restrictive imaginable means, and the Court prescribes no particular substitute regulation.

**Proposition-level authority for alternative holding:** Opinion of the Court (Kennedy), eight Justices; Stevens concurs in the judgment only.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Board of Trustees v. Fox, 492 U.S. 469: applies reasonable proportional fit and preserves rejection of a least-restrictive-means requirement.
- Discovery Network and Lanphere: apply their distinct fit inquiry to the burden and supported alternatives here.
- Ibanez v. Florida Department of Business and Professional Regulation, June 13, 1994: applies the need for justification responsive to the actual truthful-information burden.

##### The asserted need to assist state policy supplies no alternative justification on this record

**Holding:** Assuming the Government’s state-assistance rationale is properly considered, its unsubstantiated assertion that federal suppression of accurate label information is needed to facilitate state alcohol policy does not justify this restriction. Legitimate accommodation of differing state policies remains possible when its need and operation are supported; federalism and the Twenty-first Amendment do not alone replace First Amendment scrutiny.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Kennedy), eight Justices; Stevens concurs in the judgment only.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Edge Broadcasting: preserves the legitimacy of supported state-policy accommodation while distinguishing the missing demonstrated mechanism here.
- Central Hudson: requires the asserted justification to support the actual speech restriction rather than stand as an unexamined label.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The judgment sustaining the injunction against the challenged numerical label ban is affirmed, 9–0. The unappealed advertising judgment and separate state, descriptive-strength and design provisions remain undisturbed. No national format, alcohol ceiling, tax or replacement regulation is prescribed.

## Focused Holdings selection: 44 Liquormart, Inc. v. Rhode Island, 517 U.S. 484

Source: state/holdings/first-amendment.md

#### 44 Liquormart, Inc. v. Rhode Island, 517 U.S. 484

**Docket or dockets:** No. 94-1140.
**Decided:** May 13, 1996.

##### Material advancement and reasonable fit of truthful-price restrictions

**Holding:** Even under ordinary *Central Hudson* review and treating temperance as substantial, Rhode Island has not demonstrated that these truthful-price restrictions directly and materially reduce consumption. The holding concerns this record and restriction, not every possible effect of advertising, prices, or demand; supported prediction remains permissible without a mandatory state-specific numerical study or experimental certainty.

**Proposition-level authority:** Kennedy's Part I, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg; five. Stevens affirmatively adopts this independently sufficient conditional application without accepting consumer ignorance as legitimate or ordinary scrutiny as the complete constitutional account.

**Material application:** The State's search-cost/price-dispersion mechanism and opposing experts' concessions of price responsiveness and theoretical plausibility are material but do not overcome the trial finding of no significant consumption effect. General-advertising studies with control problems, missing estimates connecting this restriction's projected price change to consumption, and possible market-share gains without aggregate consumption gains fail to establish material advancement. Constitutional sufficiency is independently assessed without turning appellate economic inference into a new trial finding.

**Independent alternative holding:** Even assuming material advancement, continuously suppressing this category of truthful retail-price information is disproportionate to the temperance objective on this record. Concrete direct price and tax alternatives expose inadequately justified fit; they are neither mandatory replacements nor a least-restrictive-means rule.

**Proposition-level authority for alternative holding:** Kennedy's Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; seven. O'Connor and Breyer assume advancement; Souter adopts both failures; Stevens's application remains conditional. Scalia's judgment vote and Thomas's categorical theory supply no additional balancing votes.

**Material application:** Interstate-shopping concerns do not explain why the State's indirect price mechanism avoids the same leakage or justify the informational burden. Section 3-8-7 permits merchandise-attached or merchandise-placed price signs or tags inside licensed premises under departmental rules; Regulation 32 forbids references visible from outside Class A premises. Section 3-8-8.1 separately excepts recognized, authorized trade journals. These exceptions and lawful nonprice promotion neither automatically defeat nor establish fit; the challenged category is complete subject to those exact exceptions, rather than a ban on all alcohol speech.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Ordinary *Central Hudson/Fox* review remains operative. No majority adopts a special-care or heightened-review tier, categorical anti-ignorance rule, universal alternatives checklist, all-sources-at-once requirement, or independent interstate-information immunity. False or misleading speech, unlawful promotions, and direct alcohol controls retain their own governing law; the Court selects no tax or minimum-price program and decides no antitrust dispute.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Edenfield*, *Ibanez*, *Lanphere*, and *Rubin* require proof responsive to the actual restriction. *Fox* supplies reasonable proportional fit; *Discovery Network* and *Rubin* support concrete comparisons without least-restrictive-means review. *Edge* retains its record-specific meaningful-reduction holding; *Florida Bar*'s invalidation does not prohibit supported prediction. *Midcal* and *324 Liquor* invalidate particular price arrangements, not every state-imposed price control. Subsequent searching scrutiny denies automatic *Posadas* authorization here, but broader repudiation of its paternalism and greater-power reasoning has only four votes. *Queensgate* remains a summary merits disposition confined by *Mandel* to what it necessarily decided, rather than a rule that comparable restrictions invariably survive later developed advancement-and-fit doctrine. *Bigelow* supplies no separate adopted interstate holding.

##### Effect of the Twenty-first Amendment on speech protection

**Holding:** Alcohol-control authority supplies no additional presumption relaxing First Amendment protection for these off-premises truthful-price restrictions. Authority over lawful sales does not itself validate suppression of accurate information, and accepting a license does not surrender speech rights.

**Proposition-level authority:** Kennedy's Part III-A, unanimous; this agreement does not attribute balancing to Scalia or Thomas.

##### The broader speech rule and LaRue's enhanced-deference rationale

**Holding:** The Twenty-first Amendment does not qualify the First Amendment's protection of speech. *LaRue*'s reliance on the Twenty-first Amendment to enhance deference is disavowed; its holding remains supported by ordinary police powers over the regulated conduct.

##### I. Covered cigarette advertising is expressly preempted

**Holding and operative rule.** Under 15 U.S.C. §1334(b), a State may not impose a smoking-and-health requirement or prohibition with respect to the advertising or promotion of cigarettes whose packages satisfy the federal labeling requirements. Massachusetts's cigarette applications of §21.04(5)(a)–(b) impose such prohibitions even though they regulate advertising location; general zoning unconnected to smoking and health and independent restrictions on sales to minors are not displaced by this holding.

**Authority.** O'Connor's Part I, joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, supplies five direct votes.

**Controlling explanation.** Cipollone requires examination of the actual duty and its basis, rather than the name given a claim or rule. The amended federal clause includes both requirements and prohibitions and expressly identifies advertising and promotion as the regulated activity. These rules select cigarette advertising to reduce tobacco use and its health consequences. A location prohibition remains a prohibition with respect to that advertising; it does not become unrelated to smoking and health because children are its intended beneficiaries.

The contrary reading properly considers the federal warning scheme but gives inadequate effect to the broader enacted words. Congress did not confine this clause to additional warning language. Treating every placement ban as outside the clause would exclude a direct advertising prohibition while reaching a lesser demand to alter its message. Medtronic and Geier require attention to the particular statutory scheme; they establish neither general tobacco immunity nor an impossibility-only preemption rule. That independent deficiency requires rejection of the challenged smokeless and cigar applications; it does not establish zero conceivable effect or demand a height-specific experiment in every case.

**Authority.** O'Connor's Part III-A is joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Souter; Thomas conditionally adopts this lesser-test failure. These five expressly support the operative ground. Stone-Zsela does not join this independent explanation.

**Controlling explanation.** Central Hudson and Edenfield require a demonstrated relationship between the actual restraint and the asserted benefit. Evidence that minors encounter low signs does not establish the necessary material alleviation from a rule barring any portion of an advertisement below five feet. Older minors are not confined below that line, and younger children can look upward. Advertising above the line remains possible, while the outside-visibility prohibition separately restricts that channel's practical availability.

The geographical limitation and the exception for premises admitting no person under eighteen at any time, even with an adult, are real limits; they do not supply the missing relationship. The State's general evidence about tobacco advertising does not automatically validate this particular device. Unlike staff-mediated physical acquisition, the rule operates through the message's effect on its viewer. Calling it a sales adjunct cannot substitute for the justification required for that communicative restraint. The conclusion is one of insufficient demonstrated advancement, not a prohibition on supported preventive inference.

##### III-B. The height rule also independently fails reasonable fit

**Holding and operative rule.** The undifferentiated five-foot placement requirement also fails to justify its burden on protected adult advertising in reasonable proportion to the asserted reduction in children's exposure. This separate fit defect supports the same smokeless and cigar advertising reversal, without prescribing a different height or display arrangement.

**Authority.** O'Connor's Part III-B is joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter; Thomas conditionally adopts the ordinary-test failure. Six support this operative ground.

**Controlling explanation.** Fox requires a reasonable relationship between means and ends even when the State describes the burden as modest. The any-portion height line insufficiently distinguishes exposure of children from adults' practical access to advertising. Neither the importance of reducing youth tobacco use nor general evidence that advertising affects demand explains this particular placement burden. The retained above-height channel, its separate outside-visibility restriction and the actual adult-only exception must be considered together.

The Court does not require one exclusive statistical method. Maps, experience, testimony, the rule's design and practical adult communication can establish or defeat fit. Here the limited justification does not answer the chosen line. A direct message restraint is not sustained merely because it appears beside valid physical-sales regulation. The Court therefore rejects the rule on fit independently of Part III-A's advancement conclusion and leaves different, unpresented display arrangements outside this decision.

##### IV. Staff-mediated access survives the presented speech challenge

**Holding and operative rule.** Assuming that product display and inspection have a cognizable expressive component, the challenged staff-mediated access requirements validly regulate physical acquisition for reasons unrelated to suppressing expression and impose an appropriately limited incidental burden. They survive for Lorillard and Brown & Williamson, USSTC and the cigar petitioners, with the amended exceptions and practical assisted inspection preserved.

**Authority.** O'Connor's Part IV is joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. Six adopt this assumed-speech conduct ground; Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer concur in the access judgment on their distinct physical-conduct ground.

**Controlling explanation.** The incidental-conduct principles of United States v. O'Brien distinguish regulation of physical acquisition for an expression-independent purpose from suppression of a message. Uncontrolled product access can bypass age checks and facilitate unauthorized taking. Requiring the customer to obtain assistance directly advances the substantial age-verification interest without discounting speech because it is commercial.

The rules retain visible information, informative empty packages, actual products accessible through staff, assisted adult inspection and lawful purchases. For premium cigars, an empty box cannot convey aroma, feel or weight; assisted examination of the actual cigar answers that concern. No prohibition on all pre-purchase inspection is found. The amended adult-only self-service permission and the other applicable access exceptions remain operative. The rule need not eliminate every route by which a minor obtains tobacco to address this route. No finding that most youth tobacco is stolen, that adult-only admission is costless, or that handling goods can never communicate is necessary.

United Foods requires attention to the distinct burden and its independent function. Its compulsory-advertising-finance rule neither converts staff-mediated acquisition into direct message regulation nor supplies a new commercial-speech alternative. The Court decides only the parties' presented access claims; the cigarette challenge supplies no retail-preemption question.

##### V. Sampling and derivative inseverability remain distinct questions

**Holding and operative rule.** The Court declines a separate merits adjudication of the identified cigar-sampling restriction because the complete merits presentation does not develop its distinct constitutional challenge; the lower judgment remains undisturbed subject to the amended adult-only daily exception. USSTC's preserved argument that cigarette preemption requires derivative smokeless relief is also left undecided because neither lower court resolved that state-law consequence and the independent smokeless constitutional ruling supplies relief; any necessary unresolved severability work remains below under ordinary procedure.

**Authority.** O'Connor's Part V is joined by all eight other Justices. These are express limits on adjudication, not affirmative merits rulings.

**Controlling explanation.** The actual burdens must be considered separately. United Foods distinguishes the supported ground presented for decision from a different theory that need not be addressed. A developed challenge to staff-mediated access does not itself establish the merits of a free-distribution claim. Sampling was named; the Court does not invent a failure to preserve it or a default. Its amended exception permits adult-only sampling no more than once per person per day, with the product-specific quantity limits retained. No future waiver is declared.

USSTC's inseverability argument is likewise preserved. Section 21.06 and the separate-product operation inform that argument, but a severability clause does not automatically answer it. The earlier cross-product excision of the distinct small-sign provision decides no broader consequence. The Court can resolve the independently presented smokeless speech challenge without deciding this state-law issue in the first instance. Section 22.08 separately governs the cigar regulations; its existence does not collapse the two schemes. No unresolved question here authorizes reopening the unappealed small-sign or cigar-warning relief.

#### Current Law

Covered health-based cigarette advertising-location prohibitions fall within the federal express clause when its federal-labeling condition is met. The challenged noncigarette outdoor rules fail reasonable fit; the height rules fail both demonstrated advancement and fit under the precise majority joins stated above. Supported narrower youth protections, deception restrictions and lawful age limits remain available. Staff-mediated product access survives for the parties and provisions actually challenged. The Court establishes no tobacco-wide immunity, new empirical-proof formula, general sampling rule or complete severability rule.

The advertising provisions cover oral as well as identified written and visual proposals under their actual definitions; utilitarian items not exceeding 200 cubic inches remain excluded from the definition. The separate surviving cigar-sign permission allows, but does not require, one sign of no more than 576 square inches, black text on white, stating only that tobacco products are sold there, without brand or price. The remainder of the sales scheme is not invalidated. Ordinary face-to-face and identification provisions retain the apparent-age exception at twenty-seven. The premium provision concerns the amended branded-premium restriction, not every gift; the amended cigar unpackaging provision concerns little cigars. Training and compliance rules retain their separate employee-number thresholds, six visits at different days and times and, where practical, to different employees, and the outlet-specific next-year presumption after passing the official check. **Kennedy, joined by Scalia, concurring in part and in the judgment.** They join preemption, the sufficient advertising failures, access and nonreach, but withhold Part II-A. They also concur in access affirmance because the requirement regulates physical acquisition, without joining the Court's separate assumed-speech analysis. Assisted inspection preserves the real sensory information important to cigar purchasers. Souter instead joins the Court's independent height failures and assumed-speech access ground. All four accept the distinct sampling and inseverability nonreach. Their alternative cigarette speech positions do not add controlling constitutional grounds to the preemption disposition.

### City of Erie v. Pap's A.M., No. 98-1161

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 10, 1999. On writ of certiorari to the Supreme Court of Pennsylvania, reviewing its October 21, 1998 judgment, 719 A.2d 273. The grant concerns the application of Erie's minimal-clothing requirement to adult-entertainment dancers and the threshold question whether the Court can reach that federal dispute after Pap's ceased operating Kandyland.

Erie enacted Ordinance 75-1994 on September 28, 1994. Section 1(c) prohibits knowingly or intentionally appearing nude in a public place. Section 2 defines nudity through specified insufficiently covered anatomy and devices or coverings that simulate specified anatomy; those distinct terms are reproduced below. Section 3 extends public place to outdoor and enclosed venues, including adult or membership clubs, theaters and invited events with or without admission charges. Section 4 excepts children under ten and an individual exposing a breast while breastfeeding an infant under two. Section 6 directs the least constitutional infringement consistent with the ordinance's purposes and requires severance when a court finds a portion, application or enforcement excessively restrictive of the specified rights. It contains no enacted exception for expression judged artistically superior.

Pap's operated Kandyland, a commercial establishment with nude female dancing and no alcohol sales, though patrons could bring alcohol. The Pennsylvania majority invalidated the nudity restriction under the First Amendment and severed sections 1(c) and 2, leaving the separate prohibitions on sexual intercourse, deviate sexual intercourse and genital fondling intact. The majority did not decide the Pennsylvania Constitution, substantial overbreadth or the objection to standing to assert overbreadth. The submitted record includes the Council video, the parties' agreed facts and the City's repeated litigation interpretation that theatrical expression such as Equus, Hair and Oh! Counsel attributed that interpretation to the City and Council, acknowledged its subjectivity, and sought judicial narrowing; the trial court did not adopt a binding theatrical exemption. The parties agreed that Equus featured frontal nudity for several weeks in October and November 1994 without enforcement efforts during a run in which the prohibition became effective. They deleted the proposed characterization of the production's artistic and social merit. The admitted episode does not establish the sole reason for nonenforcement, a complete enforcement policy or official awareness of every performance. The early temporary restraint and differing effective-date descriptions prevent treating every day of the run as an available enforcement opportunity.

Pap's closed Kandyland and sold the property in 1998. #### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. The existing state judgment presents a live federal controversy

**Holding and operative rule:** In this state-review posture, the judgment's continuing withdrawal of the City's enforcement authority, coupled with the respondent's continuing adverse defense of that judgment, supplies a live federal controversy despite Pap's cessation of the regulated business. This rule depends on the actual legal disability and adversity; abstract governmental interest, incorporation, fees or an imagined return to business does not suffice.

**Authority:** Stevens's Part I, joined by O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: six direct rationale votes. Stone-Zsela separately votes to reach the merits without joining this rationale; Scalia and Thomas dissent.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this state-review controversy remains live because the judgment presently withdraws the City's enforcement authority and the respondent continues to defend that concrete alteration of legal rights. ASARCO permits the party first invoking federal jurisdiction to establish its own injury from the state judgment; it does not dispense with a case or controversy. Here reversal would change the City's actual legal disability, rather than answer an abstract question about desirable legislation.

Pap's closure, sale and retirement are credited. Continued incorporation is not proof of future dancing, and no manipulation, prospective reopening or surviving monetary claim supplies jurisdiction. The objection that only one party retains a business interest is substantial, but the operative state judgment and continued adverse defense preserve this particular dispute over municipal authority. Arizonans for Official English involved an expired individual federal claim and supplies no automatic answer erasing this distinct state-review posture. Laidlaw's defendant-cessation burden is not transferred to a plaintiff leaving its business. The Court recognizes no general exception for public-law litigation or fee disputes; without the actual legal disability and continuing adversity, abstract importance would be insufficient.

**Precedent treatment:** ASARCO supplies the state-review injury principle while retaining the case-or-controversy requirement. Arizonans's actual-expiration rule and Laidlaw's defendant-cessation doctrine retain their distinct predicates.

##### 2. Communicative targeting cannot receive incidental-burden treatment

**Holding and operative rule:** A public-nudity ordinance cannot receive incidental-burden treatment when its justification and operation principally target the communicative adult-entertainment setting; Erie's record does not satisfy the scrutiny that follows. The granted application therefore cannot be sustained, while genuinely general nudity rules and evidence-based regulation of nonexpressive harms remain available.

**Authority:** Stevens, Scalia, Thomas and Ginsburg in Part II, together with Stone-Zsela's express agreement in his concurrence, directly support this precise rule, its application and its stated boundary: five Justices across writings. Stone joins no portion of the lead. This is direct proposition-level agreement, not a Marks inference or five votes for every Part II sentence.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a general public-nudity label cannot give incidental-burden treatment to an application whose justification and operation principally target the communicative adult-entertainment setting. O'Brien requires an interest unrelated to suppressing expression before its incidental-burden analysis can sustain the restriction. The content-neutrality inquiry concerns what the government actually regulates and why, not only the breadth of its words.

Erie's defense of this application through harms attributed to the message-bearing setting does not satisfy that requirement or the scrutiny that follows. A modest clothing requirement does not by itself cure this defect. The rule leaves genuinely general nudity laws and supported regulation of crime, obstruction, sanitation, zoning or other nonexpressive harms available. It neither establishes that every adult-business effect is communicative nor resolves a different properly justified regulation. The more detailed evidentiary account and strict-scrutiny analysis in Part II have four complete joins; the independent intermediate-scrutiny alternative has three. Neither is attributed in full to the five supporters of this common rule.

**Precedent treatment:** O'Brien's nonsuppression requirement is applied rather than displaced. Existing content-neutrality doctrine continues to test operation and justification. No categorical invalidity of nudity rules, new theater exemption or independently controlling O'Brien record-insufficiency holding follows.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Justice Stevens's Part II, joined by Justices Scalia, Thomas and Ginsburg: the fuller content-selection rationale.** These four Justices find communicative selection from the City's repeated hierarchy of protected expression, its message-control explanation, the intended Kandyland application and the mixed Council record. ### United States v. Bajakajian, No. 96-1487

Decision, June 22, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 4, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit. The question is whether forfeiture of the entire $357,144 transported out of the country without the required report violates the Excessive Fines Clause.

Bajakajian pleaded guilty to willfully failing to report currency under 31 U.S.C. §§5316 and 5322. A separate false-statement count was dismissed by agreement, not decided by acquittal. After a forfeiture trial, the District Court found a lawful source, intended payment of a lawful debt and no connection to another crime. It refused full forfeiture under 18 U.S.C. §982(a)(1), ordered $15,000 forfeited and imposed three years’ probation and a $5,000 fine. Section 5322(a) authorized up to five years and $250,000; the applicable Guidelines maxima were six months and $5,000.

The Ninth Circuit affirmed on the ground that reporting currency was not an instrumentality and thus could not be forfeited, without reaching its proportionality prong. The Government seeks the full fund. Bajakajian did not cross-appeal the $15,000 forfeiture.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia and Kennedy, who would reverse the refusal of full forfeiture.

The existing judgment remains in place. The Court does not decide the constitutional validity of the unappealed $15,000 forfeiture or the statutory authority to substitute that amount. The conviction, probation and ordinary fine are not reopened; no new forfeiture amount is fixed.

#### Opinions and Joins

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | Thomas | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Governing fines framework | Punitive coverage, gross disproportionality and rejection of a categorical instrumentality prerequisite. |
| Opinion of the Court, Part II | Thomas | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Appellate-review rule | Independent review of legal proportionality; clear-error review of supported factual findings. Stone-Zsela does not join. |
| Opinion of the Court, Part III | Thomas | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Affirmance | Actual excessiveness of full forfeiture on these findings and bounded affirmance. |
| Dissent in part | Kennedy | O'Connor, Scalia | Would reverse the refusal of full forfeiture | Joins Parts I–II of the Court's opinion; rejects Part III's application and would permit the full statutory forfeiture. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. A punitive criminal forfeiture must not be grossly disproportionate to its offense

**Controlling proposition:** Section 982(a)(1)’s forfeiture imposed on this convicted offender as punishment is a fine subject to the Excessive Fines Clause. A punitive forfeiture violates that Clause when grossly disproportionate to the gravity of the offense for which it is imposed; statutory property connection alone does not answer excessiveness, and instrumentality is not a categorical prerequisite barring every currency-reporting forfeiture.

**Authority:** Part I commands all nine votes. Parts II and III have the distinct coalitions shown above. Each proposition below has direct majority support at its stated scope.

**Controlling explanation:** Austin recognizes partly punitive forfeitures as fines and leaves excessiveness separate from statutory connection. Alexander requires that independent inquiry for criminal forfeiture. The Government’s exaction here is imposed as punishment for the reporting conviction; it is not exempt because the currency is the property involved in the offense.

Substantial respect for legislative punishment judgments and the imprecision of gravity assessments make gross disproportionality, rather than strict equivalence, the governing boundary. Solem supplies that respect and proportionality analogy without deciding a new imprisonment question. The nature of the offense, culpability, harm, property’s relation to wrongdoing and authorized sanctions inform the comparison where relevant. They are not a compulsory exhaustive checklist, fixed ratio or government-cost formula. Congress may punish a reporting violation through property forfeiture, but its statutory authorization does not foreclose the constitutional inquiry. The Court rejects the circuit’s conclusion that such currency can never be forfeited for want of an instrumentality.

##### II. The legal proportionality judgment receives independent appellate review

**Controlling proposition:** Appellate courts independently review the constitutional determination whether this punitive forfeiture is grossly disproportionate, while reviewing supported underlying factual findings for clear error.

**Authority:** Part II commands the votes of all eight Associate Justices; Chief Justice Stone-Zsela does not join this proposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The facts found and the constitutional evaluation perform different work. Deference to the trial court’s supported findings does not transfer the legal boundary of the Excessive Fines Clause to unrestricted sentencing discretion. Independent review supplies no authority to invent an additional offense or substitute adverse facts for the findings. The Court announces no universal review standard for every mixed question.

##### III. Full forfeiture is grossly disproportionate on these findings

**Controlling proposition:** Forfeiture of the full $357,144 is grossly disproportionate to the willful reporting offense established here, considering the lawful funds and purpose, absence of another-crime connection, limited reporting harm and particular sentencing assessment together. The refusal of full forfeiture is affirmed without reviewing the existing $15,000 amount or setting a constitutional dollar ceiling.

**Authority:** The six Justices joining Part III directly adopt this application and bounded affirmance.

**Controlling explanation:** The reporting duty is serious, and willful failure remains culpable even when transporting the funds would otherwise be lawful. But the proved offense withheld information; the findings establish neither criminal proceeds nor laundering, trafficking, fraud or a separate revenue loss against which the whole fund may be measured. Dismissal of the false-statement count does not establish that no false statement occurred.

The applicable six-month and $5,000 Guidelines maxima reflect the particular offense’s assessed severity. Congress’s broader five-year and $250,000 maxima and the forfeiture authorization deserve weight but do not conclusively make this full exaction proportionate. Treating them as conclusive would deprive independent excessiveness review of work. The lawful source, lawful debt and lack of another-crime connection are relevant together; lawful property is not automatically immune from a fine.

The reporting system’s preventive function and the large unreported transaction supply the strongest contrary argument. They do not overcome the extraordinary mismatch on these supported findings. The Court substitutes no new amount. Because Bajakajian did not cross-appeal, neither $15,000’s validity nor authority to select it is decided; no rule identifies $15,001 as excessive. Wealth, income, livelihood effects, other materially different crime-connected forfeitures and a comprehensive rule for innocent owners or forfeitures directed against property remain unresolved.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Austin v. United States:** Partly punitive coverage and an excessiveness inquiry beyond statutory connection are applied; its holdings are not converted into an instrumentality-only test or a universal historical forfeiture theory.
- **Alexander v. United States:** Its independent offense-related Excessive Fines inquiry for criminal forfeiture is applied and given the gross-disproportionality formulation here.
- **Browning-Ferris Industries v. Kelco Disposal:** Its distinction between governmental punishment and private awards supports fine coverage.
- **Solem v. Helm:** Its legislative-deference and proportionality principles inform this fines rule without a new imprisonment holding.
- **Department of Revenue v. Kurth Ranch, United States v. Ursery and Hudson v. United States:** Their distinct Double Jeopardy classification rules remain intact and supply no automatic Excessive Fines result or universal cost ceiling.
- **One Lot Emerald Cut Stones and One Assortment of 89 Firearms:** Their distinct customs and forfeiture classifications establish no exemption or proportionality answer for this direct criminal reporting fine.

#### Law After Decision

Punitive criminal forfeiture is subject to real but deferential gross-disproportionality review. A statutory connection to the offense is not enough, but currency-reporting forfeiture is not categorically forbidden. The full exaction fails on these findings; the judgment establishes neither a ratio nor a constitutional or statutory approval of the existing partial amount.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Justice Kennedy, dissenting in part, joined by Justices O’Connor and Scalia.** They join punitive coverage, the gross-disproportionality standard, independent legal review and rejection of the circuit’s instrumentality prerequisite. They disagree with the application and would reverse the refusal of full forfeiture.

The amount concealed measures the scale of a deliberate failure to report. Congress may punish that wrong seriously without establishing that the funds were criminal proceeds or proving a second offense. Reporting helps detect financial crime that otherwise may remain hidden; treating the absence of a proved additional crime as decisive undervalues that preventive function. The five-year and $250,000 statutory maxima and the expressly mandated forfeiture demonstrate substantial legislative seriousness. The $5,000 Guidelines fine operates alongside, rather than silently replacing, forfeiture.

In their view the full amount remains within the demanding constitutional boundary despite lawful source and intended use. Their ground adds no laundering or trafficking finding and does not treat dismissal of the false-statement count as a conviction. They reject neither fines coverage nor the need for an individualized constitutional judgment, and they do not reopen the conviction, probation or ordinary fine. The disagreement supplies no ruling on the unappealed $15,000 amount.

#### Source Notes

The reported findings and appellate grounds appear at [524 U.S. 321](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-524/pdf/USREPORTS-524-321.pdf); the [original record for No. 96-1487](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385014_0060) identifies the underlying filings. Sections 5316, 5322(a) and 982(a)(1) supply reporting, statutory punishment and forfeiture provisions. The $250,000 statutory and $5,000 Guidelines maxima are distinct; the false-statement count was dismissed rather than resolved by acquittal.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The judgment refusing the Government’s demand for full forfeiture remains effective. No new calculation or further forfeiture hearing is directed. The unappealed partial forfeiture and other sentence components remain undisturbed without affirmative adjudication of their independent validity.

**End of entry: United States v. Bajakajian, Decision, June 22, 1998.**

### 44 Liquormart, Inc. v. Rhode Island, No. 94-1140

Merits decision, May 13, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 1, 1995, in October Term 1995. The Court reviews under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) the First Circuit's October 24, 1994 judgment, 39 F.3d 5, reversing a constitutional declaration and directing judgment for defendants. The earlier July opinion and judgment were withdrawn and vacated.

Rhode Island General Laws §3-8-7 prohibits manufacturers, wholesalers, out-of-state shippers and covered license holders from advertising prices of malt beverages, cordials, wine or distilled liquor offered for sale in Rhode Island. Its exception permits price signs or tags attached to or placed on merchandise for sale within licensed premises, under departmental rules. Regulation 32 prohibits alcoholic-price references on signs visible from outside Class A premises. The exception does not authorize unrestricted indoor or exterior-visible price advertising.

Section 3-8-8.1 separately prohibits Rhode Island-based newspapers, periodicals, broadcasters and advertising businesses from accepting, publishing or broadcasting in Rhode Island advertisements stating or referring to alcoholic-beverage prices. It excepts trade journals recognized and authorized by the administrator. Violations carry a $50 first-offense fine and fines of at most $100 for additional offenses; publication or broadcast may also be enjoined on a retail licensee's or association's complaint. Section 3-8-8 supplies general enforcement and license-suspension authority but is not independently invalidated. Nonprice promotion remains permitted. Section 3-1-5 declares temperance and reasonable control of alcohol traffic as purposes.

44 Liquormart's 1991 advertisement displayed liquor bottles, prices for nonalcoholic goods and prominent WOW bursts. It paid a $400 administrative fine without appealing that proceeding. Peoples, a Massachusetts retailer, could not place truthful advertisements of lawful Massachusetts liquor prices in Rhode Island media. Both plaintiffs have standing; Peoples' distinct First Amendment claim remains, while its Commerce Clause claim was abandoned on appeal.

After trial, the District Court declared the challenged provisions unconstitutional and found no significant effect of this price-advertising ban on Rhode Island consumption. It did not decide the effects of general alcohol-advertising bans. The First Circuit regarded the economic inference supporting the ban as constitutionally sufficient and invoked an added Twenty-first Amendment presumption. The parties' treatment of the proposed speech as lawful and presumably truthful does not establish the disputed consumption effect.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. This record does not establish material advancement of temperance by the price-information ban

**Controlling proposition:** Even under ordinary Central Hudson review and treating temperance as substantial, Rhode Island has not demonstrated that these truthful-price restrictions directly and materially reduce consumption. This conclusion addresses the actual record and restriction, not every possible effect of advertising, price or demand.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Part I, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg: five. Stevens affirmatively adopts this conditional sufficient ground without accepting consumer ignorance as a legitimate objective or ordinary scrutiny as the complete account.

**Controlling explanation:** The First Amendment protects the lawful, nonmisleading information at issue. Central Hudson requires material advancement, not merely an important interest and a plausible causal possibility. Saffer's theory supplies a real argument: suppressing comparison raises search costs and price dispersion, higher average prices tend to discourage purchases, and opposing experts acknowledged price responsiveness and a theoretically reasonable prediction. Their concessions cannot be omitted.

They nevertheless do not establish the necessary effect of this restriction on this record. The District Court credited contrary testimony and found no significant consumption effect. Saffer's international research concerned general advertising bans, with acknowledged control problems, rather than this price-information rule. The record lacked estimates connecting the projected Rhode Island price change to consumption, and market-share or profit gains could occur without greater total consumption. Smart's acknowledgment of the mechanism accompanied his criticism of its missing data; Pittman's theoretical concession likewise did not erase his contrary conclusion.

The Court separately assesses constitutional sufficiency while respecting the evidentiary character of findings and concessions. It neither decides credibility by counting experts nor treats the First Circuit's economic inference as a new trial finding. Supported prediction remains permissible; no state-specific numerical study, experimental certainty or universal study design is required. Here the whole showing fails the material-advancement requirement. The plaintiffs' expected private profits and the difficulty of measuring advertising effects do not discharge the State's burden.

##### II. The complete price-information restraint independently lacks reasonable fit

**Controlling proposition:** Even assuming material advancement, suppressing the challenged category of truthful retail-price information is disproportionate to the asserted temperance objective on this record. Concrete direct price and tax alternatives expose the inadequately justified fit without becoming mandatory replacements or imposing a least-restrictive-means rule.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: seven. O'Connor and Breyer assume advancement without deciding it; Souter independently adopts both failures; Stevens's fit application is conditional and independently sufficient.

**Controlling explanation:** Fox requires a reasonable proportional relationship between the speech burden and the regulatory end. Rhode Island seeks to affect the amount purchased through the price consumers pay, yet withholds accurate comparison information continuously. Saffer's own acknowledgment that minimum prices or higher taxes could serve the consumption objective supplies a concrete comparison with measures addressing price directly.

The State need not select the least restrictive conceivable policy or exhaust every alternative. But it must justify this substantial informational burden in light of practical alternatives. Its interstate-shopping objection does not show why its own indirect price-raising mechanism avoids the same leakage or why the blanket speech burden is proportionate. Midcal and 324 Liquor identify defects in particular price arrangements, not the illegality of every state-imposed price measure. The Court neither designs a replacement nor adjudicates an antitrust dispute.

Permitting nonprice promotion and the specific merchandise and trade-journal exceptions does not by itself defeat or establish fit. Price information may have a distinct competitive effect, and incremental regulation is not automatically invalid. The decisive problem remains the unexplained breadth of suppressing accurate prices to reach an objective directly addressed by the identified controls. Calling the price category complete retains those exact exceptions; it does not describe a ban on all alcohol speech.

This ground suffices even taking the predicted consumption effect seriously. It creates no new heightened tier, universal alternatives checklist or all-sources-at-once requirement. Those who separately favor broader protection do not turn this conditional application into assent by the whole coalition to that broader theory.

##### III-A. The Twenty-first Amendment supplies no added presumption validating this restraint

**Controlling proposition:** Rhode Island's alcohol-control authority supplies no additional presumption relaxing First Amendment protection for these off-premises truthful-price prohibitions. Authority over alcohol sales does not itself establish the validity of suppressing accurate information about lawful sales.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Part III-A, joined by all eight other participating Justices: nine. This common proposition does not attribute Central Hudson balancing to Thomas or either sufficient balancing ground to Scalia.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 2 of the Twenty-first Amendment addresses transportation and importation for delivery or use contrary to state law. It creates no express commercial-speech exception. LaRue and Bellanca involved regulation of conduct on licensed premises; those holdings do not decide this restraint on accurate off-premises information. The State's power to regulate the underlying product cannot alone carry its distinct First Amendment burden. Nor are constitutional speech rights surrendered simply by accepting a liquor license. The challenged bans fail without an added alcohol-based presumption; the Justices supporting the broader conclusion in III-B state that separate ground expressly.

##### III-B. The Twenty-first Amendment does not qualify freedom of speech; LaRue's contrary rationale is disavowed

**Controlling proposition:** The Twenty-first Amendment does not qualify the First Amendment's protection of speech. LaRue's reliance on the Twenty-first Amendment to enhance deference is disavowed, while its holding remains supported by ordinary police powers over the conduct at issue.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Part III-B, joined by Stevens, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg: six. Stone-Zsela, O'Connor and Breyer do not join this broader treatment.

**Controlling explanation:** Constitutional authority over the traffic in alcohol is not authority to diminish a separate constitutional freedom. Giving a liquor-related restriction an extra speech presumption would permit the subject regulated to displace the protection owed to the means of expression. LaRue's result does not require that step: its licensed-premises conduct regulation can rest on the State's police powers. Removing the Twenty-first Amendment rationale therefore neither reverses that conduct holding nor invalidates Bellanca. The rule concerns the interaction with freedom of speech, not the validity of every direct alcohol restriction.

##### V-B. The completed record supports an implementing remand preserving the constitutional declaration

**Controlling proposition:** Reversal of the operative First Circuit judgment requires implementation of the district court's constitutional declaration against the challenged price-information prohibitions. No new merits trial is required on any adopted sufficient ground. The Court does not issue an additional injunction directive.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Part V-B, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: eight. Stone-Zsela separately would direct prospective injunctive relief.

**Controlling explanation:** The case was tried and the challenged provisions declared unconstitutional. The appellate reversal rested on legal treatment that cannot sustain these restrictions under the grounds adopted here. An implementing remand preserves the declaration rather than reopening resolved record development. It does not convert the paid fine or unappealed administrative order into a refund dispute, reach the waived Commerce Clause claim or require a substitute alcohol-control program. Stone-Zsela agrees with reversal but regards an express prospective injunction as necessary relief; that additional direction does not command the remedial majority.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Central Hudson and Fox remain operative. The Court's advancement and fit grounds are independently supported at their exact levels, and no majority creates a higher tier or Thomas's categorical rule. Discovery Network, Edenfield, Ibanez, Lanphere and Rubin retain their restriction-specific burdens and limits. Florida Bar invalidates its finite waiting period on its record. Edge retains its narrower, record-specific holding sustaining the border-broadcast restriction, not an automatic vice or federalism exception.

LaRue's Twenty-first Amendment rationale is disavowed by six Justices, with its conduct holding and Bellanca preserved. The broader attack on Posadas's informational paternalism, greater-power syllogism and lawful-vice exception has four votes in IV-C; it is not a wholesale Court overruling. The controlling fit application denies automatic Posadas authorization here under subsequent developed scrutiny.

Queensgate's summary dismissal is a merits disposition. Mandel confines its force to the issues necessarily decided; it does not adopt every explanation below or require that comparable liquor-price restrictions invariably survive later developed advancement-and-fit doctrine. **Current rule:** Rule 14.1(a) creates a strong prudential presumption against deciding questions neither stated in the petition nor fairly included, subject to exceptional circumstances. Geographic tailoring is fairly included in a challenge to exclusion from designated speech zones. The duty to notice jurisdictional defects remains separate. ##### I. Section 2254(d)(1) has independent clauses and objective legal limits

**Holding:** For a claim adjudicated on its merits in state court, the contrary-to and unreasonable-application clauses perform independent work: conflict with this Court's governing legal rule can satisfy the first without factual identity, and an opposite result on materially indistinguishable facts also qualifies. **Proposition-level authority:** Part I of Justice Breyer's opinion, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg, supplies eight direct votes for these common propositions. It does not resolve the competing exclusive general allocations between the clauses.

The relevant state merits date is June 6, 1997. The legal inquiry acquires no clear-and-convincing burden from factual-review provisions; sections 2254(d)(2), (e)(1) and (e)(2) keep their independent conditions. No facial Article III or Suspension Clause question is decided.

##### II. Lawful mitigation receives ordinary prejudice review under Virginia's unanimity rule

**Holding:** In an ordinary sentencing ineffective-assistance claim, Strickland requires a reasonable probability of a different lawful result, sufficient to undermine confidence, assessed on the full supported aggravating and mitigating record; Fretwell adds no separate unfairness requirement. Because Virginia requires unanimity for death and directs life if the penalty jury cannot agree, a reasonable probability that one conscientious juror would decline death suffices to change the legally available outcome; a bare possible holdout or idiosyncratic mercy does not.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part I, with the same eight-Associate coalition, directly adopts these rules. The seven-Justice Part II applies them in finding the actual constitutional violation.

##### III. The state prejudice application falls outside the range permitted by governing law

**Holding:** A state court unreasonably applies Strickland when its prejudice inquiry materially truncates the supported mitigation or weighs it in an objectively indefensible way; mere disagreement among reasonable jurists does not satisfy section 2254(d)(1). The actual treatment of Williams's documentary deprivation, functional limitations and structured-setting evidence, considered with the complete sentencing record, is objectively unreasonable and independently satisfies the statutory condition for review of his constitutional entitlement.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part II of Breyer's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg, supplies seven direct votes. The four Justices favoring broader governing-rule review independently join this actual objective-unreasonableness conclusion; their broader construction is unnecessary to it.

The full assessment includes severe aggravation, adverse juvenile history, later family improvement, custodial misconduct and possible parole. The holding relies on neither a new diagnosis nor presumed credit or admissibility for every proffer.

**Holding:** Counsel must reasonably investigate available mitigation or make a reasonable, informed decision that further investigation is unnecessary; a later assertion of strategy does not excuse an uninformed failure to investigate identified records. Williams's counsel performed deficiently by failing to obtain the available youth records and develop the supported mitigation, independently of tactical decisions about particular cumulative witnesses.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part II's same seven-Justice coalition adopts the performance rule and actual deficiency finding. Scalia and Thomas assume deficiency without deciding it; their votes do not support a performance holding.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part II's seven-Justice coalition adopts actual prejudice and the stated remedy, independently of any broader general construction of section 2254(d)(1).

## Holdings

### Manual-task disability requires a substantial limitation of general daily-life function

**Holding and operative rule:** Under original 42 U.S.C. §12102(2)(A), an impairment substantially limits performing manual tasks when it prevents or severely restricts the individual from performing tasks central to most people's daily lives, with permanent or long-term impact. This is an individualized standard requiring considerable functional limitation, not complete incapacity or loss of virtually every manual ability; diagnosis alone is insufficient, and no numerical duration threshold applies.

**Authority:** The Opinion of the Court's manual-task-standard and application portions, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Eight direct joins establish the rule; Stone-Zsela does not join these portions.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that substantial limitation concerns a considerable restriction in the major activity asserted. The ADA's common definition is not confined to employment, so specialized assembly requirements cannot alone define manual-task disability. The original text and contemporaneous guidance concerning nature, severity, expected duration and enduring impact support the stated threshold.

Bragdon requires individual proof and rejects complete inability as an added condition. It does not erase substantiality or prohibit this activity-specific duration requirement. A permanent or long-term functional impact need not mean an incurable diagnosis; the Court sets no numerical minimum. Likewise, identifying central daily-life manual tasks does not restrict every major life activity to something performed each day or displace Bragdon's reproduction holding.

Williams is correct that sustained gripping, reaching and repetition may show important general capacities even if some household tasks remain possible. The standard admits that evidence; it rejects treating a class of demanding occupational tasks as sufficient by category alone. Household and self-care evidence also matters in its actual extent, without creating an automatic defense from the ability to perform some tasks. Ordinary summary-judgment principles govern competing evidence. The Court consults contemporaneous guidance without deciding general binding agency authority.

**Precedent treatment:** Bragdon's individual-proof and no-complete-incapacity principles remain; Sutton and Murphy's distinct occupational-breadth inquiry is not transferred to every activity; Kirkingburg supplies no additional disability method.

### Occupational classification is not a substitute for the claimed activity

**Holding and operative rule:** Inability to perform a class of occupational tasks does not by itself establish substantial limitation in the separate major life activity of performing manual tasks. Workplace evidence remains relevant to the extent it proves limitation of the general manual functions at issue; ordinary household and self-care evidence must likewise be assessed without categorical conclusions.

**Authority:** All nine Justices directly join this severable proposition in the Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** Sutton and Murphy's class-of-jobs inquiry addresses working. A court cannot use that inquiry as a shortcut to establish a different activity. The Sixth Circuit's occupational category therefore does not establish Williams's manual-task disability as a matter of law. The relevant functional evidence must be assessed under the rule governing the particular activity, rather than excluded because it arises at work or treated as dispositive because it concerns a class of job tasks.

**Precedent treatment:** Sutton and Murphy's working rules remain confined to their actual activity; individual functional proof remains necessary.

**Holding and operative rule:** The manual-task inquiry assesses the individual's underlying substantial limitation without treating beneficial correction or medication as eliminating it. Individual proof remains required, and coverage remains distinct from qualification, accommodation and safety; the claimant need not stop treatment.

**Authority:** All nine Justices directly join this severable application of Sutton and Murphy in the Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** Sutton and Murphy settle the mitigation baseline under the original ADA. Their rule identifies the functional condition assessed; it does not automatically establish its substantiality. The manual-task threshold applies to that legally defined underlying condition. Successful management does not remove protection by itself, while evidence still must establish this individual's substantial limitation. The Court therefore rejects neither the governing mitigation rule nor the independent need to prove disability.

## Holdings

### Part I. The FAA supplies no enforcement ground for this employment agreement

**Holding and operative rule:** Circuit City's section 1 employment-contract exclusion covers Baker's employment arbitration agreement, so the FAA supplies no enforcement command or federal arbitration-policy ground for restricting this EEOC action or its remedies. The exclusion does not itself determine an independent state-law basis for enforcement between Baker and Waffle House.

**Authority:** Part I of Justice Stevens's opinion is joined by all eight other Justices; nine direct joins control this ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the governing employment exclusion resolves the antecedent FAA question. Circuit City excludes employment contracts within the commerce reach asserted for the Act, including nontransportation employment. Baker's agreement belongs to that category. A general federal preference for arbitration cannot replace statutory coverage. The Fourth Circuit's FAA-based restriction consequently cannot stand. Gilmer concerns a securities-registration agreement and does not supply a contrary answer about this employment contract. Whether another source binds the employee remains distinct from the FAA holding.

### Part II. The Commission's independent action retains its individual-remedy authority

**Holding and operative rule:** An employee's private agreement selecting arbitration does not bind the nonparty EEOC or categorically eliminate victim-specific remedies Congress authorizes in the Commission's own ADA enforcement action. Even if an independent state-law ground makes the promise enforceable against the employee, that fact alone neither makes the Commission a contracting party nor removes those federal remedy categories; this is an independently sufficient ground for rejecting the relief bar.

**Authority:** Part II of Justice Stevens's opinion is joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, seven direct votes. Scalia and Thomas withhold assent to this ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the agency enforces its own statutory cause, rather than an assignment of Baker's claim. Section 12117(a) gives the Commission the enumerated Title VII enforcement powers, procedures and remedies. Section 706(f)(1) authorizes its action after the statutory process; section 706(g) authorizes appropriate individual equitable relief; and section 1981a expressly includes the Commission among plaintiffs who may seek the specified damages. Compensation and reinstatement can advance that public enforcement function.

First Options supplies the actual-assent principle, but the statutory enforcement structure completes the answer. General Telephone recognizes the independent public action including individual relief, and Gilmer preserves an agency enforcement role alongside an individual's arbitration obligation. Baker could commit himself to a forum; he did not undertake a promise for the Commission, which never agreed to arbitrate.

The dissent's strongest objection concerns the beneficiary's valid commitments, not direct compulsion of the agency. It would condition individual recovery on a state-law-contract determination. But the statutory permission for appropriate relief does not create a categorical exception whenever an employee chose an arbitral forum. Enforceability against Baker alone does not diminish the agency's enacted remedies. This conclusion decides the live private-contract argument without resolving the contract's state-law enforceability or a hypothetical FAA-covered agreement. An actual adjudication, settlement, release or satisfaction differs from the forum promise presented here and requires its own record.

### Part III. Statutory conditions govern any actual award

**Holding and operative rule:** The EEOC's authority to seek relief does not establish liability or an entitlement to an award: the applicable disability, qualification, unlawful-discrimination, injury, causation and remedy predicates must be proved, and the court must apply each remedy's conditions below. The court must prevent duplicate satisfaction of the same injury when actual overlapping recovery is established; no completed arbitration, settlement, release or prior satisfaction is established here.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join Part III's ordinary statutory and nonduplication requirements. Scalia and Thomas separately advocate the additional contractual limitation rejected by Part II; their Part III join does not adopt Part II.

**Statutory conditions incorporated in this holding:**

| Remedy provision | Operative conditions and limits |
|---|---|
| Section 706(g)(1) | On the prescribed finding of an intentional unlawful employment practice, the court may enjoin it and order appropriate affirmative action, including hiring, reinstatement, back pay and other equitable relief. Back pay may not accrue earlier than two years before the charge and is reduced by interim earnings or amounts earnable with reasonable diligence. |
| Section 706(g)(2)(A) | Orders requiring union admission or reinstatement, employee hiring, reinstatement or promotion, or back pay are unavailable when the challenged refusal, suspension, expulsion or discharge rested on a reason other than the prohibited discrimination or retaliation. This is the distinct other-reason limitation incorporated with the enforcement provisions. |
| Section 706(g)(2)(B) | For a proved section 703(m) violation where the respondent shows it would have taken the same action without the impermissible motivating factor, the court may grant declaratory relief, limited injunctive relief and fees and costs directly attributable to that claim, but may not award damages or order the admission, reinstatement, hiring, promotion or back pay described in subparagraph (A). That separate trigger does not establish a new ADA mixed-motive or causation rule here. |
| Section 1981a(a) | For this ADA action, compensatory and punitive recovery is available for unlawful intentional discrimination under section 102 or a violation of its section 102(b)(5) accommodation requirements, not disparate-impact damages. For an accommodation claim, damages may not be awarded when the employer proves good-faith efforts, in consultation with the disabled person who informed it of the need, to identify and make an equally effective reasonable accommodation without undue hardship. |
| Section 1981a(b)(1) | Punitive damages require malice or reckless indifference to federally protected rights; governmental respondents are excluded. |
| Section 1981a(b)(2) | Compensatory damages under this provision exclude back pay, its interest and other section 706(g) relief. |
| Section 1981a(b)(3) | The combined cap, for each complaining party, on compensatory damages for future pecuniary loss, emotional pain, suffering, inconvenience, mental anguish, loss of enjoyment of life and other nonpecuniary loss, together with punitive damages is $50,000 for more than 14 and fewer than 101 employees; $100,000 for more than 100 and fewer than 201; $200,000 for more than 200 and fewer than 501; and $300,000 for more than 500. The applicable size must be established for each of twenty or more calendar weeks in the current or preceding calendar year. |

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the enacted remedial menu remains conditional. Toyota supplies the operative disability rules; a seizure by itself establishes none of their required findings. Section 706(g)'s discretion concerns justified relief on proved facts, and section 1981a separately supplies damages on its own terms. Great-West interprets a different ERISA provision and does not delete this express damages authorization. The distinct limitations in section 706(g)(2)(A), section 706(g)(2)(B) and section 1981a must remain distinct rather than being combined into a new general exception. The employee's conduct can matter to mitigation, actual satisfaction or a properly established judgment or settlement. Without such a record, possible future duplication cannot justify removing every victim-specific remedy at the outset. Employer size and the other damages predicates remain matters for ordinary adjudication.

### Part IV. The categorical restriction is reversed without a contract-enforceability prerequisite

**Holding and operative rule:** The Fourth Circuit's categorical prohibition on victim-specific relief is reversed, and the action is remanded for adjudication of liability and appropriate statutory relief. The Commission may pursue that relief without first establishing that Baker's private forum promise is unenforceable under state law.

**Authority:** The same seven Justices who join Part II join Part IV; Scalia and Thomas instead support vacatur and a bounded inquiry into any properly available independent contract ground.

**Controlling explanation:** Both the absence of FAA coverage and the Commission's independent statutory authority defeat the restriction as entered. Because the second ground resolves the private forum agreement's asserted effect on the Commission's remedy categories, an antecedent state-law enforcement proceeding is unnecessary. The remand returns the ordinary statutory merits and remedy work under Parts II and III.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Circuit City Stores, Inc. v. Adams, March 21, 2001:** Applied for the employment-contract exclusion; Baker's employment agreement falls within that exclusion.
- **First Options of Chicago, Inc. v. Kaplan:** Applied for actual assent; it supports but does not replace the independent statutory-remedy analysis.
- **General Telephone Co. of the Northwest v. EEOC, 446 U.S. 318:** Applied for the Commission's independent public enforcement action, including individual relief.
- **Gilmer v. Interstate/Johnson Lane Corp., 500 U.S. 20:** Applied for the agency's preserved enforcement role; its securities-registration agreement does not govern the employment-contract exclusion.
- **Toyota Motor Manufacturing, Kentucky, Inc. v. Williams, January 8, 2002:** Preserved for the actual disability predicates on remand; the seizure allegation is not their adjudication.
- **Great-West Life & Annuity Insurance Co. v. Knudson, January 8, 2002:** Distinguished as construction of a different ERISA remedy provision; it does not remove expressly authorized ADA damages.

## Law After Decision

The FAA supplies no bar for this excluded employment agreement. Independently, an employee's private forum promise does not remove Congress's individual-remedy categories from the EEOC's own enforcement action, even if the promise binds the employee under an independent source. Liability and every actual award remain governed by their statutory predicates, mitigation rules and nonduplication limits. Concrete adjudication, release, settlement and satisfaction questions await a record establishing them.

## Separate Writings

**Justice Thomas, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by Justice Scalia.** They join Part I because Circuit City's governing employment exclusion removes the FAA as a source of enforcement, despite their continuing disagreement with that precedent. They also join Part III's ordinary liability, remedy and nonduplication conditions. The Commission may bring its independent public action and cannot itself be compelled to arbitrate.

They reject Parts II and IV because nonparty assent does not answer whether a court should grant relief for Baker's individual injury in disregard of his own enforceable commitment. Section 706(g)'s requirement of appropriate relief and the distinction between permission to sue and entitlement to a particular remedy, in their view, require respect for a valid beneficiary promise even though broader public relief remains available. First Options addresses actual assent to arbitration, not that distinct remedial question; Great-West similarly preserves attention to the statute authorizing the particular remedy.

The FAA cannot establish enforceability here. They would therefore vacate its categorical restriction and remand to consider any properly available independent state-law ground, the promise's actual scope, and its consequence for individual relief under their view. They presume neither a valid independent ground nor a completed arbitration, and ordinary preservation limits remain. Their position does not compel arbitration of the agency or affirm the existing restriction on a displaced FAA premise.

## Procedure After Action

The action returns through the Fourth Circuit for ordinary adjudication of statutory liability and appropriate relief. The EEOC may seek the authorized individual and public remedies without first adjudicating Baker's state-law forum obligation. The court must apply the stated statutory limitations and prevent duplicate satisfaction if an actual overlapping recovery is proved.

# Thomas v. Chicago Park District — 2002-01-15

[Authoritative Record](../records/thomas-v-chicago-park-district_merits_2002-01-15.md)

## Event

Thomas v. Chicago Park District, No. 00-1249; 534 U.S. 316. Merits decision, January 15, 2002; October Term 2001, chunk 2. Argued December 3, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Seventh Circuit. The ordinance requires permits for events involving more than fifty people and activities including amplified sound. Applicants seeking expressive events, including marijuana-policy advocacy, challenge its facial standards and procedures. The Seventh Circuit sustained the system. The questions concern the full censorship safeguards, constrained discretion, and timely action and review.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

## Public Action

The Seventh Circuit judgment rejecting the facial challenge is affirmed.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The Seventh Circuit's rejection of the facial challenge stands, and the permit ordinance remains operative. Properly presented challenges to discriminatory, delayed or content-based administration retain their ordinary availability; no particular permit denial is adjudicated here.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Scalia | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Affirms the facial judgment | All three holdings, thirteen qualified denial grounds, actual deadlines/review and limits on administration |

## Holdings

### I. Neutral park coordination does not require the entire censorship procedure

**Holding and operative rule:** A genuinely content-neutral permit system for allocating shared park space and coordinating activities need not adopt Freedman's entire procedure for censorship of expressive content, including a requirement that the government initiate judicial proceedings and bear the censorship burden after each denial. Such a system still requires real constraints on discretion, bounded administrative action and meaningful access to review; a logistical label alone does not establish validity.

**Authority:** Justice Scalia's opinion is joined throughout by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine directly adopt this and the following two holdings with their qualifications.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the safeguards must address the function officials may exercise. Freedman concerns official censorship of acceptable expressive content. Allocating park uses under definite scheduling, capacity and safety rules presents a different recurring risk. Cox recognizes neutral coordination of shared public space; Ward requires actual content independence rather than a benign label. The need to obtain a permit does not itself erase that distinction.

The challengers correctly stress that delay and favoritism can suppress speech even without an express message category. The answer is not to presume good administration, but to examine the authorized criteria and the concrete timing and review protections. Here those protections constrain park management rather than authorize approval of ideas. Requiring the District to commence a judicial case after every scheduling refusal would import a censorship procedure without its predicate. Freedman and FW/PBS remain in force in their own settings. Because the full censorship procedure does not apply, the Court need not decide whether Freedman's expeditious-review requirement there demands prompt commencement of proceedings or a prompt final judicial determination.

### II. The ordinance confines denial to objective management grounds

**Holding and operative rule:** The ordinance's two qualified prior-conduct predicates and eleven further grounds, set out below, sufficiently confine denial to neutral park-management subjects for this facial challenge. It permits denial on a listed ground but does not require denial whenever a ground occurs; permission to excuse a disqualifying circumstance authorizes neither an invented ground nor selective treatment by viewpoint.

**Authority:** The unanimous Court opinion adopts this standards determination and its limits.

**Denial grounds governed by this holding:**

| Ordinance basis | Permitted ground, with its condition |
|---|---|
| Prior conduct | To the extent permitted by law, prior material misrepresentation about the nature or scope of a permitted event, or violation of an earlier permit's terms, by the applicant or the person represented. |
| 1 | An application, including required attachments and submissions, is incompletely filled out or executed. |
| 2 | The application fee was not tendered with the application, or the user fee, indemnification agreement, insurance certificate or security deposit was not tendered within the General Superintendent's prescribed times. |
| 3 | The application contains a material falsehood or misrepresentation. |
| 4 | The applicant lacks legal capacity to contract or to sue and be sued. |
| 5 | The applicant or represented person has unpaid prior damage to District property or another outstanding unpaid District debt. |
| 6 | An earlier fully executed application for the same time and place has been received, and a permit has been or will be granted for uses that do not reasonably allow multiple occupancy. |
| 7 | The intended activity conflicts with a District-organized and conducted program already planned and scheduled for that time and place. |
| 8 | The proposed activity is prohibited by or inconsistent with the park's designated classification and uses under section C.1. |
| 9 | The intended activity presents an unreasonable health or safety danger to the applicant, other park users, District employees or the public. |
| 10 | The applicant has not complied or cannot comply with applicable licensing requirements, District ordinances or regulations governing sales or offers of goods or services. |
| 11 | Law, District code or ordinances, or the General Superintendent's regulations prohibit the intended activity. |

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the grounds must be understood with the receipt-order requirement, written reasons and review. Forsyth, Shuttlesworth and City of Lakewood require definite limits on expressive licensing rather than confidence in official goodwill. These provisions identify the matters officials may assess and permit a reviewing court to identify a refusal resting on some different reason.

The strongest objection concerns the judgment involved in assessing danger, applying cross-references or forgiving defects. But permission to decline denial does not confer permission to invent a disqualifying reason. Nor must every technical defect compel refusal to avoid facial invalidity. The criteria remain neutral management limits; they do not authorize message approval, viewpoint favoritism or an audience-hostility burden. Forsyth's separate rule against hostility-based fees continues to govern any covered fee. This case does not adjudicate an unpresented fee amount or validate every financial condition in every application. A materially different or discriminatory administration remains subject to challenge on its own facts, without a new numerical-pattern prerequisite to proving viewpoint discrimination.

### III. The actual deadlines, reasons and review support the facial judgment

**Holding and operative rule:** The District must decide within fourteen days unless written notice extends the period for fourteen additional days; a denial must state its grounds in writing, propose feasible cures, and suggest alternative times or places when based on an earlier competing application. An applicant has seven days to appeal in writing to the General Superintendent, who must act within seven days, followed by available state common-law certiorari review; together with the objective grounds, these protections sustain the ordinance against the facial challenge.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join this procedural-sufficiency holding and affirmance.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that these actual protections answer the presented structural objection without a universal rule for every neutral permit system. Receipt order governs processing; the bounded decision period prevents open-ended administrative withholding; written reasons identify the decision for challenge; feasible correction and alternative scheduling address curable defects; and administrative and judicial review provide avenues to contest an improper refusal. Content neutrality alone would not cure unrestricted discretion or ineffective review.

No automatic-issuance rule is established on this record, and no fixed court-adjudication deadline is supplied. The Court adds neither. It also does not resolve an actual late application or discriminatory denial absent a proper claim and record. Those are applications of continuing First Amendment constraints, not a license for indefinite delay. The Seventh Circuit's facial judgment is affirmed, leaving the ordinance operative while preserving properly presented claims about actual administration.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Freedman v. Maryland, 380 U.S. 51:** Distinguished as content censorship; its safeguards remain effective in that setting, and the meaning of its judicial-timing requirement is unnecessary to resolve here.
- **FW/PBS, Inc. v. Dallas, 493 U.S. 215:** Its application of censorship-related safeguards to the licensing mechanism there is preserved; it does not make this neutral park procedure identical.
- **Forsyth County v. Nationalist Movement, 505 U.S. 123:** Applied for objective limits on licensing and preserved for its independent prohibition of audience-hostility charges; no fee amount is approved here.
- **Cox v. New Hampshire, 312 U.S. 569:** Applied for lawful neutral coordination of shared public space, subject to nondiscriminatory administration.
- **Ward v. Rock Against Racism, 491 U.S. 781:** Applied for genuine content-independent time, place and manner management rather than acceptance of a stated benign purpose alone.
- **Shuttlesworth v. Birmingham, 394 U.S. 147, and City of Lakewood v. Plain Dealer Publishing Co., 486 U.S. 750:** Applied for narrow, definite licensing standards and meaningful protection against unconfined choice.
- **Hill v. Colorado, June 28, 2000, and Schenck v. Pro-Choice Network, February 19, 1997:** Their actual-mechanism and practical-scope limits remain; neither creates blanket immunity for purportedly neutral restraints.

## Law After Decision

Genuinely neutral park coordination may use the ordinance's objective grounds, bounded administrative timetable, written reasons, correction and alternative-scheduling provisions, and review without requiring the District to initiate a censorship proceeding after each denial. Permissive forgiveness of a listed defect does not itself create facially unbounded discretion. The same structure remains subject to meaningful review and to properly presented challenges to viewpoint discrimination, actual delay or other unauthorized administration. No automatic-issuance rule or universal court-decision deadline is established.
