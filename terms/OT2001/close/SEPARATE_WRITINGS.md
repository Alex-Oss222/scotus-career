## 2001-11-13 — TRW Inc. v. Andrews; No. 00-1045

**Stone-Zsela, concurring.** Stone-Zsela joins the Court's statutory ruling and judgment. He adds that liability arises when the elements necessary for the particular statutory claim exist: rejection of general discovery does not erase the need for an element-specific inquiry or an independently applicable preserved exception. Rotella rejects substitution of a later discovery event for the governing accrual inquiry, but does not make disclosure, discovery and actionable injury interchangeable. Properly preserved questions remain subject to ordinary adjudication. The newly presented theory receives no affirmative permission to reopen merely because the Court leaves it undecided.

**Scalia, joined by Thomas, concurring in the judgment.** They begin with the traditional rule that a limitations period ordinarily runs when the cause of action is complete and enforceable, rather than when the claimant learns of it. A general discovery presumption does not follow from Congress's failure expressly to reject discovery. Congress can prescribe a different start and did enact a confined exception here. Traditional fraud/concealment protection and the latent-injury setting remain separate rules. They object that leaving the general background uncertain invites uncertainty under other statutes. Their agreement with reversal and remand does not establish that every disclosure completes every FCRA claim or decide the newly raised actual-loss theory.

## 2001-11-13 — United States Postal Service v. Gregory; No. 00-758

**Thomas, concurring.** Thomas joins the limited statutory opinion at the scope shown in the table, but would also decide statutory sufficiency because the Postal Service's reliance on Bolling makes that ground part of the presented dispute. Under Bolling, full fresh review applies if written notice, a recorded action, or an opportunity to challenge above the original imposing authority was absent. If all three exist, the employee may show clear error on the earlier record. Thomas considers that framework consistent with §7503's protections for minor discipline and its absence of a direct Board appeal. The statute does not require retrying the facts of every earlier action merely because it bears on a later penalty. The agency must establish the earlier action, while the appealed charge and penalty retain their own proof requirements. The contractual and Board routes protect different rights; one route's conclusion is not automatically a prerequisite for the other. He accepts remand concerning the overturned warning without commanding reopening. His affirmative statutory view neither becomes the Court's holding nor resolves every constitutional application.

**Ginsburg, concurring in the judgment.** Ginsburg questions whether restricted clear-error review can satisfy the statutory preponderance requirement when the earlier merits remain unadjudicated. The opportunity to contest a recorded action does not itself prove adequate review. She therefore withholds assent to the Court's independent-review rationale, without holding Bolling invalid or requiring fresh review of every final disciplinary action. Her remand rests on the Board's actual reopening power, Gregory's election of Board review of the removal, the reservation of Bolling's adequacy, and rejection of an absolute prohibition on agency consideration of pending discipline. Reopening provides a route to request consideration of the changed disciplinary record, not guaranteed relief. A refusal to address a material invalidated predicate could present an arbitrary-and-capricious question. The removal election does not abandon earlier grievances, establish personal control over union arbitration, or substitute for adequate Board process. Her distinction between final discipline and an action still being challenged remains a noncontrolling qualification.

## 2001-11-27 — Chickasaw Nation v. United States; No. 00-507; Chickasaw and Choctaw Nations

**Souter, joined by Stone-Zsela and O'Connor, dissenting.** The dissent would give the expressly named chapter operative effect. The reporting-and-withholding description and the substantive chapter reference pull in different directions; calling the reference an accident is not the only possible reading. Punctuation does not make text inside parentheses legally inferior, and specific inclusion may show that the general description is too narrow. National Bank requires integrated interpretation, not a license to discard the inconvenient side of an enacted conflict. At least genuine ambiguity remains after ordinary reading, and the Indian-law canon resolves it in favor of the tribes; tribal economic-development purposes support that reading without independently granting immunity.

The dissent would apply state-equivalent treatment to chapter 35's pertinent exemption for a sweepstakes, wagering pool or lottery conducted by a state agency under state law, where the wager is placed with that agency or its authorized employee or agent. Section 4411's occupational tax would then follow its own liability-linked terms. This is a construction of the IGRA cross-reference for covered operations, not an exemption for every tribe, member, game or federal tax. The dissent would reverse and remand for application to each tribe's actual assessments and relief, leaving transaction coverage and calculations to the tax record.

## 2001-11-27 — Correctional Services Corp. v. Malesko; No. 00-860

**O'Connor, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, dissenting.** The dissent would reverse and dismiss the corporate constitutional claim. Meyer gives independent force to the distinction between an established officer action and judicial creation of a new entity remedy. A federal custodial duty, useful compensation and the absence of sovereign immunity do not themselves supply that cause. The private defendant weakens the public-fisc concern, but does not eliminate the separate question of who may create a corporate constitutional remedy. Richardson addresses immunity and expressly does not resolve corporate liability or cause creation. Individual actions, state tort remedies, prospective relief and contractual oversight retain their actual scope and reinforce remedial restraint; the dissent does not need to assume that tort relief exactly duplicates every constitutional injury. Limiting the proposed action to corporate policy narrows liability but still adds the disputed defendant. The dissent's objection concerns creation of that cause, not a finding that the company complied with the Constitution or a grant of sovereign status to every contractor.

**Scalia, joined by Thomas, dissenting separately.** They join O'Connor's dissent and add that the Court should not extend implied constitutional damages beyond the recognized Bivens, Davis and Carlson settings even when a proposed extension can be analogized to their reasoning. That distinct objection reaches the Court's claim of a sufficiently close custodial analogy: accepting the analogy does not, in their view, authorize another judicially created damages action. Existing recognized causes remain operative. They do not rely on treating Sandoval as a controlling general abandonment of statutory implication, because its actual statutory holding supplies no such premise. Their separate ground neither decides private employee liability nor overrules the recognized constitutional actions.

## 2001-12-10 — J. E. M. Ag Supply, Inc. v. Pioneer Hi-Bred International, Inc.; No. 99-1996

Scalia concurs and joins the Court in full. Chakrabarty already authoritatively construed the general subject-matter language to reject the supposed ambiguity based on living subject matter. The plant-specific statutes therefore cannot merely be used to clarify that language into the opposite categorical exclusion; a claimed change must satisfy the more demanding inquiry into displacement of the existing rule. This canon explanation supplements rather than narrows the Court's eligibility and coexistence rationale.

Stevens dissents, joined by Breyer, and would treat the plant-specific enactments as the exclusive protection for their covered plant categories. The 1930 Act expressly amended the general patent provision, conditioned protection on asexual reproduction and limited the reproduction right; the later variety statute supplied seed-reproduction protection with saved-seed and research limits. In their view, the utility route bypasses those particular congressional choices, and recodification did not undo them. Different eligibility thresholds do not explain why increased originality should eliminate protections Congress deliberately conferred on agricultural users. Chakrabarty's human-made microorganism lay outside those specialized categories, so the dissent preserves its holding and does not exclude all living inventions or independently qualifying processes. The dissent would reverse on the category defense.

## 2001-12-10 — Schrader v. Blackwell; 241 F.3d 783 (6th Cir. 2001)

Stevens dissents, joined by Stone-Zsela, Souter and Ginsburg, and would distinguish verified affiliation from the privileges and official representation of recognized-party nomination. Because the candidate has independently qualified and the party actually nominated him, the State must explain why every accurately qualified affiliation cue must be suppressed. Anderson-Burdick requires that fit even when the burden is less than severe; campaign speech does not eliminate the distinct ballot burden. Norman, Tashjian and Eu give weight to the real association and supported standard bearer. Timmons concerned fusion nomination and does not decide this separate informational burden. The dissent would permit the State to verify affiliation and choose accurate wording, and would recognize demonstrated administrative or effective-governing interests rather than impose a universal preferred-label right. It would remand for bounded prospective relief, preserving party-recognition thresholds and leaving the completed election intact.

## 2002-01-08 — Dusenbery v. United States; No. 00-6567

Stevens dissents, joined by Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, and agrees that due process does not guarantee receipt. ## 2002-01-08 — Great-West Life & Annuity Insurance Co. v. Knudson; No. 99-1786

## 2002-01-08 — Toyota Motor Manufacturing, Kentucky, Inc. v. Williams; No. 00-1089

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela concurs in the occupational-shortcut and mitigation propositions and in setting aside the partial judgment, but rejects a categorical severe-and-permanent threshold. He would assess individualized substantial limitation of important manual functioning through breadth, frequency, duration and practical effect. These are relevant considerations, not separate mandatory elements. Important work-related functions cannot be excluded because they occur at work, and retained household abilities do not automatically defeat coverage. Expected duration informs substantiality without making permanence indispensable. Sutton and Murphy preserve the underlying-impairment inquiry; the difference between working and manual tasks requires correction of the appellate shortcut, not a restriction to only the most pervasive daily-life limitations. His proposed standard still requires proof rather than diagnosis alone, preserves distinct qualification and accommodation issues, and would not grant Toyota automatic judgment. That broader standard is noncontrolling.

## 2002-01-09 — Kelly v. South Carolina; No. 00-9280; 534 U.S. 246.

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by no other Justice.** He joins Part I in full. The escape planning and proposed violence while confined invited the inference that the omitted parole information answers. Under Chapman, the complete presentation leaves reasonable doubt whether the misleading sentencing premise contributed to death. Strong aggravation does not resolve that separate inquiry. He would therefore reverse the death-sustaining judgment and require a new lawful penalty proceeding now if the State continues to seek death, preserving guilt. This is a record-specific harmfulness determination, not structural error or an automatic new-hearing rule in every capital case. Simmons and Shafer supply the governing protection and the separate error-effect standard; his disagreement concerns its application and the resulting immediate remedy.

**Justice Scalia, dissenting, joined by no other Justice.** He would affirm against this federal claim because the Constitution does not supply this further parole-information obligation. Gardner's unavailable adverse material and Skipper's excluded rebuttal evidence do not establish a general duty to explain parole law. He accepts Shafer's account of the statutory alternatives without joining the new constitutional application. Treating evidence capable of forecasting danger as sufficient risks erasing Simmons's conditional limit. He resolves neither informational adequacy nor harmlessness and does not join the other dissents.

**Justice Kennedy, dissenting, joined by no other Justice.** He accepts Simmons and Shafer's contextual rule but finds the trigger absent here. In his view the State actually used the material for retribution, character and inability to adapt safely to prison, rather than meaningfully invoking danger from ordinary future release. Parole ineligibility does not answer danger to guards or an escape risk. Evidence capable of supporting a prediction is not necessarily a State invocation of the particular danger the rule addresses. He requires no verbal formula and does not deny that circumstantial evidence can establish the trigger on a different record. He would affirm without separately deciding adequacy or error effect.

**Justice Thomas, dissenting, joined by no other Justice.** Assuming the sentencing alternatives and future-danger predicate, he finds the actual information adequate. Defense argument conveyed permanent imprisonment, the judge directed the ordinary meaning of life, and the jury received neither a direction barring parole consideration nor a response to a parole inquiry that displaced the explanation. The good-time evidence also belongs in the full presentation. As in his Shafer dissent, substance can satisfy Simmons without the requested statutory formula. He therefore would affirm for compliance, not absence of danger or harmlessness. His constitutional disagreement with Simmons remains a reservation, not an independent ground, and he makes no finding about the jurors' actual beliefs.

## 2002-01-09 — Riley v. St. Luke's Episcopal Hospital; 252 F.3d 749 (5th Cir. 2001) (en banc); no Supreme Court docket supplied.

**Justice Scalia, dissenting, joined by Justice Thomas.** They would reverse on both Article II grounds and bar this nonintervened prosecution while preserving lawful governmental enforcement. A partial financial assignment does not eliminate the sovereign's retained claim, penalties, settlement interests or exposure to a binding judgment. In their view, initiation and prosecution of that public component exercise executive power through a statutory role continuing until the suit ends. The Executive neither selected this prosecutor nor possesses the ordinary chain of direction that execution of the laws requires.

Scalia's Morrison dissent supplies his executive-control premise; Vermont Agency's assignment-based standing opinion expressly reserves this distinct Article II question. His and Thomas's published Laidlaw concern about private public-law enforcement likewise distinguishes an injured claimant from uncontrolled governmental enforcement. Edmond, Hilario and Landry require attention to actual authority, appointment and supervision rather than final judgment power alone. The relator's financial incentive and title do not remove the significant public function. Early qui tam practice and a court-bound assigned claim are substantial contrary considerations, but they do not cure prosecution of the retained public interest without lawful appointment and constitutionally adequate direction.

The statutory safeguards are real but conditional. Later intervention requires good cause, dismissal requires notice and a hearing opportunity, settlement has its separate judicial standard, and discovery protection depends on its stated showing. These measures do not supply the missing selection and direction. The dissent does not demand presidential at-will removal of every inferior officer or decide every private statutory action. It would return for the procedural consequence of disallowing Riley's nonintervened prosecution, including any lawful governmental election, without invalidating section 3730(a), dismissing government claims with prejudice, presuming intervention or appointment, or treating the entire Act as inseverable.

## 2002-01-15 — EEOC v. Waffle House, Inc.; No. 99-1823; 534 U.S. 279.

## 2002-01-22 — Lee v. Kemna; No. 00-6933

## 2002-02-19 — Barnhart v. Sigmon Coal Co.; No. 00-1307

**Stevens, joined by O'Connor and Breyer, dissenting.** The dissent would reverse and remand for application of an inclusive succession rule. Congress allocated existing health-benefit obligations across continuing business relationships. Reading the successor sentence in that integrated structure avoids the anomaly of reaching a related person's successor while exempting the closer direct successor that continued the coal business and assumed its agreement. The contextual treatment of successors in 1 U.S.C. sections 1 and 5, together with the labor-law succession principles illustrated by Wiley, Golden State Bottling and Fall River Dyeing, supports that reading; those authorities do not establish liability for every solvent purchaser.

The dissent recognizes that a differentiated list and a separate prospective transfer provision could reflect compromise. Its answer is that the entire succession structure supplies the context for that list, and the Court's separation defeats the allocation Congress made. Legislative explanations corroborate that construction rather than replacing the text. The dissent does not treat a bargaining discount as a proved fact, impose unrestricted federal common-law liability, or decide the constitutional question. Preserved successor-status and other application issues remain for the proper tribunal on remand.

## 2002-02-19 — Romeu v. Cohen; 265 F.3d 118 (2d Cir. 2001); lower-court Nos. 00-6287 and 00-6303

**O'Connor, joined by Scalia, Thomas and Breyer, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** The four would affirm both equality dismissals. Residence ordinarily identifies membership in a State's electorate. Congress and New York may accommodate former residents outside the domestic political system without extending the accommodation to people who establish membership in another domestic polity. Burdick, Timmons and Schrader permit meaningful electoral categories and require identification of the relevant electorate before the severity of a lost opportunity determines scrutiny. Saenz concerns equal citizenship in the new State, not a continuing former-state vote.

The dissent acknowledges that Puerto Rico's local elections and Resident Commissioner do not replace presidential participation. That absence makes the exclusion consequential but does not establish continuing membership in New York's electorate. Domestic political membership and incremental overseas accommodation provide a rational basis for the distinction, without making territorial power unreviewable or every residence rule valid. UOCAVA is a federal floor, so New York could extend eligibility; that permission does not establish a constitutional duty to do so. The proposed decree's precision does not cure the majority's antecedent membership error. The four join the prospective-review and federal-floor conclusions and the rejection of independent permanent-membership, travel, Article IV and general due-process entitlements.

## 2002-02-20 — Wisconsin Department of Health and Family Services v. Blumer; No. 00-952

**Stevens, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor and Scalia, dissenting.** The dissent would affirm the resources-first requirement and return the matter for a calculation using supported income and resource figures. Section (e)(2)(C) directs an increased resource allowance when the community spouse's protected income is inadequate. Predicting income from resources legally attributable to that spouse differs from predicting another person's optional payment. Section (d)(1)(B)'s posteligibility transfer does not add a prerequisite that the spouse depend on that payment before receiving the resource protection; denying eligibility can prevent the projected arrangement from beginning.

The dissent recognizes that (b)(1) is not a reciprocal nonattribution rule and that (b)(2) expressly addresses posteligibility attribution. Its point is the scheme's separation of ownership, resource protection and voluntary support, not an invented symmetric prohibition. The Court's answer that every method forecasts future circumstances fails to explain why Congress's resource protection may depend first on another person's voluntary transfer. A future payment might not occur or might diminish; those are objections to the legal method, not findings about Irene's later conduct. Administrative flexibility, conservation of funds and expertise cannot supply the missing compulsory transfer or displace the specific statutory command. The agency's changed view is not invalid merely because it changed, but its reasoning must answer this objection. The dissent asserts no constitutional right to preserve assets and would award no unsupported dollar amount.

## 2002-02-27 — Guest v. Leis; consolidated Sixth Circuit subscriber appeals reported at 255 F.3d 325; no Supreme Court docket supplied.

**O'Connor, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** Confidential access credentials have an exclusion function materially different from ordinary provider records. She would vacate the categorical dismissal only at that scope and require a claimant-specific showing on remand. Names, addresses and other routine identifying information deliberately conveyed for administration ordinarily fall within Miller and Smith; expected confidentiality alone does not distinguish them. Ferguson and Denius require attention to what was entrusted and the actual use, but do not establish the broader account rule adopted by five Justices. She joins objective personal immunity and rejects a requirement of actual authoritative reliance.

**Scalia, joined by Kennedy and Thomas, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** The three would affirm the subscriber-information judgment on the merits as well as on personal immunity. Miller and Smith do not depend on public dissemination: they address information voluntarily conveyed to a provider, often with an expectation of limited use. Calling the records digital or credential-related does not establish a constitutional distinction from that settled rule. Kyllo's protection of concealed home information does not control operational information held by a different person. Each claimant still must establish a personal interest and an intrusion; the existing equipment and private-message dispositions are distinct. The three join Part II's independent objective immunity ground.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** The Chief Justice joins the full privacy rule and both institutional remands but would also vacate personal immunity. After an actionable violation is established under the complete substantive rule, including its reasonable-factual-mistake allowances, he would require the official to identify binding precedent or enacted authority directly authorizing the challenged conduct, prove genuine reliance and show reasonable grounds for treating the permission as valid and applicable. An instruction accurately transmitting the authority may prove reliance; silence, unsettled law, departmental practice, general investigative authority or an ungrounded supervisor or prosecutor assurance would not. Knowing material falsification, concealed disqualifying facts or manufactured authority would defeat reasonable reliance. So would disregard of the authority's apparent limits, apparent unlawfulness under controlling constitutional principles, or a factual mistake already rejected under the substantive merits rule. The protection would include an unexpected change in controlling law that the official could not reasonably anticipate.

He would overrule Harlow and Anderson only insofar as insufficiently clear law independently excuses a proven violation, disapprove Wilson to that extent and retain Elder's consideration of relevant law for the new inquiry. Section 1983's remedial commitment, in his view, warrants that change while concrete reliance protects legitimate official decisiveness. He acknowledges an additional factual inquiry and litigation cost; ordinary pleading and summary-judgment procedures would remain. A valid warrant may defeat the constitutional claim, but its existence alone does not prove actual reliance, and later litigation citations cannot establish the official's 1995 knowledge. A prior judgment granting personal immunity would not itself constitute authority permitting the underlying conduct. He would allow the officials to make the newly required showing on remand. His proposal would not reopen final judgments, change the plaintiff's other elements, create an institutional remedy, displace an independent statutory defense or decide a separate absolute immunity.

## 2002-02-27 — Raygor v. Regents of University of Minnesota; No. 00-1514.

**Stevens, joined by Stone-Zsela, Souter and Breyer, dissenting.** Section 1367(d) protects a related claim actually asserted alongside an authorized federal controversy even when immunity prevents a federal merits judgment on that claim. The filing-related word asserted does work distinct from maintainable. The pending-period and thirty-day protection, subject to longer state tolling, preserve an otherwise available state forum rather than create a new cause, impose liability or waive immunity. The subsection (a) reference supplies original-jurisdiction and relatedness predicates; it need not make eventual adjudicability a further condition absent from the dismissal protection. Pennhurst therefore survives this reading. Kimel supplies the valid federal ADEA anchor, while Schacht keeps the supplemental defense claim-specific. Vermont Agency's exclusion from a sovereign-defendant action does not answer this different procedural effect. The dissent would reverse the categorical exclusion, leaving actual filing arithmetic and remaining independent defenses for the state court and leaving the federal judgment closed.

**Stone-Zsela, separately dissenting.** Congress's authority to implement Article III, together with the Necessary and Proper Clause, permits this reasonable protection against forfeiture while a federal tribunal determines its authority over related claims. The clear statement needed to authorize a treasury judgment cannot be transferred without analysis to a rule preserving time in a state forum the State otherwise permits. Text and procedural structure support coverage: immunity dismissal does not erase the fact that the claim was asserted with a valid federal controversy. The Chief Justice would apply the enacted pending-period and thirty-day protection, subject to a longer state period, while preserving the State's distinct forum immunity, substantive defenses and any independent finality defense. His position supplies no new state cause of action, no authority to reopen the federal dismissal and no automatic finding that these state filings were timely. His proposed construction and remand do not command a majority.

## 2002-03-04 — United States v. Vonn; No. 00-973; 535 U.S. 55

**Stevens, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** Stevens joins Part II's use of relevant material throughout the official record and agrees that the appellate judgment should be vacated. He rejects Parts I and III's transfer of the effect burden to the defendant. Rule 11 assigns the judge affirmative duties during a procedure in which an uninformed defendant may not recognize the missing advice. ## 2002-03-19 — Edelman v. Lynchburg College; No. 00-1072.

**Thomas, concurring.** He joins the Court on the understanding that section 713(a) expressly delegates procedural rulemaking, this regulation is reasonable, Title VII does not prohibit it and its promulgation complies with the Administrative Procedure Act. All four conditions matter. The rule completes verification of an existing timely charge; it neither waives a statutory deadline nor recognizes an unrestricted equitable power to excuse procedural requirements. His qualification accompanies his full join in the Court's explanation, rather than substituting a judgment-only ground.

**O'Connor, joined by Scalia, concurring in the judgment.** Reading the provisions together most naturally calls for a verified charge within the filing period. But the statute does not expressly define charge in a way that unambiguously compels that reading. Section 713(a)'s procedural delegation therefore permits the EEOC's reasonable alternative, which preserves both timely initiation and later verified accountability. ## 2002-03-19 — Milstein v. Cooley; Ninth Circuit No. 99-56682; 257 F.3d 1004.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela concurs in part and dissents in part; no other Justice joins. He agrees that investigative fabrication, personal accusation, detective work and press statements lack absolute immunity. He also preserves immunity for the counsel motion and ordinary advocacy outside his proposed exception. He would vacate the remaining dismissal insofar as it categorically protects knowing material factual fabrication or deliberate false presentation causing an actionable deprivation.

His exception requires knowing fabrication of a materially false fact, deliberate material falsification of an evidentiary record, or knowing presentation of such false facts; an independently actionable constitutional deprivation; the official's own responsibility; and the governing causal connection. He acknowledges Imbler's common-law account and the especially strong continuity owed a statutory precedent. The strongest opposing interest belongs to honest prosecutors: easy accusations of deliberate falsity may be costly to disprove and chill independent advocacy. Criminal sanctions and discipline also supply real deterrence. Stone-Zsela accepts that his proposal increases discovery and litigation costs and weakens protection from suit; saying that no one may legitimately falsify evidence does not answer those costs. The claim remains act-specific, not a general dishonesty tort or entitlement to sue for every malicious prosecution. The counsel motion does not become actionable merely because the prosecutors allegedly wanted a conviction.

He would remand for that bounded inquiry. ## 2002-05-20 — Alabama v. Shelton; No. 00-1214; 535 U.S. 654.

Thomas's dissent, joined by Scalia and Kennedy, would reverse only the present invalidation of the suspended jail term. It reads Argersinger and Scott to make actual loss of liberty, rather than a presently unexecuted threat, the constitutional attachment point. Shelton has not been imprisoned; a violation, discretionary revocation and a determination that other sanctions are inadequate would precede incarceration. The dissent would address the adequacy of execution procedures when that issue is presented.

The dissent accepts that competent self-representation is not an informed waiver. It does not approve ordinary revocation as a substitute for a counseled trial. A complete counseled retrial, or possibly other adequate safeguards, could protect against unconstitutional execution, and no such procedure is found to exist here. Nichols's defined fine-only Guidelines permission does not authorize unconditional later imprisonment for this offense. The dissent also questions the asserted equivalence between consensual pretrial diversion and post-trial probation and the inference that existing state appointment practices establish the costs of counsel for every suspended sentence. Practical convenience does not decide the Constitution, but uncertain cost comparisons do not justify moving Scott's boundary. The dissent preserves the conviction and independent monetary punishment and leaves freestanding probation and the late contempt theory undecided.

## 2002-06-24 — Ring v. Arizona; No. 01-488.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** Stone-Zsela joins the eligibility rule and the qualified actual-verdict inquiry in Parts I and II. He would reverse the ruling that judge-only death-eligibility factfinding is constitutional, vacate the appellate affirmance of the death sentence, and remand for the State's actual harmlessness argument. The appellate affirmance should be set aside for that initial inquiry rather than reversed as in Part III. The State must establish harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt under the qualified Neder framework by examining the actual elements, instructions, findings and qualifying evidence, without resolving new credibility disputes or treating robbery as an automatic pecuniary-gain finding. If the State fails, the death sentence must be vacated; any later sentencing requires independent state-law authority and compliance with constitutional and double-jeopardy limits. If it succeeds, a consistent sentence judgment may be reinstated. He preserves the felony-murder conviction and supplies no universal structural-error rule or automatic new capital proceeding.

**Kennedy, concurring.** Kennedy joins the entire Court opinion while maintaining that Apprendi was wrongly decided. Its settled maximum rule nevertheless must be implemented in a principled way, and Walton cannot survive where an additional fact is indispensable to death eligibility. His disagreement with Angle's distinct floor extension does not justify withholding the jury protection required by the governing maximum rule here. Respect for state expectations counsels caution about other extensions and lawful implementation; it does not reconcile this judge-only eligibility allocation with Apprendi. He supports the qualified actual-record remand rather than a general jury-selection command.

**Scalia, concurring in part and in the judgment; Ginsburg joins Part II.** In Part I, Scalia supports the eligibility holding despite his continued objection to the capital-narrowing requirements associated with Furman. Once the State makes an aggravating fact legally necessary, its origin does not remove the jury guarantee. Distinguishing factors adopted voluntarily from those responding to constitutional narrowing would not provide a reliable basis for dispensing with that protection. Gaudin and the punishment-authority principle underlying Apprendi and Angle require the fact to receive element safeguards. He preserves the distinction between that finding and the ultimate life-or-death selection, disagreeing with Breyer's independent Eighth Amendment approach. The concern that legislative sentencing labels could diminish the jury's constitutional role reinforces his allocation position; it is not a new factual finding about Ring.

In Part II, joined by Ginsburg, Scalia rejects the Court's broader whole-record inference method. Their Neder position permits harmlessness only through genuine equivalent findings the jury actually made. A court's assessment that the jury probably would have found pecuniary gain cannot supply an unasked element. They join Court Parts I and III and support reversal and remand, leaving the actual charge and findings to be examined, but withhold Court Part II. Ginsburg joins only this error-review limitation, not Scalia's separate account of capital-narrowing doctrine. Neither finds that robbery automatically establishes pecuniary gain or adopts a new appellate credibility determination.

**Breyer, concurring in the judgment.** Breyer continues to reject Apprendi's Sixth Amendment reclassification and joins neither the Court's eligibility rationale nor its broader harmlessness method. He nevertheless concludes that the Eighth Amendment requires a jury to take responsibility for the ultimate decision to impose death. He expressly reconsiders his own join in Harris v. Alabama and adopts the jury-responsibility view expressed by Stevens in that case and Spaziano. Gregg and Furman require special capital safeguards; Witherspoon identifies the jury's role in expressing community judgment, and proportionality requires attention to whether this defendant should suffer death. In Breyer's view, retribution is the principal justification for capital punishment, and a jury better expresses the community's moral judgment about its use in the particular case than a single official, even an elected judge. He treats death differently from ordinary guided sentencing. His ground reaches final selection, not simply an aggravator that makes death available: harmlessness of the omitted eligibility finding alone would not answer his objection to judge-only final selection. He concurs in reversal and remand, but his separate rule commands no Court majority.

**O'Connor, dissenting.** O'Connor would affirm against the allocation claim by retaining Walton and reconsidering Apprendi. She acknowledges that the two rules are incompatible; capital guidance does not harmonize them. Her Apprendi and Angle dissents defend legislative authority to distinguish offense elements from sentencing considerations and the historical judicial role in selecting punishment. She regards the Court's maximum-fact rule as constitutionally unwarranted and its extension as disruptive of settled state procedures. Those objections support her choice of which precedent to reconsider, not a finding that an aggravator is legally unnecessary. She does not join Breyer's Eighth Amendment rule, supply a vote for the Court's broader harmlessness directions, or sustain the sentence by making a substitute pecuniary-gain finding. Ordinary error review and collateral limits remain distinct from her preferred no-error ground.

He therefore reaches the result for both impeachment and affirmative-defense information without joining the Court's reliance on predicted degree of assistance and practical balancing.
