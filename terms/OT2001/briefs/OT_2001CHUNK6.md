## 81. SEC v. Zandford

* **Case Name:** SEC v. Zandford
* **Citation or Docket:** 535 U.S. 813; No. 01-147.
* **October Term:** OT2001.

* **Question Presented:** Whether a broker’s fraudulent sale of securities in a discretionary customer account, undertaken as part of a scheme to steal the proceeds, is fraud in connection with securities transactions under the federal securities laws?
* **Question Granted:** The merits questions stated above are paraphrased from the reviewed judicial record; no exact grant wording is asserted.
* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The district court granted the SEC summary judgment after Zandford’s wire-fraud convictions. The Fourth Circuit reversed and ordered dismissal because it viewed the scheme as theft insufficiently connected to securities sales. The SEC seeks reversal of that legal ruling.

### Issue 1: Integrated deceptive sales

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** United States v. O’Hagan recognizes primary securities fraud where a person secretly uses entrusted information in breach of a fiduciary relationship as part of securities trading. The source of the confidence need not be a trading counterparty. Concealed disloyalty, scienter, and the transaction connection do substantive work; a fiduciary breach without deceptive trading is not enough.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** Identify the deceptive conduct, the duty that makes concealment meaningful, the actor’s culpability, and the securities transaction through which the scheme operates. Do not transfer a personal narrower view from a separate opinion into the Court’s governing rule.
* **Other Governing Law:** The Exchange Act and the SEC’s antifraud rule prohibit deceptive devices in connection with securities purchases or sales. The SEC must prove the elements applicable to its civil enforcement claim. Ordinary theft law and state fiduciary duties remain distinct sources of responsibility.
* **Material Other Authority:** The Fourth Circuit’s distinction between misappropriation of proceeds and securities fraud is the reasoning under review. Prior cases concerning the securities connection inform the statutory context but do not supply a categorical requirement of a false statement about market value.
* **Existing Rule’s Reach:** O’Hagan already rejects the premise that deception must run only to a trading counterparty or concern the issuer’s value. It does not make any breach by a person associated with securities actionable under federal law.
* **Open Point:** Whether the alleged sales that generate the stolen proceeds are integral deceptive transactions, and what that conclusion leaves for civil proof on remand.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

**Reasoning below.** The Fourth Circuit separated sales from appropriation of the money. Because the sales themselves obtained market value and no false representation about a security was alleged, it treated the case as ordinary theft beyond the federal antifraud provision.

**The SEC.** The sale and theft were parts of the same planned abuse of discretionary authority. A broker who appears to be exercising that authority for the investor while liquidating assets to steal the proceeds engages in deception through the transaction. The Commission asks for an integrated-transaction rule, not federal control over every misuse of assets by a broker.

**Zandford.** A lawful sale followed by conversion does not become securities fraud merely because securities generated the money. An expansive connection test would federalize fiduciary and theft claims whose substance has little to do with the securities markets. He also retains arguments about which facts the criminal convictions conclusively establish.

**Limits.** The SEC must connect the deception to the sales, rather than rely on the defendant’s occupation. Zandford’s concern is substantial for later independent misuse of proceeds, but it does not require treating the alleged planned liquidation as factually separate from the theft it enabled.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

Reversal would remove the categorical connection barrier and permit the enforcement action to continue. Whether summary judgment is appropriate must then be assessed against the actual record and the issues established by the convictions. Affirmance would terminate this federal antifraud action while leaving independent criminal and state-law consequences intact.

**Plausible Holding Scope:** An integrated-scheme rule would cover a broker’s concealed sale of entrusted securities to generate proceeds he intends to steal, even absent a false statement about their value. A narrower rule would require more direct deception about the sale’s terms or the security, thereby excluding some fiduciary liquidation schemes. The existing simulated O’Hagan holding materially supports analysis of deceptive transactional use rather than a universal value-statement requirement. Neither route determines every conversion after a completed and genuinely authorized sale.

**Uncertainty:** A complaint can sufficiently allege the connection without every allegation being conclusively proven. The criminal judgment’s preclusive scope and the remaining civil elements require separate analysis.

## 82. United States v. Mackby +

# SECTION I: NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

* **Case Name:** United States v. Mackby +
* **Citation or Docket:** 261 F.3d 821 (9th Cir. 2001), amended opinion. No simulated Supreme Court docket or grant date is supplied.

* **Question Presented:** Whether civil penalties and treble damages imposed together under the False Claims Act for false Medicare claims constitute punishment subject to the Eighth Amendment’s Excessive Fines Clause?
* **Question Granted:** The supplied question governs this added merits matter. No historical Supreme Court grant is asserted.
* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The district court entered a False Claims Act judgment after a bench trial. The Ninth Circuit affirmed liability but remanded for an Excessive Fines inquiry because it held the monetary sanctions at least partly punitive. The constitutional coverage ruling is the question assigned for simulation.
* **Relief Requested:** Mackby seeks constitutional review of the monetary sanction; the United States seeks preservation of the liability judgment and lawful recovery.
* **Material Record:** Mackby, a nonphysician owner of a physical-therapy clinic, caused Medicare claims to be submitted using his physician father’s provider number although the father was not providing the represented supervision or affiliation. The clinic furnished services, but the claims’ representation of eligibility was false. The judgment totaled $729,454.92: $555,000 in statutory penalties for 111 claims, plus treble damages based on $58,151.64. The record concerns knowing use of the billing arrangement, not merely an innocent numerical mistake. Whether the aggregate sanction is grossly disproportionate requires an assessment separate from whether it has a punitive component.

It does not require redeciding the settled liability findings or choosing a constitutionally permissible dollar amount without the necessary proportionality analysis. ### Issue 1: Punishment and aggregate monetary sanctions

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** Austin recognizes that an exaction serving at least partly punitive purposes can fall within the Excessive Fines Clause notwithstanding a civil form. United States v. Bajakajian distinguishes coverage from excessiveness and applies gross disproportionality to the actual offense, culpability, and harm.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** Identify whether the sovereign extracts a payment at least partly as punishment, then separately examine proportionality. The legal constitutional assessment is reviewed independently, with appropriate respect for supported factual findings. Coverage does not decide Double Jeopardy classification or establish a constitutional cap by arithmetic alone.
* **Other Governing Law:** The False Claims Act authorizes civil penalties and multiplied damages for knowing false claims to the United States. The Eighth Amendment governs punitive monetary exactions even when Congress also pursues compensation and deterrence through civil enforcement.
* **Material Other Authority:** The district court’s liability and amount findings provide the factual starting point. The Ninth Circuit’s treatment of the combined sanctions is the ruling under review, not an effective Supreme Court holding in the simulation.
* **Existing Rule’s Reach:** The mixed remedial and punitive character of an exaction does not exclude it from constitutional review. The government need not forgo full compensation merely because another component punishes.
* **Open Point:** Whether this statutory combination is at least partly punitive and therefore requires the distinct proportionality inquiry.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

**Reasoning below.** The Ninth Circuit treated both the fixed penalties and the multiplied recovery as serving punitive purposes as well as compensation, and required further examination of excessiveness. It did not hold that the final dollar amount was necessarily unconstitutional.

**Mackby.** Fixed per-claim penalties and recovery exceeding actual loss punish culpable conduct. Because many similar claims can multiply the amount sharply, excluding the scheme from the Clause would allow a civil label to defeat a substantive constitutional protection. He seeks review of the aggregate demand under the established proportionality standard.

**United States.** The government may recover more than nominal overpayment to compensate investigative costs, fraud detection, delay, and injuries not readily measured. Statutory sanctions also secure the integrity of public reimbursement. Those functions support the amount, even if the Court concludes that the mixed scheme is within the Clause.

**Limits.** Genuine provision of treatment does not cure knowing false representations that make claims ineligible. Conversely, a finding of false claims does not answer how punitive extraction relates to the offense. The government’s compensatory explanations bear on the assessment but do not eliminate the punitive purpose of fixed penalties.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

Affirmance on coverage would preserve liability and require the lower court to evaluate the combined sanctions for gross disproportionality. Reversal would foreclose this constitutional review on the theory that the scheme is wholly remedial, leaving the statutory judgment in place. The record and existing simulated test make a categorical civil-label exclusion unavailable.

**Plausible Holding Scope:** A mixed-purpose rule can hold that the combination of civil penalties and treble damages is sufficiently punitive to trigger the Clause, without declaring every statutory multiplication punitive to precisely the same degree. The subsequent assessment can distinguish compensatory loss, enforcement costs, culpability, repeated claims, and the severity of the total extraction. It does not require a universal ratio between penalties and actual loss or decide every FCA award on this record.

**Uncertainty:** The measure of actual government injury and the relationship between the individual claims and the overall course of conduct may affect proportionality. The coverage question is answerable without supplying missing findings on those points.

**Vornado.** Compulsory patent claims cannot simply be omitted or separately sued upon without consequence, and uniform patent adjudication is an important congressional objective. Vornado therefore seeks a rule considering claims the defendant must litigate in the same action.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

**Reasoning below.** The lower courts viewed the permit regime, as narrowed, as a permissible means of protecting residents and identifying canvassers. They regarded the free permit and limited administrative process as reducing the burden.

**Speakers.** A religious minister or political advocate should not have to notify government and identify herself before approaching a willing listener. The rule prevents anonymous and spontaneous advocacy and reaches speakers who solicit no money. General crime and fraud interests do not explain licensing every peaceful advocate, especially when ordinary law can address misconduct.

**Village.** Identification helps residents and police detect fraud and investigate wrongdoing. Advance registration is modest, costs nothing, and does not authorize discrimination among messages. Residents have a strong privacy interest in their homes, and uniform rules are easier to administer than case-by-case distinctions.

**Limits.** The speakers’ claim does not remove an owner’s authority to refuse conversation or prohibit entry. The village’s public-safety purpose does not establish that every religious or political visitor presents the risk associated with commercial fraud. Reversal can invalidate the permit duty for noncommercial religious and political advocacy while leaving resident-controlled exclusion and otherwise valid fraud, trespass, and commercial regulation for their own applications. Affirmance would require a concrete justification for the full range of protected activity burdened, not just the ordinance’s neutral wording.

**Plausible Holding Scope:** A broad advance-registration requirement on ordinary noncommercial advocacy may fail because it unnecessarily compromises anonymity and spontaneity while poorly matching the asserted risks. That conclusion need not make all identification requirements unconstitutional or decide which narrowly drawn commercial-solicitation rules would be valid.

**Uncertainty:** The Court should not invent a pattern of fraudulent religious canvassing, a discretionary denial practice, or a substantial fee absent from the record. The defect alleged is the broad legal prerequisite itself and its actual consequences for protected speech.
