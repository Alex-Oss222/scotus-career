### Issue 1: Federal replacement authority and remedial limits

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** Growe v. Emison, February 23, 1993, gives timely state legislative and judicial redistricting priority while permitting federal action when the State will not provide a lawful plan in time. Voinovich v. Quilter, March 2, 1993, distinguishes a State's permissible districting choices from a federal court's authority to compel districts as relief for a proved violation.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** The federal court may remedy unconstitutional malapportionment without acquiring general authority to optimize partisan or racial outcomes. The remedy must comply with applicable population and voting-rights law. Judicial authorship does not eliminate statutory review.
* **Other Governing Law:** Article I, § 2 governs congressional representational equality; 2 U.S.C. § 2c requires single-member congressional districts in the applicable setting. Voting Rights Act § 2, then 42 U.S.C. § 1973, constrains the replacement arrangement. No claim requiring a separate amendment-history adjustment is identified.
* **Material Other Authority:** White v. Weiser, 412 U.S. 783 (1973), and Connor v. Finch, 431 U.S. 407, 414–426 (1977), distinguish judicial remedial responsibility from unrestricted legislative choice and require a legally justified map.
* **Existing Rule's Reach:** A timely lawful state plan receives priority; failure to produce one permits necessary federal relief. That authority does not decide whether each feature of the resulting plan complies with § 2.
* **Open Point:** No unresolved rule of federal replacement authority requires decision. Whether a preserved objection establishes a statutory defect in this plan is an application of the governing law, not a reason to reconsider state priority.

### Issue 2: Electoral capacity, unequal opportunity, and independent grounds

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** Valdespino v. Alamo Heights Independent School District, June 17, 1999, expressly modifies numerical exclusivity in the first Gingles condition for a single protected group. A lawful, geographically compact district may satisfy that condition through a numerical majority or through rigorous proof of dependable crossover support sufficient to elect the group's candidates of choice. Growe retains cohesion, relevant majority-bloc voting, and causation. Johnson v. De Grandy, June 30, 1994, requires independent totality, rejects maximization, and treats proportional opportunity as evidence rather than a conclusive defense.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** Electoral capacity means an actual ability to elect preferred representatives in the proposed lawful district, not merely an increased chance to influence others. Crossover support means support from voters outside the single protected group for that group's demonstrated preferred candidates. Dependability requires support grounded in relevant voting behavior and electoral conditions; it is not established by racial arithmetic, one unusual victory, or a prediction detached from the proposed electorate. A distinct-group coalition claim instead treats two or more protected groups as a combined claimant electorate; its cognizability remains reserved, and adding their populations cannot substitute for common cohesion.
* **Other Governing Law:** Section 2's results standard, enacted in its governing form in 1982, asks whether political processes are equally open under the totality; discriminatory purpose is not an additional statutory element. The statute disclaims a right to representation proportional to group population. Its enacted results inquiry remains distinct from constitutional intentional-discrimination claims.
* **Material Other Authority:** Thornburg v. Gingles, 478 U.S. 30, 50–51 (1986), supplies the causal conditions, subject to Valdespino's express modification. Nomination procedures matter where they are a decisive electoral barrier. Candidate race is not a substitute for voter preference.
* **Existing Rule's Reach:** Both capacity routes require lawful population and geography; compactness asks whether the relevant voters can form a geographically workable district, not whether a numerical target can be reached by any configuration. Political cohesion concerns a sufficiently common electoral preference demonstrated by voting behavior. Relevant majority-bloc voting concerns ordinary defeat of preferred candidates in the challenged arrangement, allowing for special circumstances. Independent totality means the further statutory judgment about unequal opportunity after the necessary conditions are addressed; no automatic liability follows from satisfying those conditions. Local deprivation cannot be excused by opportunity elsewhere.
* **Open Point:** The single-group functional-capacity route is settled. Preservation, support in this record, and the independence of compactness and totality findings present application and review questions. A universal population denominator, mathematical crossover formula, distinct-group entitlement, and influence-only entitlement remain unresolved; they are not necessary merely because the record mentions minority percentages.

* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Section 2 protects effective electoral opportunity rather than a favored racial percentage. “Effective opportunity” means a practical ability to elect the voters' demonstrated preferred candidates under the actual electoral structure; it is neither assured victory nor merely persuading an otherwise controlling electorate. “Dependable” requires support adequate to the actual causal prediction, not a mandatory number of studies, fixed winning percentage, or immutable coalition. “Compactness” requires a lawful and geographically workable alternative; broad statewide population totals cannot establish it. “Independent ground” means a finding sufficient under the correct law whose reasoning does not depend on the rejected majority-only premise.
