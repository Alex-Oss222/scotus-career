### Issue 1: A mandatory range has legal force despite a larger outer ceiling

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Angle’s sentence is already vacated for inadequate findings, so the governing rule must guide a new hearing. The broader constitutional application was expressly reserved in Apprendi v. New Jersey.
* **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether a present-offense fact that enlarges a binding punishment range receives jury and reasonable-doubt protection.
* **Stone’s Legal Position:** Extend Apprendi v. New Jersey to these binding Guidelines constraints and expressly overrule McMillan v. Pennsylvania insofar as it permits a present-offense fact found under ordinary sentencing procedures to raise a mandatory floor. Apprendi reserved these applications; this opinion would decide them rather than attribute them to that earlier holding.
* **Stone’s Proposed Holding:** A present-offense fact necessary to increase the legally binding ceiling or floor of punishment must be admitted with the required protections or found beyond a reasonable doubt by the constitutionally proper factfinder. A larger outer statutory ceiling does not make a mandatory inner range discretionary.
* **Reach Within the Question:** Ordinary judicial assessment may select a sentence within the range independently authorized by the verdict or valid admissions. The rule addresses binding legal consequences, not every factual consideration affecting the judge’s choice.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The constitutional division turns on who establishes the facts that change legal punishment authority. A mandatory guideline that forbids a lower range or authorizes a higher ceiling because of an additional fact performs that work even though Congress also supplies an outer limit. The best contrary argument invokes the traditional judicial sentencing role. That role remains where the verdict already permits the choice; it does not justify treating a legally binding change as mere advice. The Commission’s institutional validity and the existence of limited departures do not by themselves make the operative range nonbinding. McMillan v. Pennsylvania draws a formal maximum/minimum distinction that cannot account for the legal loss of sentencing freedom caused by a mandatory floor. Stone would expressly reject that distinction to this extent, while preserving judicial assessment within punishment already authorized by the verdict or valid admissions. The constitutional allocation of factfinding is decisive; the functional operation of the Guidelines identifies when that allocation is implicated. This combines a structural jury rule with examination of legal effect, not a balance of efficiency against the jury. A rule reaching the binding floor as well as ceiling is necessary here because Angle’s judicial quantity finding compelled the bottom of his 210-to-262-month range, even though his actual sentence remained below the 240-month statutory ceiling.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Prior convictions, a general advisory-system remedy, and consecutive sentencing that does not depend on the disputed binding-range premise are not decided.

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Phifer’s claim is forfeited and requires all applicable Rule 52(b) conditions; the count excess is distinct from the newly resolved Guidelines question.
* **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether possible or assumed mandatory stacking automatically defeats prejudice from an excessive count sentence.

* **Stone’s Proposed Holding:** Phifer’s 292-month drug-count term is erroneous and plainly exceeds the 240 months authorized by his quantity-free verdict. The lower court’s categorical no-prejudice conclusion fails because the assumed mandatory 292-month aggregate depends on the same judicial quantity findings challenged here. A demonstrated lawful inevitable aggregate can be relevant under Olano; unused aggregate statutory exposure by itself is not that demonstration. The remaining substantial-rights and discretionary-correction questions require the actual evidence and lawful range, not the invalid premise.
* **Reach Within the Question:** The holding rejects the categorical rationale and requires actual review. It does not declare every count error structural, eliminate the defendant’s ordinary prejudice burden, or guarantee a lower aggregate after lawful resentencing.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The imposed judgment contains a 292-month drug term; the hypothetical judgment contains different terms, a different concurrent-or-consecutive structure, and a supposedly mandatory total derived from disputed judicial quantity findings. Those are not interchangeable without analysis. Since the range premise must be reconsidered under the constitutional rule, the appellate inevitability conclusion cannot support affirmance. The lower court must address the actual Rule 52(b) conditions and any properly supported alternative, not presume either automatic correction or automatic harmlessness. The first two Rule 52(b) conditions are thus resolved for the count excess.
