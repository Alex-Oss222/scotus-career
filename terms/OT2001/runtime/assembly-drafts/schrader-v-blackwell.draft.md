## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: The state must justify the information it withholds

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** The recurring statutory restriction affects the party and its candidates in elections too short for complete review; the record supports the capable-of-repetition exception applied below. Reach the ballot-label question without ordering a new mootness inquiry merely because the election ended.
* **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether full party recognition can be the categorical prerequisite for any truthful affiliation label.
* **Stone’s Legal Position:** Apply the governing burden-and-fit framework in a new label-specific setting. Do not treat Timmons as deciding a materially different ballot burden.
* **Stone’s Proposed Holding:** Where a candidate independently qualifies for the ballot and establishes a genuine party affiliation, a state must justify withholding all accurate affiliation information by the actual burden and its demonstrated ballot interests. It may distinguish formal recognized-party nomination from affiliation through a neutral designation. Full party recognition is not, by itself, a sufficient explanation for prohibiting every truthful label.
* **Reach Within the Question:** The rule does not compel misleading or unverifiable labels, eliminate party-recognition thresholds, or confer other recognized-party privileges.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Ohio has a legitimate interest in making the ballot’s official representations accurate. That interest can support reserving a formal nomination label, but the record must explain why no truthful affiliation format would serve it. The candidate has already met the access requirement, so a separate party-wide threshold needs its own connection to the information withheld. Timmons’s recognition of alternate channels of association is relevant to burden but not dispositive. The remand should resolve actual affiliation, wording, and administrative fit rather than assume the only choices are complete party recognition or silence. Anderson–Burdick supplies the burden-sensitive standard; no racial tier or newly created right to the state’s endorsement is necessary. Timmons is distinguished because a ban on fusion nomination does not decide whether a qualified candidate can convey truthful affiliation in a format that distinguishes nomination from association. The rule’s practical operation and the State’s actual accuracy interests carry decisive weight within that received standard. A neutral verification requirement may protect accuracy; an unexplained demand for full party status does not answer the distinct labeling burden.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The exact permissible label and any genuinely disputed verification facts remain for remedial application. Full party recognition and retrospective alteration of the completed election are outside the requested relief.

## Public-content draft awaiting topology and assignment

### Event

Schrader v. Blackwell. Merits decision, December 10, 2001. Review of the Sixth Circuit judgment reported at 241 F.3d 783, reversing relief for the requested Libertarian ballot designation. Schrader qualified for the 1998 congressional ballot through independent nomination; the party had actually nominated him but lacked state recognition. A preliminary injunction permitted the label for that election. The party later regained recognition. The question is whether the First and Fourteenth Amendments permit Ohio to condition the official party designation on recognized-party status. The continuing party plaintiff, recurring recognition requirement and short election cycle keep the dispute reviewable; the earlier filing-deadline objection is outside review.

### Judgment & Remedy

**Vacated and remanded, 5–4.** Supporting: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Opposing: O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. The Sixth Circuit's judgment is vacated at the reviewed ballot-designation scope, and the case is remanded for determination of the actual affiliation, truthful wording, verification and administrative fit, with appropriately bounded prospective relief. No particular label is ordered, no recognized-party privilege is conferred and the completed 1998 election is not reopened. The four dissenters would affirm.

### Holdings

### The recurring controversy remains reviewable

**Holding and operative rule:** Completion of the election and the party's later recognition do not end this challenge to a recognition requirement capable of recurring against the continuing party plaintiff in elections too short for full review. The Court reaches the ballot-designation dispute under the capable-of-repetition exception.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join this threshold proposition in the Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** The ongoing party is subject to a periodic recognition requirement and has previously lost and regained recognition. The same challenged designation restriction can therefore recur during an election cycle that ends before full judicial review. Review does not require a new inquiry merely because the 1998 ballot is complete. The earlier filing-deadline objection is outside the accepted issue.

**Precedent treatment:** Timmons's preservation of review in a continuing nomination dispute is applied consistently with its distinct conditional-enactment facts.

### Ohio must justify listing a verified party nominee as an independent

**Holding and operative rule:** Under Anderson-Burdick, where Ohio's ballot prints party designations, a candidate who qualified by petition and was genuinely nominated by a party may not be forced to appear as an independent without a substantial justification grounded in the actual burden and the State's demonstrated ballot interests; full party recognition is not, by itself, that justification. Ohio may verify the affiliation, distinguish recognized parties from candidate-specific nominating associations and choose truthful wording, and it need not print a label that falsely implies recognized-party nomination. The exact label is left to the remedy stage.

**Authority:** The Opinion of the Court on the merits, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; five direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a verified party nomination and the formal status of a recognized party are different facts, and Ohio's ballot may mark that difference without suppressing the first. Schrader qualified by petition with the required district signatures, and the Libertarian Party actually nominated him at its convention. Where the ballot prints party designations for other candidates, listing him as "Independent" or with no affiliation tells voters that no party chose him. Independent access and campaigning reduce that burden; they do not make it disappear, because the ballot is the one communication every voter sees at the moment of choice. The burden is less than severe, but Anderson and Burdick require a substantial justification, measured by the State's precise interests and their actual connection to the information withheld.

Ohio's interests in an accurate ballot and a coherent recognition system are legitimate. They support verification of a claimed affiliation and can support reserving the formal designation of a recognized party's nominee. They do not explain why no truthful wording separating recognition from affiliation would serve them. Full party recognition answers a different question: whether the party receives recognized-party privileges statewide. Support sufficient to place this candidate on the ballot does not establish that status, and the remedy does not confer it. Timmons requires meaningful official recognition of a qualified party's genuine nomination; the Libertarian Party then lacked recognition, so Timmons does not decide this case, but its treatment of a genuine nomination as central association, not replaceable by endorsement, informs the burden. Norman, Tashjian and Eu give weight to that real association. Burdick's alternative-route holding concerns access, not ballot labels.

The rule is limited. Ohio may require verification, distinguish recognized parties from candidate-specific nominating associations, choose truthful wording and refuse any label that falsely implies recognized-party nomination. A demonstrated administrative or effective-governing interest connected to the restriction remains available. No duplicate listing, vote credit, recognized-party privilege or particular label follows, and the completed 1998 election is not reopened. The exact wording and any disputed verification facts are for the remedy stage.

**Precedent treatment:** Anderson and Burdick supply the calibrated framework, and Burdick's alternative-route holding is not transferred to labels; Timmons's recognition rule for a qualified party's genuine nomination is distinguished at its scope while its associational reasoning informs this informational burden; Norman, Tashjian and Eu support the weight given to the verified association.

### Precedent Treatment

- Anderson v. Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780, and Burdick v. Takushi, 504 U.S. 428: the actual burden, precise interests and proportional fit remain controlling; Burdick's alternative-route holding concerns access and is not transferred to labels.
- Timmons v. Twin Cities Area New Party, No. 95-1608: its rule requiring meaningful official recognition of a qualified party's genuine nomination is distinguished because the party here lacked recognition; its treatment of a genuine nomination as central association informs the burden.
- Norman v. Reed, 502 U.S. 279: its protection of authorized party identity and independently established support supports the weight given to the verified nomination; party-recognition thresholds remain.
- Tashjian v. Republican Party of Connecticut, 479 U.S. 208, and Eu v. San Francisco County Democratic Central Committee, 489 U.S. 214: associational protection supports a truthful designation, without compelling every requested ballot format.

### Law After Decision

Where a ballot prints party designations, a State must justify forcing a verified, petition-qualified nominee of an actual party to appear as an independent by the actual burden and its demonstrated ballot interests under Anderson's calibrated inquiry; full party recognition is not, by itself, sufficient. The State may verify affiliation, distinguish recognized parties from candidate-specific nominating associations, choose truthful wording and bar labels that falsely imply recognized-party nomination. Party-recognition thresholds and recognized-party privileges are unaffected, and the exact label is left to the remedy stage.

### Separate Writings

The common dissent, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, would affirm. Support sufficient to place a congressional candidate on the ballot does not itself establish the separate statewide party status represented by Ohio's official nomination cue. Anderson and Burdick require attention to the actual burden and the fit of the State's interests, and that burden is less than severe: the candidate remained on the ballot, and the party and candidate could endorse and campaign. Coherent nomination categories and reasonable preventive administration proportionately justify tying the official designation to the neutral recognition system without proof of prior disruption; a feasible alternative wording does not oblige the State to redesign its official designation. Timmons protects a qualified party's genuine nomination, and the Libertarian Party had not satisfied Ohio's recognition requirements; Burdick concerned an effective route to the ballot, which this candidate used. Norman protected authorized party expansion and independently qualified candidates from disqualification, not every official ballot format. The dissent adopts neither pure rational-basis review nor a presumption validating every affiliation prohibition, and would leave more restrictive access systems and unsupported burdens to the governing calibrated inquiry.
