### Issue 1: Potential power does not itself displace exercised authority

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Reach statutory coverage and displacement; preserve remaining factual and penalty issues.
* **Question Stone Resolves:** What exercise by another federal agency triggers the exclusion.
* **Stone’s Legal Position:** Adopt a new application of the statute’s actual-exercise language.
* **Stone’s Proposed Holding:** The other-agency exclusion requires that another federal agency actually exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards affecting the working conditions at issue. Possession of potential authority over an uninspected vessel, or investigation after a casualty, does not by itself exclude OSHA. A barge within the Act’s geographic and employment coverage may be a workplace; any displacement extends only as the actual governing regulation supports.
* **Reach Within the Question:** The rule does not require duplicative enforcement where Coast Guard standards genuinely cover the conditions, and does not equate uninspected status with absence of all Coast Guard regulation.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The statutory choice of exercise matters. Replacing it with potential jurisdiction would create regulatory gaps whenever an agency could regulate but has not done so. Mallard Bay’s coordination concern is legitimate where real standards overlap, but cannot supply regulations absent from the record. The relevant contrast is therefore between the working conditions actually covered and those merely within theoretical power. The explosion’s gravity does not expand OSHA’s authority; the statute and actual regulatory coverage do. The actual-exercise language is decisive and creates a statutory boundary, not a free-floating balance of agency expertise. Stone distinguishes comprehensive Coast Guard regulation of inspected vessels from the limited regulation of this uninspected barge, and resolves the alternative workplace objection from §4(a)’s geographic coverage. Mead’s delegation threshold cannot confer displacement merely because an agency possesses expertise. The rule governs overlapping authority at the relevant working-condition level; individual citation and penalty issues need not be decided to restore the lawful enforcement channel.

### Event

Chao v. Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc., No. 00-927. Merits decision, January 9, 2002. Argued October 31, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Fifth Circuit. An explosion on an uninspected drilling barge in Louisiana waters killed four workers and injured two. OSHA cited delayed evacuation, absence of an emergency-response plan and inadequate training. The Coast Guard investigated but had no governing regulations for the cited evacuation and emergency-response conditions. The administrative law judge sustained the citation and Commission review was declined; the employer challenged statutory coverage and displacement, rather than citation merits. The Fifth Circuit displaced OSHA on a broad Coast Guard-jurisdiction theory without deciding the independent workplace-in-a-State objection. Both statutory questions are before the Court.

### Holdings

### Other-agency displacement requires actual exercise over the relevant working conditions

**Holding and operative rule:** Under Occupational Safety and Health Act §4(b)(1), another federal agency displaces OSHA only by actually exercising statutory authority to prescribe or enforce occupational-safety or health standards affecting the relevant working conditions. Pertinent specific regulation or actual comprehensive regulation of the relevant category can suffice without word-for-word duplication; potential authority, unrelated vessel regulation or casualty investigation alone cannot establish whole-vessel or industry-wide displacement.

**Authority:** The unanimous Opinion of the Court; all nine Justices directly join the rule, application and limits.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress made exercise of power, rather than its mere possession, decisive. The Coast Guard had not prescribed pertinent standards or comprehensively regulated the evacuation and emergency-response working conditions OSHA cited on this uninspected inland barge. Its general maritime responsibilities and investigation cannot supply that missing regulatory exercise.

Mallard Bay's coordination concern has force where actual regulatory regimes overlap. It does not justify leaving working conditions outside OSHA solely because another agency could have regulated them. The statute already accommodates genuine comprehensive regulation; the Court need not demand identical wording in the two agencies' rules. Conversely, regulation of unrelated vessel matters does not establish comprehensive occupational-safety control.

The result does not depend on choosing between hazard-based and area-based descriptions of working conditions: neither pertinent nor comprehensive actual regulation is established under either description here. Uninspected status is not itself proof of no Coast Guard regulation, and the holding does not allocate every maritime setting to OSHA. Statutory text resolves the dispute without a new deference holding or an inference of power from the accident's gravity.

**Precedent treatment:** The statutory-boundary principle is consistent with Mead's distinction between possession of authority and legally relevant exercise; Mead supplies no displacement merely from agency expertise.

### Employment on this Louisiana barge is within the Act's workplace coverage

**Holding and operative rule:** Employment aboard this barge within Louisiana is employment in a workplace in a State under §4(a). Anchoring in navigable waters does not remove that workplace from the Act's geographic coverage; the independent other-agency exclusion remains governed by §4(b)(1)'s actual-exercise requirement.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join this independent holding in the Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** The Act's geographic and employment terms reach this Louisiana workplace. No general maritime exception appears in §4(a). Treating the Coast Guard's potential authority as a geographic exclusion would merge two distinct provisions and bypass §4(b)(1)'s condition. The Court therefore rejects the alternative workplace objection as well as the displacement theory. The receiving court must implement those holdings in the actual administrative posture, with the unchallenged citation merits retaining their status.

**Precedent treatment:** The decision construes the two statutory provisions independently; it creates no new maritime exclusivity or deference rule.

### Precedent Treatment

- United States v. Mead Corp.: the distinction between statutory authority and its legally relevant exercise remains; agency expertise alone supplies no displacement. The present holdings rest on §§4(a) and 4(b)(1), not a new rule of interpretive deference.

### Law After Decision

OSHA retains authority over these working conditions because Coast Guard potential authority was not exercised through pertinent standards or comprehensive regulation. Work on a barge in Louisiana falls within §4(a), subject independently to any displacement actually established under §4(b)(1). Actual overlap, rather than vessel status alone, governs other settings.
