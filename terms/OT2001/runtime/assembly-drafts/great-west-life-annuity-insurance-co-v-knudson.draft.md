## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: An equitable remedy requires its own legal and factual basis

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Reach the statutory remedy; do not decide an unnecessary broader reimbursement entitlement or absent-party claim.
* **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether the present demand is a traditional equitable recovery or a personal debt action.
* **Stone’s Legal Position:** Apply Mertens and Harris Trust while proposing a specific clarification for this reimbursement posture.
* **Stone’s Proposed Holding:** A contractual reimbursement clause does not alone make a demand for personal payment appropriate equitable relief. A claimant invoking a constructive trust or equitable lien must identify the property and defendant connection required by that traditional remedy, subject to its defenses. When the present claim seeks satisfaction of a debt from the named defendants rather than recovery of qualifying property held by them, the equitable-relief provision does not authorize it.
* **Reach Within the Question:** The holding distinguishes remedies rather than categorically excluding monetary equity. It does not foreclose an otherwise valid claim against an appropriate recipient merely because the property originated in a settlement.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Mertens makes the statutory category meaningful, while Harris Trust shows that genuine equitable recovery can reach a nonfiduciary recipient. Those propositions coexist only if the remedy’s actual prerequisites matter. Here the beneficiaries’ asserted contractual debt cannot substitute for identifying the property and holder against whom equity operates. The settlement’s distribution therefore matters to relief, not merely to collectability. A court must not cure a mismatch by treating a trust or attorney as if already before it. Traditional remedy classification carries decisive weight because Mertens gives equitable relief that statutory meaning. Stone reaffirms that construction despite his earlier disagreement; neither a changed vote nor a preference for reimbursement supplies a reason to reopen it. Harris Trust is reconciled through identifiable property, a proper recipient, and applicable equitable defenses. The named defendants’ lack of the asserted fund matters to the requested remedy, not to sympathy for either party. This remedy-specific disposition makes a broader ruling on all ERISA reimbursement clauses unnecessary.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Claims against absent recipients, any new tracing theory, and unnecessary contract interpretation remain undecided.

### Actual disagreement and propositions

O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas affirm the judgment rejecting this federal remedial demand. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer would vacate and remand the remedial rejection so that the asserted restitution claim is considered without the rejected categorical bar. All eight reach the question, preserve the distinction between the actual defendants and other holders, and retain ordinary proof and defenses. No final reimbursement entitlement or amount is found.

The affirming four jointly adopt these propositions:

1. Section 502(a)(3) requires an appropriate traditionally equitable remedy. A demand for personal payment of a past-due contractual debt does not become equitable merely because the complaint requests an injunction, specific performance or restitution. An injunction remains subject to equity's remedial limits. The ordinary rule does not deny the distinct exceptional uses of specific performance to prevent otherwise inadequately remediable future losses; none supplies this demand for an accrued sum.
2. Restitution includes legal and equitable forms. A constructive trust or equitable lien ordinarily restores specifically identifiable property or proceeds to which the claimant asserts an equitable entitlement from the defendant who holds them; ordinary liability for a benefit conferred is different. This case concerns the latter demand because the settlement property was paid to the separate trust and attorney, rather than held by the named beneficiaries in the manner the proposed property remedy requires. This proposition is not a universal tracing formula for every equitable remedy. An accounting for profits has distinct predicates and is not this claim.
3. The trust-beneficiary analogy does not itself supply personal collection from other assets. A traditional charge against a beneficiary's interest in a trust is distinct from the personal money judgment sought here. Mertens prevents treating the whole former remedial jurisdiction of trust courts as statutory authorization.
4. The specific federal claim therefore fails. The alternative construction of the actual plan terms need not be decided to sustain this judgment. A different claim against another property holder requires its own preserved pleading, jurisdiction, process and equitable predicates; none is adjudicated here.

The remanding four jointly adopt a different sufficient ground: the asserted recovery measured by the beneficiary's gain, for restoration of benefits out of a third-party recovery, should be treated as the traditionally recognized restitution category for purposes of allowing this claim to proceed. Mertens's requirement that the category be typically available in equity does not mean that the category must have been exclusive to equity, and it does not make lack of direct possession dispositive of every restorative theory. This clears the categorical statutory-remedy objection; it does not establish actual enrichment, ultimate plan entitlement, amount or authority over a nonparty's property. The lower court must then address the plan-construction ground and necessary remedial predicates. The majority's distinction is not answered merely by calling all money equitable; the remanding group relies on the alleged gain-restoration character, unlike Mertens's loss compensation.

The government's trust-beneficiary/advance argument supports that restorative characterization but cannot itself create a new property fact or direct a remedy against a person absent from the case. The remanding group need not find that the Knudsons directly possess the settlement fund; doing so would contradict the supplied record. Their disagreement is the statutory significance of that fact, not the fact's existence.

### Event

Great-West Life & Annuity Insurance Co. v. Knudson, No. 99-1786. Merits decision, January 8, 2002. Argued October 1, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, which affirmed rejection of the plan's reimbursement claim. The question is whether this demand for repayment of medical benefits after a tort settlement is appropriate equitable relief under ERISA §502(a)(3). The settlement property was distributed to a special-needs trust and an attorney rather than held by the named beneficiaries in the manner required by the asserted property remedy. The claim seeks personal payment from those beneficiaries; the plan's contractual entitlement and the available federal remedy are distinct questions.

### Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 5–4.** Supporting: Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. Opposing: Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. The particular reimbursement demand is unavailable under §502(a)(3), and its dismissal stands. The four dissenters would vacate the statutory-remedy rejection and remand for plan construction and the necessary restitution predicates.

### Holdings

### A contractual payment obligation does not establish an equitable remedy

**Holding and operative rule:** ERISA §502(a)(3) authorizes appropriate traditionally equitable relief, not personal payment of a past-due contractual debt merely because a claimant calls its demand an injunction, specific performance or restitution. A constructive trust or equitable lien ordinarily requires specifically identifiable property or proceeds, an equitable entitlement and the required connection to the defendant holding that property, subject to applicable defenses.

**Authority:** The Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; five direct joins establish the entire rule and its application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the remedy's actual predicates, not its label, determine whether it is equitable. Mertens gives the statutory limitation independent force by distinguishing traditional equitable categories from all relief once obtainable in a trust court. An injunction to pay an accrued debt does not avoid that distinction. Equity's exceptional use of specific performance to prevent otherwise inadequately remediable future losses does not supply this demand for an accrued sum.

Restitution has legal and equitable forms. The property-based form ordinarily restores identifiable property or proceeds from the defendant who holds them; an obligation to repay a benefit from other assets is different. Here settlement property went to the separate trust and attorney. The named beneficiaries' asserted contractual debt cannot substitute for the connection required by the remedy invoked against them. A charge against a beneficiary's trust interest also differs from the personal money judgment sought here. This is not a universal tracing formula for every equitable remedy; an accounting for profits has distinct predicates and is not this claim.

The contrary gain-restoration argument correctly recognizes that some restitution is equitable. It does not establish that this particular demand satisfies the traditional remedy's conditions. Harris Trust confirms genuine recipient equity within its own predicates and defenses, while Varity preserves appropriate individual relief within statutory categories. Neither turns every omitted remedy into authorized equity. The statutory ground sustains dismissal without deciding the alternative interpretation of the plan terms.

**Precedent treatment:** Mertens's traditional-category construction governs; Harris Trust's recipient equity is preserved rather than converted to personal collection; Varity retains its individual-equity channel and ordinary adequacy limits.

### Precedent Treatment

- Mertens v. Hewitt Associates, 508 U.S. 248: its traditional-equitable-category construction controls; neither its compensation exclusion nor preserved genuine monetary equity is displaced.
- Harris Trust and Savings Bank v. Salomon Smith Barney Inc., 530 U.S. 238: equitable recipient relief remains available on its actual predicates and defenses; it supplies neither this missing property connection nor a universal tracing formula.
- Varity Corp. v. Howe, 516 U.S. 489: appropriate individual equity remains within statutory categories and ordinary adequate-remedy limits.

### Law After Decision

Section 502(a)(3) does not authorize this demand for personal payment merely from the reimbursement clause. Genuine property-based equity remains available when its defendant, property and defense requirements are met. A different claim against another holder requires its own proper pleading, jurisdiction, process and equitable basis; the present judgment decides no such claim.

### Separate Writings

The common dissent, joined by Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, would recognize the asserted return of benefits from a third-party recovery as the traditionally equitable restitution category. The claim is measured by alleged gain rather than the plan-loss compensation rejected in Mertens. A category need have been typically available in equity, not exclusive to equity, and direct possession should not control every restorative theory. Varity supports giving the redress provision practical effect within its limits, while Harris Trust recognizes that appropriate restoration can reach beyond fiduciaries. The government's trust-beneficiary analogy supports that characterization without establishing new property facts. The dissent would remove the categorical remedy bar and return plan interpretation and the actual remedial predicates for adjudication. It would award no reimbursement amount and would not adjudicate absent recipients' property.

### Procedure After Action

The dismissal of the reviewed federal reimbursement claim stands. An independently available claim against an appropriate holder remains subject to ordinary procedural rules; this judgment orders neither amendment nor joinder of the trust and decides no absent recipient's property rights.
