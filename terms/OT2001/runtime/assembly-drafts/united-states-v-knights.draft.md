## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: An objectively justified search does not fail merely because it investigates crime

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Reach this search’s reasonableness, with reasonable suspicion and the express condition as necessary premises.
* **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether ordinary investigative purpose defeats an otherwise justified probation search.
* **Stone’s Legal Position:** Apply the governing reasonableness and independent-review principles in a new probation-search combination.
* **Stone’s Proposed Holding:** A search of a probationer’s residence within a known, lawful search condition is reasonable when supported by particularized reasonable suspicion of criminal activity under the circumstances presented. Its ordinary criminal-investigative purpose does not independently invalidate it. Probation and notice diminish relevant expectations without extinguishing Fourth Amendment protection or establishing blanket voluntary consent.
* **Reach Within the Question:** The holding requires the combination present here. It does not authorize suspicionless residential searches or all conditions a sentencing court might impose.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The lower court’s purpose restriction treats a justified investigation as disqualifying even though the condition expressly contemplates law-enforcement officers and objective grounds connect the residence to crime. The constitutional inquiry should examine the actual intrusion and justification. The supervision relationship is relevant to the privacy balance, while individualized suspicion prevents the condition from operating as an unrestricted license. Scott remains meaningful because an unconstitutional search does not become immune from exclusion merely through the officer’s supervisory status. This is a reasonableness balance under the Fourth Amendment, not an inference of blanket contractual consent. The condition, notice, probation relationship, residential intrusion, and individualized suspicion do distinct work; the objectively supported suspicion and limited search authority are decisive in combination. Stone distinguishes Griffin’s special-needs justification without treating it as the exclusive means of sustaining a supervision search, and reaffirms Scott’s exclusion rule for an unconstitutional search. The present combination establishes a defined sufficient ground, not a ruling on suspicionless searches or unbounded conditions.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Suspicionless searches, blanket consent, and unpresented conditions are reserved.

### Result, proposition and remedy

Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer vote to reverse the purpose-based suppression judgment and remand. All eight join this precise rule: the warrantless residence search of this probationer is reasonable because he was subject to the known express search condition, the officer had individualized reasonable suspicion of criminal activity during probation, and the search fell within the condition's lawful scope. The combination sufficiently reduces the protected privacy interest and supplies a justification for dispensing with probable cause and a warrant. An ordinary criminal-investigation purpose does not independently defeat that justification. The opinion does not rest on complete consent or forfeiture of all Fourth Amendment protection.

Griffin supports the lawful supervisory relationship and diminished privacy, while leaving other constitutional justifications possible. Whren supports objective assessment after authority for the particular intrusion has been established; it does not itself supply residential search authority. Edmond and Ferguson preserve their purpose inquiry in the actual suspicionless and special-needs settings. Ornelas governs review of ultimate suspicion under its factual/legal allocation. Scott addresses exclusion after an assumed unconstitutional supervisory search, so its different remedy rule remains fully operative: there is no established violation to which that rule must attach here.

The strongest contrary contention is that Griffin permits only a special-needs supervisory search and that a residence otherwise requires probable cause and a warrant. It fails because Griffin did not make its permitted rationale exclusive and because the actual condition and individualized suspicion materially change this search's justification. The Court's rule cannot be reduced to reasonable suspicion alone or to the search condition alone. Whether a condition permits a suspicionless search or establishes full consent is genuinely outside the necessary question, because individualized suspicion supplies the sufficient ground.

### Event

United States v. Knights, No. 00-1260. Merits decision, December 10, 2001. Argued November 6, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, which affirmed suppression because the residence search pursued ordinary criminal investigation. The question is whether a known probation search condition and individualized reasonable suspicion justify the search notwithstanding that purpose. The written condition permitted searches by probation or law-enforcement officers, and the detective had reasonable suspicion connecting the residence to criminal activity.

### Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.** Supporting: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. The categorical investigative-purpose ground for suppression is rejected. The lower courts apply the condition-and-reasonable-suspicion rule; evidence is admissible if no independently preserved defect remains. Any genuinely open scope or factual issue must be resolved on its own record.

### Holdings

### The known search condition and individualized suspicion justify this probation search

**Holding and operative rule:** A warrantless search of a probationer's residence within a known, lawful search condition is reasonable when supported by particularized reasonable suspicion of criminal activity in the circumstances presented. Ordinary criminal-investigative purpose does not independently invalidate that combination; neither the condition alone nor reasonable suspicion alone supplies this holding.

**Authority:** The unanimous Opinion of the Court; all nine Justices directly adopt this rule, its justification, and its limits.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the probation relationship and notice of the express condition reduce the privacy interest implicated by this residential search, while individualized suspicion supplies an objective justification for this intrusion. Griffin establishes that lawful probation supervision changes the constitutional setting; it does not make its special-needs rationale the exclusive ground for every probation search. Knights's proposed purpose restriction would invalidate a search supported by this combined justification merely because the officer investigates crime. The Fourth Amendment does not require that result here.

Whren supports examining the objective justification after authority for the intrusion has been established. Its ordinary probable-cause traffic rule does not itself confer residential search power or decide motive doctrine for every search based on reasonable suspicion. Edmond and Ferguson retain their purpose inquiries in their suspicionless and special-needs settings. Scott governs the consequence of an unconstitutional supervisory search, not the antecedent validity of this condition-and-suspicion search; its exclusion rule remains intact. Any ultimate suspicion question properly remaining is independently reviewed under Ornelas, with its protection for historical findings and due weight to grounded contextual inferences.

The holding rests on reasonableness, not complete contractual consent or forfeiture of Fourth Amendment protection. Suspicionless residential searches and blanket consent are outside the question necessarily resolved because individualized suspicion supplies the sufficient ground here.

**Precedent treatment:** Griffin's supervisory-relationship principle is applied without making its rationale exclusive; Whren's objective method is applied within its source-specific limits; Edmond and Ferguson are distinguished; Scott's exclusion rule and Ornelas's review allocation remain operative.

### Precedent Treatment

- Griffin v. Wisconsin, 483 U.S. 868: the lawful supervisory relationship informs diminished privacy; special needs is not the exclusive possible justification.
- Whren v. United States, 517 U.S. 806: objective assessment follows a demonstrated source of search authority; its traffic holding is not generally extended to every reasonable-suspicion search.
- City of Indianapolis v. Edmond, 531 U.S. 32, and Ferguson v. City of Charleston, 532 U.S. 67: purpose remains material in their distinct suspicionless and special-needs settings.
- Pennsylvania Board of Probation & Parole v. Scott, 524 U.S. 357: exclusion for the institution's unconstitutional supervisory search remains unchanged; this decision finds the bounded search reasonable.
- Ornelas v. United States, 517 U.S. 690: the division between factual and ultimate legal review is applied unchanged.

### Law After Decision

The Fourth Amendment permits the defined probation-condition and individualized-suspicion combination despite ordinary investigative purpose. Existing scope, execution and exclusion rules retain their separate operation; the decision creates no general suspicionless-search or consent-waiver rule.
