### Issue 1: Judicial inquiry and conflict-related prejudice

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** Williams v. * **Existing Rule's Reach:** Existing law supplies ordinary prejudice and established exceptions. The supplied simulation has not adopted a new automatic-reversal rule whenever a judge should have asked about a potential conflict, nor eliminated the need to distinguish potential division from an actual effect on representation.
* **Open Point:** Whether a known potential conflict and failure to inquire alone remove the requirement to establish an adverse effect, including where the conflict arises from representation of the deceased victim.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

The circuit required adverse effect despite the assumed inquiry failure. Mickens argues that the court’s own appointment and neglected inquiry place the case within a protective exception and that continuing duties to the victim compromised loyalty. The warden, supported by the United States, argues that a potential conflict and procedural failure do not prove deficient representation or trigger automatic relief. The warden must acknowledge the real continuing duties arising from the prior representation; Mickens must identify why the specific inquiry failure warrants a remedy independent of its effect on assistance.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

**Plausible Holding Scope, Judgment Consequences, and Uncertainty:** Affirmance could hold that inquiry failure alone does not dispense with adverse effect, leaving the existing factual determination dispositive within the granted scope. Reversal could recognize a properly defined automatic rule for this judicially created conflict or require a renewed hearing under a different burden. A limited remand might address whether the earlier hearing used the correct adverse-effect inquiry. Broad conclusions about all successive representations or all ethics violations are unnecessary. A new constitutional rule on collateral review would also require attention to applicable nonretroactivity limits; a procedural breach alone is not a writ.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

**Reasoning below.** The Eighth Circuit distinguished a serious distribution offense from the vastly larger drug quantity in Harmelin and distinguished genuine parole eligibility from possible clemency. It found an inference of gross disproportionality supported by comparisons with other Arkansas offenses and punishment elsewhere.

**State’s position.** Drug dealing is a serious public wrong even in small units; legislatures need not calibrate punishment in direct proportion to weight. Judicial comparison can become unauthorized resentencing, and the Constitution does not guarantee a first offender probation or a short term. The State requests a highly deferential threshold that leaves this sentence intact. It also argues that Teague’s restriction on new law requires federal acceptance of reasonable state interpretations of federal law even in this pre-AEDPA proceeding. That review-standard argument should not be recast as a separately specified new-rule defense that the published record does not describe.

**Henderson’s position.** Permanent imprisonment for this first conviction is qualitatively different from a severe finite sentence. The State cannot justify it by attributing all harms of the drug trade to this offender. His rule permits exceptional relief without judicially fixing an ordinary sentencing schedule.

**Limits and alternatives.** The challenge does not deny the State power to imprison drug sellers. A sentence-specific constitutional conclusion need not invalidate the underlying penalty range for all defendants or reopen the conviction.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

A threshold rejection based on amended §2254(d) deference would apply the wrong temporal regime. Rejecting the State’s Teague-based demand for general legal deference does not eliminate the separate nonretroactivity inquiry: relief still must rest on a dictated application of finality-era law or an applicable exception. On the merits, reversal would restore the denial of habeas relief and the life sentence. Affirmance would preserve a conditional sentencing remedy, giving Arkansas an opportunity to impose a lawful sentence before federal relief becomes operative.

**Plausible Holding Scope:** One path treats this life sentence as an exceptional grossly disproportionate punishment because of the combination of first-offender status, the small completed sale, and no ordinary parole. It would govern materially similar sentences without creating a numerical drug-weight ceiling. The competing path concludes that legislative judgments about distribution keep even this sentence within constitutional bounds. A remand for missing comparative or criminal-history findings would be appropriate only if the actual record is inadequate, not merely because proportionality is contestable.

**Uncertainty:** The packet does not supply a numerical maximum constitutional term. The record must not be augmented with assumed violence, recidivism, or future clemency.
