## 33. Swierkiewicz v. Sorema N.A. — February 26, 2002

**Review and result:** Reach the preserved pleading question; reverse the Second Circuit and remand for ordinary proceedings on the Title VII and intentional-age-discrimination claims. The judgment neither finds discrimination nor guarantees discovery, trial or recovery.

**Sufficient rule:** The McDonnell Douglas prima facie framework is a means of organizing circumstantial proof, not an additional Rule 8 pleading requirement. A complaint giving fair notice of the prohibited discrimination and the challenged employment acts need not plead facts establishing every element of that framework. This complaint identifies the demotion, discharge, relevant circumstances and asserted prohibited reasons and satisfies that requirement. Rule 9 supplies no separate heightened category for employment discrimination; underlying statutory elements and the eventual persuasion burden remain.

**Authority and application:** Leatherman's source-based rejection of invented heightened municipal pleading, Crawford-El's rejection of an invented motive burden and circular discovery barrier, and Reeves's distinction between a permissible evidentiary inference and compulsory proof all govern their own subjects and support this application. Conley's notice pleading remains the governing pre-divergence background. McDonnell Douglas and Thurston cannot require an indirect-proof checklist before the claimant knows whether direct evidence is available. Rule 11, clarification under applicable rules, proportionate discovery and Rule 56 continue to address unsupported claims.

**Alternative tested:** Affirmance on a genuine Rule 8 failure would be lawful; affirmance merely because the complaint omits the prima facie checklist would not. The employer's strongest objection is that a general assertion of discriminatory motive invites costly discovery. Here the alleged actions and circumstances provide the requested notice, and litigation cost does not authorize a new pleading amendment. No general immunity-pleading question is presented, so Crawford-El and Leatherman's retained limits are respected.

## 34. Porter v. Nussle — February 26, 2002

**Review and result:** Reach the scope of the admitted original §1997e(a); reverse the Second Circuit's categorical excessive-force exception and remand for application of the statutory exhaustion condition. An unexhausted action within the condition is dismissed without prejudice. The Court does not adjudicate the alleged beating.

**Sufficient rule:** Prison-conditions actions under §1997e(a) include federal actions about prison life concerning either general circumstances or a particular episode, including an officer's alleged excessive force. A confined prisoner must exhaust an actually available administrative process before bringing such an action. Booth governs a responsive process even if it cannot award the requested damages. The statutory category does not turn on whether the Eighth Amendment applies a deliberate-indifference or force-specific culpability rule.

**Two lawful paths:** The competing construction reads conditions as an ongoing institutional state and would affirm access to federal court for a single assault without exhaustion. Its strongest support is ordinary usage and the distinct constitutional force standard. The inclusive construction reads the whole prisoner-litigation provision, its mandatory amendment and the established distinction between prison-life suits and attacks on the fact or duration of custody. Its strongest objection is that it could delay judicial protection against a serious individual wrong. McCarthy v. Bronson's analogous treatment of isolated misconduct within conditions of confinement, together with the enacted command and Booth, supports inclusion; seriousness affects the merits and actual availability, not the statutory category. Bronson is an analogy from a different statute, not a claim that its magistrate-referral holding directly construed the PLRA.

**Limits:** The question is coverage. No comprehensive grievance timeliness, procedural-default, emergency or limitations rule is adopted. No intimidation or inaccessible remedy is found. The ordinary available-remedy condition remains enforceable on supported facts; the record does not supply a reason to invent a further availability dispute. Section 1997e(c)(2) permits, but does not require, dismissal before exhaustion of a facially frivolous or malicious claim, one failing to state a claim, or one seeking money from an immune defendant. That screening permission is not an entitlement to merits adjudication without exhaustion.

## 35. Raygor v. Regents of University of Minnesota — February 27, 2002

**Review channel and fixed premise:** Section 1257 reaches the Minnesota limitations judgment. The separate federal dismissal remains final and is not reopened. Under the governing Kimel rule, the ADEA claim cannot now be classified as categorically outside federal enforcement merely because Article I supposedly never authorizes private State suits. That does not waive immunity over supplemental state-law claims. Pennhurst's state-law boundary and College Savings Bank's rejection of ordinary-activity consent remain operative. Neither ordinary participation in discovery nor assertion of immunity in an answer is silently turned into waiver.

**Path A — affirm on the immune state claim's exclusion:** Section 1367(d) protects a claim asserted under subsection (a). The statute's general supplemental-jurisdiction language does not clearly include a state-law claim against a nonconsenting State that federal courts could not adjudicate. Applying it to alter the conditions of the State's own state-law remedy requires clearer direction than a provision tied to the federal jurisdictional grant. On that construction, the Minnesota limitations judgment is affirmed without deciding Congress's abstract power to enact an explicit tolling requirement. The valid federal ADEA anchor does not remove the independent state-claim bar.

**Strongest objection to Path A:** Subsection (d) concerns a claim asserted, rather than finally adjudicated, under (a). Tolling preserves resort to a competent state court and does not expose the State to a federal merits judgment. Schacht refuses to equate one claim's immunity with absence of jurisdiction over the whole case. The ADEA premise and the broad term all other claims therefore undermine an automatic jurisdictional exclusion.

**Path B — reverse the categorical tolling exclusion:** Related state claims asserted with a substantial federal controversy fall within the enacted tolling protection even when immunity prevents their federal adjudication. For a covered claim, subsection (d) suspends the limitations period while the claim is pending and for thirty days after dismissal unless state law provides a longer tolling period. Its additional protection for another claim in the same action voluntarily dismissed at the same time as or after dismissal of a subsection (a) claim is not the branch presented here. The provision preserves an otherwise available state forum; it does not waive federal immunity, create State consent or adjudicate the state-law merits. Schacht's claim-specific immunity distinction and the current valid ADEA anchor support this treatment. Reverse the categorical exclusion and remand for the remaining state-law timeliness computation and defenses.

**Strongest objection to Path B:** The words under subsection (a) refer to a jurisdictional grant that preserves constitutional limits. Reading them to include an immune claim may change a sovereign's litigation exposure indirectly without the unmistakable direction required for direct exposure. The tolling result must therefore justify its distinction between a filing-period rule and amenability, rather than treating Kimel as a general waiver.

**Remedy and record limits common to both:** The objective chronology establishes filing on or about August 29, 1996, dismissal on July 11, 1997 and refiling approximately three weeks later. The reversal path does not announce exact calendar timeliness or award damages. The final federal judgment remains undisturbed under either path. The earlier court's broad lack-of-original-jurisdiction rationale is not adopted as present law even by a Justice who would affirm on the independent state-claim ground.

## 36. Guest v. Leis — February 27, 2002

**Review scope:** The subscriber-information question and preserved defenses to it are reached. Each claimant must establish a personal privacy interest and an actual invasion. This is not a challenge to every seizure of hardware or to every stored message. The preserved rulings on hardware seizure, warrant particularity, inadequate proof of private-message reading and the distinct statutory claim are not automatically reopened.

**Privacy Path A — affirm the third-party-record rule as applied:** Miller and Smith permit acquisition of operational information voluntarily supplied to a service provider. Names, addresses, dates of birth and account credentials are provided to the operator to administer the system. This treatment can sustain the subscriber-information judgment without creating a right in the operator's equipment or treating every confidential relationship as protected by the Fourth Amendment. The actual warrants and Zurcher's evidence-search principle remain independently relevant.

**Strongest objection:** A password is designed to exclude others from a restricted account; limited entrustment necessary to operate that account is not public dissemination. Miller's bank business records and Smith's routing numbers do not resolve every private account boundary. The message-access warning proves neither public credentials nor unlimited governmental authority.

**Privacy Path B — vacate categorical forfeiture through entrustment:** Merely entrusting information for operating a restricted account does not conclusively relinquish every personal Fourth Amendment interest. Identify the particular record, its function, confidentiality, access arrangements and actual governmental acquisition. Miller and Smith remain authoritative for their specific voluntarily conveyed business and routing records; neither becomes a categorical rule that every entrusted digital record is public. Confidential access credentials present the clearest distinct case. This rule determines protection, not unlawfulness of an otherwise supported warrant.

**Strongest objection:** Confidentiality and limited purpose alone may be present in ordinary bank records and telephone data, so an undifferentiated entrustment rule would displace Miller and Smith without explaining why. The broader supporters below require an account-specific distinction and do not hold that a subscriber's preference for secrecy establishes protection. O'Connor accepts only the narrower credential ground; her vote cannot be counted for constitutional protection of all identifying data.

**Personal-immunity component:** Under Harlow and Anderson as preserved by Elder, Wilson, Denius and Saucier, the particular 1995 subscriber-record restriction was not sufficiently established to impose personal damages on these officials for the subscriber-access theory. Miller and Smith supplied substantial contrary guidance. Denius's intimate-information and current acquisition holdings and Carter/Ferguson/Kyllo's later decisions cannot provide retrospective 1995 notice. This conclusion does not require an identical precedent or proof of actual reliance; nor does it excuse a separately established, clearly forbidden warrant-scope violation. On the supplied subscriber claim, the individual defendants' judgment remains affirmed. A newly recognized privacy interest cannot by itself remove that defense.

**Warrant and institutional components:** A privacy ruling alone neither invalidates an existing warrant nor establishes search beyond its limits. Zurcher permits a supported evidence warrant at an innocent holder; subscriber innocence is not the missing probable-cause determination. Any institutional or proper official-capacity claim requires actual unconstitutional action attributable under the applicable policy, custom or authorized-decision rule. Individual qualified immunity supplies no immunity for a separately liable entity. The vacatur positions below return only the subscriber-record branch affected by the categorical rule; they establish no present institutional liability, damages, compulsory discovery or new statutory claim.

## 38. Young v. United States — March 4, 2002

**Review and result:** Reach the three-year priority lookback question; affirm the First Circuit's conclusion that the identified tax debt survives the Chapter 7 discharge. The prior Chapter 13 automatic stay suspends the lookback for the time it legally prevented collection. No bad-faith finding is necessary.

**Sufficient rule:** The three-year period for qualifying income taxes under the applicable §507(a)(8)(A)(i), carried into dischargeability through §523(a)(1)(A), is subject to traditional equitable tolling during an earlier bankruptcy stay that legally prevented the creditor from collecting. This preserves the collection opportunity embodied in the period; it does not reorder statutory priorities or confer a general power to lengthen Code deadlines. The operative impediment is the legally disabling stay, not ordinary inconvenience or an assumption that the debtor acted wrongfully.

**Two lawful paths:** Literal calendar counting would discharge the claim if the due-date lookback had elapsed before the later petition. Its strongest ground is the absence of an express suspension clause in this provision and Congress's use of extensions elsewhere. Tolling recognizes the traditional legal-impediment background unless the particular statutory scheme rebuts it. Its strongest objection is that calling a backward-looking priority condition a limitations period could substitute equitable preference for enacted priority. Here the rule preserves a finite opportunity to collect which the Code itself interrupted; it neither changes a creditor category nor creates an open-ended extension.

**Authority and statutory limits:** Irwin supplies a rebuttable background, not an automatic extension of every federal period. Brockamp refuses an incapacity extension under the detailed refund/amount scheme; its particular structure and grounds do not establish that a bankruptcy collection interval must run while another Code provision forbids collection. Noland forbids replacing Congress's priority categories with class-wide equity but leaves lawful equity within the Code. Raleigh preserves the substantive burden on the tax claim; it does not decide the separate federal dischargeability clock. Section 108(c)'s treatment of periods for covered proceedings outside bankruptcy and §523(b)'s treatment of previously excepted debts have their own functions; neither expressly forbids background suspension of this stay-disabled collection interval. No fraudulent-concealment finding or general discovery rule is borrowed from Holmberg, and TRW's express FCRA exception supplies no universal bar to traditional tolling.

**Application and remedy:** The return was due October 15, 1993; the first petition was filed May 1, 1996; the second followed March 12, 1997, while the first proceeding still supplied the collection stay. Excluding the disabled interval leaves the tax within the three-year lookback. The formal judgment affirms nondischargeability at that scope. It neither determines a new tax assessment nor awards a collection amount, penalty or new priority category. No disputed stay duration or coverage is identified, so a new evidentiary remand is unwarranted.
