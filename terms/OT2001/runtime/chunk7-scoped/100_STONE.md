## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: An additional statutory restriction does not erase a preserved defense

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** The state preserved the issue in both federal courts. The case does not present sua sponte invocation of a forfeited defense.
* **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether satisfaction of §2254(d) permits a grant without deciding the preserved nonretroactivity objection.
* **Stone’s Legal Position:** Apply Caspari v. Bohlen and the distinct function of Williams v. Taylor. No new universal ordering rule is necessary.
* **Stone’s Proposed Holding:** A federal court cannot grant habeas relief on a rule potentially barred by a properly preserved nonretroactivity objection without resolving that objection. Section 2254(d) adds a condition; it does not repeal the separate inquiry merely because the state court decided another ground.
* **Reach Within the Question:** The holding governs the justification for a grant, not a mandatory full checklist before every denial. The nonjurisdictional character, preservation rules, and event-date exceptions remain intact.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The two analyses ask different questions about different legal dates. A state court’s unreasonable application of a later established rule does not establish that a federal collateral court may apply that rule to an earlier final conviction. Conversely, a later decision may simply articulate an already dictated result, which would defeat a new-rule objection. That is a legal determination the lower court must actually make. The absence of a state retroactivity ruling removes no federal obligation to decide a defense fairly presented in the federal proceeding. The decisive reasoning is the different legal work performed by nonretroactivity and §2254(d), not a categorical preference for procedural defeat. Tyler v. Cain reinforces that retroactivity and permission to grant relief remain distinct questions, but its successive-application rule is not imported into this first-petition case. Since the grant challenges the refusal to conduct Teague review, deciding whether Mills itself was new or exceptional would exceed the correction necessary here.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The Court does not decide whether the mitigating-evidence rule was new or whether either then-existing exception applies.
