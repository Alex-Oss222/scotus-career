# Atkins — date-eligible factual supplement

This handoff supplies objective legislative, clinical and record facts through June 20, 2002. It supplies no Supreme Court disposition, rationale or judicial alignment. It is drawn from published record recitations; the model must independently validate its framing and legal significance.

## Legislative facts

In 1989, Georgia and Maryland prohibited execution on the relevant disability ground, and fourteen States had no capital punishment. By the present event, eighteen death-penalty States had adopted disability-specific legislation: Georgia, Maryland, Kentucky, Tennessee, New Mexico, Arkansas, Colorado, Washington, Indiana, Kansas, New York, Nebraska, South Dakota, Arizona, Connecticut, Florida, Missouri and North Carolina. Twelve States then lacked capital punishment; thirty-eight retained it. These are distinct denominators and neither determines a constitutional conclusion by arithmetic.

Kentucky and Tennessee acted in 1990; New Mexico in 1991; Arkansas, Colorado, Washington, Indiana and Kansas in 1993–1994; New York in 1995; Nebraska in 1998; and the final six listed States in 2000–2001. No identified enactment reinstated the authority after adopting an exemption. Several enactments were recent and their operation had limited experience.

The protections were not uniform. Eleven of the eighteen enactments used prospective effective-date restrictions tied to offenses, convictions or sentencing; seven applied without those particular temporal restrictions. New York retained a correctional-custody murder exception. Kansas defined the relevant impairment in terms of capacity to appreciate criminality or conform conduct to law; whether that definition is narrower than clinical intellectual disability must not be assumed away. Do not describe all eighteen statutes as unconditional prohibitions covering every existing death sentence.

Federal legislation in 1988 and 1994 prohibited execution of persons with the relevant disability: 21 U.S.C. §848(l), and 18 U.S.C. §3596(c). Texas's 2001 measure passed both houses but was vetoed; it was not enacted law. The veto addressed the proposed procedure. Measures passing one chamber in Virginia and Nevada were not enacted prohibitions. An April 2002 Illinois commission recommendation likewise was a recommendation, not law.

## Practice evidence and limits

The published account identifies five States as having executed offenders with known IQ scores below seventy after 1989: Alabama, Texas, Louisiana, South Carolina and Virginia. The supporting compilation was updated through June 18, 2002. A low reported IQ is not by itself proof of the complete clinical diagnosis, and this report is not a verified total of every diagnosis or execution. New Hampshire and New Jersey retained capital authorization but had carried out no executions in decades. Absence of executions may bear on practice; it does not establish each legislature's reason for inaction.

Professional, religious, foreign and polling materials were submitted as contextual evidence. Their representativeness, methods and legal relevance are contestable; no voting body or empirical finding is inferred from an advocacy submission. A constitutional position may be supported or rejected without treating these submissions as binding authority.

## Clinical and individual-record facts

The AAMR's 1992 definition and the American Psychiatric Association's 2000 diagnostic manual require significantly subaverage intellectual functioning, concurrent significant limitations in adaptive functioning in at least two specified skill areas, and onset before age eighteen. The listed skills encompass communication, self-care, home living, social interaction, community use, self-direction, health and safety, functional academics, leisure and work. The intellectual measure is one element; an IQ result alone does not establish the diagnosis. The publications discuss roughly seventy to seventy-five as the upper range relevant to the intellectual-functioning inquiry, with mild disability commonly described from about fifty–fifty-five to approximately seventy. These clinical descriptions are not a newly adopted constitutional cutoff or proof procedure.

Atkins's defense expert, Dr. Evan Nelson, administered the WAIS-III and reported a full-scale IQ of fifty-nine. His diagnosis also rested on interviews and school and court records. He described the result as consistent with lifelong limitations and rejected malingering. The Commonwealth's expert, Dr. Stanton Samenow, disputed disability and described at least average intelligence with antisocial personality disorder. He conducted interviews and reviewed records but administered no intelligence test; he asked questions from an older memory scale. These are competing expert positions. The supplement does not find Atkins disabled.

Atkins and Jones each attributed the shooting to the other. The jury convicted Atkins and twice imposed death; the first sentence was set aside for a misleading verdict form. The second sentencing included both experts. The constitutional claim concerns categorical execution eligibility; the disability determination remains contested on this record.
