D. The South Carolina Supreme Court's 
resolution of the federal issue on direct appeal 


On appeal, the state supreme court addressed Petitioner's 
claim that Simmons required a parole ineligibility charge because 
the prosecution raised future dangerousness with argument, and 
by presentation of “evidence about escape attempts, possession of 
a shank, and [Petitioner’s] behavior in jail”. State v. Kelly, 540 
S.E.2d 851, 856 (S.C. 2001). 


The court first held that the state’s evidence did not raise 
future dangerousness. The court noted that evidence of 
misconduct in prison did not implicate the future dangerousness 
“contemplated by Simmons”, which is danger if the defendant is 
released from prison. The court held that parole ineligibility did 
nothing to rebut the state’s evidence that Petitioner was an escape 
risk. Finally, the court concluded that the state’s evidence was 
directed towards showing Petitioner would not adapt to prison, 
and noted Petitioner had full opportunity 10 rebut this evidence 
with cross-examination and his expert witnesses on adaptability. 
Kelly, 540 $.E.2d at 857. 


Next, the state supreme court held that the prosecution did 
not argue future dangerousness. The court observed that the 
solicitor “consciously avoided any implication of future

dangero"sness”, and never sugg2sted that the jury “should impose 
the death penalty because [Petitioner] would be a future danger to 
the general public”. Kelly, 540 S.E.2d at 857. 


The Kelly court also went on to hold in the alternative that 
the Simons rule was not triggered because at the time of the 
judge’s charge a thirty year definite term sentence was a 
possibility, and life without parole was not the only available 
alternative sentence to death. Kelly, 540 S.E.2d at 857-58. This 
alternative rationale has since been overruled by this Court’s 
decision in Shafer v. South Carolina, 121 S.Ct. 1263 (2001). 


Finally, the state supreme court rejected Petitioner’s claim 
that the trial court should have instructed the jury with eight- 
sentence request to charge that there was “no evidence” the 
defendant would be dangerous in the future, and that any 
consideration of future dangerousness was not a proper factor to 
consider. The state supreme court ruled the charge was properly 
denied, because (1) the prosecution was entitled to argue future 
dangerousness within prison without triggering the need for a 
Simmons instruction on parole ineligibility, and (2) “the charge as 
written is extremely confusing”. Kelly, 540 S.E.2d at 859.

## Copied alternative adequacy argument

If. Since the jury never inquired about parole and the trial 
judge did not broach the issue by giving a “no concern” 
charge, Simmons was satisfied by the judge’s plain meaning 
instruction and the defense’s argument to the jury that 
Petitioner would never leave prison. 


Two important factors distinguish Petitioner’s case from 
that in Simmons, and even Shafer, for that matter. First, unlike 
those cases, the jury in Petitioner’s case did not inquire about 
parole eligibility. Thus, there is nothing whatsoever to indicate 
that the jurors were concerned at all with the possibility of 
Petitioner’s future release when they decided death was 
appropriate. Cf Shafer v. South Carolina, 121 S.Ct. 1263, 1273- 
74 (2001). Second, unlike Simmons and Shafer, the jurors in this 
case were not charged that parole eligibility or ineligibility was 
not for their consideration. Cf Simmons, 512 U.S. at 170-71; 
Shafer, 121 S.Ct. at 1274. Thus, there is no reason to think that 
the jurors were told parole was available, but “for some unstated 
reason, [they] should be blind to that fact”. Simmons, 512 U.S. at 
170. If anything, they heard testimony that goodtime reduction 
was not a possibility for Petitioner. JA 236-37.
