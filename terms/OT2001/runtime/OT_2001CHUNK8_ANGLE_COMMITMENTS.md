## 2. Governing baseline and exact scope

Apprendi establishes the present-offense maximum rule and preserves judicial selection within punishment already authorized by the conviction. It expressly leaves general Guidelines classifications and unchanged-maximum minima open and leaves McMillan standing. The present decision therefore requires a new constitutional determination. Neither that reservation nor the word “Guidelines” decides it. Witte and Watts permit ordinary within-authority consideration and retain independent element limits; they are not a universal below-statutory-maximum safe harbor. Mistretta's institutional approval of the Commission does not answer this allocation question.

The five-Justice proposed rule concerns a fact about commission or consequences of the present offense that the mandatory Guidelines make legally necessary either to raise the binding upper limit or to raise the binding lower limit of the punishment range. Such a fact must receive the protections of an aggravated-offense element: in this federal prosecution it must be charged as required by the Fifth Amendment and, absent a valid element admission or lawful jury waiver, determined by a jury beyond a reasonable doubt. A bare sentencing admission, strong evidence or knowledge of exposure is not by itself a valid element admission or waiver. The operative range follows the law and facts constitutionally established; the fact's location in a Guideline does not remove its legal effect.

The maximum relevant to this new application is the binding limit available without the additional present-offense finding, not merely the outer ceiling printed in the offense statute. The minimum application separately protects against an additional finding that legally requires a harsher floor. The floor rule expressly displaces McMillan insofar as it permits a judge to establish such a present-offense aggravator by a preponderance merely because the outer maximum remains unchanged. It is not described as something Apprendi already held. Judges retain sentencing choice and relevant-conduct consideration within the lawfully established range. Mitigation that reduces otherwise authorized punishment is distinct from a fact legally necessary to increase that punishment. Lawful departure provisions retain their own conditions; their existence does not make the ordinarily binding range wholly discretionary.

The three contrary commitments treat the offense statute's maximum as the constitutional boundary and the mandatory Guidelines as instructions governing exercise of the sentencing authority already supplied by the conviction. They acknowledge that the Guidelines are binding and that quantity affects the range; their disagreement is constitutional classification, not a factual assertion that the Guidelines were advisory. They retain McMillan and reject the proposed ceiling-and-floor extension. Their position does not permit a sentence above the verdict-authorized statutory count maximum.

No Justice's commitment converts the Guidelines into an advisory system or chooses a wholesale statutory severance. The five apply the constitutional conditions to the mandatory system presented. The actual existing charge, verdict, lawful admissions and applicable sentencing provisions must determine the permissible range on remand. No baseline Guidelines range absent the challenged quantity finding is supplied, so no numeric replacement range or term is invented.

## 3. Enhanced comparison: the competing paths

### A. Ceiling and floor

**Binding-range path.** The law has made an additional fact necessary to alter the punishment range. The authority or obligation created by that fact cannot be assigned to a judge simply by retaining a larger unused outer maximum. The strongest contrary argument is that a jury conviction already permits the statutory range, that McMillan expressly allows a minimum within it, and that replacing discretion with enforceable rules should not diminish constitutional validity. The five answer that the enacted law actually withholds some sentencing choices absent the additional fact. The Constitution regulates that law, not a broader law Congress could have enacted. An advisory consideration is different from a fact that changes an enforceable limit. For the floor, that answer requires rejecting McMillan's contrary allocation principle rather than disguising an overruling as an application.

**Outer-ceiling path.** A complete offense and its statutory punishment supply authority; the Guidelines regulate the judge's already legitimate sentencing function. The strongest contrary argument is that mandatory limits are legal limits, and the same quantity fact can move a defendant's required imprisonment dramatically without a jury finding. O'Connor, Kennedy and Breyer answer through bounded legislative classification and within-offense sentencing, accepting that safeguards and review remain required. They see no constitutional reason why constraints on lawful judicial discretion must become additional offense elements. They would resolve the Guidelines question against the defendants while preserving Apprendi's actual count-ceiling rule.

**Ceiling-only path.** A third lawful position would extend Apprendi to an enforceable Guidelines upper limit but retain McMillan for an unchanged upper limit and increased floor. This is not the commitment of any of the eight after the Justice-specific assessment below. Stevens, Scalia and Thomas have explicit published range-inclusive positions. Souter and Ginsburg conclude here that the mandatory obligation created by the floor is also a legal aggravation of punishment and reject the maximum/minimum distinction on that ground. The opposing three do not accept the antecedent Guidelines-ceiling extension. This comparison identifies an available narrower position without manufacturing a Justice who adopts it.

### B. Angle's independent resentencing

**Preserve the ordinary remand alone.** The existing inadequate-quantity-findings ruling already removes Angle's sentence; the three outer-ceiling Justices would leave sentencing to the valid mandatory Guidelines and their ordinary findings rules. Their strongest objection to added constitutional directions is that the Court would invalidate a permissible sentencing allocation unnecessary to the independent defect.

**Preserve the remand with constitutional directions.** The five binding-range Justices agree that the findings defect independently requires resentencing, but the legal authority used at that imminent resentencing is part of the presented controversy. The court below expressly resolved the constitutional question against Angle. Reversing that legal ruling determines how the required resentencing proceeds; it neither invents a new injury nor redundantly vacates a sentence already removed below. The strongest counterargument—no second remedy is needed—does not answer the governing legal rule for the actual remand.

### C. Phifer and Rule 52(b)

Phifer's actual 292-month drug-count sentence exceeds the 240-month maximum supported by that count's verdict. Two actual 240-month money-laundering terms were concurrent. Their available maxima do not retroactively become consecutive terms. His objection was forfeited, not shown to have been intentionally waived.

**Lawful identical-outcome path.** For the three outer-ceiling Justices, the judicially calculated mandatory total is constitutionally permissible. Section 5G1.2(d) therefore makes sufficient consecutive service obligatory to achieve that same total. Under Olano's ordinary effect requirement, Phifer has not shown that correcting the count allocation would reduce his imprisonment. The strongest objection is that he is serving an actually excessive count term and that available consecutive authority does not automatically establish the sentence that would have been imposed. Their answer depends on mandatory inevitability, not mere availability: the existing lawful total-punishment calculation and operative stacking rule would require the same total. They affirm for failure of the third condition, without declaring the drug-count term lawful or reaching the independent fourth condition. If the range is held constitutionally unavailable, this sufficient ground no longer operates; they would accept the resulting remand as the consequence of the Court's governing range rule rather than invent a new factual basis to affirm.

**Reconsideration under a lawful-range rule.** The five reject the lower court's sufficient ground because its mandatory-total premise depends on the judicial quantity classification they reject. A sum of possible count maxima cannot replace the missing lawful total-punishment premise. They vacate the appellate judgment affirming Phifer's sentence and remand for the remaining Rule 52(b) application. This does not itself order a reduced term or a new sentencing hearing: entitlement to correction must still be determined under all applicable conditions.

The strongest argument for immediate final relief is that the actual count is plainly excessive and the automatic no-prejudice rationale has failed. The five reject automatic correction because failure of one appellate rationale does not establish every remaining plain-error condition. The strongest alternative argument for affirmance is Cotton's refusal of correction on overwhelming and essentially uncontested threshold proof. The five distinguish its actual record: this packet establishes no equivalent finding about Phifer's threshold proof, relevant contest, notice and trial-strategy circumstances. Cotton supplies the fourth-condition standard, not those facts. Those are record-specific remand tasks, not an invitation to presume either a defendant victory or a governmental default.

On remand, error, plainness, effect on substantial rights and the independent fairness/integrity/public-reputation condition must remain separate. The defendant ordinarily bears the effect burden. The governing law must identify what sentence was actually authorized and what lawful mandatory rules would have done; mere possible aggregate exposure is insufficient. A lawful inevitable identical total can bear on effect, but an unlawful mandatory-range premise cannot establish that inevitability. Johnson and Cotton govern their settled-contrary-trial-law/intervening-law setting; the Court should not deem every newly announced Guidelines proposition clear at trial or silently decide the reserved initially unsettled-law question. Scalia does not join a broader timing holding. Vonn permits consideration of the relevant existing record. Cotton permits a record-specific refusal of correction at the fourth condition even assuming substantial-rights effect; it does not replace a jury verdict, establish harmlessness or eliminate the other conditions. Neder's preserved materiality-error method does not replace this inquiry.

## 4. Justice-specific grounds, alternatives and join barriers

### Stevens

His McMillan dissent, 477 U.S. 95–104, treats specifically aggravated prohibited conduct carrying special punishment as requiring Winship protection; a retained outer maximum does not answer that objection. His published Jones concurrence Part I expressly covers minimum as well as maximum changes. His full Apprendi join supplies the later controlling legal-role principle, while its reservation leaves the present application open. He therefore supports both Guidelines limits and express displacement of McMillan, not a ceiling-only compromise. The competing outer-ceiling path has genuine statutory and precedent support, but it preserves the same distinction his own McMillan dissent rejected.

His Angle disposition preserves the independent findings remand and adds the rule governing resentencing. For Phifer, his Cotton join establishes that he will not make constitutional sentencing error automatically correctable. His Vonn objection depended on Rule 11(h)'s specific plea regime and does not displace Rule 52(b) here. He joins the bounded appellate remand, with ordinary defendant-burden effect review and the distinct fourth condition. Join barriers: no blanket below-maximum safe harbor, no hypothetical-consecutive cure, no automatic sentence reduction, and no transfer of his Rule 11(h) burden position into this sentencing claim.

### O'Connor

Her McMillan majority join accepts a judge-found floor within the offense maximum. Her published Witte opinion and Watts join preserve ordinary within-authority sentencing, and her Apprendi dissent, joined by Kennedy and Breyer, directly defends bounded legislative sentencing classification. She recognizes that those older within-range cases do not themselves decide every Guidelines application, but adopts their allocation distinction for this mandatory scheme. The strongest competing point is the range's actual binding effect; she answers that making the judge follow rules within a conviction-authorized statutory range does not add a new aggravated crime. She rejects both the ceiling and floor extension and retains McMillan.

Her own Olano and Vonn opinions separate legal error from ordinary prejudice and discretionary correction. Cotton preserves that separation for forfeited sentencing error. She preserves Angle's ordinary quantity-findings remand and would affirm Phifer under the lawful mandatory-total premise. Her ground is lack of additional imprisonment from correcting the allocation, not an absence of constitutional count error. Join barriers: no overruling of McMillan, no element classification merely because Guidelines bind, no presumption that each excessive count automatically satisfies both remaining Rule 52(b) conditions. She can join the Court's description of the actual count error and the four-condition framework while dissenting from the new range rule and Phifer vacatur.

### Scalia

His published Jones authorship establishes aggravated statutory elements and their charging consequences. More decisively for the present open question, he expressly joined Stevens's Jones concurrence Part I covering facts that change the prescribed range, including its minimum. His later full Apprendi join accepted a sufficient maximum holding and reservation; it did not withdraw that earlier published broader position. This supplies an affirmative date-eligible basis for floor protection. Retaining McMillan while protecting only a Guidelines ceiling is legally available, but conflicts with his own published range-inclusive join when this case actually presents the binding floor. He supports express displacement, leaving ordinary within-range sentencing intact.

He supports Angle's constitutional remand directions and Phifer's appellate vacatur. His Johnson and Cotton positions permit assumed plainness where an independent condition decides the case and do not adopt a universal appellate-time rule. His Cotton join also forecloses equating a genuine jury error with automatic fourth-condition correction. Join barriers: a nominal statutory maximum cannot erase a lower binding ceiling; a mandatory floor cannot escape the range principle he adopted; the opinion must not transform evidence into a verdict, invent a general plainness rule, or order correction before the remaining Rule 52(b) conditions are applied. No separate writing is required merely to repeat a reservation the lead opinion honors.

### Kennedy

His published Jones dissent and Apprendi dissenting join defend the distinction between a completed offense and bounded punishment considerations; his Witte join accepts relevant-conduct sentencing within statutory authority. He sees the mandatory Guidelines as an exercise of that permissible structuring power. The strongest contrary point is that quantity legally determines available sentencing choices, rather than merely influencing discretion. His answer is that the conviction already authorizes the statutory range and the law may guide its use without treating each directive as a new element. He retains McMillan and rejects both Guidelines extensions, not Apprendi's actual statutory count ceiling.

He preserves Angle's ordinary remand and would affirm Phifer on independently lawful obligatory stacking. His Olano and Cotton joins require the distinction between an erroneous count and prejudice warranting appellate intervention. His affirmation depends on mandatory identical total punishment; he does not accept available aggregate maxima alone as a cure. Join barriers: no extension from statutory maximum to every Guidelines constraint; no floor overruling; no automatic relief solely from the actual count error. If the controlling range rule removes the mandatory premise, he accepts that the remaining plain-error work must be done under that rule while maintaining his disagreement with it.

### Souter

His published Jones concurrence identifies an element by the legal work a present-offense fact performs; Ginsburg, Stevens and Scalia joined that functional maximum analysis. His Apprendi join adopts the resulting constitutional maximum rule. His Jones writing expressly reserved minima, and he did not join Stevens's broader concurrence: neither prior decision is misdescribed as his already-decided floor rule. The present floor vote is a new application. He concludes that a present-offense finding which turns lesser lawful punishment into forbidden punishment changes the legal punishment obligation just as a higher ceiling changes permission. The same mandatory quantity schedule defines both changes; treating the floor as discretionary consideration obscures its actual legal effect.

The strongest narrower path is a ceiling extension plus adherence to McMillan. He rejects it because the relevant distinction is legal aggravation versus choice within an established range, not which end of the mandatory range carries the aggravation. McMillan's contrary classification therefore must be displaced explicitly. His Witte and Watts joins remain coherent because their retained holdings are bounded by conviction-based authority and do not establish a universal safe harbor. He joins Angle's constitutional directions and Phifer's bounded appellate vacatur. His Olano, Vonn and Cotton joins require ordinary effect review, the relevant existing record and a separate fourth-condition decision. Join barriers: no assertion that his prior reserved question was already settled; no invalidation of ordinary discretion; no automatic correction or presumed Cotton-equivalent evidentiary record.

### Thomas

His published Apprendi concurrence states that a fact legally necessary to increase the kind, degree or range of punishment is an element and expressly includes mandatory-minimum facts. Its reservation of general Guidelines implications requires present analysis, but not abandonment of the stated range principle. He finds that the mandatory quantity schedule supplies the legal link his principle requires. The strongest opposing path invokes McMillan and the completed statutory offense. His historical element account and the distinction between aggravation and mitigation make the legislatively attached additional punishment, not the sentencing label, decisive. He supports both limits and express displacement of the inconsistent McMillan rule, without using any assumed prior-conviction exception.

He preserves Angle's independent remand with constitutional directions. His Olano, Johnson and Cotton joins distinguish constitutional classification from appellate correction; his Neder authorship likewise separates a real element omission from its own review consequences. He supports Phifer's appellate vacatur for reconsideration, not a universal structural-error or immediate-resentencing rule. Join barriers: no ceiling-only description of his range principle, no wholesale treatment of every sentencing fact as aggravation, no automatic fourth-condition satisfaction and no invented numerical baseline or aggregate replacement sentence.

### Ginsburg

Her published Almendarez-Torres majority and avoidance joins, Jones statutory and Souter-concurrence joins, and full Apprendi join protect the legal function of an aggravating fact despite its location in a penalty provision. Her Jones join expressly left minima open; she did not adopt Stevens's broader concurrence there. She now accepts the floor extension because the mandatory quantity fact does not merely persuade a judge to select a harsher sentence: it makes a previously permissible lower sentence legally unavailable. Keeping the floor outside the protection would permit compulsory additional punishment on the same lower-standard finding whose effect on the ceiling requires a jury. That is an affirmative conclusion in this presented mandatory-range setting, not an automatic inference from her earlier maximum votes.

The strongest competing path preserves the precise McMillan distinction and her own Witte and Watts within-range joins. She rejects its application here because those joins preserved independent element limits, while the present quantified constraint supplies the legal aggravation requiring their application. She accepts express displacement of McMillan and preserves ordinary discretionary sentencing. She joins Angle's constitutional directions and Phifer's appellate vacatur. Her narrower actual-equivalent-findings position in Neder does not govern this forfeited claim; her Johnson, Vonn and Cotton joins require their distinct Rule 52(b) analysis and permit Cotton's actual fourth-condition result without converting proof into a verdict. Join barriers: no fictional earlier minimum holding, no waiver from silence, no automatic hypothetical stacking and no inferred Cotton-equivalent facts.

### Breyer

His separate published Apprendi dissent explains why structured sentencing can promote proportionate, administrable punishment without becoming constitutionally inferior to broad judicial discretion. His Jones dissenting join, Witte and Watts joins, and Apprendi allocation dissent support the outer-ceiling path. The binding character of the Guidelines is the strongest challenge; his response is that the Constitution need not force Congress to choose between broad unguided ranges and a jury finding for every rule governing sentencing within them. He retains McMillan and would reject both new Guidelines classifications while accepting the binding statutory maximum established by Apprendi.

He preserves Angle's ordinary findings remand. His Vonn and Cotton joins support ordinary forfeiture review and its separate effect and discretion requirements. He would affirm Phifer because a valid mandatory total and stacking command eliminate any reduction in aggregate imprisonment from correcting count allocation; this does not deem the actual excess lawful. Join barriers: no constitutional invalidation of the mandatory scheme from its binding form alone, no floor overruling and no fourth-condition shortcut. He can agree that an invalid range could not establish inevitable lawful stacking, while denying that this range is invalid.

### Jones: Souter concurrence and its exact joins

Source: `terms/OT1998/output/OT_1998CHUNK6.md`

**Souter, joined by Stevens, Scalia and Ginsburg.** These four join the Court's sufficient statutory ground and also conclude that a fact about the commission or consequences of the charged offense, indispensable to a greater statutory maximum than the charged elements and verdict otherwise allow, performs the constitutional function of an element. The federal charging, jury and reasonable-doubt safeguards follow, subject to valid admission and waiver rules; those rules do not repair the omitted charge by implication. Their Monge position treated an additional, previously unestablished personal-conduct qualification as an element for the finality question. They now apply that functional reasoning to injury in the present offense, with Gaudin supplying jury allocation. This is their constitutional conclusion here, not a claim that Monge already decided the jury question. Ordinary factfinding within existing conviction-authorized punishment remains distinct. The concurrence leaves bare prior-conviction status, unchanged-maximum minima, general Guidelines classification and capital sentencing unresolved. Four Justices adopt this independent constitutional ground; it is not the Court's holding.


### Jones: Stevens range-inclusive concurrence and Scalia join

Source: `terms/OT1998/output/OT_1998CHUNK6.md`

**Stevens, joined by Scalia in Part I.** Their broader position protects facts that change the legislatively prescribed punishment range, including facts increasing the minimum, and recognizes no categorical exclusion merely because the fact is a prior conviction. Stevens relies on Winship's proof requirement, Mullaney's protection against shifting the burden on a fact determining punishment, his objections to Patterson's treatment of offense definition, and Duncan's protection of jury adjudication. Scalia agrees that the legislature cannot avoid the protection by naming a range-determining fact a sentencing consideration. His narrower Monge element-function ground supports that conclusion without having already established this universal range principle. Neither ordinary relevant-conduct sentencing under Witte and Watts nor an existing conviction's validity is the same question as whether a new fact legally authorizes a different punishment range. A charged prior-status element may be established by adequate admission or other lawful proof without relitigating the conviction's validity; Custis's collateral-review limits remain. These positions do not decide a mandatory-minimum application or overrule McMillan here. In Part II, Stevens alone also places facts necessary for death eligibility within his constitutional principle and favors reconsideration of contrary Walton reasoning in an appropriate case. No capital issue is decided, and Scalia does not join that recommendation.


### Apprendi: Thomas concurrence

Source: `terms/OT1999/output/OT_1999CHUNK8.md`

**Thomas, concurring.** Thomas joins the Court's rule and remedy in full but adopts a broader principle: a fact that the law makes necessary to increase the kind, degree or range of punishment is an element of the aggravated crime. His common-law account draws on the treatment of value and grading in Smith, Hope, Larned, McDonald and Lacy and on Archbold's and Bishop's description of legally aggravated offenses. Those authorities concerning accusation help identify the offense to which jury and proof safeguards attach; he does not infer federal grand-jury incorporation against a State.

The principle includes legally aggravating prior convictions and mandatory-minimum facts. A prior adjudication's validity and the lawful manner of proving or admitting its existence remain separate; adequate admissions, stipulations or lawful bifurcation may avoid prejudicial detail. He acknowledges that his earlier Almendarez-Torres dissent relied on the special prior-adjudication setting, and now concludes that this distinction does not categorically remove a fact's element status when the law uses it to enlarge punishment. His earlier statutory joins did not already decide that constitutional point. His view of minima conflicts with McMillan's contrary reasoning, but this case does not overrule McMillan.

Thomas distinguishes lawful discretion inside an established range and facts mitigating an already authorized punishment. He reserves the capital question in light of its distinct constitutional structure, general Guidelines implications, limits on within-range discretion and legislative authority to prescribe broad ranges. He accepts the narrower present-offense holding as sufficient here. No other Justice joins this concurrence, and its broader conclusions do not control.
