## The operative grounds

### Shared statutory ground

The governing framework permits agency discipline for the prescribed service-related cause, subject to employee response, Board review and judicial review. It does not make the completion of each contractual grievance a condition of considering earlier discipline. An unresolved grievance, an earlier action shown erroneous and a completed arbitral reversal are different states. Rejecting the first as a categorical disqualification does not allow the Board to treat the latter two as immaterial.

The review election concerning removal reinforces the need to identify the particular matter being reviewed. Gregory chose Board review of removal; the earlier disciplinary grievances remain distinct. That choice does not imply that she elected to abandon those grievances or could personally force their arbitration. Nor does it establish that every Board procedure is sufficient. It does show why consideration of a disciplinary history in the removal appeal cannot simply be equated with a second direct appeal of every earlier action.

### The five-Justice framework ground

O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer would also adopt a bounded statutory response to the Postal Service's sufficiency argument. The described framework is a reasoned way to permit disciplinary-history consideration during grievance pendency. It allows restricted collateral review only when the employee received written notice, the action is a matter of record and the employee had an opportunity to dispute the charges above the original deciding authority. If any prerequisite is absent, full fresh review follows; if all are present, the employee may show clear error on the existing earlier record. The Board thus distinguishes previously contestable, recorded discipline from discipline lacking those minimum procedural predicates and does not authorize reliance on clearly erroneous prior action.

Within the preserved pendency question, neither the adverse-action provisions nor the judicial-review standard requires the Court to discard that distinction solely because a separate grievance has not ended. The Postal Service offered the framework as a ground for rejecting the categorical rule, so the Court may evaluate that argument without inventing a different question or treating Gregory as having preserved every possible attack on Bolling.

This is a limited statutory conclusion. It does not find that every predicate was correctly established for every earlier action here, approve the allocation of every burden in every collateral dispute, or hold that an opportunity to invoke union-controlled arbitration guarantees a meaningful completed adjudication. It does not settle a separately supported constitutional attack on the clear-error branch, inaccessible review, unsupported findings, harmful procedural error or arbitrary refusal to recognize a completed invalidation. The agency's burden on the appealed removal and the court's section 7703(c) review remain distinct from the collateral inquiry.

### The three-Justice reservation

Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg agree that the government's sufficiency defense bears on the presented question; they do not exclude it by a categorical preservation rule. They accept the framework's full-review branch and reopening authority as reasons that pending grievances need not always prevent lawful consideration. They nevertheless withhold an affirmative holding that the particular restricted-review branch is statutorily sufficient merely because notice, a record and an opportunity for higher-level challenge exist.

An opportunity to dispute charges is not necessarily a completed merits adjudication. The pending grievance and the employee's limited ability to control arbitration can matter to how reliably the Board may use the earlier penalty. The agency's own invocation of adequacy does not provide every missing factual or legal predicate for a broad endorsement. Because the categorical rule fails without deciding whether every aspect of the clear-error method is valid, they would leave that further question open. This reservation does not adopt a requirement of fresh review of all prior discipline, find a denial of due process, or require a completed arbitration before any use of an earlier action.

Their separate-writing function, if needed after assembly, would be to state this exact limit on assent: they join rejection of the categorical bar and the remand, but not affirmative approval of the restricted-review branch. It would also explain why choosing Board review of removal and not seeking reopening cannot themselves validate that branch. No author is assigned at this stage.

### Reopening and the actual warning

All eight recognize the Board's authority under the verified 2001 section 1201.118 to reopen on its own motion at any time. That authority gives a legally available route for considering a later modification or reversal of a disciplinary predicate. It does not establish a duty to reopen in every such case or prove that Gregory received corrective relief. Her reported failure to notify the Board while review was pending and failure to move for reopening do not become an automatic waiver holding.

The Board's authority is therefore relevant support against a rule premised on the absolute impossibility of later correction. It cannot be described as an assured cure for every defective penalty, nor can its existence replace whatever process governing law independently requires. All eight retain that permission/obligation distinction.

The receiving court must implement the rejection of categorical exclusion and address any properly available issue concerning the warning's completed arbitral reversal. Neither the effect of that warning on the selected penalty nor every procedural consequence of its late presentation has been resolved on the supplied record. The Court should not determine that removal necessarily stands, order a new penalty as a matter of course, compel reopening, grant backpay or require another evidentiary hearing. Section 1201.117's menu of possible actions reinforces that an evidentiary hearing is not the automatic consequence of review or reopening.
