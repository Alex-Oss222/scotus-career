### Leebaert v. Harrington

Merits decision, February 17, 2004

#### Chronology and Posture

Decided February 17, 2004, on merits submission. Review of the Second Circuit's June 13, 2003 judgment, 332 F.3d 134. The questions are whether parental educational liberty requires an exemption from compulsory public-school health instruction and whether combining that claim with a religious-exercise claim requires heightened scrutiny.

The father objected to his son's attendance. The case comes on stipulated facts and summary judgment; he conceded that the attendance requirement satisfied rational-basis review.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

#### Opinion Topology

GINSBURG, J., delivered the opinion of the unanimous Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, SOUTER, THOMAS, and BREYER, JJ., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**Ordinary public-school instruction does not create a parental curricular veto.** A parent's constitutional right to direct a child's upbringing and education does not include a general right to exempt a child enrolled in public school from neutral ordinary instruction. A combined parental-rights and religious-exercise claim must establish the protected interference each claim requires; combining two unsupported claims does not itself require heightened scrutiny.

**Authority:** Ginsburg's opinion, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer, controls both claims and the disposition.

Brown v. Hot, Sexy & Safer Productions supplies the governing distinction between exposure to instruction and interference with protected personal or parental choices. Meyer v. Nebraska and Pierce v. Society of Sisters protect parental teaching and educational choice; they do not give each family control over the public school's lessons. Curtis concerned an optional program, so its result alone could not settle compulsory attendance. Brown addressed that additional feature and rejected the asserted general veto while preserving independently protected interests.

The stipulated record concerns attendance at health instruction. It establishes neither compelled profession of belief nor the targeting or discriminatory treatment that would call for a different religious-exercise inquiry. The conceded reasonable relationship between attendance and education therefore supports this rule. The father's reliance on Wisconsin v. Yoder fails because that decision addressed a distinctive interference with protected religious life and parental direction, rather than ordinary disagreement with a public-school lesson. The adequacy of the claim does not depend on the age, popularity or size of his religion.

The religious claim receives its own inquiry. Smith's treatment of neutral, generally applicable rules and Lukumi's protection against religious targeting are not displaced by labeling a claim parental. Shahar confirms the need to examine religious exercise independently rather than absorb it into another constitutional analysis. The Court's holding governs genuine instruction; enrollment is not a surrender of constitutional protection, and an educational label cannot shield actual coercion or religious discrimination.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Brown v. Hot, Sexy & Safer Productions, Inc.:** Applied for the compulsory-instruction boundary and the continuing protection against independently actionable interference.
- **Meyer v. Nebraska and Pierce v. Society of Sisters:** Preserved as protections of parental instruction and educational choice, which supply no general public-curriculum veto.
- **Elizabeth G. Curtis v. School Committee of Falmouth:** Distinguished at its optional-program scope; its religious-exercise inquiry remains applicable on its own terms.
- **Wisconsin v. Yoder:** Distinguished because the stipulated instructional dispute lacks its distinctive interference with protected religious life and parental direction.
- **Employment Division v. Smith and Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye v. City of Hialeah:** Applied at their respective neutral-rule and religious-targeting boundaries.
- **Shahar v. Bowers:** Applied for independent consideration of a religious-exercise claim.

#### Law After Decision

Neutral ordinary instruction may be compulsory despite parental disagreement. Courts must identify the actual interference with parental direction and examine an asserted religious burden independently. Combining constitutional labels does not supply an otherwise missing protected interference. The separate state-law exemptions continue to operate under state law.

#### Source Notes

Leebaert v. Harrington, 332 F.3d 134 (2d Cir. 2003). The federal case rests on the stipulated instructional record; the separate statutory exemptions are not a federal entitlement to avoid the general curriculum.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The affirmed federal judgment leaves the federal curricular and religious-exercise claims dismissed and denies the requested federal exemption. The independent state-law dismissal remains without prejudice; those claims remain eligible for adjudication in the appropriate state forum under ordinary law.

**End of entry: Leebaert v. Harrington, merits decision, February 17, 2004.**

### United States v. McFarland

Merits decision, February 19, 2004

#### Chronology and Posture

Decided February 19, 2004, on merits submission. Review of the Fifth Circuit's en banc judgment, No. 00-10569, 311 F.3d 376. The equally divided court affirmed the convictions and sentence. The question is whether the Commerce Clause requires a substantial individual interstate effect from each robbery of the operating assets of a local retail store purchasing interstate goods.

Four robberies took cash-register receipts from active stores. The related firearm convictions depend on their corresponding robbery predicates for the objection under review.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed against the commerce and wholly derivative firearm objections, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

#### Opinion Topology

KENNEDY, J., delivered the opinion of the unanimous Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, SCALIA, SOUTER, THOMAS, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**Direct robbery of operating business assets supplies the commercial connection.** The Hobbs Act may reach robbery that directly removes operating assets from an active business engaged in interstate commerce when the prosecution proves the Act's commerce element and the assets' actual commercial function beyond a reasonable doubt. Each robbery within that connected class need not have a separately substantial interstate effect.

**Authority:** Kennedy's opinion is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine adopt the commercial rule, its application and the disposition of the wholly derivative firearm objection.

United States v. Robinson supplies the controlling distinction between taking working capital from an interstate business and inferring commerce from a private victim's eventual spending. United States v. Lopez requires a real connection before the magnitude of class-wide effects can matter. Aggregation cannot supply a missing relationship, and the statutory commerce language is not an automatic constitutional safe harbor. Here the robberies removed cash-register funds from four operating retail stores whose inventories included goods obtained from outside Texas. The testimony identified those receipts as capital available for replenishment and the stores' commercial obligations. That immediate removal from active operations establishes the required business connection.

The defendant's demand for a separately substantial effect from each taking confuses the connection with its magnitude. Robinson permits small individual losses within the properly connected business-robbery class. The Government need not identify a particular cancelled inventory order to show removal of working capital; substitution of other available funds does not undo that removal. The Court relies on the proved operating function of the receipts, rather than a finding that purchases actually declined.

The preserved substantiality objection to the degree-of-effect instruction fails for the same reason. The charge permitted an inference and retained the Government's burden to prove the commerce element beyond a reasonable doubt; the governing rule does not make a business label conclusive proof. The four firearm convictions depend, on the objection presented, on the validity of their corresponding Hobbs Act predicates. Because the commerce challenge does not defeat those predicates, it supplies no derivative ground to disturb the firearm convictions.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **United States v. Robinson:** Reaffirmed and applied to direct removal of operating commercial capital, the absence of an individual-substantiality requirement and the wholly derivative firearm objection.
- **United States v. Lopez:** Applied to require an actual commercial mechanism before aggregation; neither a statutory label nor remote spending effects supply the missing relationship.

#### Law After Decision

The direct-business-assets rule governs these retail robberies. The prosecution must prove the enacted nexus and the assets' operating function beyond a reasonable doubt; slight individual magnitude does not defeat a properly connected application. The constitutional rule rests on interference with active commerce, not on the proposition that all stolen money may eventually be spent.

#### Source Notes

United States v. McFarland, 311 F.3d 376 (5th Cir. 2002) (en banc), No. 00-10569, judgment October 28, 2002, revised opinion December 13, 2002. The en banc division left no majority appellate rationale. Removal of operating receipts is distinct from proof of reduced inventory purchasing.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The four Hobbs Act convictions, the corresponding firearm convictions, and the sentence remain in force against the commerce and wholly derivative firearm objections under review. The existing judgment remains enforceable.

**End of entry: United States v. McFarland, merits decision, February 19, 2004.**

### Illinois v. Fisher, No. 03-374

Merits decision, February 23, 2004

#### Chronology and Posture

Decided February 23, 2004, on the submitted record. Review of the Illinois Appellate Court's reversal of Fisher's cocaine-possession conviction. The question is whether a pending discovery request or the central importance of destroyed, potentially useful evidence excuses the absence of police bad faith.

The police destroyed the substance under their ordinary procedures after four positive cocaine tests. Fisher had requested production before his prolonged absence from the proceedings. The state appellate judgment relied on federal due process.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

#### Opinion Topology

The Court issued a per curiam opinion. STEVENS, J., filed an opinion concurring in the judgment.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Per curiam opinion | The Court | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Eight-Justice controlling rationale supporting reversal and remand | Rule for potentially useful evidence, application, and remand |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Stevens | | Agrees with reversal and remand on a distinct ground | Retains the exceptional-fundamental-unfairness position; does not join an unconditional bad-faith rule |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**A discovery request does not change the rule for evidence of unknown exculpatory value.** When destroyed evidence was only potentially useful, due process requires a showing of police bad faith. A pending discovery request or the evidence's importance to the prosecution does not dispense with that requirement; evidence whose exculpatory character was apparent before destruction and for which reasonably available comparable evidence is lacking remains governed by Trombetta's distinct rule.

**Authority:** The per curiam opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Those eight Justices adopt the rule, its application and the remand; Stevens concurs in the judgment on the ground stated separately.

Arizona v. Youngblood distinguishes evidence known to be exculpatory from material that might help the defense if further tested. California v. Trombetta supplies the former category's apparent-exculpatory-value and comparable-evidence requirements. The material here tested positive for cocaine four times before its destruction. That history does not establish that a later defense test would have been exculpatory, and the record contains no allegation or finding of bad faith. Fisher therefore has not satisfied the federal condition applicable to the lost testing opportunity.

The contrary rule would treat the request itself, or the central place of the evidence in the prosecution, as a substitute for the missing constitutional condition. Neither fact supplies the material's unknown exculpatory character. A request may bear on an actual inquiry into knowledge and purpose; it creates no automatic exception. Kyles v. Whitley addresses suppression of favorable evidence and its cumulative lawful uses, not a presumption that destroyed testing material had favorable contents. The exceptional-unfairness concern expressed in the concurrence supplies no basis for sustaining this federal dismissal on the present facts.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Arizona v. Youngblood:** Applied to potentially useful evidence; a request or centrality alone does not displace its bad-faith requirement.
- **California v. Trombetta:** Preserved for apparently exculpatory evidence lacking reasonably available comparable evidence.
- **Kyles v. Whitley:** Distinguished because its suppression and cumulative-materiality rules presuppose favorable evidence, rather than establish the result of an unperformed test.

#### Law After Decision

The federal preservation inquiry continues to turn on the evidence category and its applicable conditions. A discovery request may be relevant proof of bad faith, but neither the request nor importance automatically changes the potentially-useful-evidence rule. States may enforce independent preservation and discovery protections under their own law.

#### Separate Writings

**Stevens, concurring in the judgment.** Stevens retains the position expressed in his Youngblood concurrence that destruction of evidence could make a trial fundamentally unfair in an exceptional case despite the absence of bad faith. He does not join an unconditional bad-faith rule covering every conceivable loss. Four inculpatory tests and the ordinary destruction shown here do not establish the exceptional unfairness he would require, so he agrees that this federal ground cannot support the judgment. Independent state discovery law remains available on its own terms.

#### Source Notes

Illinois v. Fisher, No. 03-374; Arizona v. Youngblood, 488 U.S. 51, 57–58, 61 (1988); California v. Trombetta, 467 U.S. 479, 488–489 (1984). The reviewed appellate judgment rested on federal due process; an independent Illinois discovery ground requires its own state-law decision.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The federal due-process basis for setting aside the conviction fails. On remand, the Illinois courts must address any properly preserved independent state-law ground and enter the resulting judgment under ordinary appellate law, consistently with the federal rule.

**End of entry: Illinois v. Fisher, merits decision, February 23, 2004.**

### Banks v. Dretke, No. 02-8286

Merits decision, February 24, 2004

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 8, 2003; decided February 24, 2004. Review of the Fifth Circuit's rejection of the Farr sentencing claim and denial of appellate access on the Cook transcript claim.

Banks filed his federal habeas application on March 7, 1996. The case concerns evidence of Farr's paid-informant role in the penalty presentation, an alternative penalty-assistance claim, and the later-disclosed transcript of Cook's preparation as a guilt witness.

#### Judgment

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Farr sentence claim | Reversed; conditional sentence writ required, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Scalia, Thomas | A conditional sentence writ; a concrete period for lawful capital resentencing or a lawful noncapital disposition. |
| Cook certificate denial | Reversed; issue-specific certificate and appellate consideration required, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer |  | An issue-specific certificate and appellate adjudication of the Cook pleading and constitutional questions. |

#### Opinion Topology

SOUTER, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, KENNEDY, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined in full, and in which SCALIA and THOMAS, JJ., joined as to the factual-development and Cook certificate portions. THOMAS, J., filed an opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part, in which SCALIA, J., joined.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Souter | The full-opinion joiners listed above, plus Scalia and Thomas | Unanimous factual-development and Cook certificate rulings | Holding 1: factual-development rules and consideration of Farr evidence; Holding 3: both certificate requirements and appellate proceedings |
| Opinion of the Court | Souter | The full-opinion joiners listed above | Seven-Justice ruling requiring sentence relief | Holding 2: Farr materiality and the conditional sentence writ |
| Concurrence in part and dissent in part | Thomas | Scalia | Joins factual development and the Cook appeal; would affirm denial of sentence relief | Rejects Farr materiality and finds no prejudice on the alternative penalty-assistance claim |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**1. Concealed facts may be considered under the applicable hearing rule.** For a properly presented habeas claim governed by former law, ordinary negligent failure to develop facts does not bar a necessary federal hearing; Townsend's deliberate-bypass qualification governs. A distinct state claim default or statutory restriction requires its own predicate, and an incomplete state record does not itself establish either.

Keeney v. Tamayo-Reyes retained that former-law rule. Banks presented the Farr suppression claim to the state courts; the later evidence supplied concealed facts supporting it. He questioned Farr and sought disclosure, while the State's assurances and uncorrected denials concealed Farr's paid relationship. This is not deliberate bypass. Texas's diligence argument identifies neither an independent adequate state claim bar nor a transformation of the existing claim into a different one. Vasquez v. Hillery distinguishes factual supplementation from such a new claim, and Coleman v. Thompson concerns an actual independent procedural default.

Carriger v. Stewart preserves former merits law for this pending capital application while reserving the temporal reach of section 2254(e)(2). That general temporal question remains unnecessary here: Michael Wayne Williams v. Taylor requires applicant-attributed lack of diligence before the statutory restriction operates, and the supported concealment and reasonable efforts defeat that trigger as well. Strickler v. Greene explains why reasonable reliance on disclosure representations is relevant; it does not require a freestanding cause ruling where no separate default is established.

**2. Farr's concealed paid role is material to the death sentence.** Suppressed favorable evidence is material when its supported lawful uses, considered with the whole relevant record, create a reasonable probability of a different lawful result sufficient to undermine confidence in that adjudication. The prisoner must make that showing; once a material Brady violation is established, no additional harmless-error showing is required.

Farr's paid cooperation and role in bringing about the gun-retrieval encounter change the future-dangerousness presentation. Timely disclosure would have supported investigation, preparation and examination about why the encounter occurred, as well as impeachment of the denials of cooperation. It would have undermined the impression that Farr independently corroborated Banks's willingness to commit future armed robberies. The denials and disclosure assurances reinforced the misleading account, while the defense's attempted impeachment was weakened by the limited credibility of its witnesses.

Kyles v. Whitley requires consideration of these lawful uses together, rather than an isolated credibility deduction or a test of whether enough aggravating evidence remains. Strickler requires separate attention to confidence in the penalty decision. The murder, the pistol assault described by Jefferson and Banks's acknowledged willingness to obtain a gun remain serious evidence. They do not make the concealed origin and purpose of the Farr encounter merely cumulative. The inquiry concerns the jury's lawful death-selection decision, not simply eligibility or proof of guilt. The complete record establishes materiality and requires a conditional sentence writ.

**3. Cook's transcript claim meets both certificate requirements.** When a habeas claim is rejected on a procedural pleading ground, an issue-specific certificate requires reasonable debate both about the validity of the constitutional claim and about the procedural ruling. Banks satisfies both requirements concerning the concealed Cook rehearsal transcript and whether the federal litigation tried that claim by implied consent.

Slack v. McDaniel supplies both conditions, and Miller-El v. Cockrell distinguishes threshold debatability from ultimate entitlement. The transcript raises a substantial suppression question about the preparation of an important guilt witness. Its receipt in evidence, the prosecutor's examination about coaching and disclosure, and the ensuing suppression submissions support reasonable disagreement about the pleading ruling. Rule 15(b) requires examination of what the parties actually litigated; admission of evidence without objection alone is not consent to try every claim involving it. The appellate court must decide the actual pleading and constitutional questions under the governing rules.

**Authority:** Souter's opinion is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer. Scalia and Thomas join Holdings 1 and3 and their procedural consequences. Holding 2 and the Farr sentence remedy therefore have seven direct joins; the factual-development and Cook certificate holdings have nine. The seven granting full sentence relief do not reach the alternative penalty-assistance ground because it seeks the same complete remedy.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Townsend v. Sain and Keeney v. Tamayo-Reyes:** Applied for the deliberate-bypass qualification governing necessary factual development of a properly presented former-law claim.
- **Lindh v. Murphy and Carriger v. Stewart:** Applied to the pending application's former merits framework; Carriger's separate capital hearing-transition question retains its reserved status.
- **Michael Wayne Williams v. Taylor:** Applied for the applicant-attributed-failure trigger, which concealment and reasonable efforts do not establish merely because later discovery succeeds.
- **Vasquez v. Hillery and Coleman v. Thompson:** Applied to distinguish supplementation of a presented claim from a new claim or an actual independent state procedural default.
- **Brady v. Maryland, Kyles v. Whitley and Strickler v. Greene:** Applied to favorable suppression, cumulative lawful uses, phase-specific materiality and reasonable reliance on disclosure representations.
- **Slack v. McDaniel, Miller-El v. Cockrell and Hohn v. United States:** Applied for the two-part procedural certificate standard, its threshold character and review of certificate denial.
- **Strickland v. Washington and Terry Williams v. Taylor:** Retain their independent assistance standards; the Court's complete Farr sentence remedy makes an additional ruling on that alternative unnecessary.

#### Law After Decision

Former-law factual development is governed by the actual deliberate-bypass rule, with separate defaults and statutory restrictions assessed under their own triggers. Concealed informant evidence must be evaluated through all supported lawful uses in the relevant penalty record. A material suppression violation entitles Banks to the stated sentence remedy. A procedural certificate requires both constitutional and procedural debatability, and grants appellate access at the identified issue-specific scope.

#### Separate Writings

**Thomas, joined by Scalia, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** They join the ruling permitting consideration of the developed evidence and the Cook certificate disposition. They reject Farr materiality after considering disclosure's investigative and impeachment uses: the murder, the independent pistol-assault account and Banks's admitted gun assistance leave them confident in the penalty result despite Farr's concealed role.

Because they reject the Farr route, they also address the independently presented penalty-assistance claim. Assuming deficient investigation and considering the full supported proffer, they find no reasonable probability of a different lawful sentence. The proffer includes family violence and harsh discipline, the skin condition and resulting isolation, asserted learning or developmental limitations, and the fuller Jefferson account. Their conclusion rests on whole-record prejudice under Strickland and Terry Williams, rather than excluding later evidence as automatically unexhausted or applying amended merits deference. They would affirm denial of sentence relief while allowing the Cook appeal.

#### Source Notes

Banks v. Dretke, No. 02-8286; Fifth Circuit judgment August 20, 2002, 48 Fed. Appx. 104. The federal application predates AEDPA; the later certificate proceeding has a distinct temporal basis. The Jefferson threat is attributed to testimony; the disposition does not depend on treating every reported threat as Banks's personal admission.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The district court must implement a conditional sentence writ and fix a reasonable, concrete period within which Texas must undertake lawful capital resentencing or replace the death sentence with a lawful noncapital disposition. The murder conviction remains intact on the Farr penalty claim.

The Cook certificate admits the concealed-transcript claim and its implied-consent issue to appeal. The appellate court must adjudicate the actual pleading and constitutional questions on the litigation record; the certificate does not resolve ultimate pleading or constitutional entitlement.

**End of entry: Banks v. Dretke, merits decision, February 24, 2004.**

### Doe v. Chao, No. 02-1377

Merits decision, February 24, 2004

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 3, 2003; decided February 24, 2004. Review of the Fourth Circuit's denial of the Privacy Act's $1,000 minimum without proof of actual damages.

The Department of Labor used and disclosed Social Security numbers in administering benefits claims. The district court accepted Doe's uncontroverted account of distress and awarded the statutory minimum. Only Doe seeks review of the monetary prerequisite.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas.

**Opposing:** Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer.

#### Opinion Topology

SCALIA, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and O'CONNOR, KENNEDY, SOUTER, and THOMAS, JJ., joined. GINSBURG, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which STEVENS and BREYER, JJ., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**Actual damages are a prerequisite to the Privacy Act's minimum monetary recovery.** A claimant seeking damages under 5 U.S.C. section 552a(g)(4) must establish actual damages caused by the qualifying intentional or willful violation before receiving the statutory minimum of $1,000. The adverse effect that permits suit under section 552a(g)(1)(D) does not by itself satisfy that additional monetary condition.

**Authority:** Scalia's opinion, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, controls the prerequisite, its application and affirmance.

The statute first identifies the conduct and culpability that permit a monetary claim and then specifies actual damages as the recoverable loss. The minimum follows that authorization and applies to a person entitled to recovery under it. Reading the proviso as an unconditional alternative to actual damages would detach entitlement from the immediately preceding payment condition. The opening liability language establishes the class that may invoke the remedy; it does not erase the limits Congress places on that remedy.

The contrary construction has force because a fixed minimum can compensate harms difficult to price. But Congress tied this minimum to an existing entitlement to recovery, rather than making every qualifying adverse effect independently sufficient for payment. The Court must enforce that distinction within the monetary authorization Congress enacted. The express monetary authorization is applied within its enacted terms.

J. Roderick MacArthur Foundation and Lance E. Lindblom v. Federal Bureau of Investigation distinguishes a personal adverse effect from final monetary entitlement and reserved this very question. Doe's accepted distress and the unchallenged violation and culpability establish the predicates assumed here; they do not resolve the separate actual-damages prerequisite. Because Doe seeks the minimum without the additional showing required by the judgment under review, the denial stands. The complete definition of actual damages, including compensable emotional loss and its required proof, lies outside the granted question and remains open.

#### Precedent Treatment

**J. Roderick MacArthur Foundation and Lance E. Lindblom v. Federal Bureau of Investigation:** Reaffirmed at its personal-adverse-effect scope. Its reserved minimum-recovery question is resolved by requiring actual damages before the minimum becomes available; its adverse-effect holding is not converted into a monetary award.

#### Law After Decision

A Privacy Act plaintiff must distinguish the violation, culpability and adverse-effect predicates from the actual-damages condition for monetary recovery. Once qualifying actual damages are established, the statutory minimum governs the amount recoverable when those damages are smaller. The complete definition and proof of actual damages remain outside this decision's granted scope.

#### Separate Writings

**Ginsburg, joined by Stevens and Breyer, dissenting.** They would restore Doe's $1,000 award. In their reading, the opening liability command identifies the persons entitled to recovery once an intentional or willful violation causes a genuine adverse effect, and the proviso supplies an independent minimum for those persons. The actual-damages language permits larger compensation for proved loss rather than imposing a further threshold on the minimum.

They distinguish a fixed amount expressly enacted by Congress from unlimited presumed general damages. The intentional-or-willful requirement and genuine adverse effect restrict recovery and answer the concern that a harmless technical violation would produce an award. Breyer's emphasis on that culpability limit is part of the shared dissent. MacArthur left this monetary issue open; its separation of injury from ultimate recovery does not, for the dissenters, decide which reading of the proviso is correct.

#### Source Notes

5 U.S.C. section 552a(g)(1)(D), (g)(4); Doe v. Chao, No. 02-1377. The grant concerns access to the minimum, with the violation, intentional-or-willful conduct and Doe's adverse effect accepted for this review.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Fourth Circuit's denial of Doe's monetary recovery remains in force. The separate prospective order and obligations continue under their existing terms.

**End of entry: Doe v. Chao, merits decision, February 24, 2004.**

### General Dynamics Land Systems, Inc. v. Cline, No. 02-1080

Merits decision, February 24, 2004

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 12, 2003; decided February 24, 2004. Review of the Sixth Circuit's recognition of an ADEA claim by workers in their forties challenging a benefit preference for older workers.

A collective agreement preserved retirement health benefits for employees fifty and older while withdrawing that future benefit for younger workers. The question is whether the ADEA prohibits that relative preference when both groups are within the protected age range.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

#### Opinion Topology

BREYER, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, SOUTER, and GINSBURG, JJ., joined. THOMAS, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which KENNEDY, J., joined. SCALIA, J., filed a dissenting opinion.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**The ADEA's age-discrimination prohibition protects against disadvantage because of advancing age.** The Age Discrimination in Employment Act does not forbid an employer from favoring older workers over younger workers merely because both groups are at least forty. A claimant must first establish disadvantage of the kind the statutory prohibition covers before invoking its remedies.

**Authority:** Breyer's opinion is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter and Ginsburg. Those six Justices adopt the directional construction, its application and the disposition.

The word age must be read within the statute Congress enacted. Its findings and structure address the obstacles older workers face because they are older. The statutory protection is therefore directional: entering the protected forty-and-over class does not make every age distinction within that class unlawful. The collective agreement's preservation of retirement health benefits for workers fifty and older favors the older group. The younger protected workers' objection to that preference alone does not state this ADEA claim.

Hazen Paper Co. v. Biggins separates age motivation from other features, such as pension tenure, that may correlate with age. O'Connor v. Consolidated Coin Caterers Corp. explains that a replacement's membership in the protected class does not defeat an older worker's claim of discrimination in favor of a substantially younger replacement. Neither decision answers the distinct directional-coverage question by treating all age differences symmetrically. Their proof rules remain consistent with the protection against disadvantage because of advancing age.

The dissent's reading gives the phrase because of age a bilateral meaning when isolated from the enacted scheme. The Court instead reads the prohibition, protected class and statutory findings together. The EEOC regulation's contrary position cannot override that clear contextual construction. The Court resolves the statutory meaning directly; choosing between different degrees of deference to an otherwise permissible agency construction is unnecessary to this conclusion.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Hazen Paper Co. v. Biggins:** Preserved for the distinction between actual age motivation and pension-related or other correlated factors; it did not settle directional statutory coverage.
- **O'Connor v. Consolidated Coin Caterers Corp.:** Preserved for older-worker discrimination relative to substantially younger replacements even within the protected class; its proof rule does not make the prohibition bilateral.

#### Law After Decision

The ADEA reaches disadvantage because of advancing age. A preference for older workers over younger protected workers does not, by that feature alone, violate its age-discrimination prohibition. Courts must determine whether the challenged disadvantage falls within that protection before applying the statute's proof and remedial rules.

#### Separate Writings

**Thomas, joined by Kennedy, dissenting.** They read the enacted prohibition on discrimination because of age as covering either direction within the protected class. Congress specified the age-forty boundary but did not place a second, older-versus-younger restriction in the operative command. They reject narrowing that language through general statutory purpose or assumptions about the usual victim of age discrimination. They would sustain statutory coverage and leave remaining liability and defenses for ordinary proceedings.

**Scalia, dissenting.** Scalia would sustain coverage because the EEOC's bilateral interpretation is a permissible construction entitled to acceptance. His ground does not require agreement that the employees' reading is the single best interpretation. The regulation treats discrimination between protected-age workers bilaterally. Its separate additional-benefits provision says such benefits may be lawful if the employer has a reasonable basis to conclude they will counteract problems related to age discrimination, while forbidding their use to carry out otherwise prohibited practices. Scalia would preserve that qualification for application below with any statutory defenses, rather than exclude this claim categorically.

#### Source Notes

29 U.S.C. sections 621, 623(a), 631(a); 29 C.F.R. section 1625.2 (2003); General Dynamics Land Systems, Inc. v. Cline, No. 02-1080. The challenged explicit age cutoff presents a coverage question, rather than a dispute about an inferred age motive.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The federal claim that the preference for older employees itself violates the ADEA must be dismissed. The case returns for removal of that reviewed directional-discrimination theory and entry of judgment consistent with the Court's construction.

**End of entry: General Dynamics Land Systems, Inc. v. Cline, merits decision, February 24, 2004.**

### Groh v. Ramirez, No. 02-811

Merits decision, February 24, 2004

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 4, 2003; decided February 24, 2004. Review of the Ninth Circuit's reinstatement of the Fourth Amendment damages claim against the officer who prepared and executed a home-search warrant in 1997.

The warrant identified no property to be seized and incorporated no list. The questions are whether the application or asserted conduct cures that defect and whether the preparing officer has qualified immunity.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

#### Opinion Topology

STEVENS, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and O'CONNOR, SOUTER, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined in full, and in which KENNEDY, J., joined as to the particularity holding. KENNEDY, J., filed an opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part. THOMAS, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which SCALIA, J., joined.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stevens | The full-opinion joiners listed above, plus Kennedy | Seven-Justice holding that this search violates the Fourth Amendment | Holding 1: the wholly deficient warrant fails particularity and cannot authorize this search |
| Opinion of the Court | Stevens | The full-opinion joiners listed above | Six-Justice ruling denying qualified immunity and affirming | Holding 2: the preparer's reliance on the obvious complete omission was not objectively reasonable |
| Concurrence in part and dissent in part | Kennedy | | Agrees with the violation; would grant immunity | Joins Holding 1 but rejects Holding 2 and the disposition |
| Dissent | Thomas | Scalia | Would examine execution's actual reasonableness and independently grant immunity | Alternative merits and notice grounds |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**1. The warrant must identify what the officers are authorized to seize.** A warrant for a home search that identifies no things to be seized and incorporates no document identifying them fails the Fourth Amendment's particularity requirement, and conducting this home search under that wholly deficient authorization violates the Fourth Amendment. The preparing officer's knowledge of an application does not supply the judicial limitation omitted from the warrant.

The warrant described the premises where its form called for the objects of the search. It listed no items. The separate application and affidavit described them, but the warrant neither incorporated those documents nor brought their terms into the authorization. The warrant therefore failed on its face. Particularity protects the homeowner and limits execution through a judicial command; an officer's intended restraint is not a substitute for that command.

Wilson v. Layne explains that a warrant's authorized scope limits execution. The particularity text itself makes the omitted description essential. The contrary contention that a reasonable search can be reconstructed from the application and actual restraint would replace the warrant's missing limitation with the officer's private understanding. The disputed assertion of an oral explanation cannot support summary judgment against the homeowners and would not rewrite the judicial authorization in any event.

**2. The preparing officer cannot reasonably rely on this obvious complete omission.** Qualified immunity does not protect the officer who prepared and executed this warrant when its complete failure to identify any items made the constitutional defect apparent under law existing at the time of the search. The magistrate's signature does not make objectively reasonable the preparer's reliance on an omission he was responsible for supplying.

United States v. Leon had already identified a warrant so facially deficient in describing things to be seized as outside reasonable reliance. The Fourth Amendment's express demand and that preconduct authority supplied notice in 1997. Massachusetts v. Sheppard involved reliance on a judge's undertaking to correct a defective form after the officer brought the problem to the judge; it does not excuse this complete omission by the preparing officer. The apparent clerical origin of an error does not determine whether executing the resulting warrant was objectively reasonable.

Hope v. Pelzer describes the current notice inquiry, but its later date supplies no retroactive warning for 1997 conduct. The controlling notice here comes from the text and existing authority, not a requirement that an earlier case reproduce every feature of this form. The refusal to grant immunity permits ordinary adjudication of the reviewed individual claim.

**Authority:** Stevens's opinion is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Kennedy joins Holding 1 but dissents from Holding 2 and the disposition. All nine agree that the warrant is facially defective; the holding that this search was unconstitutional has seven direct joins; immunity and the affirmance have six.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **United States v. Leon:** Applied for the preconduct proposition that an obvious facial failure to specify items defeats objectively reasonable reliance.
- **Massachusetts v. Sheppard:** Distinguished by the officer's reliance there on the judge's undertaking to correct the form; Groh prepared the complete omission at issue here.
- **Wilson v. Layne:** Applied for the relationship between judicial authorization and execution, without treating its later date as notice for 1997 conduct.
- **Hope v. Pelzer:** Applied as the governing notice method, while the required warning is supplied by the constitutional text and preconduct authority.

#### Law After Decision

The judicial warrant must supply its particularized authorization. An unincorporated application and the preparer's private knowledge do not cure a complete omission of items to be seized. The responsible preparer receives no qualified immunity for executing the obviously deficient warrant on these accepted facts; the individual damages claim proceeds under ordinary liability and remedy rules.

#### Separate Writings

**Kennedy, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** He agrees that the warrant violated particularity. He would nevertheless grant immunity because the detailed application, magistrate's approval and clerical character of the omission made the officer's mistaken reliance objectively reasonable in the legal circumstances. He distinguishes the existence of a defect from sufficiently clear notice imposing individual liability.

**Thomas, joined by Scalia, dissenting.** They resist treating a defective warrant as automatically making the execution an unreasonable warrantless search. On the officer's asserted account of a confined search following the detailed application, they would examine the actual reasonableness of execution rather than make the form defect conclusive. That account remains an assertion, including the disputed oral explanation. Independently, they would grant qualified immunity even assuming a constitutional violation, because the application and judicial approval leave sufficient uncertainty about individual liability. The Court's denial of immunity therefore conflicts with both their alternative merits approach and their separate notice ground.

#### Source Notes

Groh v. Ramirez, No. 02-811; U.S. Const., Amend. IV; United States v. Leon, 468 U.S. 897, 923 (1984); Massachusetts v. Sheppard, 468 U.S. 981 (1984). The search occurred in 1997. The asserted oral notification is disputed and is not a finding against the homeowners.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The reinstated claim against Groh proceeds in the district court. On the accepted facts he is not entitled to qualified immunity. The district court must adjudicate the remaining disputed facts, causation, and compensable injury under ordinary liability and remedy rules, consistently with the particularity and immunity holdings.

### Olympic Airways v. Husain, No. 02-1348

Merits decision, February 24, 2004

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 12, 2003; decided February 24, 2004. Review of the Ninth Circuit's affirmance of liability following a bench trial over a passenger's death on an international flight.

An asthmatic passenger sat near the smoking section. His wife repeatedly asked the attendant to move him, explaining his medical vulnerability. The question is whether the established unusual refusal of assistance can be an Article 17 accident contributing to the death.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 6–2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg.

**Opposing:** O'Connor and Scalia.

**Not participating:** Breyer. He took no part in consideration or decision; no reason is publicly stated.

#### Opinion Topology

THOMAS, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, KENNEDY, SOUTER, and GINSBURG, JJ., joined. SCALIA, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which O'CONNOR, J., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**An unusual refusal of assistance can be an accident in the causal chain.** Under Article 17 of the Warsaw Convention, a carrier's unexpected or unusual refusal to respond to a passenger's known medical need may be an external event contributing to death or bodily injury and thus an accident. The claimant must establish that qualifying event and its causal contribution; injury or a failure to exercise reasonable care alone does not satisfy the treaty.

**Authority:** Thomas's opinion is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg. Those six Justices adopt the event rule, its application and affirmance.

Air France v. Saks defines an accident by an unexpected or unusual event external to the passenger and requires examination of the causal chain, rather than only the final physical mechanism of injury. An internal reaction to ordinary aircraft operation is different. Dr.Hanson's death followed exposure to smoke, but the relevant chain includes the attendant's repeated refusal to assist after his wife explained his vulnerability and requested reseating. The trial court found that response unusual in light of the available accommodation and the carrier's policies and practices. Its unusual character and causal role are established for the question under review.

The airline's proposed division between action and inaction does not answer whether this concrete response was an event. A deliberate refusal to provide requested assistance can be unexpected external conduct even though it leaves a passenger in an existing seat. Treating the smoke as the only possible event would remove that link from the causal inquiry Saks requires. The ruling depends on the found unusual response, rather than making every failure to assist or every departure from reasonable care an accident.

The attendant's response to the third request included an invitation to seek another passenger's seat without crew assistance, and the plane was not actually full despite the attendant's representations. Those facts are part of the encounter considered below. The accepted unusual-refusal finding supplies the treaty event; the Court need not replace it with a new finding that the misinformation independently caused the death. Zicherman v. Korean Air Lines Co. keeps treaty liability conditions distinct from domestic compensable categories, and El Al Israel Airlines v. Tsui Yuan Tseng preserves that treaty framework. The existing judgment survives the reviewed accident objection.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Air France v. Saks:** Applied to the unusual external link in a causal chain; an accepted unusual refusal is distinguished from an internal reaction to ordinary operation.
- **Zicherman v. Korean Air Lines Co.:** Preserved for the distinction between treaty liability conditions and domestic rules governing compensable damage categories.
- **El Al Israel Airlines, Ltd. v. Tsui Yuan Tseng:** Preserved for the Convention's governing remedial framework.

#### Law After Decision

A specifically established unusual refusal of assistance may satisfy Article 17 when it is external to the passenger and contributes causally to the injury. Courts must identify the event and causal link rather than substitute negligence or injury alone for the treaty condition. The applicable domestic damage rules remain distinct from that liability inquiry.

#### Separate Writings

**Scalia, joined by O'Connor, dissenting.** They would vacate and remand. In their reading of Saks and the treaty text, failure to alter ordinary cabin conditions is not itself the event that Article 17 requires. Calling nonperformance an unexpected response risks converting the accident condition into a duty-of-care inquiry.

They distinguish the attendant's affirmative statement that the plane was full. A false statement may be an external event even if mere nonperformance is not. They would have the lower courts examine any properly available theory based on that affirmative conduct and its actual causal significance under the existing record and ordinary preservation rules. They do not assume that the misinformation independently caused the injury or that the unoccupied seats establish that separate causal link.

#### Source Notes

Warsaw Convention, Article 17; Air France v. Saks, 470 U.S. 392,405–406(1985); Olympic Airways v. Husain, No. 02-1348. The flight occurred in January 1998. The controlling application rests on the trial court's established unusual-refusal and causal findings.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The existing liability judgment remains in force against the Article 17 accident objection and remains enforceable under its existing terms.

**End of entry: Olympic Airways v. Husain, merits decision, February 24, 2004.**

### Locke v. Davey, No. 02-1315

Merits decision, February 25, 2004

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 2, 2003; decided February 25, 2004. On certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, which reversed summary judgment for Washington. The question is whether excluding pursuit of a devotional theology degree from the Promise Scholarship violates the Free Exercise Clause. Davey attended an eligible religious college and pursued pastoral ministries and business; the devotional character of the pastoral degree is undisputed.

#### Judgment

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Refusal of payment for Davey's pastoral-formation degree | Reversed, 6–3 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Reversal within the granted Free Exercise claim concerning the devotional degree. |

The four Justices opposing restoration without a remand seek different relief. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela would remand any preserved genuinely separated secular component. Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas would remove the devotional-degree condition altogether and require equal consideration under the remaining lawful requirements.

#### Opinion Topology

SOUTER, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STEVENS, O'CONNOR, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined. STONE-ZSELA, C. J., filed an opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part. SCALIA, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which KENNEDY and THOMAS, JJ., joined.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Souter | The full-opinion joiners listed above | Supports both judgment components | Five-Justice permission rationale and complete restoration of the State's judgment |
| Concurrence in part and dissent in part | Chief Justice Stone-Zsela | | Supports devotional-formation reversal; opposes restoration without a limited remand | Distinct actual-function ground; would remand any preserved genuinely separated secular component |
| Dissent | Scalia | Kennedy, Thomas | Opposes both judgment components | Would remove the devotional-degree condition and require equal scholarship consideration |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### A State may decline support for the devotional ministry degree presented here

**Controlling proposition:** The Free Exercise Clause permits a State to exclude pursuit of a devotional degree preparing the student for ministry from this individual educational scholarship while allowing religious students, religious institutions, individual theology courses and secular courses of study to remain eligible. The permission concerns the devotional-degree condition as applied to Davey's undisputed pastoral training; it does not authorize disqualification merely because of religious belief, institutional affiliation or an isolated theology course.

**Authority:** The Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies five direct joins to the rule, application and remedy. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela supports reversal concerning devotional formation on a distinct ground and does not join this rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** Washington may make this confined choice against supporting preparation for ministry even though the Establishment Clause does not require exclusion of the student benefit. Walz recognizes room between what the Religion Clauses require and prohibit. Witters preserves individual vocational assistance as a permissible relationship; permission to include religious training does not establish an obligation to support every devotional degree.

The actual award is student assistance. Funds pass through the institution for distribution to the student and may meet tuition, room, board and other educational expenses. It is not the restricted provider purchase adjudicated in Zelman or the ordinary integrated tuition in Strout. Those decisions establish the importance of actual function but do not eliminate the separate Free Exercise question here. Washington permits religious-school attendance, religious students and theology courses. It also allows a scholarship for a secular degree at another institution while devotional study occurs elsewhere. The exclusion targets the course of devotional preparation for ministry rather than a religious identity.

The contrary equality argument correctly insists that K.D.M. and Columbia Union protect eligible secular services and work from exclusion based on religion. But this benefit is conditioned on the course of study, and the undisputed pastoral-ministry major falls within the confined condition. Davey's business double major does not convert the scholarship into the restricted secular-department grant adjudicated in Columbia Union. The State need not establish that every dollar purchases prohibited devotion before declining this particular ministry support. The Ninth Circuit's contrary Free Exercise judgment is reversed, and the State's favorable judgment on the granted claim is restored.

**Operative qualification:** The parties agree that the statute's theology-degree term covers degrees devotional in nature or designed to induce religious faith. That agreed application supplies this decision's scope.

#### Precedent Treatment

Walz v. Tax Commission is applied for the distinction between constitutional prohibition and permissible separation. Witters v. Washington Department of Services for the Blind remains effective for the individual-benefit relationship; its permission does not compel support for the devotional degree here. Strout v. Albanese retains its refusal rule for proved integrated tuition, and Zelman v. Simmons-Harris retains its rule for restricted purchases; their payment relationships differ from this scholarship. Agostini v. Felton retains the actual-function inquiry rather than an identity presumption. K.D.M. v. Reedsport School District and Columbia Union College v. Oliver retain equal eligibility for independently identifiable secular services and work. Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye, Inc. v. City of Hialeah retains its religious-targeting rule; the Court distinguishes this confined ministry-support choice from the disability there.

#### Law After Decision

States may retain the confined devotional-ministry-degree condition described here within an otherwise available individual education scholarship. Existing protection for religious students, eligible religious institutions, individual theology courses and separately eligible secular work continues. The permissive Free Exercise holding does not rest on a constitutional command to exclude these payments.

#### Separate Writings

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela concurs in reversal insofar as the judgment compels payment for the established pastoral-formation function, and dissents from ending any preserved genuinely separated secular claim. He applies Strout's actual-function refusal rule and Columbia Union's requirement that proof concern the work the public funds purchase. The claimant must identify the denied benefit and unequal treatment; the State's funded-function justification must match the actual course and payment design. Religious governance, faith and speculative freeing of resources do not establish a devotional purchase. The scholarship's actual terms must determine whether independently eligible secular study exists; the business major alone proves neither separation nor inseparability. He would remand that preserved question for equal consideration, while rejecting unconditional payment and damages on the devotional-subsidy theory.

Scalia, joined by Kennedy and Thomas, dissents. The actual award is independently directed educational assistance, including living costs, within Witters's surviving relationship. Under Lukumi and the equality principles of K.D.M. and Columbia Union, excluding an otherwise eligible student for devotional study requires a compelling justification and narrow tailoring. A historical preference against ministry support does not supply the necessary justification for withholding this student benefit. Allowing a business degree only at another institution does not answer the discrimination at the eligible college. The dissent would require equal consideration under the remaining lawful academic, income and enrollment conditions and separate adjudication of any properly presented monetary claim.

#### Source Notes

U.S. Const., Amdt. 1; Wash. Rev. Code §28B.10.814; Wash. Admin. Code §§250-80-020, 250-80-050 and 250-80-060. The institution distributes the funds to the student for educational expenses. The State requires institutional enrollment certification; the form Davey declined to sign was requested by the college, and his refusal supplies no independent default. The program allows support for a secular degree at a different institution while devotional theology is pursued elsewhere.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The State's favorable judgment on the granted Free Exercise exclusion claim is restored without a remand for a preserved separated secular component. The case returns through the ordinary mandate to effectuate that reversal and restoration.

### Muhammad v. Close, No. 02-9065

Merits decision, February 25, 2004

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 1, 2003; decided February 25, 2004. On certiorari to the Sixth Circuit. The question is whether Heck bars a prison-discipline-related damages claim compatible with the operative custody judgment. Muhammad alleges that an inflated threatening-behavior charge caused six days of retaliatory prehearing detention, accepts the subsequent insolence adjudication, and alleges no lost credit or extended sentence.

#### Judgment

**Vacated and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

#### Opinion Topology

The Court delivered a unanimous per curiam opinion, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**Controlling proposition:** Heck's favorable-invalidation prerequisite does not bar a §1983 claim whose success leaves the prisoner's operative conviction, sentence and release-related disciplinary determination valid. Courts must compare the actual civil theory and its necessary legal consequence with the identified custody determination; the occurrence of prison discipline alone does not trigger the prerequisite.

**Authority:** The unanimous per curiam opinion supplies nine direct joins to this rule, its application and the vacatur-and-remand remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** Heck preserves independent injuries compatible with the outstanding judgment. Edwards v. Balisok applies the same necessary-invalidity inquiry to discipline affecting earned credits; a procedural label does not decide the issue. Randell requires identification of the operative custody determination before comparing it with the civil theory.

Muhammad accepts the insolence adjudication and challenges an allegedly retaliatory overcharge that caused six days of prehearing detention. Success on that theory would invalidate neither the accepted adjudication nor the duration of his sentence. Close's reliance on disciplinary finality therefore cannot sustain the Sixth Circuit's categorical bar. The district court instead found the retaliation evidence insufficient, and the appellate court must consider that independent ground and the remaining preserved defenses under ordinary civil law. Any independently applicable administrative exhaustion under the PLRA remains distinct from Heck's favorable-invalidation requirement.

#### Precedent Treatment

Heck v. Humphrey is applied to independent injuries compatible with a valid custody judgment. Edwards v. Balisok retains its necessary-invalidity rule for credit adjudications and its separate treatment of genuinely independent injuries. Randell v. Johnson is applied for comparison with the actual operative determination; the present claim falls within that common threshold rule.

#### Law After Decision

A prison-discipline connection alone cannot bar a compatible civil injury claim. Actual success must necessarily invalidate the pertinent custody determination before favorable invalidation is required. Claims within that rule continue under ordinary civil requirements.

#### Source Notes

42 U.S.C. §§1983 and 1997e(a). Heck v. Humphrey; Edwards v. Balisok; Randell v. Johnson.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

On remand, the Sixth Circuit must review the district court's independent retaliation-evidence basis for summary judgment and any remaining properly preserved defenses without applying the rejected categorical Heck bar. The accepted insolence determination remains intact.

#### Chronology and Posture

### Freedom From Religion Foundation, Inc. v. McCallum

Merits decision, February 26, 2004

#### Chronology and Posture

Decided February 26, 2004, after plenary review. Review of Seventh Circuit No. 02-3102, 324 F.3d 880. The Seventh Circuit affirmed the bench-trial rejection of the taxpayer challenge to Department of Corrections payments for Faith Works rehabilitation placements. The question is whether voluntary selection from secular and religious alternatives sustains payment for integrated devotional treatment. The separate Workforce Development judgment is outside this review.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

#### Opinion Topology

BREYER, J., delivered the opinion of the unanimous Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, SOUTER, THOMAS, and GINSBURG, JJ., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The identified expenditure challenge supplies the standing predicate

**Controlling proposition:** The taxpayer plaintiffs may pursue this challenge to identified state contract payments as public financing of religious treatment. The accepted taxpayer-spending and Establishment Clause nexus rests on the Department of Corrections reimbursements and the prospective expenditure relief sought, rather than a generalized objection to religious recommendations.

**Authority and explanation:** All nine Justices directly accept this threshold on the tried expenditure claim. The challenged governmental action is payment of part of placement costs under the Faith Works contract, and the asserted constitutional limit concerns that use of public funds. That concrete relationship permits the Court to reach the granted financing question.

##### Contract payments are governed by the work the State purchases

**Controlling proposition:** Government may not finance devotional formation integrated into the rehabilitation service it purchases through restricted contract payments merely because an offender voluntarily selects the provider from options including a secular alternative. A claimant must establish the actual financed religious function; religious affiliation, a religious theme elsewhere in the institution or speculative freeing of other resources is insufficient. Independently identifiable secular services remain eligible when documented operational separation and reliable accounting preserve their secular function.

**Authority:** The unanimous Opinion of the Court supplies nine direct joins to the financing rule, the rejection of the private-choice-only rationale and the payment-specific remand.

**Controlling explanation:** Agostini directs attention to the governmental function, Mitchell requires actual-use proof and effective relief at the proved scope, and Columbia Union protects genuinely separated secular work. Strout and Zelman establish that private selection does not change the character of an integrated devotional service government actually buys. These rules apply to correctional treatment as to the public purchases they already govern.

Wisconsin reimburses part of the cost of a selected placement under its contract with Faith Works, whose treatment integrates Christianity. The offender selects among contracted providers rather than receives an independently distributed grant for broad personal expenses. Locke's individual scholarship and its confined permission not to support ministry preparation therefore supply no private-choice immunity for this contractual expenditure.

The accepted findings of secular alternatives, nonbinding recommendations and absence of religious favoritism remain effective. The longer program's attractiveness does not itself establish coercion; voluntariness, however, answers a different question from what government purchases. The State's secular rehabilitative purpose cannot authorize a proved devotional purchase.

The appellate account does not settle the contract's reimbursable cost categories, operative secular-use restrictions or full extent of actual separation. The lower courts must apply the financing rule to the trial record rather than presume either that every provider activity is publicly financed or that the reimbursements pay only secular care. Established findings of a particular financed use remain binding for this work; further findings are required only to resolve a material application gap.

##### Effective relief must reach the proved payment practice

**Controlling proposition:** The remand court must enjoin public payments for every established integrated devotional purchase at the supported contract and placement scope, while preserving reliably separated secular services. Responsible defendants seeking a narrower restriction must demonstrate that it fully cures the proved expenditure; public funding of a claimed secular remainder requires documented operational and accounting separation.

**Authority:** The unanimous Opinion of the Court directly adopts this implementation of the financing rule and its conditional, record-specific remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** Mitchell requires correction of established financed religious use without demanding reproof or waiting for every application to be quantified. Columbia Union permits secular work on demonstrated operational controls rather than religious identity. Those rules place the remedial boundary at the actual publicly financed function: a narrower restriction is sufficient only if it stops that function, and the proof supporting any secular remainder must identify its actual separation. Shared corporate structure or staff alone does not defeat reliable secular performance.

The lower court must promptly determine the contract's covered work, payment terms and necessary scope of relief, using the existing trial record where sufficient. It must identify the current placement situation and provide a concrete lawful transition where needed, preserving secular services and voluntary private participation while stopping established prohibited payments. An unresolved allocation issue cannot justify indefinite continuation of a purchase already proved devotional. The separate Workforce Development judgment remains undisturbed.

#### Precedent Treatment

Agostini v. Felton is applied through its unified governmental-function inquiry. Mitchell v. Helms is applied to actual-use proof and effective relief at the demonstrated scope. Strout v. Albanese and Zelman v. Simmons-Harris are extended to the analogous restricted purchase of correctional treatment; private selection does not alter the function purchased. Columbia Union College v. Oliver and K.D.M. v. Reedsport School District retain equal treatment for reliably separated secular work at religious institutions. Locke v. Davey retains its confined ministry-degree nonfunding permission and distinct individual-assistance classification; it supplies no immunity for this provider-reimbursement contract. Witters’s independent vocational-benefit relationship remains distinct. Lee v. Weisman retains its coercion inquiry, which does not exhaust the financing question.

#### Law After Decision

Restricted correctional-service payments must fund lawful functions even where the beneficiary chooses freely among providers. A proved integrated devotional purchase requires effective prospective correction at its actual scope. Religious providers remain eligible for reliably separated secular work; neutral choice and religious identity alone answer neither the financing violation nor its remedy.

#### Source Notes

U.S. Const., Amdt. 1; 324 F.3d 880. The accepted findings establish secular alternatives, nonbinding recommendations, disclosure of Faith Works’s Christian character, and absence of religious favoritism. The reported bidding waiver was attributed to the longer program’s rehabilitative features. The Department’s contract payment categories and the extent of reliable secular separation remain matters for the trial record.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns for prompt application of the actual-function rule to the trial record and payment-specific findings only where materially needed. The lower court must determine the supported contract and placement scope and enter effective prospective relief: established integrated devotional purchases must cease, while genuinely separated secular services remain eligible upon documented operational and accounting separation.

The court must identify actual current placements and provide any necessary concrete lawful transition, preserving secular services and voluntary private participation while stopping established prohibited payments. An unresolved allocation issue cannot justify indefinite continuation of a purchase already proved devotional. The existing Workforce Development judgment remains undisturbed.

### Baldwin v. Reese, No. 02-964

Merits decision, March 2, 2004

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 8, 2003; decided March 2, 2004. On certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, which found fair presentation because the Oregon Supreme Court could consult a lower opinion when considering discretionary review. The question concerns federal notice of Reese’s appellate-counsel claim in the proper state submissions. His federal habeas application was filed in 1998.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 8–1.**

**Supporting:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Justice Stevens.

#### Opinion Topology

STONE-ZSELA, C. J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, SOUTER, THOMAS, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined. STEVENS, J., filed a dissenting opinion.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**Controlling proposition:** A prisoner ordinarily fails to fairly present a federal claim when the petition, brief or properly incorporated submission does not identify its federal basis and the state court would have to search a separate lower-court opinion to discover it. Clear federal notice may be supplied by a constitutional provision, pertinent federal authority or an understandable description of the claim; no particular citation or verbal formula is indispensable.

**Authority:** The Opinion of the Court has eight direct joins: Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Justice Stevens dissents on the application to Reese's actual petition.

**Controlling explanation:** Picard requires a fair opportunity to decide the federal claim itself. Boerckel carries that claim-specific obligation through ordinary available state review, and Castille distinguishes proper presentation from a claim's mere presence elsewhere in a record. A discretionary-review court must be able to identify the claims it is asked to decide from the papers properly submitted.

Reese's petition mentions trial and appellate assistance, but its express Fifth, Sixth and Fourteenth Amendment references concern coercion to waive a jury. It leaves the distinct appellate-assistance allegation without federal identification. The postconviction opinion's Jones v. Barnes citation does not cure that omission: the intervening appellate order was a summary affirmance, and the requirement to attach that appellate decision does not establish attachment or incorporation of the underlying postconviction opinion.

The dissent reads the petition's constitutional references collectively with its description of counsel's failings. The Court instead reads the specific references in their stated setting. Treating possible access to another opinion as enough would replace meaningful notice with possible discovery and require the court to reconstruct the federal claim. The record establishes neither proper incorporation nor actual judicial consideration supplying the missing notice.

**Preserved scope:** The argument that identical state and federal assistance standards independently established notice was first advanced in the merits brief here and was not addressed below. The Court recognizes discretion to consider an included legal argument but declines to decide this newly presented equivalence contention under Rule 15.2. Its complexity, broader implications and absence of lower-court treatment support that choice; the Court does not decide Guinn’s ultimate effect on equivalence or fair notice. The remand applies the actual presentation and state-remedy record, and ordinary default and gateway law, to any remaining properly preserved issue.

#### Precedent Treatment

Picard v. Connor is applied to meaningful notice of the federal claim. O’Sullivan v. Boerckel is applied to claim-specific presentation through ordinary available review without importing its preenactment posture into this 1998 petition. Duncan v. Henry retains the federal-nature notice requirement. Castille v. Peoples is applied to proper presentation rather than mere inclusion elsewhere in a record. United States v. X-Citement Video and Lebron v. National Railroad Passenger Corporation retain permission to consider an included legal defense of the same judgment; neither compels consideration of the late equivalence argument here.

#### Law After Decision

State courts must receive clear federal notice through the properly presented papers, including valid incorporation. They need not reconstruct an unstated federal theory by searching a separate opinion. Clear substance controls over a mandatory citation formula; the governing procedural consequences remain those of the actual state-review record.

#### Separate Writings

Stevens dissents. He would exercise the Court’s discretion to consider Guinn and finds its Oregon-law context sufficient for fair notice on these papers. Oregon’s highest court discussed appellate assistance under both constitutions and supplied a unified formulation for failure to raise an appellate issue. A short petition expressly complaining of trial and appellate assistance, within a federal constitutional challenge to representation and conviction, would naturally alert that court to the recognized claim. Guinn itself involved express dual pleading and an omitted-shackling issue, but those qualifications do not defeat the contextual notice Stevens finds here. He does not require universal identity of every state and federal element or a search through all lower opinions. He would affirm federal consideration of the appellate-assistance claim.

#### Source Notes

28 U.S.C. §2254(b), as applicable to the 1998 federal application; Oregon’s discretionary-review procedure. The required attachment was the appellate decision, and the record does not establish incorporation of the underlying postconviction opinion. Guinn v. Cupp, 304 Or. 488, 747 P.2d 984 (1987), concerned express federal and state pleading and used a more-probable-than-not result formulation in its omitted-shackling-issue analysis. That wording is not relabeled as a different federal prejudice standard.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The appellate court must apply the actual presentation and state-remedy record, including the closed state-review route, and existing presentation, availability, default, and gateway rules to any remaining properly preserved issue. The late equivalence contention remains undecided on this review.

**End of entry: Baldwin v. Reese, merits decision, March 2, 2004.**

### Raymond B. Yates, M.D., P.C. Profit Sharing Plan v. Hendon, No. 02-458

Merits decision, March 2, 2004

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 13, 2004; decided March 2, 2004. On certiorari to the Sixth Circuit. Yates, the working owner of a professional corporation, participated in its plan with genuine nonowner employees and repaid a plan loan shortly before bankruptcy. The court below denied participant status because of ownership. The question is whether ownership categorically prevents participation in an otherwise covered employee plan.

#### Judgment

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Set aside the categorical owner-exclusion judgment and remand | Vacated and remanded, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer |  | Remand to apply the participation rule and decide remaining statutory and bankruptcy conditions. |
| Definitive working-owner coverage rule, without an additional Darden classification requirement for this statutory category | Adopted, 8–1 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Thomas | Apply the working-owner rule without the additional common-law classification inquiry Thomas would require. |

#### Opinion Topology

GINSBURG, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, KENNEDY, SOUTER, and BREYER, JJ., joined. SCALIA and THOMAS, JJ., filed opinions concurring in the judgment.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | The full-opinion joiners listed above | Supports vacatur and remand and the definitive working-owner rule | Seven full joins to the statutory-and-regulatory rationale |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Scalia | | Supports vacatur and remand and directly adopts the same operative coverage rule | Authoritative agency interpretation; does not join the Court's independent statutory rationale |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Thomas | | Supports vacatur and remand but rejects the definitive classification rule | Rejects categorical owner exclusion; would require an additional common-law employee inquiry |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**Controlling proposition:** A working owner may hold both employer and employee-beneficiary capacities and qualify as an ERISA participant in a pension plan that also covers one or more genuine employees other than the owner and spouse. The owner-only exclusion determines whether an employee plan exists; it does not categorically expel a working owner from an otherwise covered plan. The claimant must establish participant and covered-plan predicates and the independent requirements for any anti-alienation or bankruptcy protection claimed.

**Authority:** The Opinion of the Court is joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Scalia's concurrence in the judgment directly adopts the same operative coverage rule through the official agency interpretation, giving that rule eight direct supporters; he does not join the seven's independent statutory rationale. Thomas agrees that categorical ownership exclusion is wrong but would require an additional common-law classification inquiry.

**Controlling explanation:** ERISA's integrated provisions recognize overlapping capacities. Section 1108(c)(1) permits a fiduciary's participant benefit when computed and paid consistently with the plan as applied to all other participants and beneficiaries; subsection (c)(3) recognizes fiduciary service alongside service for a party in interest. Subsection (d) separately restricts owner-employee transactions. These provisions regulate the risks of dual capacities rather than declare them impossible.

The regulation likewise defines employee-plan coverage. Section 2510.3-3 contrasts owner-only arrangements with plans covering common-law employees as well as self-employed individuals, and confines its owner exclusion to that section's purpose. Its boundary remains effective without excluding an owner from an existing employee plan. Darden's common-law agency inquiry supplies a default when the statute otherwise leaves employee status unresolved; it does not displace ERISA's specific structure. Clackamas also requires attention to the particular statute, rather than treating ownership as a talisman.

Yates's plan covers genuine nonowner employees. His ownership therefore cannot sustain the categorical exclusion adopted below. Patterson v. Shumate protects a beneficial trust interest only when an enforceable transfer restriction satisfies the applicable conditions. The remand must determine the claimed protection of the repayment and the remaining plan, anti-alienation and bankruptcy questions, giving effect to the unchallenged preference determination and the independent conditions of the asserted protection.

#### Precedent Treatment

Nationwide Mutual Insurance Co. v. Darden retains its default common-law relationship inquiry; the specific ERISA structure resolves the dual-capacity question first. Clackamas Gastroenterology Associates v. Wells retains its statute-specific classification rule and does not impose categorical exclusion here. Patterson v. Shumate retains its protection for a qualifying beneficial trust interest subject to an enforceable restriction; participant status does not itself establish every requirement of that protection.

#### Law After Decision

Working ownership is compatible with participation in a plan covering genuine nonowner employees. Owner-only arrangements retain their distinct regulatory treatment. Courts must separately apply the requirements for plan coverage, participation, enforceable anti-alienation protection and the bankruptcy treatment of the actual transaction.

#### Separate Writings

Scalia concurs in the judgment. The Department of Labor's official interpretation permitting working-owner participation is reasonable within ERISA's structure and governs under his approach to authoritative agency interpretation. That ground supplies the same operative coverage result without independently declaring the judicial construction the only permissible reading. He agrees that the remaining qualification and transaction questions must be decided separately.

Thomas concurs in the judgment setting aside categorical exclusion. In his view, provisions regulating benefits or transactions for some owner-employees do not establish that every working owner in the proposed category is an employee. Darden's common-law relationship inquiry remains necessary because those structural indications do not fully resolve classification. Ownership alone is insufficient to disqualify Yates, and corporate separateness may support employee status, but Thomas would require the lower court to decide that question first and then address the remaining statutory and bankruptcy conditions.

#### Source Notes

29 U.S.C. §§1002, 1056(d), 1103(c) and 1108(c)–(d); 29 C.F.R. §2510.3-3; 11 U.S.C. §§541(c)(2) and 547.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The lower court must apply the working-owner participation rule and decide the remaining qualification, anti-alienation, anti-inurement, repayment, and bankruptcy-protection questions under their independent conditions. Genuine coverage of employees other than the owner is established, and the unchallenged preference determination remains in place. Thomas would first require the additional common-law classification inquiry.

**End of entry: Raymond B. Yates, M.D., P.C. Profit Sharing Plan v. Hendon, merits decision, March 2, 2004.**

### Crawford v. Washington, No. 02-9410

Merits decision, March 8, 2004

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 10, 2003; decided March 8, 2004. On certiorari to the Washington Supreme Court, which reinstated Crawford’s assault conviction. The granted question concerns admission of his unavailable wife’s recorded police accusation without cross-examination and whether reliability may substitute for confrontation. Sylvia did not testify because of marital privilege; the prosecution introduced her recording for its truth to rebut self-defense.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

#### Opinion Topology

SCALIA, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, KENNEDY, SOUTER, THOMAS, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined. O'CONNOR, J., filed an opinion concurring in the judgment.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Scalia | The full-opinion joiners listed above | Supports reversal and remand | Eight direct joins to the categorical confrontation rule, its application, and remedy |
| Concurrence in the judgment | O'Connor | | Supports the same constitutional-error judgment and remedy | Applies the existing intrinsic-reliability rule; does not join the replacement rule |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**Controlling proposition:** A formal, recorded custodial police interrogation eliciting a witness's account of past criminal events for evidentiary use against the accused is testimonial. When the prosecution offers that accusation for its truth, it must produce the declarant for constitutionally adequate confrontation or establish both unavailability and a prior opportunity for cross-examination; a hearsay label, judicial assessment of reliability or agreement with other evidence cannot replace that opportunity.

**Authority:** The Opinion of the Court has eight direct joins: Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Justice O'Connor agrees with the judgment through the existing reliability rule and does not join this replacement rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Confrontation Clause prescribes a way to test an accusation assembled as testimony. Its protection cannot depend on a judge's assessment that the untested account is probably accurate. Mattox allows unavailable testimony that has already undergone cross-examination; the history of examinations conducted outside the accused's presence explains why police-created trial accusations require the same procedural protection even without an oath.

The State's strongest answer is that reliability rules already screen untrustworthy accusations, and Wright and Lilly could exclude this recording without changing doctrine. But that method permits another judge to admit the same class of accusation upon a different assessment of trustworthiness. Repeated reliance on that substitute does not cure its constitutional defect. The Court overrules Roberts to the extent it permits admission within this defined testimonial class through a hearsay exception or reliability finding without the required confrontation.

Sylvia's repeated, recorded custodial questioning sought an account of the completed encounter for investigation and prosecution. Crawford never had an opportunity to cross-examine her. Her accusation's asserted self-inculpatory character and its supposed agreement with Crawford's account cannot supply the missing procedure. Marital privilege does not itself forfeit his confrontation right. The recording was constitutionally inadmissible for its truth against him.

**Operative qualifications and reach:** Existing forfeiture by wrongdoing remains an independent equitable ground for loss of the confrontation claim. The rule concerns substantive truth use; genuinely nontruth use remains distinct. A declarant who appears for constitutionally adequate cross-examination can be questioned about an earlier statement. Outside the defined testimonial class, existing exception-sensitive constitutional law continues at its established scope. The new rule applies on this direct review and under ordinary direct-review principles.

#### Precedent Treatment

Ohio v. Roberts is overruled insofar as it permits the defined formal investigative accusation to be admitted through a hearsay category or reliability finding without the required confrontation. Mattox v. United States is applied for unavailability and prior examination of absent testimony. White v. Illinois is qualified at the same testimonial scope; its accepted spontaneous-declaration and medical-treatment applications, and rejection of an exclusively formal boundary for the whole Clause, remain effective. A hearsay label cannot exempt an accusation within the new rule. Lilly v. Virginia and Idaho v. Wright retain their exclusion of unsupported custodial accusations and their intrinsic-guarantee rules where the received framework remains applicable; reliability cannot authorize the defined testimonial accusation without confrontation. Hale’s bounded independent-public-record search and nonentry holding remains effective because that function differs from eliciting an eyewitness accusation about the charged encounter. Coy v. Iowa and Maryland v. Craig retain their distinct rules for constitutionally adequate trial-witness presentation. Chapman v. California retains the State’s beyond-a-reasonable-doubt harmlessness burden for properly available contentions; it does not revive the abandoned contention here.

#### Law After Decision

Formal recorded custodial accusations assembled by police for evidentiary use against the accused require confrontation, with unavailability and prior cross-examination when the declarant cannot appear. Courts may not admit them for their truth merely because they seem reliable. The rule changes the method of constitutional review for that class while retaining the existing protections and accepted applications outside it.

#### Separate Writings

O'Connor concurs in the judgment. White, Wright and Lilly already exclude this recording. The penal-interest label does not establish a firmly rooted exception for the accusation, and the State's comparison of omissions in the two accounts is external corroboration, not an intrinsic guarantee. Directed custodial questioning, a changed account and uncertainty about Sylvia's observation undermine the offered reasons for trustworthiness; the trial court's description of the investigator as neutral supplies no substitute. O'Connor would decide the case on that complete existing-law ground. She declines a replacement whose historical reach and practical application remain uncertain, while agreeing that admission was unconstitutional and that the conviction requires relief.

#### Source Notes

U.S. Const., Amdts. 6 and 14; Wash. Rev. Code §5.60.060(1); Wash. Rule Evid. 804(b)(3). The police accounts and the parties’ descriptions of the encounter remain statements in the record, not findings of the truth of either account.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The recording was inadmissible for its truth on this record. Washington did not challenge the intermediate court's conclusion that an established confrontation violation was not harmless; that conclusion remains operative. The remand therefore requires relief from the assault conviction and further proceedings consistent with exclusion of the recorded accusation. A new trial may proceed under lawful evidence and confrontation rules without reopening harmlessness.

**End of entry: Crawford v. Washington, merits decision, March 8, 2004.**

### Iowa v. Tovar, No. 02-1541

Merits decision, March 8, 2004

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 21, 2004; decided March 8, 2004. On certiorari to the Iowa Supreme Court. Tovar’s 1996 uncounseled guilty plea to operating while intoxicated resulted in two days in jail. In a later third-offense prosecution he challenged use of that conviction. The Iowa Supreme Court excluded it and directed a lesser judgment because the plea court had not given two additional warnings about counsel’s advice.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

#### Opinion Topology

O'CONNOR, J., delivered the opinion of the unanimous Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, SCALIA, KENNEDY, SOUTER, THOMAS, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**Controlling proposition:** An uncounseled guilty plea resulting in imprisonment requires a knowing, intelligent and voluntary relinquishment of counsel, including understanding the nature of the charge, the availability and assistance of counsel at the plea, and the applicable punishment. The Sixth Amendment does not invariably require additional warnings that a lawyer may discover an overlooked defense or give an independent opinion about whether to plead guilty; their omission alone does not establish invalid waiver.

**Authority:** The unanimous Opinion of the Court supplies nine direct joins to the rule, disposition of the presented collateral attack and remand.

**Controlling explanation:** Johnson v. Zerbst requires intentional relinquishment of a known right. Godinez distinguishes competence from actual informed waiver, and Patterson makes the information required sensitive to counsel's role at the stage in question. Faretta's warning about conducting one's own contested trial addresses different tasks; it does not prescribe these two additions for every plea.

The contrary argument rightly emphasizes counsel's help in evaluating defenses and the plea decision. That supports a real-understanding inquiry, but it does not show that every accused lacks the necessary understanding without these precise warnings. Advice only about counsel at a possible trial is not automatically equivalent to understanding counsel's availability before and at the plea. Particular wording is not controlling, and States may require fuller colloquies.

Tovar's first conviction imposed two days in jail, so a valid waiver matters. His collateral theory, however, asserted the two omissions and a possible mistaken belief, rather than actual unawareness of counsel's availability before and at arraignment. Under the applicable collateral framework he must identify the actual asserted waiver defect and support factual assertions under Iowa's preponderance rule. The omitted nonmandatory warnings do not establish the suggested possibility. The warning-based attack fails, and the resulting direction to exclude the first conviction and enter the lesser judgment must be reversed.

#### Precedent Treatment

Godinez v. Moran is applied to the distinction between competence and actual informed waiver. Johnson v. Zerbst supplies intentional relinquishment of a known right. Patterson v. Illinois supplies the stage-sensitive measure of necessary information. Faretta v. California retains its trial-representation protections; its warnings are not mechanically extended to every guilty plea. Parke v. Raley retains its bounded collateral burden sequence for the circumstances it adjudicated and supplies no automatic validation of an uncounseled plea. Watts v. State supplies Iowa’s preponderance rule for factual assertions in collateral proceedings; its counseled-plea facts do not establish Tovar’s knowledge.

#### Law After Decision

Courts assess actual understanding of counsel’s availability and role at the plea, the charge and punishment. The two prescribed warnings are not universal federal constitutional prerequisites. A State may impose fuller colloquies, and a collateral challenge must identify and support the actual defect under the applicable burden rules.

#### Source Notes

U.S. Const., Amdts. 6 and 14; Iowa enhancement and collateral-review law; Watts v. State. The actual asserted challenge rests on the omitted warnings and a possible mistaken belief, rather than an assertion of unawareness of the right to counsel before and at the plea.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The direction to disregard the first conviction and enter the lesser judgment on the mandatory-two-warning theory is set aside. Tovar's presented warning-based collateral attack has been resolved. The enhancement proceeding continues under the correct waiver rule and otherwise applicable law.

**End of entry: Iowa v. Tovar, merits decision, March 8, 2004.**
