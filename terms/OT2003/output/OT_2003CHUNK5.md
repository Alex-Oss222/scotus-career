### Karaha Bodas Co., LLC v. Perusahaan Pertambangan Minyak Dan Gas Bumi Negara

Merits decision, March 9, 2004

#### Chronology and Posture

**Route and judgment below:** On review of the Fifth Circuit's June 18, 2003 judgment, 335 F.3d 357, vacating a preliminary foreign anti-suit injunction and its dependent surviving civil-contempt consequence; decided on the merits briefs.

**Question:** Whether a federal court enforcing a Swiss arbitral award under the New York Convention may bar the losing party's Indonesian annulment and anti-enforcement proceedings on the showing made here.

**Posture:** The contracts concerned an Indonesian geothermal project and required Swiss arbitration. An award issued in December 2000, and a United States recognition judgment followed in December 2001. The separate appeal concerning that recognition judgment is distinct from this review.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 9-0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The preliminary foreign anti-suit injunction and its dependent surviving civil-contempt consequence remain vacated. The separate recognition judgment remains undisturbed.

#### Opinion Topology

KENNEDY, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, SCALIA, SOUTER, THOMAS, GINSBURG and BREYER, JJ., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Necessity for a foreign anti-suit injunction

**Holding and operative rule:** A federal court enforcing a foreign arbitral award may not enjoin a party's parallel foreign annulment or enforcement-related proceedings merely because they contest the award, impose ordinary litigation costs or seek a different result. The applicant must establish a concrete threat to the domestic court's lawful adjudication or effective judgment that the Convention's ordinary recognition rules cannot adequately address, together with a lawful source of injunctive authority, the usual equitable prerequisites and a restraint no broader than necessary; the showing here fails that rule.

**Authority:** Kennedy's opinion for the unanimous Court controls this rule, its application and the dependent civil-contempt disposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Convention's enforcement policy operates through a system in which courts in different countries perform distinct tasks. Article V(1)(e) concerns an award set aside or suspended by a competent authority of the country in which, or under whose law, it was made. When an application has been made to that authority, Article VI permits the enforcing court, if it considers it proper, to adjourn its decision and, on the enforcing party's application, to order suitable security. Neither option is a command. An enforcement preference cannot replace those provisions with a general power to suppress foreign proceedings.

The creditor emphasizes its existing United States recognition judgment. But pursuit of Indonesian annulment and anti-enforcement relief does not establish that the domestic court has lost the practical ability to decide those orders' domestic effect and effectuate its own lawful judgment. Baker distinguishes recognition from additional coercive control over another forum's process. Sky Reefer likewise distinguishes ordinary foreign-forum burdens from an actual loss of protected domestic rights. Those distinctions support requiring a demonstrated obstruction here. The district court's broad restraint lacked that necessary showing, and the dependent surviving civil-contempt consequence falls with it.

**Limits and questions outside this review:** The separate recognition judgment remains undisturbed. The separate recognition appeal, the definitive allocation of primary annulment jurisdiction and the effect of an actual foreign annulment are outside the injunction judgment presented; courts address orders actually presented under the Convention's recognition provisions. The rule preserves legally authorized, appropriately tailored relief against a demonstrated obstruction.

**Precedent treatment:** Baker supplies the distinction between a judgment's lawful recognition and additional coercive control, by analogy to this foreign setting. Sky Reefer supplies the distinction between ordinary forum burdens and loss of substantive legal protection. Grupo Mexicano's separate treatment of postjudgment enforcement remains intact.

#### Precedent Treatment

Baker v. General Motors Corp. is applied by analogy to distinguish recognition from additional coercive control; its domestic full-faith-and-credit holding retains its own scope. Vimar Seguros y Reaseguros, S.A. v. M/V Sky Reefer supplies the distinction between ordinary foreign litigation burdens and a concrete loss of substantive protection. Grupo Mexicano de Desarrollo, S.A. v. Alliance Bond Fund, Inc. retains its limitation on the unsecured creditor's prejudgment asset freeze and its distinct treatment of postjudgment remedies. The Fifth Circuit's necessity and comity reasoning is sustained at the scope of the Court's stated rule.

#### Law After Decision

In Convention enforcement proceedings, an anti-suit applicant must show an actual threat that ordinary domestic recognition and enforcement adjudication cannot adequately address, lawful equitable authority and proportionate relief. Parallel foreign challenges alone do not satisfy that requirement. Domestic courts retain responsibility for the legal effect of foreign orders under the Convention.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The district court must remove the challenged restraint and the civil enforcement measures dependent on it. Authorized recognition and execution proceedings may continue, with the domestic effect of any foreign order determined under the Convention.

#### Source Notes

Karaha Bodas Co., LLC v. Perusahaan Pertambangan Minyak Dan Gas Bumi Negara, 335 F.3d 357 (CA5 2003); Convention on the Recognition and Enforcement of Foreign Arbitral Awards, Arts. III, V(1)(e), VI; Federal Arbitration Act, Chapter 2; 28 U.S.C. § 1292(a)(1).

**End of entry: Karaha Bodas Co., LLC v. Perusahaan Pertambangan Minyak Dan Gas Bumi Negara, merits decision, March 9, 2004.**

### Batzel v. Smith

Merits decision, March 18, 2004

#### Chronology and Posture

**Route and judgment below:** On review of the Ninth Circuit's June 24, 2003 judgment, 333 F.3d 1018; decided on the merits briefs. The court accepted an interlocutory appeal from denial of a California anti-SLAPP special motion, construed § 230 to turn on reasonable perception that an email was supplied for Internet publication, and remanded that defense. A distinct appeal concerned the sponsor's final summary judgment.

**Questions:** Whether denial of the California special motion is immediately appealable under the federal collateral-order doctrine and, if so, whether § 230 protection for republication of a third-party email turns on the sender's actual intent or the moderator's reasonable perception of intended publication.

**Material posture:** A handyman sent a museum-security website operator allegations connecting Batzel's artwork to Nazi theft, and the operator circulated them. Batzel disputes the allegations. The sender later denied intending publication, and the recipient's reasonable perception is disputed.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Judgment

**Vacated and remanded with instructions to dismiss the moderator's interlocutory appeal, 9-0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The independent final sponsor disposition, outside the questions under review, remains undisturbed.

#### Opinion Topology

STEVENS, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, SOUTER, THOMAS, GINSBURG and BREYER, JJ., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Federal appellate jurisdiction over the special-motion denial

**Holding and operative rule:** Denial of a California anti-SLAPP motion in federal court is not immediately appealable under 28 U.S.C. § 1291 merely because state law permits an appeal or describes protection from litigation. This category of merits-linked probability-of-success screening and ordinary liability defense remains effectively reviewable after final judgment, and therefore fails the cumulative collateral-order requirements; the Ninth Circuit lacked jurisdiction to decide the moderator's § 230 defense on this appeal.

**Authority:** Stevens's opinion for the unanimous Court controls the jurisdictional rule and mandate.

**Controlling explanation:** Cohen permits immediate review only within a narrow class of conclusively determined, sufficiently important and separate questions that would be effectively unreviewable after final judgment. State appeal permission cannot enlarge federal jurisdiction. The special motion's inquiry into the plaintiff's probability of success overlaps the liability issues, including the asserted publisher defense. Describing the motion as protection from litigation does not make those issues sufficiently separate and unreviewable merely because the suit continues.

California's purpose of protecting public expression is more than the private settlement interest addressed in Digital Equipment. But that public purpose cannot dispense with the remaining collateral-order conditions. Digital Equipment's independently controlling adequate-final-review holding establishes that irretrievable litigation expense and the loss of early protection do not alone make later review ineffective. Swint applies that distinction to an ordinary liability defense. Here, final review can correct an erroneous determination of the moderator's statutory protection. The possible burden or chilling effect of continued litigation does not supply the missing effective-unreviewability condition. That failure suffices for this disposition.

The sponsor's independent final appeal supplies no jurisdiction over the moderator's distinct interlocutory defense. Swint rejects adding an otherwise unauthorized appeal merely because deciding related issues would save litigation. Review of the sponsor's judgment does not require resolution of this defense.

**Question not reached:** The actual-intent, reasonable-perception and broader supplied-content constructions of § 230 cannot be adjudicated through this unauthorized interlocutory appeal. The jurisdictional ground prevents reaching those merits questions; their factual predicates return to the district court under ordinary procedure.

**Precedent treatment:** Cohen's narrow, cumulative exception is applied. Digital Equipment's adequate-final-review holding and Swint's ordinary-defense and separate-party rules supply the governing law; the Court extends their application to this category of California special-motion denial without altering their requirements.

#### Precedent Treatment

Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp. retains its narrow cumulative collateral-order requirements. Digital Equipment Corp. v. Desktop Direct, Inc. is applied through its independent adequate-final-review holding; the private-settlement importance rationale does not erase California's public expression interest. Swint v. Chambers County Commission supplies the distinction between ordinary liability defenses and independently appealable immunity, and the limit on adding a separate party's nonappealable defense for convenience.

#### Law After Decision

Denials of the identified California anti-SLAPP screening motion do not become federal collateral orders through state appeal permission, a litigation-avoidance label or ordinary liability defenses. Section 230 defenses proceed through the district court and review through a valid final judgment or an independently authorized interlocutory route.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Ninth Circuit must dismiss the moderator's interlocutory appeal and remove its unauthorized merits disposition. The district court may adjudicate the defenses and factual disputes through ordinary proceedings; subsequent appellate review requires a valid final or independently authorized interlocutory route.

#### Source Notes

Batzel v. Smith, 333 F.3d 1018 (CA9 2003); 28 U.S.C. §§ 1291, 1292; Cal. Code Civ. Proc. § 425.16; 47 U.S.C. § 230.

**End of entry: Batzel v. Smith, merits decision, March 18, 2004.**

### South Florida Water Management District v. Miccosukee Tribe of Indians, No. 02-626

Merits decision, March 23, 2004

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 14, 2004.

**Route and judgment below:** On writ of certiorari to the Eleventh Circuit, which affirmed summary judgment requiring a Clean Water Act permit for the S-9 pump on the premise that C-11 and WCA-3 are distinct waters.

**Question:** Whether a permit is required for a pump conveying pollutant-containing water when the pollutants did not originate in the pump, including the distinction between movement within one water body and addition to another.

**Material posture:** S-9 conveys canal water containing phosphorus into the Everglades conservation area. The relationship between the canal and conservation area remains genuinely disputed. The Government advances a broader construction treating all navigable waters as one for the addition inquiry.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Judgment

**Vacated and remanded, 9-0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

All nine support the conveyance ruling and remand of the genuine distinct-water dispute. Eight join the additional permission concerning a properly presented broader statutory theory, as shown in the opinion topology.

#### Opinion Topology

O'CONNOR, J., delivered the opinion of the Court. SCALIA, J., filed an opinion concurring in part and concurring in the judgment.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Parts I–II | O'Connor | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports vacatur and bounded factual remand | Conveyance rule, distinct addition predicate, genuine factual dispute and remedy |
| Opinion of the Court, Part III | O'Connor | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Additional remand permission | Proper presentation of the broader statutory theory |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Scalia | — | Supports the same vacatur and bounded factual remand | Withholds assent from the affirmative broader-theory invitation |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. A point source may convey pollutants generated elsewhere

**Holding and operative rule:** A pump, pipe, ditch or other point-source conveyance may discharge pollutants that it transports from elsewhere; the Clean Water Act does not require the conveyance itself to generate them. A permit claimant must still establish an addition of a pollutant to covered receiving waters from that point source.

**Authority:** Part I of O'Connor's opinion, joined by all nine Justices, controls the conveyance rule and its distinct discharge predicates.

**Controlling explanation:** The Act defines a point source by its conveyance function and expressly includes mechanisms that transport pollutants, such as pipes, ditches and conduits. Requiring the mechanism to manufacture the pollution would exclude the ordinary function Congress identified. S-9's transportation of phosphorus already present in the canal therefore can satisfy the disputed point-source characteristic.

Borden Ranch requires an actual statutory conveyance and deposit, rather than machinery movement or environmental injury alone. Its rejection of an offsite-origin requirement supports treating transported pollutants according to what the instrument does. Its soil-deposit application under § 404 remains distinct from this § 402 transfer. Deaton's coverage of connected waters concerns geographic jurisdiction; the addition and point-source requirements retain their separate work. The district's origin argument would collapse that structure by turning a transport mechanism into a required manufacturer.

**Precedent treatment:** Borden Ranch's distinction between the instrument's conveyance function and the material's origin is applied at that level. Deaton's connected-system coverage remains separate from the discharge elements.

##### II. The addition predicate requires resolution of the actual water-body dispute

**Holding and operative rule:** Moving pollutant-bearing water wholly within a single water body does not, by that movement alone, add pollutants to it. A party seeking summary judgment on a permit obligation must establish the absence of a genuine material dispute over the addition and other discharge predicates; the genuine dispute whether C-11 and WCA-3 are meaningfully distinct prevents the present permit summary judgment from standing.

**Authority:** Part II of O'Connor's opinion, joined by all nine Justices, controls the addition rule, the summary-judgment application and the bounded factual remand.

**Controlling explanation:** A conveyance can move pollutants without adding them to the same receiving water. The legal source ruling therefore cannot alone sustain the permit judgment. The parties actually disputed the relationship between the canal and the conservation area, and the question before the Court includes movement within one water body as compared with addition to another. Separate names do not conclusively establish distinct waters, and some hydrological connection does not conclusively establish one water body. The lower court must assess the material evidence about their actual relationship.

Arkansas v. Oklahoma recognizes statutory consequences tied to actual water-quality relationships and supported findings. Deaton establishes coverage for a demonstrated connected tributary system without identifying all covered waters as one body for every other element. Neither permits the Court to substitute a categorical label for this disputed addition predicate. A source-only affirmance would leave an operative permit judgment resting on an unresolved material premise within the question presented. Vacatur and a confined factual remand resolve that defect under the ordinary summary-judgment standard.

**Precedent treatment:** Arkansas's supported-record approach and Deaton's distinction between geographic coverage and factual proof are applied; their respective permitting and jurisdictional holdings retain their scopes.

##### III. The broader statutory transfer theory remains open to proper presentation

**Holding and operative rule:** The Government's broader contention that all covered navigable waters should be treated as one for the addition inquiry remains open to the parties on remand if properly presented. The Court leaves its merits undecided because that distinct construction was not developed below in its present form; it supplies no controlling rule for this disposition.

**Authority:** Part III of O'Connor's opinion establishes this eight-Justice remand permission at the joins stated above. Scalia does not join Part III.

**Controlling explanation:** The proposed all-waters construction differs from the factual contention that this canal and conservation area form one body. Its statutory and regulatory implications require proper presentation and development. The Court can decide the transport-origin question and correct the premature summary judgment on the actual water-body dispute without deciding that broader theory. The remand permits preserved statutory arguments to receive ordinary consideration while the enacted discharge elements and the conveyance rule announced today govern.

**Precedent treatment:** The existing water cases retain their distinct geographic, discharge and permitting functions; none supplies an already adopted all-waters rule.

#### Precedent Treatment

Borden Ranch Partnership v. Army Corps of Engineers is applied to the requirement of actual conveyance and addition and to rejection of an origin prerequisite; its § 404 soil-deposit and farming-recapture application retains its distinct setting. Arkansas v. Oklahoma preserves the Act's water-quality and supported-record requirements in its federal permitting context. United States v. Deaton remains controlling on the demonstrated connected-system coverage it decided; coverage does not merge all connected waters for the addition inquiry.

#### Law After Decision

The point-source element reaches transport of existing pollutants by pumps and other statutory conveyances. A separate addition to covered receiving waters remains necessary, and movement wholly within one water body does not establish it by itself. Meaningful water-body identity turns on the actual hydrological relationship, while the broader all-waters statutory theory remains unresolved on the stated presentation ground.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia concurs in the conveyance ruling and in vacating the premature permit judgment for resolution of the actual distinct-water dispute within the question presented. The statutory addition requirement and that litigated dispute suffice. He withholds assent from the Court's separate invitation to pursue the broader all-waters theory, which exceeds the limited basis on which he supports further proceedings. His agreement in the judgment rests on the actual disputed predicate, not a new factual hypothesis.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

On remand the lower court must resolve the material hydrological evidence concerning C-11 and WCA-3, apply the conveyance and addition rules, and enter a permit-compliance judgment if the statutory elements are proved. If an addition to distinct receiving waters is not established, it must reject this permit claim. The parties may present the broader statutory theory in accordance with ordinary preservation and adjudication requirements.

#### Source Notes

33 U.S.C. §§ 1251(g), 1311(a), 1342, 1362(6), (7), (12), (14); South Florida Water Management District v. Miccosukee Tribe of Indians, No. 02-626. The factual relationship of C-11 and WCA-3 is disputed.

**End of entry: South Florida Water Management District v. Miccosukee Tribe of Indians, merits decision, March 23, 2004.**

### United States v. Galletti, No. 02-1389

Merits decision, March 23, 2004

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 12, 2004.

**Route and judgment below:** On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, which sustained rejection of the Government's bankruptcy claims because the general partners had not received separate assessments within the assessment period.

**Question:** Whether timely assessment of a partnership's employment tax preserves the federal collection period for the same debt against general partners liable under state law without separate partner assessments.

**Material posture:** The partnership failed to pay employment taxes for periods from 1992 through 1995, and the IRS timely assessed the partnership. The general partners later entered Chapter 13 proceedings, where the IRS asserted claims for that partnership debt under California partnership law.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 9-0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The absence of separate partner assessments cannot alone support disallowance of the claims for the timely assessed partnership debt.

#### Opinion Topology

BREYER, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, SOUTER, THOMAS and GINSBURG, JJ., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The same assessed tax may be collected from independently liable general partners

**Holding and operative rule:** A timely assessment of a partnership's employment tax preserves the statutory collection period under § 6502(a) for judicial collection of that tax from general partners independently liable for the same debt under state law; the Code does not require duplicate assessments solely because collection is sought from those partners. The Government must establish the timely assessed obligation and amount, the applicable basis of each partner's liability and the requirements of the collection proceeding, so the absence of a second assessment alone cannot defeat these bankruptcy claims.

**Authority:** Breyer's opinion for the unanimous Court controls the assessment rule and reversal.

**Controlling explanation:** Sections 6201 and 6501 concern assessment of the tax obligation. Section 6502 supplies the collection period when that tax has been timely assessed. Here the assessed item is the partnership's employment tax. California law supplies the asserted responsibility of additional obligors for that debt; it does not create a separate federal employment tax in each partner's name.

The partners emphasize that each may be described as a taxpayer. That description does not answer which obligation was assessed or add a repeated-assessment requirement for every person independently answerable for it. Updike recognizes that proceedings against another legally responsible debtor may still collect the original tax. Its distinction between the obligation and the collection target supports applying the period to the assessed debt. The governing modern statute supplies the actual period.

Fior D'Italia likewise ties assessment to the enacted obligation. Its employer-estimation issue differs from collection of an existing partnership debt, but the need to identify the particular liability remains useful here. The Government's position succeeds on that statutory distinction, with actual partner liability and lawful collection procedure retaining their independent force. Filing a claim in bankruptcy changes the forum in which the debt is pursued, not the identity of the assessed tax. The categorical repeat-assessment bar therefore cannot sustain rejection of these claims.

**Operative limits:** The rule governs judicial collection of the same timely assessed partnership tax from partners liable for it under applicable law. Independent personal taxes and separately created penalties retain their own assessment requirements; the chosen collection proceeding retains its own safeguards.

**Precedent treatment:** Updike is applied to the obligation-versus-obligor distinction under the current assessment and collection provisions. Fior D'Italia retains its separate employer-assessment and estimation holdings; its identification of the enacted liability is consistent with, but does not itself decide, this partner-collection issue.

#### Precedent Treatment

United States v. Updike supplies the distinction between assessment of an obligation and collection from persons legally responsible for it; the current statutory collection period governs this case. United States v. Fior D'Italia, Inc. remains the employer-estimation decision it was: identifying the enacted obligation does not merge distinct liabilities or their assessment rules.

#### Law After Decision

A timely assessment of partnership employment tax supplies the collection-period predicate for judicial proceedings against general partners independently liable for that same debt. The Government must satisfy debtor-specific substantive liability and the actual collection procedure; the legal assessment question is settled, while application to the claims proceeds under those rules.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The bankruptcy claims return for adjudication under the collection period applicable to the timely assessed partnership tax. The courts must resolve the amount, each debtor's liability and the remaining properly available claim defenses and procedural requirements, without imposing a duplicate-assessment prerequisite.

#### Source Notes

26 U.S.C. §§ 6201, 6501(a), 6502(a); United States v. Updike, 281 U.S. 489; applicable California general-partnership liability and Bankruptcy Code claim procedures.

**End of entry: United States v. Galletti, merits decision, March 23, 2004.**

### Nixon v. Missouri Municipal League, Nos. 02-1238, 02-1386 and 02-1405 (consolidated)

Merits decision, March 24, 2004

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 12, 2004.

**Route and judgment below:** On writs of certiorari to the Eighth Circuit. The court reversed the FCC's refusal to preempt Missouri's restriction on its political subdivisions' provision or sale of telecommunications services.

**Question:** Whether 47 U.S.C. § 253, through its protection of the ability of any entity to enter telecommunications markets, authorizes federal preemption of the State's allocation of service authority to its own subdivisions.

**Posture:** Missouri, the FCC and the carrier petitioner seek reversal of the same circuit judgment; the municipal respondents seek displacement of the state restriction.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Judgment

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| No. 02-1238 | Reversed, 8–1 | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | Stevens | FCC refusal to preempt sustained |
| No. 02-1386 | Reversed, 8–1 | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | Stevens | FCC refusal to preempt sustained |
| No. 02-1405 | Reversed, 8–1 | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | Stevens | FCC refusal to preempt sustained |

The FCC's refusal to preempt remains effective for the challenged allocation of municipal service authority.

#### Opinion Topology

SCALIA, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and O'CONNOR, KENNEDY, SOUTER, THOMAS, GINSBURG and BREYER, JJ., joined. STEVENS, J., filed a dissenting opinion.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Section 253 does not clearly reassign municipal service powers

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 253 does not authorize the FCC to displace a State's assignment or withdrawal of telecommunications-service authority to its own political subdivisions merely through the provision's general reference to the ability of any entity to provide service. A claimant seeking that alteration of state governmental authority must identify unmistakable congressional direction in the statute's text and structure; § 253 supplies no such direction here and does not preempt Missouri's restriction on the municipal respondents.

**Authority:** Scalia's opinion for the eight-Justice Court identified above controls the statutory rule and judgment in all three consolidated dockets. Stevens dissents.

**Controlling explanation:** Gregory requires clear congressional direction before general statutory language is read to alter important state governmental arrangements. A State's choice of the powers its own subdivisions may exercise presents that concern. Section 253 targets state and local restrictions on telecommunications entry, but identifying state regulation as an object of preemption does not clearly identify the State's allocation of governmental capacity as another object.

The municipalities and the dissent read the entity language to protect existing, authorized municipal providers against targeted restraints. They distinguish removal of a market barrier from creation or funding of a municipality. That distinction explains their proposed construction, but it does not resolve the text's ambiguity about state-created capacity. The provision reaches measures that prohibit service and those with a prohibitory effect. It supplies no unmistakable boundary under which a targeted withdrawal of municipal service authority is federally forbidden while a general allocation of the same powers remains permissible. The problem persists whether the restraint is direct or indirect; it is not resolved by the order of state enactments. Preemption of a restraint also depends on whatever affirmative capacity state law supplies. These features explain the absence of the required statutory clarity.

New York distinguishes regulation of activity from assignment of governmental responsibility. AT&T gives the FCC the telecommunications field Congress actually delegated, with the statutory allocation intact. Ours Garage preserves a State's choice to delegate retained authority to its subdivisions. Together these cases support the distinction between regulating a provider's conduct and deciding which governmental body may become that provider. Gregory's clarity requirement resolves this case while preserving § 253's ordinary operation against covered market barriers.

**Operative scope:** This holding concerns a State's allocation of service powers to its own political subdivisions. Independent private providers and the distinct status of independently chartered municipal corporations remain governed by their own legal predicates. The constitutional validity of a different, explicit congressional direction is outside the statutory question presented.

**Precedent treatment:** Gregory's clear-statement principle is applied to the internal State–subdivision allocation. New York, AT&T and Ours Garage retain their respective structural, delegated-regulation and state-delegation holdings and supply the stated distinctions.

#### Precedent Treatment

Gregory v. Ashcroft supplies the requirement of statutory clarity before altering important state governmental arrangements. New York v. United States preserves the distinction between regulating state activity and directing governmental responsibility. AT&T Corp. v. Iowa Utilities Board retains the FCC's actual delegated national implementing authority and the State's assigned functions. City of Columbus v. Ours Garage & Wrecker Service, Inc. supplies the State's ability to act through authorized subdivisions within retained authority; that state choice is the relevant analogy here.

#### Law After Decision

Section 253 leaves a State's assignment or withdrawal of telecommunications-service powers to its own political subdivisions intact absent the requisite congressional direction. Its ordinary preemption of covered market barriers remains operative. Courts must identify whether a challenged measure regulates the conduct of an empowered provider or determines the authority of a State's subdivision to become that provider.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stevens dissents. He would affirm that municipal providers are included within § 253 and return the proceeding for application to their actual state-law authority. In his reading, the general entity language, the express targeting of state and local restrictions and the Act's competition structure reach public utilities already authorized and capable of operating without compelled state assistance. Ordinary preemption removes an unlawful barrier; it need not create a municipality, provide funding or supply an otherwise nonexistent power.

Drawing on the distinction in White's Gregory writing and his support for AT&T's federal local-competition program, Stevens considers the statute's express regulation of state barriers sufficient without an additional clear statement naming municipal providers. He would distinguish a general redefinition of municipal powers from a restraint specifically targeting municipal telecommunications, including an indirect restraint with that prohibitory effect. The effects language strengthens his protection against disguised market barriers rather than eliminating that distinction. His proposed remand would determine the surviving state-law capacity and the actual restriction, rather than presume federal creation of authority.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The challenged Missouri restriction remains effective against the municipal respondents, and the FCC's refusal to displace it is sustained. The consolidated review is concluded; any change to municipal service authority must occur through an independently lawful route.

#### Source Notes

### California ex rel. Lockyer v. FERC

Merits decision, March 29, 2004

#### Chronology and Posture

**Route and judgment below:** On review of the Ninth Circuit's May 15, 2003 judgment, 329 F.3d 700, denying review of FERC proceedings EC01-49-002 and EC01-41-002; decided on the merits briefs.

**Questions:** Whether abbreviated notice, written presentation and completed reconsideration satisfied the Federal Power Act and the creditor's asserted constitutional right to a hearing, and whether FERC's own consideration supports the approvals on the reviewed objections.

**Posture:** Utility-affiliate ownership reorganizations were approved January 12, 2001, following applications in December 2000 and seven- and four-day comment periods. Substantive reconsideration followed in February 2001 and January 2002.

All nine Justices participated at submission and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer.

The challenged FERC approvals remain operative.

#### Opinion Topology

GINSBURG, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, SOUTER, THOMAS, BREYER, JJ., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Statutory notice and opportunity for hearing

**Holding and operative rule:** An expedited approval under Federal Power Act section 203 can satisfy the statutory notice and hearing requirements through informative notice and written proceedings when the agency genuinely considers the material submissions while retaining effective authority to alter its decision, and the issues require no additional fact hearing. The abbreviated periods here, followed by substantive reconsideration, furnished that opportunity; an objector seeking further process must identify a material factual issue or a lost opportunity that the actual process failed to address.

**Authority:** The unanimous Opinion of the Court controls this holding, the constitutional holding and the record-review holding below; all nine Justices join each proposition and its application.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 203 requires notice and an opportunity for hearing. Its command is distinct from the constitutional inquiry. The notices identified the proposed ownership transactions, and the record supports the prescribed mailing without making actual receipt an additional statutory condition. FERC admitted the objections, permitted late intervention in one proceeding and addressed the creditor and public-interest submissions on reconsideration. Section 313 left it able to change its orders. The agency therefore provided an effective opportunity to influence the decision even though the transactions had closed.

The objectors emphasize the seven- and four-day comment windows and rapid closing. Those facts require examination of the whole process, including whether later consideration could still afford effective relief. They do not establish a lost procedural opportunity by themselves. The objections here were resolved through written submissions and reasoned orders; no identified material factual dispute required testimony or cross-examination. An unchanged result alone would not establish adequate reconsideration. The holding depends on the responsive process and retained authority shown in this record, rather than a general minimum-period or postapproval-cure rule.

##### The creditor's constitutional process claim

**Holding and operative rule:** Assuming the public power agency had a protected creditor interest, the notice, written submissions and effective reconsideration afforded it constitutionally sufficient process in these proceedings. The nature of the actual dispute required meaningful participation and responsive consideration, rather than a trial-type hearing merely because the proposed transactions might affect creditors.

**Authority:** The same unanimous Opinion of the Court; protected-interest status is assumed for resolving the process claim and remains undecided because the process was sufficient even on that premise.

**Controlling explanation:** Mullane requires notice reasonably adapted to the affected interests. Mathews considers the private interest, the risk of error and value of additional safeguards, and the government's interests and practical needs. Here the creditor could submit its asset-shielding objection, the agency considered it, and the legal and policy issues could be addressed on the written record. The financing concerns during the energy crisis explain the need for expedition, but the effective later consideration supplies the necessary opportunity to be heard. Repeating the proceedings without identifying a material issue excluded from that consideration would add no safeguard required by this record.

##### Review of the agency's own public-interest determination

**Holding and operative rule:** A court reviewing these approvals must evaluate FERC's actual reasons and the supporting administrative record. FERC's consideration of the creditor objection together with the financing and generation interests supports the challenged approvals; disagreement with that assessment supplies no basis to replace the agency's supported determination.

**Authority:** The same unanimous Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** National Railroad Passenger Corp. v. Boston & Maine Corp. permits recognition of a ground embodied in the agency's decision while forbidding a court-created substitute. Arkansas v. Oklahoma requires whole-record support for agency findings rather than a court's preferred policy balance. FERC's reconsideration orders themselves addressed the asserted creditor consequences and the transactions' financing and generation purposes. Those reasons sustain the decision on the issues reviewed. Independent creditor, fraudulent-transfer and bankruptcy claims lie outside this procedural review and retain their own governing requirements.

**Precedent treatment:** Mullane and Mathews are applied to the actual constitutional procedure; Boston & Maine and Arkansas are applied to FERC's own reasons and supported determination. Alaska Department of Environmental Conservation v. EPA preserves the distinction between statutory authority and reasoned exercise of that authority; sections 203 and 313 supply the authority relevant here.

#### Precedent Treatment

Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co. supplies the requirement of notice suited to the affected interests. Mathews v. Eldridge supplies the functional constitutional inquiry; the statute's independent notice and hearing commands retain their force. National Railroad Passenger Corp. v. Boston & Maine Corp. supplies the agency-ground requirement and permits recognition of a discernible actual reason. Arkansas v. Oklahoma supplies whole-record review and bars substitution of a court's preferred policy. Alaska Department of Environmental Conservation v. EPA preserves source-specific statutory authority and reasoned exercise of that authority.

#### Law After Decision

The Federal Power Act's notice and hearing commands continue to govern expedited approvals. This record establishes that informative notice, written participation and genuinely effective reconsideration can satisfy those commands and the assumed constitutional interest when the actual issues require no additional evidentiary hearing. Courts assess whether consideration remained capable of meaningful relief and review the agency's own supported reasons. The decision adds a confined application of existing procedural and administrative-review doctrine.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The judgment denying review is affirmed and the approvals remain in effect. The mandate returns the case for implementation of that judgment; the reviewed procedural objections require no additional agency hearing.

#### Source Notes

California ex rel. Lockyer v. FERC, 329 F.3d 700 (CA9 2003); Federal Power Act §§203, 313; 5 U.S.C. §706; FERC proceedings EC01-49-002 and EC01-41-002.

**End of entry: California ex rel. Lockyer v. FERC, merits decision, March 29, 2004.**

### National Archives and Records Administration v. Favish, No. 02-954

Merits decision, March 30, 2004

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 3, 2003.

**Route and judgment below:** On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, which required disclosure of four photographs following the district court's privacy and public-interest proceedings.

**Questions:** Whether FOIA Exemption 7(C) protects surviving relatives' privacy in death-scene images and what evidentiary foundation and governmental-transparency contribution are required for this misconduct-based disclosure demand.

**Posture:** The four images depict Vincent Foster's death scene. Relatives presented their own privacy evidence, and Favish presented documentary criticisms and image-specific hypotheses concerning the official investigation. The Office of Independent Counsel transferred the records to the National Archives on March 23, 2004, and the custodian was substituted before decision.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 9-0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The judgment permits withholding of the protected material in the four disputed photographs. Complete withholding requires the ordinary determination that no reasonably segregable nonexempt material remains; any such material must be disclosed.

#### Opinion Topology

STEVENS, J., delivered the opinion of the Court. STONE-ZSELA, C. J., concurred in the judgment as to Part III.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Parts I–II | Stevens | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | All nine support reversal and summary judgment permitting withholding of the protected depictions. | Family privacy, public interest and four-image balance. |
| Opinion of the Court, Part III | Stevens | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | All nine support this remedial component; Stone-Zsela agrees in judgment only. | Mandatory segregation. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. Living-family privacy and the asserted public interest

**Holding and operative rule:** Exemption 7(C) permits withholding law-enforcement material whose disclosure could reasonably be expected to constitute an unwarranted invasion of surviving relatives' own privacy through dissemination of a close family member's death images. The agency must establish the law-enforcement and privacy predicates and ultimately justify withholding; when the asserted public benefit is exposing official misconduct or a meaningful investigative deficiency, the requester must identify a significant governmental-transparency interest, supply an objective foundation warranting a reasonable belief that such a deficiency might have occurred, and show a plausible connection between the requested material and that issue, without first proving misconduct itself.

**Authority:** Part I of Stevens's opinion establishes the unanimous rule. All nine also join Part II's application below.

**Controlling explanation:** The relatives assert their own interest against intrusive public dissemination, supported by declarations describing distress and unwanted publicity. That interest fits personal privacy under 7(C) without inheritance of the deceased's rights or a family veto. Law-enforcement compilation is undisputed here. The statutory standard is 7(C)'s reasonable expectation of an unwarranted invasion, distinct from Exemption 6's clearly-unwarranted standard.

Ray requires concrete privacy and demonstrated incremental transparency. Bibles and Reporters Committee focus the public-interest inquiry on what disclosure contributes to understanding government conduct. A requester cannot give substantial public-interest weight to a bare accusation merely by requesting sensitive images. But requiring established wrongdoing would withhold evidence because the requester has not yet proved the very deficiency under investigation. A reasonable evidentiary foundation and plausible image-specific connection avoid that circular demand while permitting the actual balance. Other demonstrated transparency purposes receive their own analysis. The agency retains persuasion throughout.

FLRA preserves an interest in controlled official dissemination despite scattered public exposure. Unauthorized publication of a degraded gun-hand image therefore does not itself waive the family's remaining interest in official release of the original. FLRA also distinguishes permission to withhold under FOIA from a separate legal prohibition on disclosure.

**Precedent treatment:** Ray's privacy and incremental-value rule is extended to living relatives' death-image interests; Bibles and Reporters Committee supply the governmental-transparency inquiry. FLRA's dissemination and permission-versus-prohibition distinctions are applied.

##### II. These four protected depictions may be withheld

**Holding and operative rule:** On this record, the demonstrated family intrusion outweighs the sufficiently supported incremental governmental-transparency value of the four disputed death-scene depictions. The custodian is entitled to summary judgment permitting withholding of their protected material under Exemption 7(C), subject to the ordinary segregability requirement stated in Part III.

**Authority:** Part II of Stevens's opinion, joined by all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Favish supplies documents and specific hypotheses. His gun-hand argument concerns thumb entrapment, alleged backspatter, the gun's location beneath the leg and the superior detail of an original over a leaked copy. His shoulder-image argument concerns blood transfer, possible later flow and an asserted investigative omission. Those are the closest connections between the requested images and claimed deficiencies, and the Court evaluates them as such.

The disclosed report addresses the thumb indentation and trigger position, laboratory residue, high-velocity spatter, transfer to the face and possible head movement while checking vital signs. It also exposes limitations, including possible contamination of trace evidence through common packaging of clothing. These explanations show what investigators examined; their official source does not make every inference conclusive. Favish's blood-sequence criticism and the possibility of a clearer independent visual assessment are considered against that disclosed work. On the supplied evidence, the additional contribution of these photographs to identifying a meaningful investigative failure remains insufficient to outweigh the concrete family intrusion.

Alleged changes to the Haut report, gun-identification statements, autopsy attendance, x-ray records and vehicle observations also question the investigation. Their relevance depends on the contribution of these four images: death-scene photographs do not resolve who attended the later autopsy, which version of a report was altered or which vehicle witnesses observed. The other two photographs have a more general asserted contribution. The governing balance thus permits withholding the protected depictions after consideration of the actual evidence, rather than from the number of official investigations or a presumption of official infallibility. The Court resolves the disclosure demand without adjudicating the underlying cause of death.

**Precedent treatment:** Ray and Bibles are applied to the actual additional agency transparency these records would provide; Center for National Security Studies preserves independent assessment and the agency's ultimate burden under the particular exemption.

##### III. Reasonably segregable nonexempt material must be released

**Holding and operative rule:** Before complete withholding of a photograph is implemented, the agency must establish that no reasonably segregable nonexempt material remains; any such material must be disclosed after exempt portions are removed. Photographic form creates no exception, and the requester need not prove that a nonexempt portion would be useful or would independently advance the asserted theory.

**Authority:** Part III of Stevens's opinion supplies eight direct joins to the complete rule and its application, at the scope stated in the opinion topology. Stone-Zsela concurs in this component's judgment without joining its rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 552(b) makes disclosure of reasonably segregable nonexempt material mandatory, with the agency retaining its withholding burden. Center for National Security Studies requires that determination under the actual exemption and record. The conclusion that protected death depictions may be withheld does not establish that every part of every photograph is exempt. The agency and reviewing court must make the ordinary segregation determination before complete withholding. A crop that continues to depict protected remains retains the privacy analysis applicable to that content even if it omits the face. Favish's particular neck-only example concerns an additional photograph outside this four-image review.

**Precedent treatment:** Center for National Security Studies and Landano are applied to mandatory, actual segregation; the same duty applies to photographs as to other agency records.

#### Precedent Treatment

United States Department of State v. Ray supplies concrete privacy and incremental-transparency balancing while preserving the possibility of a supported derivative inquiry. Bibles v. Oregon Natural Desert Association and Department of Justice v. Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press supply the governmental-conduct focus; Exemption 7(C)'s own threshold governs. Department of Defense v. Federal Labor Relations Authority preserves controlled official dissemination and the distinction between FOIA permission and an independent disclosure prohibition. Center for National Security Studies v. United States Department of Justice and United States Department of Justice v. Landano preserve agency persuasion and mandatory segregation under the applicable exemption.

#### Law After Decision

Exemption 7(C) protects living relatives' demonstrated privacy against unwarranted disclosure of death images. A misconduct-based disclosure claim requires a reasonable evidentiary foundation and plausible connection to the requested material, assessed with the actual evidence and agency burden intact. The rule permits withholding of these protected depictions while requiring disclosure of reasonably segregable nonexempt portions; other demonstrated transparency purposes retain their own analysis.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The lower court must enter summary judgment permitting withholding of the four protected depictions and enforce the statutory segregation duty. Before complete withholding, the agency must establish that no reasonably segregable nonexempt material remains or release any such material. The judgment concerns the present four-image FOIA demand; already released material remains released.

#### Source Notes

5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B), (b)(7)(C), and the concluding segregability requirement of § 552(b); National Archives and Records Administration v. Favish, No. 02-954.

### United States v. Flores-Montano, No. 02-1794

Merits decision, March 30, 2004

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued February 25, 2004.

**Route and judgment below:** On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, which on March 14, 2003, summarily affirmed suppression under its categorical reasonable-suspicion requirement for fuel-tank removal.

**Question:** Whether customs officials at an actual international border require reasonable suspicion before removing, disassembling and reassembling a vehicle's fuel tank to inspect for contraband.

**Material posture:** At Otay Mesa, a mechanic removed the tank and officers discovered marijuana. The procedure was reversible and took roughly one hour including the wait; serious destruction was not established. The Government declined to rely on reasonable suspicion in the district court.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 9-0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The absence of individualized suspicion for the reversible fuel-tank inspection does not support suppression on the categorical ground applied below.

#### Opinion Topology

SOUTER, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, THOMAS, GINSBURG and BREYER, JJ., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Reversible fuel-tank inspection at the international border

**Holding and operative rule:** Officials conducting a lawful international-border inspection may remove, disassemble and reassemble an entering vehicle's fuel tank to inspect for contraband without individualized reasonable suspicion when the method does not inflict serious destruction or otherwise make the search unreasonable. The Government must establish the actual border and inspection predicates; mechanical complexity or use of specialized labor alone adds no suspicion requirement, while the means and extent of property interference remain independently subject to Fourth Amendment reasonableness.

**Authority:** Souter's opinion for the unanimous Court controls the complete rule, method-and-duration application and suppression disposition.

**Controlling explanation:** Ramsey grounds inspection of entering effects in the actual international crossing. Montoya de Hernandez addresses the distinct privacy and dignity intrusion of detention of a person; its reasoning does not turn use of a mechanic on a vehicle compartment into a bodily search. An entering vehicle's concealed fuel compartment remains subject to border inspection.

Flores-Montano emphasizes possession, mechanical risk, specialized labor and delay. Possessory interests remain real at the border, but they call for examination of the actual method and effects rather than a new suspicion tier based on complexity. Ramirez and Banks separate authority to search from the reasonableness of execution. They preserve independent limits on unnecessary or disproportionate damage even when the underlying search is authorized. Soldal's protection of possession of a home likewise does not equate constitutional coverage with unreasonable interference or establish a fuel-tank suspicion rule.

The Government established an actual border examination at Otay Mesa. A mechanic removed and reassembled the tank, the procedure and wait took approximately one hour, and serious damage or destruction was not established. Those actual circumstances make this inspection reasonable; speculative mechanical harm does not establish a different method or add a categorical suspicion requirement. The seized contraband supplies no retrospective justification, and the Court relies on the border ground the Government preserved rather than finding reasonable suspicion that it declined to assert below.

**Operative scope:** The rule covers entering vehicles and reversible compartment examination within a genuine border inspection. Bodily searches and materially different destructive methods or prolonged detention require their own factual and legal assessment; destructive drilling and serious damage are not established by this record.

**Precedent treatment:** Ramsey's border basis and Montoya de Hernandez's personal-intrusion distinction are applied. Ramirez and Banks retain independent reasonable-execution scrutiny. Soldal is distinguished at its actual home-possession holding without denying the possessory interest in the vehicle.

#### Precedent Treatment

United States v. Ramsey supplies the actual-border inspection authority. United States v. Montoya de Hernandez retains the distinct rules governing personal detention and bodily intrusion; mechanical work on a vehicle does not import that intrusion. United States v. Ramirez and United States v. Banks supply the distinction between authority and reasonable execution, including independently limited property damage. Soldal v. Cook County retains its home-possession holding and does not establish an additional fuel-tank suspicion requirement.

#### Law After Decision

Reasonable suspicion is unnecessary for the reversible fuel-tank inspection described here within a lawful international-border examination. Courts must require the Government to establish that examination's predicates and independently assess the actual manner and extent of interference. Specialized labor or mechanical complexity alone does not create a heightened suspicion category.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The district court must resume the prosecution without excluding the contraband on the rejected categorical fuel-tank ground. Any genuinely independent, properly raised issue remains for its ordinary adjudication, and the prosecution retains its burden to prove the charged offenses.

#### Source Notes

U.S. Const., Amdt. 4; United States v. Flores-Montano, No. 02-1794; 19 U.S.C. § 1581(a). The inspection occurred at the actual international border and involved reversible disassembly without established serious damage.

**End of entry: United States v. Flores-Montano, merits decision, March 30, 2004.**

### BedRoc Limited, LLC v. United States, No. 02-1593

Merits decision, March 31, 2004

#### Chronology and Posture

**Citation:** 541 U.S. 176.

Argued January 20, 2004. On review of the Ninth Circuit's judgment sustaining the United States' mineral reservation in Nevada land patented under the Pittman Underground Water Act of 1919. The question is whether ordinary sand and gravel are valuable minerals reserved by that Act. The patent owners seek recognition of their title and rejection of the Government's mineral-trespass premise.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg.

The disputed ordinary sand and gravel passed under the Pittman patents. On remand the lower court must implement the owners' title, reject mineral-trespass liability resting solely on a reservation of these deposits, and resolve any remaining accounting or independently supported claim consistently with that construction.

#### Opinion Topology

KENNEDY, J., announced the judgment of the Court and delivered an opinion. THOMAS, J., filed an opinion concurring in the judgment. STEVENS, J., filed a dissenting opinion. GINSBURG, J., filed a dissenting opinion.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion announcing the judgment | Kennedy | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia | Supports reversal | Complete Pittman-specific textual and historical-category rationale |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Thomas | Breyer | Supports reversal on a distinct ground | Ordinary-material exclusion; refusal to extend Western Nuclear while preserving its settled statutory-property result |
| Dissent | Stevens | Souter and Ginsburg | Would affirm | Related statutory mineral category and continuity of reserved title |
| Additional dissent | Ginsburg | — | Would affirm; also joins Stevens | Genuine statutory uncertainty and the sovereign-grant canon |

No single explanatory rationale commands a majority.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The Pittman reservation excludes ordinary sand and gravel

**Controlling proposition:** The Pittman Act's reservation of valuable minerals does not reserve the ordinary sand and gravel at issue, and those materials passed with the patented land. Contemporary commercial profitability alone does not establish federal title under that reservation.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia and Kennedy adopt the opinion announcing the judgment; Thomas and Breyer expressly adopt the ordinary-material exclusion through their separate ground. These six directly support the stated statutory result and category, while their distinct explanations do not form a single majority rationale or a controlling logical subset under Marks.

**Explanation of the divided grounds:** The plurality reads the limiting adjective in the Act's contemporary mining-law setting. Congress reserved a qualified resource category; a later market does not enlarge that estate. Western Nuclear construed the broader reservation in the Stock-Raising Homestead Act and therefore does not erase Pittman's qualifier. Amoco supplies the distinction between identifying the resource originally reserved and preserving later valuable uses of that resource. The Government's reliance on present utility skips the first inquiry. The plurality rejects the argument that valuable merely repeats an economic quality already implicit in minerals because that reading gives insufficient force to the particular enacted phrase. Thomas and Breyer agree that ordinary sand and gravel fall outside the Pittman category, but consider the adjective immaterial. They regard Western Nuclear's inclusion of those ordinary materials as mistaken, preserve its settled property-law operation under its own statute, and decline to extend its reasoning to this Act. Neither path depends on a finding of these deposits' market value in 1919.

The governing exclusion concerns ordinary sand and gravel under Pittman patents. Special deposits and differently worded reservations require their own statutory construction.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Watt v. Western Nuclear:** Its construction remains effective for the Stock-Raising Homestead Act. The plurality distinguishes Pittman's qualified wording; Thomas and Breyer preserve the earlier statutory-property result but decline to extend its reasoning.
- **Amoco Production Co. v. Southern Ute Indian Tribe:** The plurality applies its requirement to identify the resource actually reserved, preserving new uses of a resource already within that estate. Its distinct-methane holding remains intact.
- **Marks v. United States:** The different grounds for reversal do not yield a logical-subset rationale. The expressly shared statutory exclusion has six supporters at its stated scope.

#### Law After Decision

Ordinary sand and gravel are conveyed under the Pittman Act patents rather than retained by the valuable-minerals reservation. The competing explanations do not establish a general rule that all newly profitable materials belong to patentees or a common rule for differently worded land grants. Western Nuclear continues to govern its own statute.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Thomas, joined by Breyer, concurring in the judgment.** The two reservation statutes should be read alike; the commercial character of minerals is already part of Western Nuclear's analysis, so the adjective does not supply a meaningful distinction. In their view that decision incorrectly placed ordinary sand and gravel in the reserved category. Reliance on settled property rights counsels leaving its construction of the Stock-Raising Homestead Act in place, but does not require repeating the mistake in a different statute. They therefore support the owners' title and reversal while withholding assent to the plurality's central textual distinction.

**Stevens, joined by Souter and Ginsburg, dissenting.** Western Nuclear already limits its mineral category to resources with commercial use. Pittman's related provisions use minerals and valuable minerals interchangeably, so the added word does not exclude these commercially useful materials. The statutes' relationship and settled land-title expectations favor continuity. Amoco involved a chemically distinct gas outside a coal reservation; it does not establish that new demand removes the same sand and gravel from a mineral class originally reserved. The dissent would affirm federal title.

**Ginsburg, additionally dissenting.** The retained mineral estate and the practical historical allocation of control leave genuine uncertainty about these useful deposits. Western Nuclear's rule for construing uncertain sovereign grants favors the estate retained by the United States. That ground concerns the original conveyance; it does not rest on automatic deference to federal officials or create title merely from present profitability.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns for proceedings implementing the title construction and the remand directions stated in the judgment. The Supreme Court's merits review is concluded.

#### Source Notes

Pittman Underground Water Act of 1919, §8; No. 02-1593; 541 U.S. 176. [Argument and decision dates](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/02-1593.ZS.html). The disposition rests on the statutory category of the commonplace deposits, not a finding of their local price or profitability in 1919.

### Arakaki v. Hawaii

Merits decision, April 8, 2004

#### Chronology and Posture

On full merits submission. Review of the Ninth Circuit's December 31, 2002 judgment, 314 F.3d 1091. The questions concern Hawaii's ancestry qualification for elected Office of Hawaiian Affairs trustees and the plaintiffs' standing to challenge the distinct appointment restriction. The court below affirmed the elected-candidate injunction under the Fifteenth Amendment and Voting Rights Act §2 and vacated appointment relief for lack of standing. The equal-protection ground was also preserved.

An otherwise qualified candidate was refused nomination papers because he lacked the ancestry defined by descent from the pre-1778 aboriginal peoples. The plaintiffs established election injury but supplied no actual or imminent appointment-related injury.

All nine Justices participated at submission and decision.

#### Judgment

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Elected OHA trusteeships | Affirmed, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | — | Declaratory and injunctive relief against the ancestry qualification remains in force. |
| Appointment restriction | Affirmed, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | — | Appointment relief vacated; claim dismissed for lack of standing, without prejudice. |

Election officials must accept otherwise qualified nominations and permit elected candidates to serve without the ancestry condition. Before the next candidate-filing period they must remove that prerequisite from forms and eligibility instructions; the district court may enforce compliance. The relief reaches the qualification itself and everyone subject to it, because individual permission would leave the public ballot restricted. Neutral qualifications and fiduciary duties continue to apply.

#### Opinion Topology

GINSBURG, J., delivered the opinion of the Court. STONE-ZSELA, C. J., filed an opinion concurring in the judgment as to elected eligibility.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer in full; Stone-Zsela for appointment standing | Affirms both components | Eight-Justice election rationale; unanimous appointment-standing rationale |
| Concurrence in the election judgment | Stone-Zsela | — | Agrees with election relief | Categorical public-candidacy equal-protection rule; joins the Court's appointment ruling |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The complete ancestry exclusion fails equal protection

**Controlling proposition:** A State restricting eligibility for public office by race must establish an important lawful objective and a substantial, evidence-supported relationship between that exclusion and the objective, accounting for actual coverage, burdens and concrete practicable alternatives that would comparably advance the objective with materially less racial disadvantage. Hawaii's complete ancestry exclusion from elected OHA trusteeships fails that standard; otherwise qualified non-Hawaiians must be permitted to seek and hold the office.

**Authority:** Ginsburg's opinion of the Court, joined in full on this issue by the other seven Associates named in the opinion topology, supplies eight direct rationale votes.

**Controlling explanation:** Rice establishes that OHA trustees are chosen in a public state election and that the ancestry definition operates as a racial qualification; it expressly left candidacy undecided. Adarand supplies the common substantial-justification standard, and Concrete Works requires government to justify the actual classification and burden rather than rely on purpose alone. Nixon v. Herndon supplies the equal-protection protection against state-created racial exclusion from public political participation. Faithful service to Hawaiian beneficiaries is an important lawful objective. But that objective does not establish that every person lacking the ancestry is unable to serve faithfully, or that ancestry guarantees competent trusteeship. Enforceable fiduciary duties can bind every trustee without a universal racial disqualification. Hawaii has not established the required relationship or justified rejecting that concrete alternative. Its distinction between trustee qualifications and the electorate explains why Rice left a new question; opening voting to everyone nevertheless does not justify the separate exclusion from eligibility to serve. The otherwise qualified candidate's denial of nomination papers establishes the concrete burden, and the record raises no disputed ordinary qualification requiring another trial. This application addresses the ancestry condition for public office, while preserving the distinct legal rules governing beneficiary programs and genuine tribal political membership.

##### Election injury does not establish standing to challenge appointments

**Controlling proposition:** A plaintiff must establish an actual or imminent personal injury from the challenged appointment restriction to obtain a judgment on that restriction. Because these plaintiffs show only election-related injury, the appointment claim must be dismissed for lack of standing, without prejudice.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join this portion of the Court's opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** Warth v. Seldin requires the claimant's own injury on the claim presented. Exclusion from an election does not establish exclusion from an appointment or a sufficiently imminent prospect of one. The distinct appointment restriction therefore cannot be adjudicated at these plaintiffs' request; the lower court correctly vacated the appointment ruling and directed dismissal.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Rice v. Cayetano:** Applied for OHA's public state-election character and ancestry's racial operation. Its reserved candidacy question is resolved through equal protection, not by treating the Fifteenth Amendment voting holding as already dispositive.
- **Adarand Constructors v. Peña:** Applied as the common substantial-justification rule, retaining government's burden to prove objective, relationship, coverage and burdens and to answer supported practicable alternatives.
- **Concrete Works of Colorado v. Denver:** Applied for actual evidence and fit under that standard; a permissible beneficiary objective alone does not establish the fit missing here.
- **Nixon v. Herndon:** Applied for protection against state-created racial exclusion from public political participation.
- **Warth v. Seldin:** Applied to require the plaintiffs' own injury on the distinct appointment claim.

#### Law After Decision

The ancestry qualification for elected OHA office is unenforceable under the governing equal-protection standard. Hawaii's beneficiary responsibilities remain lawful purposes, but they do not supply the missing justification for this complete exclusion. The appointment branch ends on standing. The Court's sufficient equal-protection ground supplies no independent Fifteenth Amendment or §2 candidacy rule.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in the election judgment.** Equal protection should categorically forbid a racial-ancestry condition on eligibility for elected public state office, even when the electorate is open to everyone. A challenger must prove the public election, the operative racial qualification and personal exclusion by a preponderance of the evidence; proof that the candidate would win is unnecessary. Once those predicates are established, a racial constituency cannot justify the unequal civic status. Rice supplies the public-election premise, Nixon the equal-protection foundation and Smith v. Allwright a related participation analogy. Opening the ballot to voters does not cure exclusion from eligibility to serve. This specific civic-exclusion rule would leave Adarand's ordinary classification standard intact and would not decide genuine tribal governance or other Hawaiian programs. Stone-Zsela agrees with the election-wide relief and joins the Court's separate appointment-standing ruling.

The district court shall maintain and enforce the elected-candidate relief and dismiss the appointment component without prejudice. The Supreme Court's merits review is concluded.

### United States v. Lara, No. 03-107

Merits decision, April 19, 2004

#### Chronology and Posture

**Citation:** 541 U.S. 193.

Argued January 21, 2004. Review of the Eighth Circuit's en banc judgment barring a federal prosecution following a Spirit Lake Tribe prosecution for conduct involving an officer on the reservation. Lara is an Indian who belongs to another tribe. The question is whether Congress may restore the Tribe's inherent criminal authority over nonmember Indians so that the tribal and federal prosecutions derive from separate sovereign sources.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Scalia and Souter.

The federal case must be reinstated and proceed under ordinary criminal procedure. The lower court must resolve the charge and independently preserved defenses; the United States bears its ordinary burden to prove the federal offense beyond a reasonable doubt.

#### Opinion Topology

STONE-ZSELA, C. J., delivered the opinion of the Court. KENNEDY and THOMAS, JJ., each filed an opinion concurring in the judgment. SOUTER, J., filed a dissenting opinion.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stone-Zsela | Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports reversal | Constitutional restoration of this retained authority and its double-jeopardy consequence |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Kennedy | — | Supports reversal | Statutory nondelegation ground; withholds the affirmative constitutional-restoration holding |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Thomas | — | Supports reversal | Statutory recognition under inherited federal-common-law premises; withholds the Court's constitutional-power account |
| Dissent | Souter | Scalia | Would affirm | Nonmember prosecution power derives from federal delegation |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Congress may remove this restriction on retained tribal criminal authority

**Controlling proposition:** Congress may lift the federal common-law restriction on an existing tribe's retained criminal authority over nonmember Indians in the presented reservation setting. Section 1301(2) validly recognizes and restores that authority; a prosecution exercising it is tribal rather than federal in source and therefore does not bar this subsequent federal prosecution under the Double Jeopardy Clause.

**Authority:** The five-Justice opinion of the Court identified above supplies five direct votes for the complete rule and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** Wheeler makes the ultimate source of prosecutorial power decisive. Tribes existed as sovereigns before the United States, and substantial federal supervision does not itself make their retained powers federal. Duro described the nonmember limitation under the federal relationship then governing; it did not adjudicate an affirmative restoration statute as constitutionally forbidden. The Indian Commerce and Necessary and Proper Clauses permit Congress to adjust this federal restriction on an existing tribal sovereign, rather than create a new federal prosecutor. Section 1301(2) expressly recognizes inherent tribal criminal authority over all Indians. Subsection (4) defines Indian as a person who would be subject to 18 U.S.C. §1153 if committing one of its listed offenses in Indian country. The amendment removes the particular federal-law barrier.

The principal contrary argument treats Duro's membership boundary and reference to delegation as a permanent constitutional determination of sovereign source. That reads the decision beyond the issue it resolved. Wheeler itself left restored authority open, and Duro addressed the absence of affirmative congressional action. Congress's chosen label alone could not establish constitutional power, but the surviving Tribe, the source of the restriction and the limited adjustment here supply that power independently. The Spirit Lake Tribe consequently exercises its own criminal authority; the later federal prosecution invokes a different sovereign source. The holding concerns nonmember Indians in this reservation setting. Non-Indian criminal jurisdiction lies outside the question, and the tribal proceeding's separate due-process and equal-protection validity was not presented. Applicable individual protections, including the Indian Civil Rights Act, retain their force.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **United States v. Wheeler:** Applied for the ultimate-source inquiry and the distinction between federal supervision and inherent tribal prosecution. Its previously open restoration question is resolved at the stated scope.
- **Duro v. Reina:** Its former nonmember-Indian restriction is recognized as altered by Congress within Congress's constitutional authority. Its reasoning does not establish an immutable bar to this restoration.
- **Strate v. A-1 Contractors; Atkinson Trading Co. v. Shirley; Nevada v. Hicks:** Their source-specific civil-jurisdiction holdings remain intact. They do not constitutionalize the criminal restriction that the express statute changes here.

#### Law After Decision

Section 1301(2)'s restoration permits the existing Tribe to exercise inherent criminal authority over the covered nonmember Indian. That tribal prosecution does not supply a same-sovereign bar to the later federal prosecution. Individual-rights challenges retain their own governing law and proper proceedings; the sovereign-source rule creates no procedural exemption.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Kennedy, concurring in the judgment.** Congress unmistakably chose recognition of inherent tribal authority rather than delegation of federal prosecutorial power. The second-federal-jeopardy theory therefore fails on the statute's choice. But that conclusion does not require approval of Congress's constitutional power to subject a nonmember citizen to another sovereign's criminal jurisdiction. Duro's concern with citizenship, membership and political participation presents a structural objection independent of the procedural fairness of a particular trial. Reserving due process alone would not answer it. Kennedy leaves the legitimacy of that asserted authority to a proceeding that actually contests it and withholds assent to the Court's affirmative constitutional-restoration holding.

**Thomas, concurring in the judgment.** Within Wheeler's inherited premises, a surviving tribal sovereign's criminal authority may be restricted by federal policy; the political branches have removed the particular inconsistency Duro identified. Section 1301 recognizes inherent authority instead of delegating a federal prosecution. That is sufficient for this defense. Thomas nevertheless questions the coexistence of broad congressional control and inherent tribal sovereignty, and rejects treating the Indian Commerce or Treaty Clause as an unexplained general legislative power over sovereign status. He accepts the result under the inherited federal-common-law framework while withholding the Court's broader affirmative constitutional account.

**Souter, joined by Scalia, dissenting.** Duro and Wheeler tie retained criminal sovereignty to membership; power to prosecute an outsider did not survive as inherent authority. Congress may authorize the Tribe to fill the resulting jurisdictional gap, but it cannot alter the constitutional source of that newly supplied power by calling it inherent. General federal supervision differs from conferring a power the Tribe lacked. The tribal prosecution thus exercises delegated federal authority for double-jeopardy purposes, and the subsequent federal prosecution is barred. This source conclusion does not require invalidating Congress's authorization of the tribal proceeding itself.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns to the federal courts for the reinstated prosecution and the ordinary proceedings stated in the judgment. The Supreme Court's review of the double-jeopardy defense is concluded.

25 U.S.C. §1301(2), (4); Indian Civil Rights Act; Fifth Amendment Double Jeopardy Clause; United States v. Wheeler, 435 U.S. 313; Duro v. Reina, 495 U.S. 676; No. 03-107; 541 U.S. 193. [Argument and decision dates](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/03-107.ZS.html). The tribal conviction's separate individual-rights validity is outside the question presented.

### Ammex, Inc. v. United States

Merits decision, April 20, 2004

#### Chronology and Posture

On full merits submission. Review of the Federal Circuit's July 1, 2003 judgment, 334 F.3d 1052, No. 02-1498, affirming the Court of International Trade's refusal to enforce an earlier judgment through contempt. Ammex's bonded store at the Ambassador Bridge obtained relief against Customs' original prohibition of duty-free fuel sales. After a requested IRS determination concerning federal excise tax, Customs initiated a later revocation under the definition of duty-free merchandise. The questions are whether the first judgment precludes that later action and whether contempt can stop it.

All nine Justices participated at submission and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The refusal to bar the later tax-dependent revocation through contempt remains in force. The earlier judgment continues to bind within its actual scope; Ammex must use the appropriate statutory review procedure to challenge the later decision.

#### Opinion Topology

KENNEDY, J., delivered the unanimous opinion of the Court.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The earlier judgment does not preclude this distinct later action

**Controlling proposition:** A judgment invalidating a particular administrative determination does not bar a genuinely later, distinct agency action based on a subsequently developed determination outside the adjudicated claim; a different legal label on the same operative action would not suffice. The party asserting preclusion must establish the required claim identity or actual issue determination within the earlier judgment's scope, which Ammex has not established for this IRS-dependent revocation.

**Authority:** Kennedy's unanimous opinion of the Court controls.

**Controlling explanation:** First Jersey and Lawlor distinguish later operative transactions from another effort to litigate the same claim. Arizona v. California's unanimous actual-adjudication holding separately requires attention to the issue a judgment truly determined. Semtek directs the court to identify the source and proper scope of preclusion, but its diversity-specific rendering-state borrowing rule does not govern this federal-question judgment. The first action rejected Customs' original determination; the later action followed the IRS response and turned on a distinct tax-dependent administrative determination. The earlier judgment did not determine that predicate. Ammex's strongest objection is that Customs could have raised a tax theory earlier and cannot hold back defenses to avoid finality. Unlitigated theories can indeed be barred within an adjudicated claim. But the difference here is the later operative action and subsequently developed determination, not simply another legal reason for the unchanged first action. Enforcing finality at the first judgment's actual scope therefore leaves this later decision subject to its own judicial review.

##### Contempt enforces the command actually issued

**Controlling proposition:** Contempt requires violation of a sufficiently definite operative judicial command and cannot enlarge an earlier judgment to adjudicate a later issue outside that command. Because the first judgment did not prohibit this distinct later tax-dependent action, contempt cannot bar the revocation.

**Authority:** The same unanimous opinion controls.

**Controlling explanation:** The trade court must enforce its actual judgment, including its final rejection of Customs' original determination. That obligation does not turn the judgment into permanent authorization for every later stage of Ammex's business. The operative command does not resolve the excise-tax predicate or prohibit a new decision based upon it. A contempt proceeding cannot supply that missing merits adjudication. Requiring Ammex to use the proper review channel leaves the new interpretation answerable to law while respecting the first judgment.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **SEC v. First Jersey Securities:** Applied to preserve finality while distinguishing later operative transactions; similarity in business activity does not expand the first adjudicated claim.
- **Lawlor v. National Screen Service:** Applied for the same distinction between later conduct and relitigation of one claim.
- **Arizona v. California, June 19, 2000, Part II:** Applied for the distinction between a final judgment's actual scope and a predicate never determined.
- **Montana v. United States:** Applied to the actual-determination requirement for issue preclusion.
- **Semtek International v. Lockheed Martin:** Applied to identify preclusion's proper source and scope; its diversity-specific incorporation rule is distinguished.

#### Law After Decision

The first judgment binds Customs on the determination it resolved. It does not preclude this later IRS-dependent revocation, and contempt cannot expand its command. A genuinely later administrative action remains reviewable through its appropriate channel; the Government receives no exemption from ordinary finality for a renamed version of the same action.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The judgment refusing contempt remains operative. Any challenge to the later revocation proceeds through the applicable statutory review channel, where its validity must be determined on that action's own legal and factual grounds. This Supreme Court enforcement review is concluded.

#### Source Notes

Ammex, Inc. v. United States, 334 F.3d 1052, No. 02-1498 (Federal Circuit); 19 U.S.C. §§1555(b), 1557(a); 26 U.S.C. §4081. The reviewed matter is enforcement and preclusion, not direct review of the later revocation's substantive tax ruling.

### Household Credit Services, Inc. v. Pfennig, No. 02-857

Merits decision, April 21, 2004

#### Chronology and Posture

**Citation:** 541 U.S. 232.

Argued February 23, 2004. Review of the Sixth Circuit's judgment rejecting Regulation Z's exclusion of credit-card over-limit fees from the finance charge. Pfennig's agreement specified a credit limit and a monthly charge for exceeding it. Her statements disclosed the charge separately. The question concerns the Board's authority to classify that fee, rather than concealment of the fee itself.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The lower court must apply the valid over-limit exclusion and dismiss the theory that these fees necessarily had to be included in the finance-charge subtotal. Any actually pleaded independent claim must be resolved under its own statutory duty.

#### Opinion Topology

THOMAS, J., delivered the unanimous opinion of the Court.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The Board may separately classify these over-limit charges

**Controlling proposition:** Under 15 U.S.C. §1604(a)'s express classification authority, the Board may reasonably exclude fees for exceeding an agreed credit limit from TILA's finance-charge category while leaving applicable separate disclosure duties in force. Regulation Z §226.4(c)(2) validly makes that classification, and knowingly honoring an over-limit transaction does not by itself require a different classification; the statutory-conflict or unreasonableness challenge fails here.

**Authority:** Thomas's unanimous opinion of the Court controls.

**Controlling explanation:** Mead requires actual delegated authority, Haggar requires independent enforcement of statutory limits, and Heimmermann keeps authorized interpretation within the underlying prohibition. Section 1604(a) expressly permits classifications, differentiations, adjustments and exceptions necessary or proper to effectuate TILA's purposes, prevent circumvention or evasion, or facilitate compliance. Its exclusion for section 1602(aa) mortgages retains its own scope; this case concerns a card account. The distinct exemption power in section 1604(f) does not supply the rule here.

Section 1605(a)'s broad definition of charges incident to credit does not unambiguously decide this contingent excess-limit category. Pfennig reasonably argues that an issuer permitting an additional advance imposes the fee because it extended credit. But permission for a transaction does not necessarily renegotiate the agreed limit, and the statutory scheme distinguishes finance charges from other open-end charges. The Board reasonably separates the agreed price of credit from a fee triggered by exceeding its terms, facilitating intelligible price comparison and compliance. Administrative convenience alone could not contradict Congress, and disclosure elsewhere alone could not validate an unlawful classification; the express delegation and permissible statutory distinction do that work.

Section 226.4(c)(2) specifically addresses exceeding a credit limit. Subsection (c)(3)'s different exclusion for paying items that overdraw an account contains a prior-written-agreement exception; that exception does not govern (c)(2). Smiley's treatment of charges as interest under another statute supplies no contrary TILA definition. The Court therefore sustains this category while requiring compliance with its actual terms and all independently applicable disclosure duties.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **United States v. Mead Corp.:** Applied to actual authority exercised through a binding regulation; its different conclusion about transaction-specific Customs letters does not govern this mechanism.
- **United States v. Haggar Apparel Co.:** Applied for independent statutory-boundary review followed by reasonableness within genuine delegated ambiguity.
- **Heimmermann v. First Union Mortgage Corp.:** Applied for actual delegation and preservation of substantive statutory limits; uncertainty alone cannot confer power.
- **Chevron U.S.A. v. Natural Resources Defense Council:** Applied within those actual authority and statutory limits to the reasonable regulatory choice.
- **Smiley v. Citibank:** Distinguished as a construction of national-bank interest. That statute's category does not determine the TILA finance-charge category.

#### Law After Decision

The challenged Regulation Z provision validly treats qualifying over-limit fees as other charges rather than finance charges, including when an issuer knowingly permits the transaction that exceeds the limit. The category remains subject to its regulatory terms and separate disclosure obligations. Courts must identify the actual charge and apply those duties; this decision supplies no general exemption for all default or credit-related fees.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns for application of the valid exclusion and the disposition stated in the judgment. The Supreme Court's review of the classification question is concluded.

#### Source Notes

15 U.S.C. §§1604(a), 1605(a), 1637; 12 C.F.R. §226.4(c)(2), (3); No. 02-857; 541 U.S. 232. [Argument and decision dates](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/02-857.ZS.html). The operative regulation was in force when these fees were charged.

**End of entry: Household Credit Services, Inc. v. Pfennig, merits decision, April 21, 2004.**

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### A compulsory purchase criterion can enforce an emissions standard

**Controlling proposition:** A state or local mandatory emissions criterion for new motor vehicles or engines subject to Clean Air Act §209(a) can adopt or enforce a prohibited standard even when the legal command addresses purchasers rather than manufacturers or sellers. Existing commercial availability of compliant vehicles does not by itself exempt an otherwise covered regulatory standard; preemption and relief require application of the statute to each actual criterion, covered vehicle and legally available qualification.

**Authority:** Breyer's opinion for the eight-Justice majority identified above supplies eight direct rationale votes.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 209(a)'s first sentence prohibits adoption or attempted enforcement of standards relating to emissions from covered new vehicles and engines. An emissions criterion is distinct from the mechanism enforcing it. A command to buy only qualifying vehicles can enforce the same criterion as a sales restriction. The second sentence separately forbids specified emissions approvals as conditions of initial sale, titling or registration; it does not confine the first sentence to manufacturers.

Cipollone requires attention to the actual duty, and Medtronic requires the comparison the particular statute commands. Their method applies here without importing another statute's different-or-additional requirement. The District and Souter emphasize manufacturing context and selection among products already certified and available. That context distinguishes private purchasing choice from compelled compliance, but does not remove an emissions rule imposed on independent purchasers from the first sentence. The statute requires neither proof that a manufacturer must invent a product nor a newly selected numerical limit. The exact availability qualifications matter to operation and remedy; they do not establish categorical immunity.

Boston Harbor distinguishes genuine proprietary action under its own statutory scheme. It calls for examination of the actor and transaction, not an automatic Clean Air Act procurement exception. Section 246's structured fleet program supplies context but is not an asserted authorization for these Rules. The Court therefore rejects the purchase-versus-sale premise while requiring the lower courts to apply the statute to the actual rules and preserved applications. Public procurement, statutory newness, contested rule construction and severability require the particular analysis the categorical judgment omitted. Certification of a vehicle, waiver of state standards and approval of these Fleet Rules are distinct legal questions.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Cipollone v. Liggett Group:** Applied to identify the enforceable substantive duty rather than its label; its cigarette-specific text and separate proposition coalitions retain their scope.
- **Medtronic v. Lohr:** Applied for actual statutory comparison and the distinction between inclusion within a clause and ultimate preemption. Its different-or-additional requirement is not inserted into §209(a).
- **Building and Construction Trades Council v. Associated Builders and Contractors (Boston Harbor):** Applied as a bounded distinction between genuine purchasing and regulation, subject to the governing statute; its labor-law holding creates no universal procurement exemption.

#### Law After Decision

Section 209(a) reaches covered emissions standards enforced through compulsory purchases as well as standards enforced through other commands. Commercial availability alone does not remove that regulatory character. Courts must still establish the statutory coverage and determine the effect of the actual rule terms and legally available application defenses. The all-six declaration of validity cannot rest on a categorical purchaser exemption, while rule-specific adjudication determines the lawful scope of prospective relief.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Souter, concurring in the judgment.** Section 209 should be read in its manufacturing and certification setting, with the presumption protecting traditional local authority. Selecting among vehicles actually available under the federal and California regimes need not impose a competing production requirement. His Cipollone and Medtronic positions require identification of the particular federal choice displaced; market consequences alone do not establish a forbidden standard. Section 246 also shows that purchase programs and production standards can have distinct statutory roles, although it does not authorize these Rules.

The broad prospective judgment nevertheless cannot stand on an assumption that all six Rules merely select available products. Rule 1186.1's purchase-certificate route ends July 1, 2004; its separate service-contract route does not replace that exception for every purchase. The other Rules contain different certificate procedures, vehicle classes, offsets and continuing control obligations. The lower courts must construe those provisions and determine whether any challenged command operates as a production requirement under his narrower reading. April 28 precedes the cutoff, and the uncertainty establishes neither an unavailable vehicle nor a post-cutoff violation. Souter would preserve severable applications shown to select available products, while accepting preemption of an application that actually compels a new emissions-related production choice. He rejects both an unconditional all-six availability premise and acceptance of the manufacturers' hypothetical-product interpretation as a fact, and therefore concurs only in the judgment.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns for the rule-specific adjudication and lawful prospective relief described in the judgment. The Supreme Court's review of the categorical purchase exemption is concluded.

### Vieth v. Jubelirer, No. 02-1580

Merits decision, April 28, 2004

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. A personal political-opportunity claim is justiciable

**Holding and operative rule:** The Equal Protection Clause permits a voter personally affected by electoral boundaries to challenge their intentional, material impairment of an existing or demonstrably attainable opportunity for effective political organization, participation, nomination or election. The claim requires the four distinct elements below, proved through an independently grounded lawful comparison, and is subject to the State's same-decision defense.

**Authority:** Parts I–III and V of Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's opinion, for the five-Justice majority identified above, directly adopt the complete framework, evidentiary rules, burdens and remand; Part IV has all nine joins.

**Controlling explanation:** Baker v. Carr requires a claim-specific inquiry into whether courts can apply legal standards. Davis v. Bandemer establishes that partisan districting is not categorically beyond judicial review, but leaves the substantive standard unsettled. The claim recognized here protects an individual's effective political opportunity against purposeful governmental obstruction. It requires neither a judicially preferred party allocation nor a general assessment of political fairness.

Shaw v. Reno and Johnson v. Miller require injury independent of a district's design. Cano v. Davis preserves that requirement while recognizing that actual organization, nomination or participation can be impaired before an election is lost. The Court extends those principles to the particular purposeful partisan deprivation defined here; the earlier cases did not themselves establish this complete claim. The objection that every disappointed voter could obtain a better map fails because the claim requires an actual opportunity, substantial practical impairment, prohibited purpose and causation measured against lawful conditions independent of the desired partisan result. Gaffney v. Cummings continues to permit ordinary political accommodation.

##### II. Opportunity, material impairment, prohibited purpose and causation

**Holding and operative rule:** A claimant must establish each of the following elements; evidence relevant to one does not substitute for another.

1. **Opportunity:** Identify a practical capacity, already existing or demonstrably attainable, for the affected voters or association to organize, participate, nominate or elect. Evidence must identify the people and activity involved. An emerging opportunity can qualify without a prior victory; party identity, desired additional seats and expected incumbent support alone do not establish one.
2. **Material impairment:** Show a substantial practical obstruction to that activity caused by an identified districting operation. Concentration, division, reassignment or disruption of a coalition must actually impair the opportunity. Ordinary inconvenience or an unfavorable electoral prospect alone is insufficient. Complete extinction of the opportunity, exclusion from every political activity and inevitable electoral defeat are unnecessary.
3. **Prohibited purpose:** Show that suppressing the identified opposition's effective opportunity because of its political association or participation, and thereby insulating governing power from electoral challenge, substantially motivated the decision. The purpose must actually contribute, but need be neither exclusive nor predominant. Awareness of partisan consequences or pursuit of ordinary partisan advantage alone does not establish this purpose.
4. **Material causation:** Identify how the challenged assignments materially produced the impairment, separating their effect from voter geography, ordinary competition and independently lawful requirements. The boundaries need not be the sole cause; a correlation between party preference and an unfavorable outcome is insufficient.

**Authority:** The five-Justice majority joins every element and each qualification.

**Controlling explanation:** Page v. Bartels requires an identified protected interest and an operation impairing it; Robertson v. Bartels prevents preferred constituents or anticipated incumbent support from becoming the injury. Cano supplies the distinction between political design and a real interference with participation. Reynolds v. Sims supports equal participation, and White v. Regester directs attention to practical access to political processes. These principles protect effective opportunity without guaranteeing a party's preferred representation.

The substantial-impairment requirement answers the argument that any lost advantage becomes unconstitutional. A difficult election is not itself a disabled political opportunity. Conversely, an actual obstruction of organization or nomination is actionable when imposed; the affected voters need not first lose a general election. Packing, cracking, shape, departures from customary criteria and projected seats can illuminate the mechanism or purpose, but their labels and measurements do not establish the required effect. Arlington Heights supplies contextual evaluation of purpose. The rule addresses deliberate suppression producing an actual material deprivation, while leaving legitimate political considerations within lawful districting.

##### III. Lawful comparison, ordinary proof and the State's defense

**Holding and operative rule:** The claimant bears persuasion by a preponderance of the evidence on all four elements, using a comparison with an independently justified, legally and practically feasible political opportunity to establish actual impairment and boundary-specific causation. Once that showing is made, the State bears persuasion by the same standard that materially the same lawful districting decision would have occurred without the prohibited purpose; if it fails, further balancing of partisan advantages cannot excuse the violation.

A preponderance means more likely true than not. A genuinely applied, independently justified fairness policy or actual population, geographic or statutory requirements can establish the defense only when the evidence shows that they account for materially the same lawful decision. A merely conceivable explanation or another asserted political objective does not carry that burden.

No particular drawing, expert method, number of maps or type of evidence is universally mandatory. A feasible alternative district, actual organizational activity, the effects of a reassignment or another supported comparison may establish the necessary propositions. Whichever method is used must independently establish lawful feasibility and causation; past success, an old map or a preferred seat allocation alone cannot do so. A claim whose comparison depends on a different configuration must establish that configuration's feasibility, including effects necessary to make the comparison work, and may require mapping evidence for that reason.

**Authority:** The same five Justices join the complete proof and defense sequence, including the substantive comparison requirement and flexibility in methods of proof.

**Controlling explanation:** Holder v. Hall supplies the discipline of a comparison grounded independently of the desired winner count, rather than a uniquely ideal arrangement. Its statutory elements are not incorporated into this constitutional claim. Miller and Easley v. Cromartie allow varied evidence while retaining the actual claim's necessary proof. They support the distinction between a mandatory substantive showing and an invariably required cartographic exhibit.

Brooks v. Barnes and Montiel v. Davis apply the distinction between a substantially motivating forbidden purpose and the government's proof of the same lawful decision. Mt. Healthy supplies that causal sequence, and Crawford-El preserves ordinary proof rather than an invented heightened threshold. Plaintiffs must establish the prohibited contribution to the actual impairment, but need not disprove every independent explanation before the burden shifts. The State must prove its actual explanation. Consistent use of an asserted fairness policy helps establish that explanation; the defense does not impose a separate obligation to enact a comprehensive fairness code.

Act 1 was adjudged malapportioned. Its use under a stay cannot establish a lawful baseline for Act 34, and this record supplies no finding that the two plans produced the same relevant effects. The trial court must examine a supported lawful comparison for the operative assignments.

##### IV. Article I does not require proportional party seats

**Holding and operative rule:** A projected congressional-seat supermajority for a party without a statewide majority of votes does not, by itself, violate Article I, sections 2 or 4. Congressional district elections create no entitlement to a proportionate statewide party delegation; such figures may serve as evidence under a separately established claim.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join Part IV and affirm dismissal of this independent theory.

**Controlling explanation:** Article I provides for election of Representatives; it does not turn separate district elections into a statewide proportional contest. Cook v. Gralike prohibits the official ballot penalty adjudicated there, rather than guaranteeing a party's share of seats. Reynolds and Gaffney likewise do not supply that guarantee. The voters' proposed vote-seat trigger therefore fails as an independent rule, even though the same evidence may illuminate purpose or actual operation under Parts I–III.

##### V. Apply the rule to the live Act 34 claim through ordinary proceedings

**Holding and operative rule:** The partisan equal-protection dismissal is vacated, and the District Court must permit conforming amendment, determine personal standing and assess the live Act 34 claim under Parts I–III. If a violation is proved, the State receives the first opportunity to correct the identified deprivation; any judicial remedy must address the responsible boundaries, extend no further than necessary and comply with the other governing districting requirements.

**Authority:** The five-Justice majority joins Part V; the four dissenters would affirm the partisan dismissal.

**Controlling explanation:** The appeal comes from dismissal, not findings establishing the new rule's factual predicates. Ordinary notice pleading requires identification of the opportunity and challenged operation, with allegations supporting purpose and causation; it does not require merits proof or a replacement map at the pleading stage. Personal exposure, causation and effective redress remain distinct standing requirements.

The renewed challenge concerns Act 34. The lower court must evaluate the actual amended allegations rather than assume a prior plan's findings or election establish current liability. Growe v. Emison preserves the State's initial remedial responsibility. The court shall use a prompt, workable schedule consistent with the election calendar and fit any relief to the proved injury, rather than impose a proportionate delegation or guarantee a seat. The operative plan remains in effect until supported relief is entered, and the separate population disposition remains intact. An additional standalone association claim requires preservation or amendment allowed by ordinary procedure; the equal-protection holding resolves the question properly before the Court. The rule applies to this dispute and other open cases in which the claim is properly presented.

#### Precedent Treatment

Baker v. Carr is applied to permit adjudication through identifiable legal standards. Davis v. Bandemer's justiciability holding is retained, and the present four-element rule supplies the controlling substantive standard. Gaffney v. Cummings retains legitimate political accommodation. Reynolds v. Sims and White v. Regester support meaningful participation without a guarantee of proportional party seats.

Shaw v. Reno, Johnson v. Miller and Cano v. Davis retain the independent-injury requirement; the Court extends their participation principles to the purposeful partisan impairment defined here. Page v. Bartels and Robertson v. Bartels retain the distinction between protected political activity and favorable constituency expectations. Holder v. Hall supplies an independently grounded comparison by analogy; its statutory capacity requirements remain specific to its cause. Easley v. Cromartie and Miller preserve varied evidence subject to the actual claim's proof.

Brooks v. Barnes and Montiel v. Davis supply the distinct purpose and same-decision sequence; Brooks's nomination-opportunity holding also illustrates a political activity distinct from general-election victory. Mt. Healthy supplies the causal defense, Arlington Heights the contextual purpose inquiry, and Crawford-El the ordinary proof standard. Cook v. Gralike retains its official-ballot-penalty scope. Growe v. Emison governs initial state remedial responsibility. Cano's independent racial-opportunity protection and the Voting Rights Act's distinct statutory causes retain their own elements and remedies.

#### Law After Decision

A personal equal-protection claim now lies for an identified, existing or demonstrably attainable political opportunity materially impaired by electoral boundaries substantially motivated by the defined purpose of suppressing opposition and insulating governing power. The five-Justice rule requires all four elements, an independently grounded lawful comparison and ordinary preponderance proof; the State then bears the same-decision defense. Proof may take varied forms, but must establish lawful feasibility and actual causation. Actual participation injury can arise before an electoral defeat.

Ordinary partisan considerations remain permissible, and a projected statewide vote-seat disparity supplies neither the personal deprivation nor an independent Article I violation. Courts apply the rule to properly presented open cases, determine individual standing and actual proof, and give the State the first remedial opportunity after a violation is established.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Stone-Zsela, concurring.** The Chief Justice would also ground the four-element protection in the First Amendment's guarantees of political expression and association. O'Hare Truck Service and Umbehr establish that government may burden association through a political penalty even when the affected person has no entitlement to the governmental benefit. Districting similarly cannot deliberately impose the material deprivation defined by the Court to suppress opposition and insulate governing power. The Chief Justice applies the same opportunity, substantial impairment, purpose, causation and same-decision requirements, with no automatic injury from partisan design or unfavorable seats. A standalone First Amendment claim must still be properly presented; this additional constitutional ground does not supply a finding about the Act 34 pleadings. No other Justice joins this writing.

**O'Connor, concurring in part and dissenting in part; Scalia and Thomas join.** The three join rejection of the Article I seat-ratio claim but would affirm dismissal of partisan dilution as a political question and overrule Bandemer's contrary conclusion for that category. O'Connor's Bandemer position distinguishes ordinary individual equality from a judicial allocation of partisan strength. The new opportunity and comparison language, they conclude, still requires judges to select the political arrangement against which disadvantage is measured. A lawful alternative can be feasible without being constitutionally owed, and purpose cannot supply that missing entitlement. Their Holder positions reinforce the objection to a court-selected allocation, while Growe protects legislative responsibility. They retain adjudication of independently defined exclusion, retaliation, population and racial claims under their own standards. The objection concerns the affirmative partisan-opportunity cause, not the Court's unanimous Article I rule.

**Kennedy, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** Kennedy joins Part IV and would affirm this partisan dismissal because the asserted theories and proposed standards fail to identify a sufficiently manageable personal constitutional burden in Act 34. He declines to declare that every future partisan claim is nonjusticiable. Holder requires a principled basis for comparison, while Cook, Cano and the political-expression principles reflected in his McConnell position leave room for an actually defined participation or association injury. The Court's new formulation, in his view, does not yet furnish a sufficient measure for this claim. A possible future expression-based standard is therefore a reason to retain Bandemer's openness, rather than to remand this case or join the announced four-element rule. He joins neither the categorical dissent's overruling proposal nor the Court's affirmative standard.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns to the three-judge District Court. It shall permit conforming amendment, determine personal standing and apply the announced standard to the live Act 34 claim under ordinary pleading and proof rules, on a prompt schedule suited to the election calendar. An additional association theory remains subject to ordinary preservation and amendment rules. If the plaintiffs establish a violation and defeat the State's defense, the court shall identify the deprivation and responsible boundaries, afford the State the first opportunity to correct them and fit any further relief to that violation. The Article I dismissal and separate population disposition retain their stated force.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. The intentional-racial claims fail on supported findings

**Holding and operative rule:** A claimant alleging intentional racial exclusion through redistricting procedures or plans under equal protection or Title VI § 601 must prove by a preponderance that racial discrimination was a motivating factor. Supported findings receive clear-error review; the trial court's findings sustain judgment for the defendants on the preserved intentional claims.

**Authority:** Part I of Souter's opinion controls unanimously.

**Controlling explanation:** Washington v. Davis and Arlington Heights require the prohibited purpose, assessed from the whole relevant record. Crawford-El preserves ordinary preponderance proof rather than a heightened evidentiary threshold. The trial court examined software access, allocation methods, notice, hearing locations and times, and treatment of public input. It considered short notice, inconvenient scheduling, limited geography and substantive debate, and exclusion of Democrats from deliberations. It also considered numerous hearings with increased attendance, accessible low-cost software, recordings, submissions and ordinary access to legislators. Accepting the criticisms did not compel a finding of racial purpose. The finding has not been shown clearly erroneous, and partisan advantage neither establishes racial purpose nor excuses racial injury.

The same supported findings sustain the intentional challenge to the plans. The trial court found a purpose to secure Republican advantage and legal survival, while finding no purpose to injure Black or Hispanic electoral opportunity. Those purposes can coexist; the actual finding controls here.

The pleaded Title VI claim is the § 601 intentional-discrimination action. Powell, Sandoval and South Camden distinguish that action from the bounded direct prospective action for a covered recipient's ongoing violation of a valid, applicable § 602 effects regulation. No such regulatory claim is presented. Powell and South Camden also reject a regulation-created effects right enforceable through § 1983. The lower court's broader suggestion that every Title VI route requires intent conflicts with those decisions. Sandoval assumes the regulations' validity and preserves agency enforcement.

**Precedent treatment:** Davis, Arlington Heights and Crawford-El govern purpose and ordinary proof. Powell, Sandoval and South Camden preserve the distinct § 601, bounded direct-regulatory and § 1983 routes; the current case applies only the preserved intent claim.

##### II. Section 2 requires demonstrated local electoral opportunity

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 2 does not require replacement of districts affording reliably demonstrated equal opportunity to elect protected voters' preferred candidates merely to increase racial percentages or seat totals. Plaintiffs bear the preponderance burden on unequal opportunity under the applicable voting predicates and complete circumstances; proportionality is relevant evidence, not a conclusive defense, and opportunity elsewhere cannot offset a proved local barrier.

**Authority:** Parts II–IV of Souter's opinion, for the seven-Justice majority identified above, control the statutory rule and district-specific applications; Scalia and Thomas concur only in the statutory judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** Gingles treats districting arrangements as actionable when they cause the statutory inequality. It supplies distinct electoral-capacity, cohesion and ordinary opposing-bloc-defeat predicates, followed by an independent totality inquiry. Whether opposing-bloc voting ordinarily defeats preferred candidates must account for circumstances such as an unopposed preferred candidate or incumbency that can make a particular success unrepresentative. Voinovich preserves the challengers' burden and the distinction between lawful state districting choices and a court's remedy for an established violation. De Grandy rejects both maximization and automatic proportionality protection. Valdespino permits judgment on an independently sufficient evidentiary ground. The supported performance and comparison findings provide that ground here.

The record finds racial polarization in the relevant areas. The question is whether the challenged arrangements nevertheless provide actual equal opportunity, based on supported local evidence. Neither a Black candidate's race nor incumbency alone identifies the voters' preference or immunizes a district. Forecasts must be supported by actual behavior, demographics and relevant electoral conditions; they are not guarantees of particular victories. Brooks requires attention to nomination as well as general-election opportunity. The distinct congressional and House applications below retain those limits.

**Precedent treatment:** Gingles and Voinovich supply the causal and totality framework; De Grandy rejects maximization and aggregate offsets; Valdespino supplies the independent-evidence path; Brooks preserves examination of the affected nomination process.

##### III. The challenged congressional districts retain supported opportunity

**Holding and operative rule:** The supported findings concerning CDs 3, 17 and 23 sustain rejection of the presented Section 2 claims. The assessment concerns dependable opportunity under their actual electoral conditions, including the conditional primary structure, rather than a racial headcount or a guarantee of every future result.

**Authority:** The seven-Justice coalition identified for Parts II–IV controls this application.

**Controlling explanation:** The findings draw on voting history, registration, demographics and election analyses. CDs 3 and 17 were expected to elect Black voters' preferred candidates, with no evidenced lawful alternative materially improving performance, a point virtually conceded. CD 23 received a separate contested assessment. The challengers' 48.0-percent projection assumed an open primary, particular turnout and cohesion estimates, and no incumbency benefit; the court explained why those assumptions understated likely performance and also found open-seat opportunity.

Florida's conditional open-primary rule permits all electors to vote when the candidates share one party and its nominee will face no general-election opposition. Under the applied construction, a qualified write-in candidate supplies opposition and closes that primary. The court's expectation that a write-in candidate would qualify when CD 23's performance was threatened rested on election history and increased write-in qualification after the amendment. That remains a forecast, not an actual filing or statutory guarantee.

For 2004, chapter 2003-415 provides nomination by the highest vote total, with tied highest totals resolved by lot, and suspends second primaries until January 1, 2006. The earlier projected return of majority runoffs in 2004 is obsolete. Independent local findings address possible vote splitting and expected coalescence or withdrawal when opportunity is threatened. The continued plurality system was itself considered, and the surviving evidence includes local demographics, voting history and the express open-seat finding. Those supported findings sustain the judgment under the actual primary law. Their predictions remain conditional, rather than guarantees of coalescence or a statutory assurance of success.

**Precedent treatment:** De Grandy and Valdespino require actual supported opportunity; Brooks requires the nomination conditions to be evaluated directly rather than assumed away.

##### IV. The House performance and packing claims retain their separate proof

**Holding and operative rule:** The local findings concerning HDs 104, 108 and 118 sustain the expected-performance judgments on the claims presented, while the packing claims concerning HDs 103 and 109 fail for lack of a supported lawful rearrangement improving affected voters' opportunity. An improvement in another district cannot cure a proved impairment in HD 108 or any other district.

**Authority:** The same seven-Justice coalition controls these distinct local applications.

**Controlling explanation:** HDs 104 and 118 have favorable closed-primary findings and open-primary projections of 53.7 and 51.3 percent, respectively. HD 108 requires closer attention: the court accepted 47.2 percent as reasonable in the unlikely open-primary setting, with less than an even chance of prevailing if other circumstances stayed equal. Candidates and issues could change the outcome. Its favorable assessment includes a virtually uncontested 52.0-percent closed-primary projection assuming a single candidate with substantial Black support, history including an actual open-seat contest, and express predictions of a closed primary and coalescence around one preferred candidate.

The incorporation of the earlier runoff discussion requires evaluation under the continuing plurality system. The coalescence finding independently addressed that mechanism. Its continuation until 2006 supplies no new performance evidence or demonstration that the surviving local predictions are clearly erroneous. Plaintiffs still must prove unequal opportunity caused by the boundaries; a plausible adverse contingent election does not itself carry that burden. The result rests on the supported combined local assessment, not conjectured citizenship growth or a two-candidate projection treated as proof of every multi-candidate outcome. A structural nomination barrier cannot be dismissed simply as voters' failure to coalesce. HD 118's gains cannot offset a proved barrier in HD 108.

The plaintiffs conceded expected performance in HDs 103 and 109 but alleged packing. The court examined neighboring districts, their opportunities, county geography and proposed transfers; it found no demonstrated lawful rearrangement improving the affected voters' opportunity. HD 109's comparison depends in part on HD 108's performance, at the bounded scope sustained here. Racial concentration alone does not establish that injury. HD 118 also has the independent finding that no evidenced lawful alternative materially improves performance.

**Precedent treatment:** De Grandy preserves the local inquiry and prohibits conclusive geographic offsets. Voinovich and Valdespino require supported opportunity and a lawful comparison, while Brooks preserves nomination-stage analysis.

**Preserved statutory scope:** The active Section 2 disputes concern CDs 3, 17 and 23 and HDs 103, 104, 108, 109 and 118. Relief is no longer sought on the performance objections to SDs 1 and 18, HDs 15, 39 and 94, or the Cuban/non-Cuban Hispanic theory after the stated absence of an evidenced lawful improvement. Expected Hispanic-preferred performance in CDs 18, 21 and 25 was accepted rather than newly contested. Those scope limitations are distinct from the Senate partisan evidentiary concession.

**Controlling explanation:** Vieth supplies a personal political-opportunity claim under equal protection, applying to properly presented open cases. Its actual four-element rule governs Congress and the House. Baker permits adjudication through identifiable legal standards; Bandemer preserves justiciability. Gingles addresses racial statutory opportunity and supplies neither a fixed-party cohesion requirement nor a universal compactness or opposing-bloc prerequisite for this constitutional claim.

The objection that every disappointed voter could demand a more favorable constituency fails under Vieth's actual injury, independently lawful comparison, prohibited-purpose and causal requirements. Cano requires injury independent of political design. A substantial practical obstruction is actionable when imposed; an affected voter need not await a lost general election. Partisan predominance, like ordinary party advantage, cannot establish the complete violation by itself. The purpose element concerns actual suppression of identified political opportunity, rather than knowledge that a map benefits a party.

Personal standing separately requires the claimant's own injury, traceability and likely redress. Vieth's flexible methods of proof preserve the substantive comparison requirement. A different configuration must be shown workable and lawful when the claim depends on it. Its State defense concerns the actual same lawful decision, supported by evidence, rather than a conceivable explanation. These requirements answer the objection that a feasible alternative alone creates a constitutional entitlement to its electoral distribution.

**Precedent treatment:** Vieth governs the complete constitutional claim. Baker and Bandemer retain their justiciability roles; Cano supplies independent injury; Gingles remains the racial statutory framework rather than a source of additional partisan elements.

##### VI. The congressional and House partisan claims require application of the correct rule

**Holding and operative rule:** The congressional and House partisan equal-protection judgments are vacated because their legal-test-dependent reasoning does not resolve the preserved claims under Vieth. The supported racial findings and the distinct affirmed judgments remain intact.

**Authority:** The five-Justice coalition identified for Parts V–VI controls both remand components.

**Controlling explanation:** The congressional claim includes the projected 18-to-7 delegation, registration and statewide voting indices, local testimony concerning approximately 69,909 residents, predominantly Black Democrats, reassigned from CD 10 to CD 11, and the Campbell alternative S33C0009. The lower court partly discounted the local evidence because it did not prove statewide injury and evaluated the alternative through imported Gingles gates. Vieth instead requires the affected persons, protected activity, actual impairment, lawful comparison and boundary-specific cause to be identified. The stipulated pursuit of Republican advantage does not establish its more specific prohibited purpose.

The lower court's criticisms of registration and statewide indices remain pertinent: registration is not actual voting, and indices may involve different electorates and omit congressional races. Its separate totality discussion also contains adverse evidence concerning slating, access and responsiveness. But complete exclusion, unresponsiveness and inevitable defeat do not exhaust Vieth's protection. The ability to recruit voters, change party identity or reverse earlier political fortunes cannot by itself negate a present boundary-caused obstruction. That alternative reasoning therefore does not resolve the complete opportunity claim under the governing rule.

The House claim was separately pleaded and rejected under the same legal test. Its disposition supplies no independent House-specific ground resolving Vieth's elements. The actual House record must be evaluated; congressional testimony cannot substitute for it, and a brief treatment in the judgment is not a House evidentiary concession. The difference from the Senate claim is material. Applying the correct test to these tried claims may lead to judgment on existing evidence; ordinary procedure governs any amendment or supplementation.

**Precedent treatment:** Vieth displaces the imported statutory gates and the requirement of complete political exclusion; its actual proof and standing requirements govern the remaining factual application.

##### VII. The Senate partisan claim fails on its separate proof concession

**Holding and operative rule:** The Senate partisan judgment is affirmed because the plaintiffs expressly conceded that they offered no evidence to prove political gerrymandering of that plan. A change in the governing legal standard does not supply the missing proof of this tried claim.

**Authority:** The five-Justice majority on Part VII of Souter's opinion supplies the controlling proof-failure rationale; O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas join the judgment on this component, and Stone-Zsela dissents.

**Controlling explanation:** Footnote 58 records the concession in the plaintiffs' second amended proposed findings and conclusions. Vieth requires evidence of an actual personal political opportunity and the other elements. Its change in the legal test therefore leaves this independently sufficient evidentiary failure intact, even assuming a cognizable claim. The concession concerns the Senate partisan claim alone; the racial Senate districts and the common intentional-process claims receive their separate dispositions.

**Precedent treatment:** Vieth's ordinary burden of proving the constitutional elements applies; a new legal formulation does not erase an express failure to offer evidence.

##### VIII. Congressional election power supplies no automatic party allocation

**Holding and operative rule:** A projected congressional-seat supermajority without a corresponding statewide vote majority does not by itself violate Article I, sections 2 or 4. Characterizing congressional districting as delegated rather than reserved state power also does not establish a lower equal-protection injury threshold that would make the asserted disproportionality sufficient.

**Authority:** The eight-Justice majority on Part VIII of Souter's opinion adopts these grounds; Stone-Zsela joins the judgment on these components.

**Controlling explanation:** Vieth's Article I holding directly rejects an entitlement to proportional statewide party representation. Separate district elections do not become a statewide proportional contest because projected party outcomes are described as predetermined. Article I's delegation of election-related power supplies no substitute for the independently defined deprivation that the separate constitutional claim requires. Thornton's treatment of the source and limits of congressional election power does not establish that missing injury. The officials therefore retain judgment on these proposed automatic theories while the distinct congressional opportunity claim proceeds under its own rule.

**Precedent treatment:** Vieth's Article I holding applies to reject the statewide party-seat entitlement. Thornton does not turn the delegated-power characterization into the proposed automatic injury rule.

Washington v. Davis and Arlington Heights retain the ordinary motivating-factor rule; Crawford-El preserves ordinary proof. Powell, Sandoval and South Camden retain the distinct § 601 intent action, bounded direct prospective action under valid applicable § 602 effects regulations, and rejection of a regulation-created effects right under § 1983.

Gingles and Voinovich govern statutory causal proof and totality. De Grandy retains the prohibition on maximization, conclusive proportionality defenses and geographic offsets. Valdespino supplies the independent-evidence ground applied here; Brooks requires attention to actual nomination opportunity.

Vieth governs the personal partisan-opportunity claim, its complete four-element test, comparison, burdens, defense and remedy, as well as its separate Article I rule. Baker and Bandemer retain their justiciability work. Cano retains the requirement of an independently cognizable injury. Thornton does not support the proposed automatic injury from the source of congressional districting power. Growe preserves the State's initial responsibility to correct a proved districting violation.

#### Law After Decision

The racial and statutory judgments stand on supported local findings under their own governing rules. Predictive performance findings retain their actual primary-law assumptions and limits; a local injury cannot be offset by gains elsewhere.

Vieth's constitutional rule governs properly presented partisan claims in the Florida congressional and House plans. Imported racial statutory gates and complete political exclusion are not additional constitutional prerequisites. Its personal injury, lawful comparison, purpose, causation and proof requirements control the remaining application. The Senate claim ends on its separate proof concession, and the independent automatic theories fail.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** The Chief Justice joins the Court's congressional and House remand and would also vacate the preserved Senate partisan portion for application of the governing Vieth rule. He would require the three-judge court to evaluate the preserved partisan claims under that rule, permitting amendments and further proof only through ordinary procedure, and to enter judgment for officials on any branch where injury is not established. His broader partisan remand preserves the independently resolved racial findings and statutory judgments.

**O'Connor, joined by Scalia and Thomas, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** These Justices retain the objection stated in Vieth that partisan-dilution claims lack a judicially enforceable entitlement to an alternative distribution of political support. A feasible map and partisan purpose do not establish which distribution the Constitution owes. They would affirm the congressional and House judgments on that threshold ground. Their objection concerns this partisan category; independently defined population, racial, exclusion and retaliation claims retain their own reach. They agree with the Senate judgment and recognize its proof failure conditionally if the Court's cause is applied. They do not join that cause through conditional agreement with its implementation.

**Kennedy, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** Kennedy would affirm the presented congressional and House partisan claims because the announced measure does not adequately distinguish a constitutionally defined burden from an unfavorable composition of the electorate. Holder's comparison discipline and Cano's independent-injury requirement inform his objection. The congressional index and local reassignment evidence do not persuade him that the required independent entitlement has been identified. His House conclusion rests on that constitutional objection, rather than an inferred absence of House evidence. He retains the possibility of a properly defined future participation or association injury and rejects categorical foreclosure. He joins the Senate proof-failure rationale and the other portions specified in the topology.

**Thomas, joined by Scalia, concurring in the statutory judgment.** They adhere to their Holder/De Grandy position, continued in Valdespino, Montiel and Cano, that Section 2 does not authorize these district-dilution claims. That noncoverage rule supplies their ground for affirmance. Their conditional recognition that the cited percentages and plurality extension do not automatically establish liability is not adoption of the Court's dilution framework or its local sufficiency rationale. They join the distinct intentional-racial and Title VI disposition at the scope stated in Part I.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns to the three-judge District Court for application of the complete Vieth rule to the preserved congressional and House partisan claims and their actual record. The court must determine personal standing and address conforming amendment or supplementation under ordinary procedure, including valid preservation and disclosure rulings and any genuinely sufficient independent ground. A failure to establish injury requires judgment for officials on that branch.

Upon a proved violation, the State receives the first opportunity to correct the actual deprivation. Any further remedy must address the responsible boundaries, comply with governing districting requirements and extend no further than necessary, on a prompt workable schedule suited to the election calendar. Existing operative plans remain in effect pending supported relief. The racial, process and Senate partisan affirmances remain intact.
