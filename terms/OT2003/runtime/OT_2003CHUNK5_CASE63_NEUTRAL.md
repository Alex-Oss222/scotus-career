## 63. National Archives and Records Administration v. Favish

# SECTION I: NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

* **Case Name:** National Archives and Records Administration v. Favish
* **Citation or Docket:** No. 02-954; 541 U.S. 157.
* **October Term:** 2003
* **Entry Stage and Event:** MERITS; review of the identified lower-court judgment.
* **Simulated Event Date:** 2004-03-30
* **Question Presented:** Whether FOIA Exemption 7(C) protects surviving relatives’ privacy in death-scene photographs and what showing of agency-transparency value is required when disclosure is sought to investigate alleged official misconduct.
* **Question Granted:** The question presented above; review is confined to the preserved issues and judgment identified here.
* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** Ninth Circuit required release of four photographs after district-court proceedings balancing family privacy against the claimed public interest.
* **Relief Requested:** The federal custodian seeks summary judgment permitting withholding; Favish seeks disclosure of the four remaining images.
* **Material Record:** The photographs depict the death scene of Vincent Foster. Multiple official investigations found suicide. Relatives submitted evidence of their own distress and unwanted publicity from disclosure. Favish challenged the official investigation and sought the remaining images to investigate alleged irregularities.
* **Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** The exemption protects living relatives’ asserted interests, not merely a transferable personal right of the deceased. Law-enforcement compilation is the relevant statutory threshold. The court must evaluate actual evidence and incremental disclosure value, not adjudicate every allegation about the death.
* **Relevant Dates:** The Office of Independent Counsel closed and transferred records to the National Archives on March 23, 2004; the custodian was substituted before the March 30 event. The four-image dispute remains the same.
* **Companion or Related Matters:** Center for National Security Studies v. United States Department of Justice concerns anticipated interference under Exemption 7(A) and a separate access claim. This matter concerns personal privacy under 7(C); neither exemption’s elements substitute for the other’s.
* **Participation Matter:** No case-specific nonparticipation identified.

## B. LAW ENTERING THE CASE

* **Current Through:** June 30, 2003. Any controlling decision effective before the case's event date applies according to its actual holding; a pending matter supplies no rule.
* **Earlier Same-Term Decisions:** No earlier same-term holding is relied on unless identified below.
* **Unresolved Same-Term Dependencies:** No earlier same-term FOIA holding is relied on. Any effective earlier decision must be checked at its actual exemption and holding scope.
* **Institutional or Procedural Law:** The agency bears the ultimate burden to justify withholding under FOIA. Requester neutrality does not eliminate the need to establish a genuine public-interest basis when balancing against demonstrated third-party privacy.

### Issue 1: Family privacy and demonstrated governmental transparency

* **Controlling Law:** United States Department of State v. Ray permits withholding identifiers when concrete privacy interests outweigh demonstrated incremental transparency, but does not require proof of wrongdoing before invoking FOIA or exclude every derivative investigation. Bibles v. Oregon Natural Desert Association limits the public-interest side to agency transparency while retaining the Government’s burden. Department of Defense v. Federal Labor Relations Authority distinguishes FOIA permission to withhold from an independent statutory disclosure prohibition.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** Establish the applicable exemption and concrete privacy interest, identify what the particular material would add to understanding government conduct, and balance those interests. Exemption 7(C)’s law-enforcement and unwarranted-invasion language differs from Exemption 6’s threshold.
* **Other Governing Law:** 5 U.S.C. §552(b)(7)(C), the agency burden under §552(a)(4)(B), and the requirement to release reasonably segregable nonexempt material.
* **Material Other Authority:** Department of Justice v. Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press supplies the received agency-transparency focus. The content sought and its likely contribution, not the requester’s preferred theory alone, matter.
* **Existing Rule's Reach:** Existing law protects concrete privacy and requires an actual transparency contribution. It does not yet conclusively decide relatives’ death-image privacy or impose a universal misconduct prerequisite for FOIA.
* **Open Point:** Whether relatives have the asserted privacy interest and whether Favish’s evidence supplies a public contribution sufficient to outweigh it for these images.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

The custodian and family rely on intrusive dissemination of death images and argue that unsupported suspicion supplies no meaningful counterweight. Favish argues that the photographs may reveal flaws in official investigation and that he cannot prove a concealed defect before obtaining the evidence. The lower court credited sufficient public interest for four images. The legal choice is not between presumed perfect government and unrestricted disclosure: the court must evaluate whether the request has a concrete investigative foundation and whether these particular images could advance it.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

Reverse and permit withholding if the family interest is protected and the record offers only speculative incremental transparency. Affirm or remand if credible evidence connects these images to a significant unresolved agency issue sufficient for the actual balance. No universal requirement to prove misconduct before any FOIA request is necessary; nor does every allegation trigger disclosure.

* **Plausible Holding Scope:** A holding can recognize living relatives’ death-image privacy and require a reasonable evidentiary foundation for this misconduct-based disclosure claim, while preserving other transparency purposes and record-specific segregability.
