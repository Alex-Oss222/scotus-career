## Event

**Leebaert v. Harrington.** Decided February 17, 2004, on merits submission. Review of the Second Circuit's June 13, 2003 judgment, 332 F.3d 134. The questions are whether parental educational liberty requires an exemption from compulsory public-school health instruction and whether combining that claim with a religious-exercise claim requires heightened scrutiny.

The father objected to his son's attendance. The case comes on stipulated facts and summary judgment; he conceded that the attendance requirement satisfied rational-basis review.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated in submission and decision.

## Public Action

The judgment is affirmed.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 9–0. Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The federal curricular and religious-exercise claims remain dismissed, and the requested federal exemption is denied. The independent state-law dismissal remains without prejudice.

## Opinion Topology

Ginsburg delivered the opinion of the unanimous Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer. Both constitutional grounds, their operative limits and the full disposition have nine direct joins.

## Holdings

**Ordinary public-school instruction does not create a parental curricular veto.** A parent's constitutional right to direct a child's upbringing and education does not include a general right to exempt a child enrolled in public school from neutral ordinary instruction. A combined parental-rights and religious-exercise claim must establish the protected interference each claim requires; combining two unsupported claims does not itself require heightened scrutiny.

**Authority:** Ginsburg's opinion, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer, controls both claims and the disposition.

Brown v. Hot, Sexy & Safer Productions supplies the governing distinction between exposure to instruction and interference with protected personal or parental choices. Meyer v. Nebraska and Pierce v. Society of Sisters protect parental teaching and educational choice; they do not give each family control over the public school's lessons. Curtis concerned an optional program, so its result alone could not settle compulsory attendance. Brown addressed that additional feature and rejected the asserted general veto while preserving independently protected interests.

The stipulated record concerns attendance at health instruction. It establishes neither compelled profession of belief nor the targeting or discriminatory treatment that would call for a different religious-exercise inquiry. The conceded reasonable relationship between attendance and education therefore supports this rule. The father's reliance on Wisconsin v. Yoder fails because that decision addressed a distinctive interference with protected religious life and parental direction, rather than ordinary disagreement with a public-school lesson. The adequacy of the claim does not depend on the age, popularity or size of his religion.

The religious claim receives its own inquiry. Smith's treatment of neutral, generally applicable rules and Lukumi's protection against religious targeting are not displaced by labeling a claim parental. Shahar confirms the need to examine religious exercise independently rather than absorb it into another constitutional analysis. The Court's holding governs genuine instruction; enrollment is not a surrender of constitutional protection, and an educational label cannot shield actual coercion or religious discrimination.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Brown v. Hot, Sexy & Safer Productions, Inc.:** Applied for the compulsory-instruction boundary and the continuing protection against independently actionable interference.
- **Meyer v. Nebraska and Pierce v. Society of Sisters:** Preserved as protections of parental instruction and educational choice, which supply no general public-curriculum veto.
- **Elizabeth G. Curtis v. School Committee of Falmouth:** Distinguished at its optional-program scope; its religious-exercise inquiry remains applicable on its own terms.
- **Wisconsin v. Yoder:** Distinguished because the stipulated instructional dispute lacks its distinctive interference with protected religious life and parental direction.
- **Employment Division v. Smith and Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye v. City of Hialeah:** Applied at their respective neutral-rule and religious-targeting boundaries.
- **Shahar v. Bowers:** Applied for independent consideration of a religious-exercise claim.

## Law After Decision

Neutral ordinary instruction may be compulsory despite parental disagreement. Courts must identify the actual interference with parental direction and examine an asserted religious burden independently. Combining constitutional labels does not supply an otherwise missing protected interference. The separate state-law exemptions continue to operate under state law.
