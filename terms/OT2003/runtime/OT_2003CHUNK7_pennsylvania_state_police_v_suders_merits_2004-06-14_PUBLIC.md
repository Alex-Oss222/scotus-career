## Event

Pennsylvania State Police v. Suders, No. 03-95; 542 U.S. 129. Merits decision, June 14, 2004. Argued March 31, 2004. On writ of certiorari to the Third Circuit, which reversed employer summary judgment. Suders alleges sustained sex-based supervisory harassment, inadequate assistance in obtaining a complaint route, an examination-paper episode, a supervisory arrest and resignation during that episode. The questions concern objectively compelled resignation, employer attribution and the effect of preventive procedures and employee nonuse. The allegations and supported inferences are viewed in Suders' favor at this stage.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The judgment reversing employer summary judgment is affirmed. The case is remanded under the actual-authority rule and the distinction between liability and proved avoidable loss.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Judgment:** Affirmed and remanded, 5–4.

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. **Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, who would vacate and remand to permit the bounded complete defense described in their dissent.

The reversal of employer summary judgment stands. Suders must prove the discrimination, compulsion, resignation causation, attribution and injury required for her claims. Supported disputes requiring factfinding must be resolved through ordinary trial procedures, with genuinely preserved independent legal grounds addressed in their proper posture. Any monetary relief requires causal proof, the governing statutory limits and determination of any employer-proved avoidable loss.

## Holdings

##### I. Constructive discharge and employer attribution require distinct proof

**Holding and operative rule:** A Title VII hostile-environment constructive-discharge claimant must prove unlawful discrimination because of sex, conditions so objectively intolerable that a reasonable employee would feel compelled to resign, and a causal connection between those conditions and the resignation. On the actual-authority route, the plaintiff must separately prove by a preponderance that delegated supervisory power materially aided creation or maintenance of the actionable environment; title, rank or workplace proximity alone is insufficient.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join Part I of Stone-Zsela's opinion, including these requirements and the plaintiff-favorable application.

**Controlling explanation:** Harris requires an objectively and subjectively abusive environment that is severe or pervasive, and Oncale requires discrimination because of sex. Duncan establishes that a supported hostile environment does not itself establish the greater objective compulsion to resign. Realistic corrective opportunities matter to that inquiry without imposing exhaustion: an employee need not endure danger, futile procedures or indefinite delay, and announcement of an investigation alone supplies no safe harbor.

Faragher and Ellerth require a real connection between delegated power and the violation. A completed personnel action, economic injury, higher-management notice or an express discharge threat is not invariably necessary. Apparent authority depends on employer manifestations and reasonable reliance; negligence has its separate requirements. Neither route substitutes for proof of actual delegated aid by assertion.

Suders' account describes a sustained supervisory course, difficulty obtaining a practical complaint route, the examination-paper episode and resignation during a supervisory arrest. Viewed favorably to her, that material presents genuine questions about hostility, objective compulsion, practical protection, delegated aid and causation. Resignation alone proves neither intolerability nor attribution; police rank alone likewise does not establish every necessary authority connection. The categorical grounds urged for employer summary judgment cannot foreclose those inquiries.

**Precedent treatment:** Harris and Oncale supply the underlying discrimination standards; Duncan's objective-compulsion and practical-opportunity rules are applied; Faragher and Ellerth's actual-authority rule remains controlling.

##### II. Preventive care and nonuse do not erase proved authority-aided discrimination

**Holding and operative rule:** Once the employee establishes an actionable violation materially aided by delegated supervisory authority, preventive care and employee nonuse do not furnish a complete defense extinguishing that liability. After otherwise compensable injury is established, the employer may obtain an otherwise available reduction under ordinary remedial law only by proving an actual, legally cognizable loss that was causally avoidable; recovery is reduced only to that proved extent, while the statutory wrong and unavoidable injury remain.

**Authority:** Part II has five direct joins: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. This common rule controls without aggregation of separate rationales.

**Controlling explanation:** Faragher rejects reliance on ineffective protection to erase proved authority-aided harassment, and Ellerth requires actual delegated aid even without a completed adverse action. Those decisions left the general complete-defense question open. The Court now resolves it: a plaintiff-proved use of employer-delegated authority remains attributable discrimination despite a preventive policy or unreasonable nonuse. General diligence does not change that historical connection.

The competing defense would encourage employers to prevent misconduct and employees to seek prompt correction. Those considerations already have concrete work to do. Practical opportunities inform whether resignation was objectively compelled under Duncan, and the employer may prove actual avoidability when the court determines recoverable loss. They do not justify eliminating an established wrong or injury the procedure could not have prevented. A policy's existence or nonreporting alone establishes neither liability erasure nor a reduction in relief.

This distinction preserves both burdens: the employee establishes the underlying claim and compensable injury, while the employer establishes the actual loss for which ordinary remedial law permits an avoidability reduction. It also preserves causation. A supported harassment injury remains distinct from loss caused by resignation; failure to prove constructive discharge does not eliminate an otherwise established harassment claim, and discharge loss cannot be recovered by relabeling it.

**Precedent treatment:** Faragher and Ellerth are applied on attribution and their reserved complete-defense question is resolved against categorical erasure; Duncan's practical-opportunity inquiry remains distinct from compensation.

##### III. The supported claims return for ordinary factfinding and lawful relief

**Holding and operative rule:** The judgment reversing employer summary judgment is affirmed, with further proceedings under Parts I and II. The lower courts must resolve supported factual disputes through ordinary trial procedures and apply genuine independent legal grounds in their proper posture; a claim may not be disposed of solely because a policy existed or no formal discharge occurred.

**Authority:** Part III has the same five direct joins as Part II.

**Controlling explanation:** The plaintiff-favorable account supports the required legal inquiries rather than conclusive employer nonliability. The Third Circuit's premise that resignation automatically establishes an official act or employer responsibility is incorrect, but its reversal of the categorical summary judgment stands under the actual-authority and loss-specific rules. Disputed discrimination, compulsion, delegated aid, causation and practical protection must receive their proper evidentiary resolution. Harassment injury, resignation injury, backpay and other authorized relief require their own causal and statutory predicates; ordinary avoidability may reduce a proved loss without deleting the underlying violation.

**Precedent treatment:** Ellerth's separation of attribution from final liability and remedy is applied; Duncan preserves claim-specific causal relief.

## Precedent Treatment

- Harris v. Forklift Systems: applied for objectively and subjectively severe-or-pervasive hostility without a separate psychological-injury prerequisite.
- Oncale v. Sundowner Offshore Services: applied for the distinct requirement that the discrimination occur because of sex.
- Duncan v. General Motors Corp.: applied for objective compulsion and real corrective opportunities without exhaustion, and for separate attribution and claim-specific relief.
- Faragher v. City of Boca Raton: its actual-power attribution and ineffective-protection rules remain; the previously reserved complete-defense issue is now resolved.
- Burlington Industries, Inc. v. Ellerth: its plaintiff-proved material-aid rule and distinction between policy knowledge and effective protection remain. Its complete-defense reservation is replaced by the present five-Justice rule.
- Meritor Savings Bank v. Vinson: its requirement of an agency analysis remains, rather than automatic liability from supervisory rank or a universal management-notice prerequisite.

## Law After Decision

An employee must establish actionable discrimination, objective compulsion, resignation causation and an independent attribution basis. Employer policies and employee nonuse cannot categorically erase an established authority-aided violation. The employer bears the burden of proving an actual legally cognizable avoidable loss under otherwise applicable remedial law, and any reduction is confined to that loss.

## Separate Writings

**Ginsburg, concurring, joined by Stevens, Souter and Breyer.** These Justices join the entire Court opinion and would specify additional terms for the loss inquiry. After the employee proves the claim and otherwise compensable injury, the employer would have to prove by a preponderance that internal remedies were adequately publicized and likely to provide redress without undue risk, effort or expense; that the employee unreasonably failed to use them; and that the particular loss consequently could have been avoided. Reduction or denial would extend only to that proved loss.

A paper policy, nonreporting alone, unsafe or futile channels, correction after injury or the mere possibility of a complaint would not suffice. The employee need not complain to the harasser. Injury before a reasonable protective opportunity and injury the procedure could not prevent would remain compensable if otherwise established. Even proof that all compensable loss was avoidable would eliminate that recovery rather than erase the violation or automatically decide other relief and fees. The rule would contain neither special immunity where no official act occurs nor a categorical exception from ordinary avoidability whenever one occurs. An official coercive act may make effective protection or avoidability impossible to establish on the facts. Their proposal develops the causal recovery approach in the Ellerth concurrence; it has four votes here.

**Kennedy, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas.** These Justices join Part I but would vacate and remand under a newly adopted complete defense. An employer would have to prove by a preponderance both reasonable care to prevent and promptly correct the harassment and the employee's unreasonable failure to use provided preventive or corrective opportunities or otherwise avoid harm. The measures must be practically accessible and reasonably effective; unsafe or futile channels, paper policies and nonreporting alone would not establish the defense, and no particular written-policy form is invariably required. The employee would retain the actual-authority/material-aid burden.

The defense would be unavailable when an official discriminatory employment act precipitated the compelled resignation—for example, a demotion, extreme pay cut or transfer imposing unbearable working conditions through delegated employer authority. Authority to make the employment decision does not mean the employer authorized its discriminatory motive. The employee would establish that official act and its causal connection; the employer would retain persuasion on both defense elements. Resignation itself would not become an official act merely because it was objectively compelled. The alleged arrest matters to authority, coercion and safe complaint opportunities but does not conclusively determine the employment-act exception.

The dissent distinguishes delegated power materially aiding an environment from an official decision directly consummating the discrimination. Agency responsibility, in its view, permits a defense for genuine diligence and unreasonable nonuse in the former setting while withholding it in the latter. That rule deliberately does more than reduce particular avoidable losses; the dissent rejects the Court's view that attributable discrimination forecloses the complete defense. It would allow adjudication of the disputed defense rather than restore summary judgment for the employer.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns through the Third Circuit for proceedings consistent with the affirmed reversal. Supported factual disputes requiring decision must be resolved through ordinary trial procedures. The courts must keep discrimination, objective compulsion, resignation causation, actual-authority attribution and claim-specific injury distinct, apply genuine independent grounds in their proper posture, and determine any employer-proved avoidable-loss limitation and lawful relief under the governing statutory limits.
