## Lomont's material addition to EDC

Lomont's unanimous opinion by Scalia adds two operative rules. An official's concrete, traceable and redressable own-work injury does not require separate authorization to represent the State, and an adverse merits answer does not retroactively eliminate that concrete controversy. Optional applicant certification does not commandeer the officer when refusal is unconditional, without federal penalty, compulsory investigation or substitute work. Constituent pressure alone is insufficient; the actual legal consequences and operation control. The opinion expressly preserves Condon's distinct own-activity rule, Printz's rule against compelled state executive administration, which does not turn on reimbursement, and the need for actual statutory delegation.

For EDC's standing, the relevant asserted injury concerns obligations imposed on the municipal operator's own operations. Lomont supports distinguishing that concrete burden from a claim to represent the State and from the eventual merits determination. It does not itself decide municipal First Amendment personhood; the neutral packet's assumption of the asserted protection remains a distinct possible means of resolving the speech question.

For commandeering, Lomont makes the distinction between a real option and an optional label directly relevant. But its unconditional right to refuse applicant-assistance duties is not a requirement that a municipality may refuse valid direct regulation of its own discharges. EDC's alternative may impose lawful discharge controls and compliance expense. The actual inquiry is whether a legally available, operative route regulates the municipal system's own discharges without requiring the municipality to administer a third-party program. If both alternatives actually compel that third-party administration, labeling one an individual permit does not supply a genuine choice. Conversely, an alternative does not become illusory merely because valid own-discharge compliance is costly or inconvenient. Those propositions require application of Condon and New York to the actual permitting arrangement, not transposition of Lomont's different factual result.

The exact operative rule remains important. Section 122.33(b)(2)(ii) permits a different individual program under the section 122.26(d) application requirements and does not require the specified legal-authority information unless the applicant wants the permit writer to consider it. Section 122.34(a)'s minimum-control requirement expressly excepts an application under section 122.26(d). Those application provisions do not predetermine every final permit condition. The minimum measures' state-law qualifications, the distinction between commands and labeled Guidance, the construction-site waiver, and the non-stormwater qualifications remain in the script-copied official text. Neither permission to omit information nor a recommendation may be rewritten as an obligation. The separate general-permit administrative remands are not erased by the constitutional questions.

Lomont supplies no payment entitlement for municipal operations and no permission for an agency to enlarge its statutory authority merely by offering an alternative. Printz itself creates no compensation rule. The direct-regulation question therefore must not be recast as a compulsory-assistance reimbursement dispute.

## Prospective screen for West Virginia v. HHS

Lomont is relevant to the distinction between political pressure and a legal command, but its unconditional-refusal rule does not replace Kansas's distinct genuine-practical-choice rule for conditional funding. The challenged Medicaid statute expressly permits an all-or-part funding consequence after its prescribed proceedings, whereas Lomont upheld an arrangement with no adverse federal consequence for refusal. The absence of a completed withholding decision in West Virginia is a record limit, not proof that the statutory funding pressure is nonexistent or automatically noncoercive.
