The six Associates joining the class ruling hold that the commercial-development ground is insufficient under Home Builders, and all eight hold that Home Builders's necessary-inclusion rule sustains the application:

- Necessary inclusion requires that inclusion of the regulated class be a reasonably adapted and integral means of executing an independently valid interstate regulation; the Act's integrated regulation of interstate exploitation, movement and commercial disposition of listed wildlife may include the unauthorized taking of listed wildlife, without species-by-species, border-crossing or independent-effects proof. A declared national objective, comprehensiveness alone, general importance and potential marketability do not themselves establish the relationship.
- Independent externality requires an identified concrete ecological, commercial or comparable interstate effect, substantial rather than speculative or merely nationally interesting, which individual States cannot reasonably confine or internalize through ordinary territorial authority; the prohibition must reasonably fit that demonstrated spillover. It remains an independent alternative that need not be satisfied here.

The Government must identify the valid interstate regulation, the covered class and a genuine functional relationship, and must materially support the externality route if it invokes that route. Courts independently determine constitutional eligibility and fit while respecting reasonable congressional judgments about regulatory necessity and practical enforcement. Aggregation cannot create that connection. No universal formal findings, defendant-specific congressional findings, fixed numerical threshold, clear-and-convincing burden or least-restrictive-means test is required.

## Individual-position reconciliation and strongest competing paths

O'Connor and Kennedy apply the Home Builders opinion they joined in full. Their concurrence's national/local boundary is retained: the Court independently identifies the valid interstate regulation and the taking prohibition's actual function within it, and for the externality route effects occurring in many States, national uniformity and a desirable policy cannot establish that ordinary State authority cannot contain the actual cross-border effect. Holston demonstrates a valid necessary-inclusion application; its prohibited-image facts do not establish facts about this toad or development.

Ginsburg and Breyer take the same concrete position as their Home Builders partial concurrence: the class premise has independently controlling force, and the taking prohibition's specific function within the Act's integrated regulation of interstate trade in listed wildlife sustains the application. They preserve disagreement with the class ruling without turning that disagreement into a second judgment. Their support for congressional latitude does not permit judicial creation of absent facts, and the affirmance rests on no such fact. Their recorded affirmance joins therefore remain full and compatible.

Scalia and Thomas require the most explicit reconciliation with prior public positions. In Home Builders they accepted the enacted class, dissented from the necessary-inclusion holding, opposed the new independent-externality doctrine and would have reversed with confined relief. That original doctrinal objection is not erased by Holston, which decided only a demonstrated supply mechanism. The current difference is legal and procedural: Home Builders now supplies an existing operative rule sustaining the same taking prohibition as applied to a listed single-State species, and this later application presents no outside-class, invalid-regime or pretext showing that would distinguish it. Each chooses to apply that settled rule in this later case rather than reopen its original constitutional derivation. Their direct joins authorize the necessary-inclusion holding at its stated scope, without an enlarged ecological power or a new externality extension. Their original disagreement does not become a current refusal of the rule they affirmatively apply. This is a qualitative application of their recorded present commitments, not a mechanical requirement that prior dissenters always follow or always repeat a dissent.

The contrary confined-invalidation path would require a materially different premise: an outside-class, invalid-regime or pretext showing, or an affirmative change in the governing doctrine. Neither premise is established by the supplied record or a majority proposal. The remand path would require a factual question on which the necessary-inclusion relationship depends; none is identified, because that relationship rests on the Act's text and structure rather than on facts about this species. A further hearing would therefore not bear on the constitutional question.

Stevens and Souter retain their specific economic-application view. Their argument identifies habitat-changing construction itself as the conduct reached, informed by Sweet Home, Wickard, Perez and Hodel; it does not rely merely on the developer's unrelated purchases or a generalized environmental policy. The strongest objection is that Home Builders directly rejected the same sufficient-ground theory. Their present response openly asks that the contrary class ruling be reconsidered at this economic-application scope. They do not pretend that the old dissent is law, manufacture a factual distinction or claim Holston overruled Home Builders. The substantive concurrence should state why actual commercial habitat conversion should qualify as an additional ground for the same judgment, while acknowledging the controlling rule it would change; they join the necessary-inclusion holding, as they did in Home Builders. No new ecological or market finding is attributed to them.

These differing choices about applying or renewing disagreement with precedent rest on the actual individual commitments and earlier writings. They are not a reason to create a new full-court Commerce category or force all four earlier dissenters into one position.

## Holston and statutory boundaries

Holston's actual-product reasoning concerns production of the reproducible supply targeted by the identified interstate image prohibition. Rancho's habitat injury does not create that same prohibited stock. The method transfers; the factual relationship does not. The relationship here comes from the Act's own integrated wildlife regime, as Home Builders held.

Sweet Home continues to require actual death or injury proximately caused, with ordinary foreseeability, by significant habitat modification impairing essential behavior; speculative harm or bare but-for linkage is insufficient. An omission requires an independent legal duty. The Commerce affirmance establishes no taking, no future occurrence of injury and no permission for the development. Otherwise lawful land use is no exemption. Any incidental-take permission remains governed by its own statutory findings and conditions; this case adjudicates no entitlement to it. These are preserved legal boundaries, not newly decided statutory applications or a new habitat-maintenance duty.
