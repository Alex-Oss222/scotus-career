## Event

Iowa v. Tovar, No. 02-1541; merits. Argued January 21, 2004; decided March 8, 2004. On certiorari to the Iowa Supreme Court. Tovar’s 1996 uncounseled guilty plea to operating while intoxicated resulted in two days in jail. In a later third-offense prosecution he challenged use of that conviction. The Iowa Supreme Court excluded it and directed a lesser judgment because the plea court had not given two additional warnings about counsel’s advice.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated.

## Public Action

The Iowa Supreme Court judgment is reversed and the enhancement proceeding remanded.

## Judgment & Remedy

Reversed and remanded, 9–0. Supporting: Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. The direction to disregard the first conviction and enter the lesser judgment on the two-warning theory is set aside. The enhancement proceeding continues under the correct waiver rule and otherwise applicable law.

## Opinion Topology

Justice O'Connor delivered the unanimous Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer.

## Holdings

**Controlling proposition:** An uncounseled guilty plea resulting in imprisonment requires a knowing, intelligent and voluntary relinquishment of counsel, including understanding the nature of the charge, the availability and assistance of counsel at the plea, and the applicable punishment. The Sixth Amendment does not invariably require additional warnings that a lawyer may discover an overlooked defense or give an independent opinion about whether to plead guilty; their omission alone does not establish invalid waiver.

**Authority:** The unanimous Opinion of the Court supplies nine direct joins to the rule, disposition of the presented collateral attack and remand.

**Controlling explanation:** Johnson v. Zerbst requires intentional relinquishment of a known right. Godinez distinguishes competence from actual informed waiver, and Patterson makes the information required sensitive to counsel's role at the stage in question. Faretta's warning about conducting one's own contested trial addresses different tasks; it does not prescribe these two additions for every plea.

The contrary argument rightly emphasizes counsel's help in evaluating defenses and the plea decision. That supports a real-understanding inquiry, but it does not show that every accused lacks the necessary understanding without these precise warnings. Advice only about counsel at a possible trial is not automatically equivalent to understanding counsel's availability before and at the plea. Particular wording is not controlling, and States may require fuller colloquies.

Tovar's first conviction imposed two days in jail, so a valid waiver matters. His collateral theory, however, asserted the two omissions and a possible mistaken belief, rather than actual unawareness of counsel's availability before and at arraignment. Under the applicable collateral framework he must identify the actual asserted waiver defect and support factual assertions under Iowa's preponderance rule. The omitted nonmandatory warnings do not establish the suggested possibility. The warning-based attack fails, and the resulting direction to exclude the first conviction and enter the lesser judgment must be reversed.

## Precedent Treatment

Godinez v. Moran is applied to the distinction between competence and actual informed waiver. Johnson v. Zerbst supplies intentional relinquishment of a known right. Patterson v. Illinois supplies the stage-sensitive measure of necessary information. Faretta v. California retains its trial-representation protections; its warnings are not mechanically extended to every guilty plea. Parke v. Raley retains its bounded collateral burden sequence for the circumstances it adjudicated and supplies no automatic validation of an uncounseled plea. Watts v. State supplies Iowa’s preponderance rule for factual assertions in collateral proceedings; its counseled-plea facts do not establish Tovar’s knowledge.

## Law After Decision

Courts assess actual understanding of counsel’s availability and role at the plea, the charge and punishment. The two prescribed warnings are not universal federal constitutional prerequisites. A State may impose fuller colloquies, and a collateral challenge must identify and support the actual defect under the applicable burden rules.
