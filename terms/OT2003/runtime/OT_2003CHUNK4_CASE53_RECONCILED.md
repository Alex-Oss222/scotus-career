## Seven-Associate operative rule and supported application

A state prisoner must fairly alert the appropriate state court to the federal nature of each claim through the petition, brief or similar papers ordinarily presenting it. Ordinarily that requirement is not met when the court must read beyond submissions that give no such notice to find the federal claim in another lower-court opinion. The rule does not require particular words: a federal constitutional source, a decision identifying the federal right, or a description that actually identifies the federal claim can suffice. Mere physical availability elsewhere is not proper presentation, and federal citations attached to one claim do not necessarily federalize another.

Picard supplies the fair opportunity to decide the federal claim itself; Boerckel requires that opportunity through the ordinary available review round; Castille preserves proper procedural presentation. Reese named trial and appellate assistance, but the express federal constitutional references concerned trial rights and jury waiver. The lower postconviction opinion's Jones v. Barnes citation was not shown to be incorporated in the highest-court submission. The intervening appellate disposition was a summary affirmance; a rule requiring attachment of that disposition does not establish attachment or incorporation of the different postconviction opinion. The Ninth Circuit's presumed-search approach therefore cannot sustain presentation.

The allegation's use of the word ineffective does not establish that it exclusively invokes federal law. The record uses ineffective and inadequate in referring to the assistance allegations; no exclusive federal term-of-art showing is established. This is distinct from the broader state/federal-equivalence theory, which the seven decline to decide. The holding does not require a citation beside every sentence, forbid genuine incorporation, establish that state-law citations can never identify a federal claim, or decide a case with demonstrated actual notice through proper submissions.

It gives an identified state-law basis for reading the appellate-assistance allegation as the constitutional claim in the context of this short, federally framed petition.

His notice conclusion neither decides the assistance merits nor treats Guinn's more-probable-than-not language as a different federal prejudice formulation. The earlier case's express dual pleading and particular omitted-shackling context remain qualifications; they do not persuade him that Oregon's highest court lacked a fair opportunity to recognize this submitted claim.
