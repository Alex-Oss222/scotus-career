Houghton's controlling rule permits an object-based search of a detached, capable passenger container within a vehicle supported by drug probable cause. It does not presume the passenger guilty or engaged in a common criminal enterprise, authorize a bodily search, or decide an attached effect. Vehicle-search authority cannot itself justify Pringle's arrest. Conversely, Houghton does not prohibit a common-possession inference when actual facts support it; the inquiry remains whether this record supplies the necessary personal connection.

The separate opinion is materially more specific than the Wardlow and Valentine comparisons in the original freeze. Stevens, joined by Souter and Ginsburg among the Associates, required particularized facts connecting the passenger or identified effect to the object sought, or an independently sufficient search ground. Concrete indicators of transfer, shared ownership or common concealment could qualify; companionship could not establish them by presumption. Their proposed passenger-effect exception did not control, and its container rule cannot be treated as binding arrest law. Their own explanation nevertheless bears directly on how they assess an inference of shared criminal participation, particularly for the more personal intrusion of arrest.

## Reassessment of the facts and rival paths

The strongest case for retaining all original commitments is substantial: Pringle concerns five packaged cocaine baggies in a shared passenger compartment, rolled cash in the front glove compartment, early-morning shared travel and no reliable information assigning exclusive possession to another occupant. That differs from Houghton's conceded vehicle search based on the driver's syringe and drug-use admission, followed by intrusion into an identified passenger's private purse. There is an actual drug cache here, not merely suspicion that drugs may be somewhere in the car. Probable cause does not require direct observation of knowledge or trial proof. The five Associates identified below continue to accept that cumulative inference.

For Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg, however, the supplement makes the contrary path stronger. The discovered packages establish illegal possession within the car; they do not themselves identify Pringle's knowing participation. The cash adds possible drug-trade context but was in the driver-owner's glove compartment, and the cocaine was concealed behind the rear armrest. Accessibility establishes opportunity, not a concrete act of sharing or concealment by this front passenger. There was no reported prearrest transfer, observed joint handling, incriminating conduct or other fact linking Pringle personally to a shared possession arrangement. These are examples of potentially meaningful links, not a newly mandatory evidentiary checklist. No ownership admission displacing a supposed joint inference cannot furnish that inference's missing affirmative foundation. Silence remains nonguilt evidence, and the later confession and account of friendship remain unavailable to justify the arrest.

The important difference is not that knowledge must be certain or innocent explanations eliminated. It is that, for these three, the original P2 description of shared transport inferred knowing criminal cooperation from the very companionship and common access that their Houghton explanation refused to treat as concrete evidence of cooperation. The absence of a known passenger-owned container here removes their proposed search exception; it does not convert an unclaimed common compartment into evidence that each occupant knowingly possesses its contents. This is a Justice-specific application judgment, not a claim that Houghton's binding container holding mechanically dictates Pringle.
