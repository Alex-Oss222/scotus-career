## 70. Vieth v. Jubelirer

# SECTION II: STONE POSITION SUPPLEMENT

## A. STATUS

* **Current-Through Baseline:** June 30, 2003, subject to any controlling decision effective before this case's event date.

## B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

* **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** VACATE AND REMAND. Reject categorical nonjusticiability and vacate the judgment dismissing the partisan-gerrymandering claim. Claims under the First and Fourteenth Amendments are justiciable when they allege intentional material deprivation of an identifiable political opportunity for the prohibited purpose of entrenching governing power. If the Court adopts Stone's reasoning, Stone writes the opinion.
* **Stone's Remedy and Remand Position:** Return the case to the three-judge District Court with instructions to permit amendment of the pleadings and determine whether the plaintiffs can establish the injury, purpose and causation defined below, subject to the State's same-decision defense. The District Court must determine personal standing and apply the test to the challenged boundaries. The mandate neither invalidates Pennsylvania's congressional map on the present record nor prescribes replacement districts.
* **Remedial Reach:** The plaintiffs' adequately pleaded claims of intentional political opportunity deprivation. Relief following proof of a violation must correct the established deprivation and extend no further than necessary. The State receives the first opportunity to adopt lawful replacement boundaries. No party receives a right to a proportional seat allocation, a particular district or electoral success.
* **Practical Relief:** Permit conforming amendment and set a prompt schedule for pleading, proof and findings consistent with the election calendar. An adequately pleaded claim proceeds without requiring a subsequent electoral defeat. The existing map remains operative unless and until the court grants properly supported relief; this remand supplies no finding of liability and no immediate redraw.

## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Justiciability and personal partisan injury

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Resolve justiciability and the governing constitutional standard on review of the dismissal. Personal standing, the sufficiency of amended allegations and ultimate liability remain distinct inquiries. No missing finding of deprivation, purpose or causation is assumed.
* **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether intentional political opportunity deprivation presents a justiciable claim under the First and Fourteenth Amendments; what personal political injury, governmental purpose and causal connection establish that claim; and whether partisan predominance or a projected delegation-wide seat majority without a statewide vote majority suffices by itself.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Recognize a constitutional claim when electoral boundaries materially deprive identifiable voters of an existing or demonstrably attainable opportunity for effective political organization, participation, nomination or election, substantially because officials seek to suppress political opposition and preserve governing power against electoral challenge. Apply four elements and a State-proved same-decision defense. This is an affirmative extension of independent-injury and political-association principles; neither Bandemer nor Cano already establishes this complete partisan-entrenchment rule.
* **Reach Calibration:** Broad as to the constitutional rule, limited as to the present judgment. The rule protects effective political opportunity without requiring prior victory, inability to elect in every destination district, a prescribed historical range or a separate durability threshold. It does not constitutionalize ordinary partisan disappointment, district shape, every departure from traditional districting principles or projected seat distributions. Political affiliation, past electoral performance, incumbency, political balance and other legitimate districting objectives remain permissible considerations.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** The First Amendment protects political expression and association; the Fourteenth Amendment protects equal participation in the political community. A State may consider politics in drawing districts, but may not deliberately use electoral boundaries to disable identifiable political opposition's effective opportunity and insulate governing officials from ordinary electoral accountability. The constitutional wrong is intentional material deprivation of an effective political opportunity through a concrete electoral operation. Partisan design alone is insufficient, and material deprivation does not require complete destruction of the opportunity. A plaintiff must establish four elements:

  **1. Identifiable political opportunity.** Identify an existing or demonstrably attainable opportunity for effective political organization, participation, nomination or election. An opportunity is a practical capacity to undertake the identified political activity, supported by evidence of the voters, association or coalition and the activity at issue. Prior electoral victory is not indispensable; an emerging opportunity may qualify. A party label, desire for additional seats, expected support for an incumbent or preferred statewide partisan distribution does not by itself identify the protected opportunity.

  **2. Material deprivation.** Show that the challenged boundaries materially impair that opportunity through a demonstrated electoral operation. Packing concentrates voters in a district in a way that impairs their effective opportunity elsewhere; cracking divides them among districts in a way that impairs their effective opportunity to act together. Displacement, division of an effective coalition or another proved districting mechanism may also cause the deprivation. These descriptions do not establish liability without proof of the resulting impairment. The impairment must be a substantial practical obstruction to the identified activity, rather than ordinary disappointment, inconvenience or a less favorable electoral prospect alone. District shape, partisan composition, predicted seat totals and departures from traditional criteria are evidence; none alone constitutes the injury. The plaintiff need not show complete destruction of the identified opportunity, that every possible political activity has been eliminated or that the affected voters could never elect their preferred candidate.

  **3. Prohibited entrenchment purpose.** Establish that the deprivation was substantially motivated by an objective to suppress identifiable political opposition's effective opportunity because of the affected voters' political association or participation, and thereby preserve governing power against electoral challenge. The objective must actually contribute to the decision, but need not be exclusive or predominant. Ordinary pursuit of partisan advantage is not by itself the prohibited purpose, and knowledge of partisan effects alone does not establish an objective to impose the protected opportunity's deprivation. Purpose is evaluated from the decision's context and evidence of what the officials sought to accomplish.

  **4. Causation.** Establish that the challenged boundaries materially caused the deprivation, rather than merely reflecting voter geography, ordinary electoral competition or independently lawful districting requirements. Identify the assignments or electoral operation responsible for the impairment. The boundaries need not be the only contributing cause, but a correlation between party preference and an unfavorable electoral result is insufficient.

  **Proof and same-decision defense.** Plaintiffs must prove each element by a preponderance of the evidence, meaning that it is more likely true than not. Once they establish the protected opportunity's deprivation, prohibited purpose and causal connection, the State bears the burden of proving by a preponderance that materially the same lawful districting decision would have occurred without the prohibited entrenchment objective. A genuine policy of partisan fairness or compliance with legitimate districting criteria may establish that defense if it independently accounts for the challenged result. An independently established and consistently applied fairness policy, or genuine population, geographic or statutory requirements, must actually account for materially the same lawful decision. Merely naming an additional political objective, offering a conceivable justification or stating that politics always enters districting does not defeat the claim. Once the four elements are proved and that defense fails, no additional balancing of partisan advantages excuses the violation.

  **When injury occurs.** The injury is complete when the plan imposes the proved deprivation. A plaintiff need not wait for a preferred candidate to lose a later election. Organization, participation and nomination are protected opportunities in their own right; a claim concerning one of those activities does not require proof of a lost general election.
* **Reach Within the Question:** The stated rule governs personal claims of intentional political opportunity deprivation in electoral districting. Subordination of neutral criteria to political aims, unusual boundaries and a projected seat supermajority without a statewide vote majority do not alone establish a violation of the First Amendment, the Fourteenth Amendment or Article I. There is no entitlement to proportional party representation, competitive elections in every district, a guaranteed nomination or electoral success. Independent constitutional racial-opportunity claims retain their own elements and defenses.
* **Next-Case Guidance:** Standing requires personal exposure to the challenged electoral operation, a causal connection to the claimant's injury and relief capable of redressing it. Statewide vote-seat disparities alone establish neither personal injury nor a violation. Under the ordinary notice-pleading rules governing this case, the complaint must identify the protected opportunity and the districting operation alleged to impair it, with allegations supporting the claim's purpose and causation requirements; merits proof is not required at the pleading stage. The amendment ordered here allows plaintiffs to present their claims under the announced standard.

  At the merits stage, comparative maps, voting patterns, organizational activity, district boundaries, legislative evidence and electoral history may illuminate opportunity, impairment, purpose and causation. No single kind of evidence is mandatory or dispositive. A comparison must illuminate a real political opportunity and the challenged operation; a map producing the plaintiff's preferred partisan outcome does not establish injury simply because it can be drawn. Historical success may support an opportunity, but is not a prerequisite. No alternative map or prescribed number of comparison maps is mandatory. Examine the operative congressional plan and any legally permissible comparison without treating a prior malapportioned plan as a presumptively lawful baseline; Act 1's use under a stay does not cure its adjudicated population defect.

  If a violation is proved, identify the deprivation and the boundaries materially responsible for it. Give the State the first opportunity to adopt a lawful correction under a workable election timetable. Judicial relief, if required, must remove the established deprivation and extend no further than necessary, while respecting other governing districting requirements. It may not impose proportional partisan representation or guarantee a candidate, party or coalition a seat. A separate racial-opportunity claim must be assessed independently even when the same boundaries also support a partisan claim.
* **Transition:** Apply the announced rule to this live dispute and other open cases in which the claim is properly presented. Permit amendment here under the remand mandate, while retaining ordinary jurisdictional and procedural requirements. Announcing the rule does not reopen final judgments or invalidate completed elections.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Lawful consideration of political interests does not confer an unrestricted power to disable political opposition. Equal participation and freedom of political association require a distinction between an unfavorable political arrangement and government-imposed deprivation of an identifiable effective opportunity. Requiring opportunity, material impairment, prohibited purpose and material causation supplies that distinction. The same-decision defense protects independently lawful choices without allowing an additional political explanation to conceal an established constitutional injury.

  Pennsylvania's asserted pursuit of Republican electoral advantage and the alleged statewide disparity between votes and seats do not, without more, invalidate its congressional plan. Susan Furey alleges that the redistricting reassigned her residence from former Congressional District 13 to reconfigured District 6, affecting her political association and representation. That allegation identifies a concrete setting in which the existence of an effective opportunity, its impairment, the decisionmakers' purpose and map causation can be examined. It is not itself proof of any element or a finding that the assignment is unconstitutional. The District Court dismissed the partisan claim without applying this standard; vacatur and permission to amend allow the required inquiry without declaring an unproved deprivation or ordering a replacement map.
* **Precedent Treatment:** Baker v. Carr establishes that constitutional districting claims are not categorically barred by the political-question doctrine. Davis v. Bandemer's justiciability holding is retained; this decision supplies the more precise substantive standard. Gaffney v. Cummings preserves room for legitimate political accommodation. Reynolds v. Sims supports meaningful, equal representation without guaranteeing proportional party seats, and White v. Regester directs attention to genuine access to political processes beyond formal eligibility.

  Page v. Bartels requires an actual protected political interest and a governmental operation materially impairing it; design and political disappointment are insufficient. Robertson v. Bartels prevents expected incumbent support or electoral preferences from becoming automatic constitutional injuries. Montiel v. Davis supplies the substantial-motivating-purpose principle and confirms that a partisan explanation does not automatically excuse an independent racial deprivation. Cano v. Davis distinguishes independent injury from design and recognizes that interference with organization, nomination, candidacy or participation need not await electoral defeat. Cano expressly left a standalone First Amendment claim outside its appeal. The present partisan-entrenchment rule extends those principles; it is not a holding already compelled by Cano.

  Brooks v. Barnes illustrates why nomination opportunity matters independently of ultimate general-election victory. Its Section 2 holding supplies an analogy, not a partisan-entrenchment test or an automatic incorporation of statutory racial-dilution elements. O'Hare Truck Service v. City of Northlake and Board of County Commissioners v. Umbehr show that a governmental penalty on political association can violate the First Amendment even without an entitlement to the governmental benefit at issue. That principle supports the present extension without making their distinct factual settings or doctrinal requirements identical to districting.

  Village of Arlington Heights v. Metropolitan Housing Development Corp. supports evaluating governmental purpose through contextual evidence. Mt. Healthy City School District Board of Education v. Doyle supplies the motivating-factor and State-proved same-decision framework. These authorities support the announced elements and burdens without turning every political consideration into a constitutional violation.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The present record does not establish liability or the boundaries needed to cure a violation. Those questions remain for proceedings on remand. Preserve Cano's separate intentional racial-opportunity claim, with its own deprivation, purpose, causation and same-decision requirements: a State cannot defeat that claim merely by relabeling its racial objective as partisan strategy. The partisan rule neither displaces that independent protection nor makes racial and partisan claims interchangeable. Sections 2 and 5 of the Voting Rights Act retain their own statutory elements and remedies. Structural Entrenchment remains a separate proposed claim concerning control of an elected governing body despite an opposing voting majority, without a prohibited-purpose requirement. This opinion does not adopt that claim, and Pennsylvania's congressional delegation is not such a governing body. The entrenchment purpose within the four-element claim does not dispense with any element or decide that separate structural question.
