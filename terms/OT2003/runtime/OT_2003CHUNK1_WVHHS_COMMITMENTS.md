## Disposition commitment and sufficient rule

Affirm the judgment rejecting facial invalidation. The recovery obligation concerns expenditures made under the funded medical-assistance program. The challenged enforcement mechanism conditions participating-plan payments on compliance; the case does not present an independently enforced administration command against a State outside the funded program. This classification establishes no contract-exit term or new rule about obligations concerning previously paid benefits. Neither program relationship nor notice nor prior acceptance independently disposes of coercion. The condition and actual funding consequences must still leave genuine practical choice under Kansas.

The supplied record establishes substantial dependence, receipts exceeding one billion dollars annually, political resistance to estate recovery, recovery receipts much smaller than the grant, an HHS warning of loss of all or part, and subsequent compliance. Those facts must be accepted as supplied. They establish powerful pressure but do not establish that a particular total cutoff has occurred or that every authorized sanction eliminates genuine choice. Section 1396c permits a narrower consequence confined through payments to unaffected categories or parts; the record establishes no decision rejecting that route and imposing an inescapable program-wide loss. The facial challenge therefore does not justify invalidating the condition and enforcement provision in all their operation.

This is a merits disposition of the actual facial challenge. Compliance under the ongoing condition does not extinguish the controversy. Conversely, the absence of an imposed sanction is not a categorical rule requiring a State to endure a cutoff before it can present a supported coercion claim. A concrete, credible demand shown in its practical operation to leave no genuine choice remains reviewable, including through competent qualitative evidence. The present record supplies the all-or-part warning rather than a selected total cutoff or binding construction eliminating the authorized lesser route.

The judgment commitment denies facial invalidation and leaves the recovery condition effective. A particular-sanction challenge would apply Kansas to the actual threatened or imposed consequence; an actual total-cutoff application is not established in the present record. This is not a categorical rule that only a completed sanction may be challenged. The Court should not prescribe an administrative-funds-only ceiling, an invented penalty, or a new administrative proceeding.

## Statutory propositions necessary to the application

The exact statute is already script-copied in the frozen source addendum. The following is analytical description, not a substitute statutory transcription or a finding about any beneficiary:

- Section 1396p(b)(1) generally prohibits adjustment or recovery of correctly paid assistance except the identified categories. Under paragraph (1)(A), the State shall seek recovery from the estate or upon sale of property subject to the institutional lien for an individual described in subsection (a)(1)(B). That predicate concerns an inpatient in a nursing facility, an intermediate care facility for the mentally retarded, or another medical institution, who must spend all income except a minimal personal-needs amount on medical care as a condition of receiving institutional services under the plan. The State must determine, after notice and an opportunity for hearing under state procedures, that the individual cannot reasonably be expected to be discharged and return home. These are statutory predicates, not findings that any particular beneficiary satisfies them.
- For assistance received at age 55 or older, subsection (b)(1)(B) makes estate recovery mandatory for nursing-facility and home-and-community-based services and related hospital and prescription-drug services. Recovery for other plan items or services is the State's option; it is not a universal mandatory recovery command.
- Subsection (b)(1)(C) separately addresses recipients or persons entitled to long-term-care insurance benefits connected to disregarded assets or resources and requires estate recovery for nursing-facility and other long-term-care assistance. That requirement does not apply when assistance was received under a state plan with an amendment approved as of May 14, 1993 providing disregard of assets or resources to the extent of insurance payments or because the individual received or was entitled to those insurance benefits. This exception is not the general hardship exception.
- Under subsection (b)(2), recovery may occur only after any surviving spouse's death and when no surviving child under 21 remains. A surviving child also prevents recovery if blind or permanently and totally disabled in a State eligible to participate in the State program established under subchapter XVI, or blind or disabled as defined in §1382c in a State not eligible for that program. For recovery under an institutional home lien, recovery must additionally wait while either of the following qualifying occupants lawfully resides in the home and has continuously resided there lawfully since admission: a sibling who resided there for at least the year immediately before admission; or a son or daughter who resided there for at least the two years immediately before admission and establishes to the State's satisfaction that the care provided allowed the individual to remain home rather than enter an institution. The recovery-stage sibling protection does not require an equity interest; the separate pre-lien sibling protection below does. These protections restrict recovery, not the occupants' right to remain in the home, and do not exempt every adult heir.
- Subsection (b)(3) requires the state agency to establish procedures in accordance with the Secretary's standards, under which it shall waive application of the subsection, except paragraph (1)(C), when application would cause undue hardship determined under the Secretary's criteria. It is a mandatory beneficiary-level safeguard, not a discretionary excuse for the State to omit its entire recovery program.
- Subsection (b)(4)(A) includes all property and other assets within the state-probate estate. Subsection (b)(4)(B) permits inclusion of other real and personal property and assets in which the individual had a legal title or interest at death, to the extent of that interest, including conveyance to a survivor, heir or assign through joint tenancy, tenancy in common, survivorship, life estate, living trust or another arrangement. That broader inclusion becomes mandatory for an individual to whom paragraph (1)(C)(i) applies. Mandatory and optional inclusions remain distinct.
- Subsection (a)(1) generally forbids a predeath lien on account of assistance paid or to be paid under the plan. Its distinct exceptions permit a lien pursuant to a court judgment concerning incorrectly paid benefits, or a lien on the institutionalized individual's real property under the predicates in paragraph (1)(B) described above. The court-judgment exception for incorrectly paid benefits is not the estate-recovery rule for correctly paid assistance. Subsection (a)(2) separately forbids the institutional home lien while a spouse, child in the age or blindness/disability categories just stated, or a sibling with an equity interest who resided there for at least the year immediately before institutional admission lawfully resides in the home. Under subsection (a)(3), the institutional lien dissolves upon discharge and return home. These predicate provisions are described because subsection (b) incorporates the institutional class; they do not enlarge this case into a separate lien challenge.

Section 1396c requires reasonable notice and a hearing opportunity for the state agency administering or supervising the approved plan. The Secretary must then find either a changed plan no longer complying with §1396a or failure in plan administration to comply substantially with a provision of that section. On that finding, the Secretary shall notify the state agency that further payments will not be made, or, in the Secretary's discretion, that payments will be limited to categories under or parts of the plan not affected by the failure. Payments must then stop or remain so limited until the Secretary is satisfied that there will no longer be that failure to comply. The statute does not mandate a particular lesser sanction, cap withholding at administrative funds, or establish that the Secretary has exercised the discretion here. The discretionary alternative concerns continued payments to unaffected categories or parts; it does not mean withholding those unaffected payments while leaving the affected categories paid.

The estate hardship waiver and category-limited funding discretion operate at different levels. Beneficiary protection helps describe the condition's actual demand; it does not prove that a threatened statewide cutoff is noncoercive. Likewise, a possible lesser sanction defeats the claim that the statute inherently requires total withdrawal, but it is not a guarantee that every future exercise will be lawful.

## Adversarial paths

**Affirmance path.** Program-related recovery with its actual qualifications and a sanction scheme permitting lawful narrower applications does not establish facial compulsion on the supplied record. Kansas rejects substituting substantial dollar amounts or political difficulty for an examination of actual choice. The strong counterargument is that a threat to end an established health program can itself compel compliance before any formal sanction, and statutory discretion offers cold comfort if the credible governmental demand is total withdrawal. That objection is legally sound in principle; it does not become an established fact here. The supplied warning is all-or-part, the statute retains the category route, and the record does not establish that HHS has closed that route or made a present demand that practically destroys choice. No statistics-only evidentiary demand is imposed.

**Invalidation or remand path.** A supported present demand leaving no genuine practical choice could warrant relief despite formal voluntariness, clear notice, relatedness, or a small recovery requirement. The strongest basis would be concrete operational evidence of the threatened funding consequence and practical inability to refuse; a compelled total loss would be especially material, but total loss is not a categorical prerequisite to coercion. The counterargument is that the Court would be deciding a different sanction application and assuming facts not supplied in this facial case. The present record does not establish such a demand. A remand merely to search for a different theory would convert an operator's limited source account into an invented evidentiary entitlement. These commitments therefore reject that path for this judgment while preserving the applicable constitutional rule.

An interpretation limiting §1396c to administrative funds is not a third lawful route: it lacks support in the enacted alternatives. Nor may Kansas's different statute supply a numerical Medicaid cap. Judicial review of a concrete sanction is the relevant limit, not textual rewriting.
