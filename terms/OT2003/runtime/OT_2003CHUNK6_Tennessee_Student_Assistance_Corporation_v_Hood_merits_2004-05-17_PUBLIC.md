## Event

**Tennessee Student Assistance Corporation v. Hood, No. 02-1606.** Argued March 1, 2004. Decided May 17, 2004, on review of the Sixth Circuit's affirmance of the denial of TSAC's immunity objection. The question is whether state immunity bars a bankruptcy court from determining undue hardship and dischargeability of a state-held student loan.

Hood reopened her Chapter 7 case and sought a hardship discharge. TSAC had filed no proof of claim and objected to the adversary complaint and summons. The relief determines the debt's enforceability through Hood's discharge, rather than seeking affirmative monetary recovery from the State.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated in the decision.

## Public Action

Affirmed and remanded, 7–2; the bankruptcy court may adjudicate undue hardship and dischargeability.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed and remanded, 7–2. Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. **Opposing:** Scalia and Thomas.

The denial of TSAC's immunity motion stands. The bankruptcy court may adjudicate dischargeability under §523(a)(8), with Hood bearing the applicable undue-hardship burden and TSAC afforded notice and an opportunity to contest it.

## Holdings

### I. Determining the debtor's discharge does not require the State's consent

**Holding and operative rule:** A bankruptcy court may determine whether a state-held student-loan debt falls within the debtor's discharge when the requested relief fixes the debtor's obligations rather than awarding affirmative personal recovery against the State. The prescribed hardship complaint and summons do not change the jurisdictional character of that discharge determination, and the State's choice not to file a claim supplies no veto over it.

**Authority:** Part I of Souter's opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer; seven Justices directly adopt this independently sufficient ground.

**Controlling explanation:** Bankruptcy jurisdiction determines the debtor's estate, obligations and discharge. Local Loan v. Hunt supplies the discharge-status principle and ancillary authority to give a discharge effect. A hardship inquiry determines whether an education debt remains outside that statutory discharge; it does not impose an independent duty on the State to pay. The creditor receives notice and an opportunity to oppose the debtor's showing. Calling the prescribed notice a summons does not convert this limited inquiry into personal recovery.

The strongest contrary argument identifies the real burdens of defending a debtor-specific adversary proceeding. Those burdens matter, but they do not alter the object and legal effect of this proceeding. Nordic Village distinguishes genuine bankruptcy jurisdiction from an affirmative money demand dressed in property language. Here the operative relief actually defines the debtor's discharge. The holding is confined to that function, rather than every proceeding bearing a bankruptcy label. Irving Trust supplies bankruptcy-administration context; its State-participation setting supplies no waiver by TSAC.

### II. Express Article I authorization independently permits the proceeding

**Holding and operative rule:** Congress may subject a State to private enforcement when the obligation and implementing adjudication lie within a valid Article I power and Congress unmistakably authorizes that exposure. Bankruptcy adjudication of the education-debt discharge exception meets those conditions, and §106(a)'s express inclusion of §§523 and 524 independently defeats TSAC's immunity objection.

**Authority:** Part II of Souter's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer; five Justices directly adopt this alternative holding. O'Connor and Kennedy do not join this part.

**Controlling explanation:** Florida Prepaid establishes the valid-obligation, appropriate-enforcement and unmistakable-authorization rule; Garrett and Hibbs retain it. Determining which debts survive the debtor's discharge is part of the Bankruptcy Clause's debt-adjustment function, and ordinary adjudication of the statutory exception implements that function. Section 106(a)(1) expressly names the relevant provisions, subsection (a)(2) authorizes determination of their application, and subsection (a)(3) permits necessary process. Clarity and constitutional power are both present. The objection distinguishing substantive power from compulsory private adjudication is the objection those precedents rejected; the Court adheres to their rule.

Authorization is distinct from consent. TSAC's nonfiling supplies no claim-linked waiver under §106(b); the estate-claim offset in §106(c) is a separate mechanism. Section 106(a) prohibits punitive damages, subjects costs and fees to 28 U.S.C. §2412(d)(2)(A)'s provisions and limitations, requires enforcement consistent with appropriate nonbankruptcy law, and creates no otherwise absent substantive cause of action.

### III. The education-debt exception still requires an undue-hardship determination

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 523(a)(8) excepts the covered education obligations from discharge unless that exclusion would impose undue hardship on the debtor and the debtor's dependents. Hood must establish that statutory hardship through the applicable adjudicative process; immunity and the State's nonparticipation do not replace the showing.

**Authority:** Part III is joined by the same seven Justices as Part I.

**Controlling explanation:** The provision covers an educational benefit overpayment or loan made, insured or guaranteed by a governmental unit; a loan made under a program funded in whole or part by a governmental unit or nonprofit institution; and an obligation to repay funds received as an educational benefit, scholarship or stipend. Determining whether the statutory hardship condition is met is ordinary merits work on remand. The Court's two jurisdictional grounds permit that determination; the loan remains subject to the statutory discharge exception until Hood carries her burden.

**Material precedent treatment:** Local Loan's discharge principle is applied. Nordic Village's original-§106(c) rule for affirmative federal monetary recovery remains distinct. Florida Prepaid, Garrett and Hibbs supply the independent Article I ground; each keeps its own statutory scope.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Local Loan Co. v. Hunt, 292 U.S. 234:** Applied for discharge as a determination of the debtor's status and for authority ancillary to that determination; the present State-creditor application rests on the actual relief here.
- **New York v. Irving Trust Co., 288 U.S. 329:** Supplies bankruptcy-administration context; its participation and distribution holding does not establish waiver by this nonfiling creditor.
- **United States v. Nordic Village:** Distinguished from an affirmative personal recovery under original §106(c); its remedy-specific consent rule remains in force.
- **Florida Prepaid; Garrett; Hibbs:** Applied in Part II to express enforcement of a valid Article I obligation through an appropriate measure.
- **Federal Maritime Commission v. South Carolina State Ports Authority:** Its function-and-remedy analysis remains distinct; this decision rests on bankruptcy discharge and express statutory authorization.

## Law After Decision

The bankruptcy court may adjudicate the education-debt exception against a State creditor on both the seven-Justice discharge ground and the five-Justice Article I ground. The debtor must prove undue hardship to obtain the discharge determination sought. An affirmative recovery from the State, recovery of property it holds or a separate coercive enforcement remedy lies outside the requested discharge relief and requires its own source and remedy analysis.

## Separate Writings

**O'Connor, concurring in part and in the judgment, joined by Kennedy.** The two join the discharge-specific analysis and statutory remand. They retain their structural objection to general Article I authorization of private suits against a State, and therefore withhold Part II. Their agreement rests on the bankruptcy court's actual determination of Hood's discharge and the absence of an affirmative State payment obligation. Nordic Village's remedy distinction and the function inquiry emphasized in their Federal Maritime Commission positions support that limited basis. Their concurrence creates no exemption for every bankruptcy adversary proceeding.

**Thomas, dissenting, joined by Scalia.** The two would reverse the immunity denial and remand for dismissal of this adversary action against TSAC. In their view, compelling a nonparticipating State creditor to defend a separate, individualized hardship claim imposes the private-suit burden immunity protects. A claim presumptively outside the discharge cannot escape that objection merely by being characterized as an adjustment of status. Local Loan involved a private creditor, and Irving Trust involved participation in distribution; neither resolves the coercive proceeding here.

They accept §106(a)'s clear authorization but would reconsider Florida Prepaid, Garrett and the related Article I enforcement rule to the extent necessary to preserve immunity against this private judicial compulsion. Their objection expressly challenges existing precedent. They would dismiss the adversary action against TSAC while leaving Hood's underlying bankruptcy case intact; they prescribe no substitute motion procedure.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns to the bankruptcy court for a scheduled hardship adjudication. TSAC must receive notice and an opportunity to present its position; Hood must prove the statutory basis for bringing the debt within her discharge. The court must then enter the dischargeability determination with the binding effect bankruptcy law authorizes.
