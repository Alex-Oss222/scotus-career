## Event

United States Postal Service v. Flamingo Industries (USA) Ltd., No. 02-1290; merits. Argued December 1, 2003; decided February 25, 2004. On certiorari to the Ninth Circuit. After its mail-sack contract ended, Flamingo alleged suppression of competition; the circuit reversed dismissal of its antitrust claims. The question is whether the Service is a covered antitrust person under its operative federal establishment and sue-and-be-sued provisions.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated.

## Public Action

The Ninth Circuit antitrust-coverage judgment is reversed.

## Judgment & Remedy

Reversed and remanded, 9–0. Supporting: Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. The lower court must dismiss the federal antitrust counts against the Postal Service and give the independent procurement and state-law claims their own treatment.

## Opinion Topology

Justice Thomas delivered the unanimous Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer.

## Holdings

**Controlling proposition:** Under the operative Postal Reorganization Act, the Postal Service remains part of the United States and is not a person substantively liable as a defendant under the federal antitrust laws. Its sue-and-be-sued clause waives immunity within its terms but supplies neither the missing statutory defendant coverage nor an independent cause of action; Flamingo's antitrust claims against the Service must be dismissed.

**Authority:** The unanimous Opinion of the Court supplies nine direct joins to the coverage rule and dismissal remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** FDIC v. Meyer requires separate answers to amenability to suit and substantive liability. The broad waiver allows litigation against the Postal Service, but each underlying statute still determines who owes the asserted duty. United States v. Cooper Corp. supplies the received distinction between the United States and an antitrust person; subsequent authorization for the Government to sue does not place it within the defendant class.

Congress designated the Postal Service an independent establishment of the executive branch. Its public obligations and governmental powers retain that federal character despite operational independence and commercial contracting. Loeffler and Franchise Tax Board give the postal waiver its broad effect in ordinary litigation; they do not alter the coverage of every liability statute. Flamingo's competition-policy argument therefore cannot substitute for enacted antitrust coverage. The Ninth Circuit's immunity confined to congressionally commanded conduct assumes the very statutory person status that is absent. Coverage governs the Service across its operations under these statutes; the reviewed antitrust counts fail on that antecedent legal ground.

## Precedent Treatment

FDIC v. Meyer is applied to the independent requirements of waiver and substantive coverage. United States v. Cooper Corp. retains its federal-person distinction for antitrust defendant status. Loeffler v. Frank and Franchise Tax Board v. Postal Service retain the broad operation of the express postal waiver and incidents of litigation; that waiver does not enlarge the underlying antitrust defendant class.

## Law After Decision

The Service’s operational independence and commercial conduct do not create antitrust coverage absent from the applicable enactments. The broad sue-and-be-sued waiver remains effective for causes of action supported by their own substantive law.
