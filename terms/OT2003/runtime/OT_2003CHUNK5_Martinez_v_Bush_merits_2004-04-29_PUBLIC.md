## Event

**Case:** Martinez v. Bush; review of Nos. 02-20244-CIV-JORDAN and 02-10028-CIV-JORDAN.
**Event:** Merits decision, April 29, 2004, on full merits submission.
**Route and judgment below:** Appeal under 28 U.S.C. § 1253 from the three-judge District Court for the Southern District of Florida, 234 F. Supp. 2d 1275, decided December 3, 2002, entering judgment for defendants.
**Questions:** Whether Florida's redistricting procedures and plans intentionally excluded racial minorities, denied protected voters equal electoral opportunity under Section 2, or imposed unconstitutional partisan injury. The review concerns the preserved claims and judgments, including the congressional, House and Senate partisan branches.
**Related governing decision:** Vieth v. Jubelirer, decided April 28, 2004, governs the partisan claims at its adopted scope.

## Participation

Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer participated at submission and decision.

## Public Action

The Court affirms the intentional-racial, Section 2, Senate partisan and independent automatic-theory judgments. It vacates the congressional and House partisan equal-protection judgments and remands.

## Holdings

### I. The intentional-racial claims fail on supported findings

**Holding and operative rule:** A claimant alleging intentional racial exclusion through redistricting procedures or plans under equal protection or Title VI § 601 must prove by a preponderance that racial discrimination was a motivating factor. Supported findings receive clear-error review; the trial court's findings sustain judgment for the defendants on the preserved intentional claims.

**Authority:** Part I of Souter's opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer and controls unanimously.

**Controlling explanation:** Washington v. Davis and Arlington Heights require the prohibited purpose, assessed from the whole relevant record. Crawford-El preserves ordinary preponderance proof rather than a heightened evidentiary threshold. The trial court examined software access, allocation methods, notice, hearing locations and times, and treatment of public input. It considered short notice, inconvenient scheduling, limited geography and substantive debate, and exclusion of Democrats from deliberations. It also considered numerous hearings with increased attendance, accessible low-cost software, recordings, submissions and ordinary access to legislators. Accepting the criticisms did not compel a finding of racial purpose. The finding has not been shown clearly erroneous, and partisan advantage neither establishes racial purpose nor excuses racial injury.

The same supported findings sustain the intentional challenge to the plans. The trial court found a purpose to secure Republican advantage and legal survival, while finding no purpose to injure Black or Hispanic electoral opportunity. Those purposes can coexist; the actual finding controls here.

The pleaded Title VI claim is the § 601 intentional-discrimination action. Powell, Sandoval and South Camden distinguish that action from the bounded direct prospective action for a covered recipient's ongoing violation of a valid, applicable § 602 effects regulation. No such regulatory claim is presented. Powell and South Camden also reject a regulation-created effects right enforceable through § 1983. The lower court's broader suggestion that every Title VI route requires intent conflicts with those decisions. Sandoval assumes the regulations' validity and preserves agency enforcement.

**Precedent treatment:** Davis, Arlington Heights and Crawford-El govern purpose and ordinary proof. Powell, Sandoval and South Camden preserve the distinct § 601, bounded direct-regulatory and § 1983 routes; the current case applies only the preserved intent claim.

### II. Section 2 requires demonstrated local electoral opportunity

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 2 does not require replacement of districts affording reliably demonstrated equal opportunity to elect protected voters' preferred candidates merely to increase racial percentages or seat totals. Plaintiffs bear the preponderance burden on unequal opportunity under the applicable voting predicates and complete circumstances; proportionality is relevant evidence, not a conclusive defense, and opportunity elsewhere cannot offset a proved local barrier.

**Authority:** Parts II–IV of Souter's opinion are joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg, Breyer. These seven Justices adopt the statutory rule and district-specific applications; Scalia and Thomas concur only in the statutory judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** Gingles treats districting arrangements as actionable when they cause the statutory inequality. It supplies distinct electoral-capacity, cohesion and ordinary opposing-bloc-defeat predicates, followed by an independent totality inquiry. Whether opposing-bloc voting ordinarily defeats preferred candidates must account for circumstances such as an unopposed preferred candidate or incumbency that can make a particular success unrepresentative. Voinovich preserves the challengers' burden and the distinction between lawful state districting choices and a court's remedy for an established violation. De Grandy rejects both maximization and automatic proportionality protection. Valdespino permits judgment on an independently sufficient evidentiary ground. The supported performance and comparison findings provide that ground here.

The record finds racial polarization in the relevant areas. The question is whether the challenged arrangements nevertheless provide actual equal opportunity, based on supported local evidence. Neither a Black candidate's race nor incumbency alone identifies the voters' preference or immunizes a district. Forecasts must be supported by actual behavior, demographics and relevant electoral conditions; they are not guarantees of particular victories. Brooks requires attention to nomination as well as general-election opportunity. The distinct congressional and House applications below retain those limits.

**Precedent treatment:** Gingles and Voinovich supply the causal and totality framework; De Grandy rejects maximization and aggregate offsets; Valdespino supplies the independent-evidence path; Brooks preserves examination of the affected nomination process.

### III. The challenged congressional districts retain supported opportunity

**Holding and operative rule:** The supported findings concerning CDs 3, 17 and 23 sustain rejection of the presented Section 2 claims. The assessment concerns dependable opportunity under their actual electoral conditions, including the conditional primary structure, rather than a racial headcount or a guarantee of every future result.

**Authority:** The seven-Justice coalition identified for Parts II–IV controls this application.

**Controlling explanation:** The findings draw on voting history, registration, demographics and election analyses. CDs 3 and 17 were expected to elect Black voters' preferred candidates, with no evidenced lawful alternative materially improving performance, a point virtually conceded. CD 23 received a separate contested assessment. The challengers' 48.0-percent projection assumed an open primary, particular turnout and cohesion estimates, and no incumbency benefit; the court explained why those assumptions understated likely performance and also found open-seat opportunity.

Florida's conditional open-primary rule permits all electors to vote when the candidates share one party and its nominee will face no general-election opposition. Under the applied construction, a qualified write-in candidate supplies opposition and closes that primary. The court's expectation that a write-in candidate would qualify when CD 23's performance was threatened rested on election history and increased write-in qualification after the amendment. That remains a forecast, not an actual filing or statutory guarantee.

For 2004, chapter 2003-415 provides nomination by the highest vote total, with tied highest totals resolved by lot, and suspends second primaries until January 1, 2006. The earlier projected return of majority runoffs in 2004 is obsolete. Independent local findings address possible vote splitting and expected coalescence or withdrawal when opportunity is threatened. The continued plurality system was itself considered, and the surviving evidence includes local demographics, voting history and the express open-seat finding. Those supported findings sustain the judgment under the actual primary law. Their predictions remain conditional, rather than guarantees of coalescence or a statutory assurance of success.

**Precedent treatment:** De Grandy and Valdespino require actual supported opportunity; Brooks requires the nomination conditions to be evaluated directly rather than assumed away.

### IV. The House performance and packing claims retain their separate proof

**Holding and operative rule:** The local findings concerning HDs 104, 108 and 118 sustain the expected-performance judgments on the claims presented, while the packing claims concerning HDs 103 and 109 fail for lack of a supported lawful rearrangement improving affected voters' opportunity. An improvement in another district cannot cure a proved impairment in HD 108 or any other district.

**Authority:** The same seven-Justice coalition controls these distinct local applications.

**Controlling explanation:** HDs 104 and 118 have favorable closed-primary findings and open-primary projections of 53.7 and 51.3 percent, respectively. HD 108 requires closer attention: the court accepted 47.2 percent as reasonable in the unlikely open-primary setting, with less than an even chance of prevailing if other circumstances stayed equal. Candidates and issues could change the outcome. Its favorable assessment includes a virtually uncontested 52.0-percent closed-primary projection assuming a single candidate with substantial Black support, history including an actual open-seat contest, and express predictions of a closed primary and coalescence around one preferred candidate.

The incorporation of the earlier runoff discussion requires evaluation under the continuing plurality system. The coalescence finding independently addressed that mechanism. Its continuation until 2006 supplies no new performance evidence or demonstration that the surviving local predictions are clearly erroneous. Plaintiffs still must prove unequal opportunity caused by the boundaries; a plausible adverse contingent election does not itself carry that burden. The result rests on the supported combined local assessment, not conjectured citizenship growth or a two-candidate projection treated as proof of every multi-candidate outcome. A structural nomination barrier cannot be dismissed simply as voters' failure to coalesce. HD 118's gains cannot offset a proved barrier in HD 108.

The plaintiffs conceded expected performance in HDs 103 and 109 but alleged packing. The court examined neighboring districts, their opportunities, county geography and proposed transfers; it found no demonstrated lawful rearrangement improving the affected voters' opportunity. HD 109's comparison depends in part on HD 108's performance, at the bounded scope sustained here. Racial concentration alone does not establish that injury. HD 118 also has the independent finding that no evidenced lawful alternative materially improves performance.

**Precedent treatment:** De Grandy preserves the local inquiry and prohibits conclusive geographic offsets. Voinovich and Valdespino require supported opportunity and a lawful comparison, while Brooks preserves nomination-stage analysis.

**Preserved statutory scope:** The active Section 2 disputes concern CDs 3, 17 and 23 and HDs 103, 104, 108, 109 and 118. Relief is no longer sought on the performance objections to SDs 1 and 18, HDs 15, 39 and 94, or the Cuban/non-Cuban Hispanic theory after the stated absence of an evidenced lawful improvement. Expected Hispanic-preferred performance in CDs 18, 21 and 25 was accepted rather than newly contested. Those scope limitations are distinct from the Senate partisan evidentiary concession.

### V. Vieth supplies the complete partisan political-opportunity rule

**Holding and operative rule:** A claimant must establish each of the following elements; evidence relevant to one does not substitute for another.

1. **Opportunity:** Identify a practical capacity, already existing or demonstrably attainable, for the affected voters or association to organize, participate, nominate or elect. Evidence must identify the people and activity involved. An emerging opportunity can qualify without a prior victory; party identity, desired additional seats and expected incumbent support alone do not establish one.
2. **Material impairment:** Show a substantial practical obstruction to that activity caused by an identified districting operation. Concentration, division, reassignment or disruption of a coalition must actually impair the opportunity. Ordinary inconvenience or an unfavorable electoral prospect alone is insufficient. Complete extinction of the opportunity, exclusion from every political activity and inevitable electoral defeat are unnecessary.
3. **Prohibited purpose:** Show that suppressing the identified opposition's effective opportunity because of its political association or participation, and thereby insulating governing power from electoral challenge, substantially motivated the decision. The purpose must actually contribute, but need be neither exclusive nor predominant. Awareness of partisan consequences or pursuit of ordinary partisan advantage alone does not establish this purpose.
4. **Material causation:** Identify how the challenged assignments materially produced the impairment, separating their effect from voter geography, ordinary competition and independently lawful requirements. The boundaries need not be the sole cause; a correlation between party preference and an unfavorable outcome is insufficient.

**Holding and operative rule:** The claimant bears persuasion by a preponderance of the evidence on all four elements, using a comparison with an independently justified, legally and practically feasible political opportunity to establish actual impairment and boundary-specific causation. Once that showing is made, the State bears persuasion by the same standard that materially the same lawful districting decision would have occurred without the prohibited purpose; if it fails, further balancing of partisan advantages cannot excuse the violation.

A preponderance means more likely true than not. A genuinely applied, independently justified fairness policy or actual population, geographic or statutory requirements can establish the defense only when the evidence shows that they account for materially the same lawful decision. A merely conceivable explanation or another asserted political objective does not carry that burden.

No particular drawing, expert method, number of maps or type of evidence is universally mandatory. A feasible alternative district, actual organizational activity, the effects of a reassignment or another supported comparison may establish the necessary propositions. Whichever method is used must independently establish lawful feasibility and causation; past success, an old map or a preferred seat allocation alone cannot do so. A claim whose comparison depends on a different configuration must establish that configuration's feasibility, including effects necessary to make the comparison work, and may require mapping evidence for that reason.

**Authority:** Parts V–VI of Souter's opinion are joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Ginsburg, Breyer. Those five Justices adopt the complete Vieth framework and the congressional and House remand.

**Controlling explanation:** Vieth supplies a personal political-opportunity claim under equal protection, applying to properly presented open cases. Its actual four-element rule governs Congress and the House. Baker permits adjudication through identifiable legal standards; Bandemer preserves justiciability. Gingles addresses racial statutory opportunity and supplies neither a fixed-party cohesion requirement nor a universal compactness or opposing-bloc prerequisite for this constitutional claim.

The objection that every disappointed voter could demand a more favorable constituency fails under Vieth's actual injury, independently lawful comparison, prohibited-purpose and causal requirements. Cano requires injury independent of political design. A substantial practical obstruction is actionable when imposed; an affected voter need not await a lost general election. Partisan predominance, like ordinary party advantage, cannot establish the complete violation by itself. The purpose element concerns actual suppression of identified political opportunity, rather than knowledge that a map benefits a party.

Personal standing separately requires the claimant's own injury, traceability and likely redress. Vieth's flexible methods of proof preserve the substantive comparison requirement. A different configuration must be shown workable and lawful when the claim depends on it. Its State defense concerns the actual same lawful decision, supported by evidence, rather than a conceivable explanation. These requirements answer the objection that a feasible alternative alone creates a constitutional entitlement to its electoral distribution.

**Precedent treatment:** Vieth governs the complete constitutional claim. Baker and Bandemer retain their justiciability roles; Cano supplies independent injury; Gingles remains the racial statutory framework rather than a source of additional partisan elements.

### VI. The congressional and House partisan claims require application of the correct rule

**Holding and operative rule:** The congressional and House partisan equal-protection judgments are vacated because their legal-test-dependent reasoning does not resolve the preserved claims under Vieth. The supported racial findings and the distinct affirmed judgments remain intact.

**Authority:** The five-Justice coalition identified for Parts V–VI controls both remand components.

**Controlling explanation:** The congressional claim includes the projected 18-to-7 delegation, registration and statewide voting indices, local testimony concerning approximately 69,909 residents, predominantly Black Democrats, reassigned from CD 10 to CD 11, and the Campbell alternative S33C0009. The lower court partly discounted the local evidence because it did not prove statewide injury and evaluated the alternative through imported Gingles gates. Vieth instead requires the affected persons, protected activity, actual impairment, lawful comparison and boundary-specific cause to be identified. The stipulated pursuit of Republican advantage does not establish its more specific prohibited purpose.

The lower court's criticisms of registration and statewide indices remain pertinent: registration is not actual voting, and indices may involve different electorates and omit congressional races. Its separate totality discussion also contains adverse evidence concerning slating, access and responsiveness. But complete exclusion, unresponsiveness and inevitable defeat do not exhaust Vieth's protection. The ability to recruit voters, change party identity or reverse earlier political fortunes cannot by itself negate a present boundary-caused obstruction. That alternative reasoning therefore does not resolve the complete opportunity claim under the governing rule.

The House claim was separately pleaded and rejected under the same legal test. Its disposition supplies no independent House-specific ground resolving Vieth's elements. The actual House record must be evaluated; congressional testimony cannot substitute for it, and a brief treatment in the judgment is not a House evidentiary concession. The difference from the Senate claim is material. Applying the correct test to these tried claims may lead to judgment on existing evidence; ordinary procedure governs any amendment or supplementation.

**Precedent treatment:** Vieth displaces the imported statutory gates and the requirement of complete political exclusion; its actual proof and standing requirements govern the remaining factual application.

### VII. The Senate partisan claim fails on its separate proof concession

**Holding and operative rule:** The Senate partisan judgment is affirmed because the plaintiffs expressly conceded that they offered no evidence to prove political gerrymandering of that plan. A change in the governing legal standard does not supply the missing proof of this tried claim.

**Authority:** Part VII of Souter's opinion is joined by Stevens, Kennedy, Ginsburg, Breyer. Those five Associates supply the controlling proof-failure rationale; O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas join the judgment on this component, and Stone-Zsela dissents.

**Controlling explanation:** Footnote 58 records the concession in the plaintiffs' second amended proposed findings and conclusions. Vieth requires evidence of an actual personal political opportunity and the other elements. Its change in the legal test therefore leaves this independently sufficient evidentiary failure intact, even assuming a cognizable claim. The concession concerns the Senate partisan claim alone; the racial Senate districts and the common intentional-process claims receive their separate dispositions.

**Precedent treatment:** Vieth's ordinary burden of proving the constitutional elements applies; a new legal formulation does not erase an express failure to offer evidence.

### VIII. Congressional election power supplies no automatic party allocation

**Holding and operative rule:** A projected congressional-seat supermajority without a corresponding statewide vote majority does not by itself violate Article I, sections 2 or 4. Characterizing congressional districting as delegated rather than reserved state power also does not establish a lower equal-protection injury threshold that would make the asserted disproportionality sufficient.

**Authority:** Part VIII of Souter's opinion is joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer. Those eight Associates adopt these grounds; Stone-Zsela joins the judgment on these components.

**Controlling explanation:** Vieth's Article I holding directly rejects an entitlement to proportional statewide party representation. Separate district elections do not become a statewide proportional contest because projected party outcomes are described as predetermined. Article I's delegation of election-related power supplies no substitute for the independently defined deprivation that the separate constitutional claim requires. Thornton's treatment of the source and limits of congressional election power does not establish that missing injury. The officials therefore retain judgment on these proposed automatic theories while the distinct congressional opportunity claim proceeds under its own rule.

**Precedent treatment:** Vieth's Article I holding applies to reject the statewide party-seat entitlement. Thornton does not turn the delegated-power characterization into the proposed automatic injury rule.

## Precedent Treatment

Washington v. Davis and Arlington Heights retain the ordinary motivating-factor rule; Crawford-El preserves ordinary proof. Powell, Sandoval and South Camden retain the distinct § 601 intent action, bounded direct prospective action under valid applicable § 602 effects regulations, and rejection of a regulation-created effects right under § 1983.

Gingles and Voinovich govern statutory causal proof and totality. De Grandy retains the prohibition on maximization, conclusive proportionality defenses and geographic offsets. Valdespino supplies the independent-evidence ground applied here; Brooks requires attention to actual nomination opportunity.

Vieth governs the personal partisan-opportunity claim, its complete four-element test, comparison, burdens, defense and remedy, as well as its separate Article I rule. Baker and Bandemer retain their justiciability work. Cano retains the requirement of an independently cognizable injury. Thornton does not support the proposed automatic injury from the source of congressional districting power. Growe preserves the State's initial responsibility to correct a proved districting violation.

## Law After Decision

The racial and statutory judgments stand on supported local findings under their own governing rules. Predictive performance findings retain their actual primary-law assumptions and limits; a local injury cannot be offset by gains elsewhere.

Vieth's constitutional rule governs properly presented partisan claims in the Florida congressional and House plans. Imported racial statutory gates and complete political exclusion are not additional constitutional prerequisites. Its personal injury, lawful comparison, purpose, causation and proof requirements control the remaining application. The Senate claim ends on its separate proof concession, and the independent automatic theories fail.

## Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** The Chief Justice joins the Court's congressional and House remand and would also vacate the preserved Senate partisan portion for application of the governing Vieth rule. He would require the three-judge court to evaluate the preserved partisan claims under that rule, permitting amendments and further proof only through ordinary procedure, and to enter judgment for officials on any branch where injury is not established. His broader partisan remand preserves the independently resolved racial findings and statutory judgments.

**O'Connor, joined by Scalia and Thomas, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** These Justices retain the objection stated in Vieth that partisan-dilution claims lack a judicially enforceable entitlement to an alternative distribution of political support. A feasible map and partisan purpose do not establish which distribution the Constitution owes. They would affirm the congressional and House judgments on that threshold ground. Their objection concerns this partisan category; independently defined population, racial, exclusion and retaliation claims retain their own reach. They agree with the Senate judgment and recognize its proof failure conditionally if the Court's cause is applied. They do not join that cause through conditional agreement with its implementation.

**Kennedy, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** Kennedy would affirm the presented congressional and House partisan claims because the announced measure does not adequately distinguish a constitutionally defined burden from an unfavorable composition of the electorate. Holder's comparison discipline and Cano's independent-injury requirement inform his objection. The congressional index and local reassignment evidence do not persuade him that the required independent entitlement has been identified. His House conclusion rests on that constitutional objection, rather than an inferred absence of House evidence. He retains the possibility of a properly defined future participation or association injury and rejects categorical foreclosure. He joins the Senate proof-failure rationale and the other portions specified in the topology.

**Thomas, joined by Scalia, concurring in the statutory judgment.** They adhere to their Holder/De Grandy position, continued in Valdespino, Montiel and Cano, that Section 2 does not authorize these district-dilution claims. That noncoverage rule supplies their ground for affirmance. Their conditional recognition that the cited percentages and plurality extension do not automatically establish liability is not adoption of the Court's dilution framework or its local sufficiency rationale. They join the distinct intentional-racial and Title VI disposition at the scope stated in Part I.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns to the three-judge District Court for application of the complete Vieth rule to the preserved congressional and House partisan claims and their actual record. The court must determine personal standing and address conforming amendment or supplementation under ordinary procedure, including valid preservation and disclosure rulings and any genuinely sufficient independent ground. A failure to establish injury requires judgment for officials on that branch.

Upon a proved violation, the State receives the first opportunity to correct the actual deprivation. Any further remedy must address the responsible boundaries, comply with governing districting requirements and extend no further than necessary, on a prompt workable schedule suited to the election calendar. Existing operative plans remain in effect pending supported relief. The racial, process and Senate partisan affirmances remain intact.
