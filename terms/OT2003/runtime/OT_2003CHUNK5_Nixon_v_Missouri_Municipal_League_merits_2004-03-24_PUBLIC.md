## Event

**Case:** Nixon v. Missouri Municipal League, Nos. 02-1238, 02-1386 and 02-1405, consolidated.
**Event:** Merits decision, March 24, 2004. Argued January 12, 2004.
**Route and judgment below:** On writs of certiorari to the Eighth Circuit. The court reversed the FCC's refusal to preempt Missouri's restriction on its political subdivisions' provision or sale of telecommunications services.
**Question:** Whether 47 U.S.C. § 253, through its protection of the ability of any entity to enter telecommunications markets, authorizes federal preemption of the State's allocation of service authority to its own subdivisions.
**Posture:** Missouri, the FCC and the carrier petitioner seek reversal of the same circuit judgment; the municipal respondents seek displacement of the state restriction.

## Participation

Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the Eighth Circuit in all three consolidated dockets and sustains the FCC's refusal to preempt the challenged state restriction.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed in Nos. 02-1238, 02-1386 and 02-1405, 8-1 in each.**
**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Stevens.
The FCC's refusal to preempt remains effective for the challenged allocation of municipal service authority.

## Opinion Topology

Scalia, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which Stone-Zsela, C. J., and O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, JJ., joined. Stevens, J., filed a dissenting opinion.

## Holdings

### Section 253 does not clearly reassign municipal service powers

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 253 does not authorize the FCC to displace a State's assignment or withdrawal of telecommunications-service authority to its own political subdivisions merely through the provision's general reference to the ability of any entity to provide service. A claimant seeking that alteration of state governmental authority must identify unmistakable congressional direction in the statute's text and structure; § 253 supplies no such direction here and does not preempt Missouri's restriction on the municipal respondents.

**Authority:** Scalia's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, controls the statutory rule and judgment in all three consolidated dockets. Stevens dissents.

**Controlling explanation:** Gregory requires clear congressional direction before general statutory language is read to alter important state governmental arrangements. A State's choice of the powers its own subdivisions may exercise presents that concern. Section 253 targets state and local restrictions on telecommunications entry, but identifying state regulation as an object of preemption does not clearly identify the State's allocation of governmental capacity as another object.

The municipalities and the dissent read the entity language to protect existing, authorized municipal providers against targeted restraints. They distinguish removal of a market barrier from creation or funding of a municipality. That distinction explains their proposed construction, but it does not resolve the text's ambiguity about state-created capacity. The provision reaches measures that prohibit service and those with a prohibitory effect. It supplies no unmistakable boundary under which a targeted withdrawal of municipal service authority is federally forbidden while a general allocation of the same powers remains permissible. The problem persists whether the restraint is direct or indirect; it is not resolved by the order of state enactments. Preemption of a restraint also depends on whatever affirmative capacity state law supplies. These features explain the absence of the required statutory clarity.

New York distinguishes regulation of activity from assignment of governmental responsibility. AT&T gives the FCC the telecommunications field Congress actually delegated, with the statutory allocation intact. Ours Garage preserves a State's choice to delegate retained authority to its subdivisions. Together these cases support the distinction between regulating a provider's conduct and deciding which governmental body may become that provider. Gregory's clarity requirement resolves this case while preserving § 253's ordinary operation against covered market barriers.

**Operative scope:** This holding concerns a State's allocation of service powers to its own political subdivisions. Independent private providers and the distinct status of independently chartered municipal corporations remain governed by their own legal predicates. The constitutional validity of a different, explicit congressional direction is outside the statutory question presented.

**Precedent treatment:** Gregory's clear-statement principle is applied to the internal State–subdivision allocation. New York, AT&T and Ours Garage retain their respective structural, delegated-regulation and state-delegation holdings and supply the stated distinctions.

## Precedent Treatment

Gregory v. Ashcroft supplies the requirement of statutory clarity before altering important state governmental arrangements. New York v. United States preserves the distinction between regulating state activity and directing governmental responsibility. AT&T Corp. v. Iowa Utilities Board retains the FCC's actual delegated national implementing authority and the State's assigned functions. City of Columbus v. Ours Garage & Wrecker Service, Inc. supplies the State's ability to act through authorized subdivisions within retained authority; that state choice is the relevant analogy here.

## Law After Decision

Section 253 leaves a State's assignment or withdrawal of telecommunications-service powers to its own political subdivisions intact absent the requisite congressional direction. Its ordinary preemption of covered market barriers remains operative. Courts must identify whether a challenged measure regulates the conduct of an empowered provider or determines the authority of a State's subdivision to become that provider.

## Separate Writings

Stevens dissents. He would affirm that municipal providers are included within § 253 and return the proceeding for application to their actual state-law authority. In his reading, the general entity language, the express targeting of state and local restrictions and the Act's competition structure reach public utilities already authorized and capable of operating without compelled state assistance. Ordinary preemption removes an unlawful barrier; it need not create a municipality, provide funding or supply an otherwise nonexistent power.

Drawing on the distinction in White's Gregory writing and his support for AT&T's federal local-competition program, Stevens considers the statute's express regulation of state barriers sufficient without an additional clear statement naming municipal providers. He would distinguish a general redefinition of municipal powers from a restraint specifically targeting municipal telecommunications, including an indirect restraint with that prohibitory effect. The effects language strengthens his protection against disguised market barriers rather than eliminating that distinction. His proposed remand would determine the surviving state-law capacity and the actual restriction, rather than presume federal creation of authority.

## Procedure After Action

The challenged Missouri restriction remains effective against the municipal respondents, and the FCC's refusal to displace it is sustained. The consolidated review is concluded; any change to municipal service authority must occur through an independently lawful route.
