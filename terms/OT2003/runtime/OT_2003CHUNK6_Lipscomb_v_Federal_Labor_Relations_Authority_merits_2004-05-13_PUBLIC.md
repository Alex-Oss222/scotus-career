## Event

**Lipscomb v. Federal Labor Relations Authority.** Decided May 13, 2004, on merits review of the Fifth Circuit's June 24, 2003 judgment, 333 F.3d 611, affirming summary judgment against the Mississippi National Guard and its adjutant general. The question is whether federal law permits the FLRA to conduct a representation election among federally designated civilian technicians and whether that proceeding violates the Tenth Amendment or state sovereign immunity.

The technicians are federally paid civilian employees whose employment the adjutant general administers under 32 U.S.C. §709. Required military affiliation accompanies the civilian employment. The Guard and adjutant general seek to enjoin the representation election.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated in the decision.

## Public Action

Affirmed, 9–0; the civilian-technician representation proceeding may continue.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 9–0. Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The judgment rejecting the requested injunction stands. The FLRA representation proceeding may continue for the covered civilian technicians.

## Holdings

### Federal civilian employment determines the representation obligation

**Holding and operative rule:** A National Guard organization and adjutant general administering the federal civilian technician employment created by 32 U.S.C. §709 must comply with the applicable federal labor statute in that federal employer capacity. The uniformed-services exclusion governs military service; required military membership does not extinguish the separately designated civilian appointment or defeat this representation election.

**Authority:** O'Connor's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; all nine Justices adopt both holdings and their qualifications.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 709 makes these technicians employees of the relevant military Department and the United States and designates the adjutant general to employ and administer them. Sections 7103 and 2105 preserve the distinction between that appointment and military membership. Congress's express allocation supplies the federal employment relationship; the holding does not depend on treating every Guard activity as an Executive agency activity. The argument that the employer's state organizational identity precludes coverage overlooks the particular departmental employment function the statute assigns. NASA v. FLRA directs attention to the actual statutory representation relationship, while NFFE v. Department of the Interior preserves the Authority's enacted limits. Feres addresses military tort liability and does not displace express civilian labor coverage.

Representation eligibility is distinct from the legality of a bargaining demand. Section 7103(a)(12) requires neither agreement nor concession; §7103(a)(14)(C) excludes matters specifically provided for by federal statute from conditions of employment. Section 709's particular military-management, discipline and appeal exceptions remain operative. A later dispute over a concrete proposal must apply the applicable statutory procedure and the actual exclusion.

### Neither structural objection defeats this federal employment proceeding

**Holding and operative rule:** Applying the federal labor statute to this expressly created federal employer function neither commands state administration of federal regulation of unrelated private persons nor subjects the State in its sovereign capacity to the asserted private suit. The official's additional state military authority supplies no immunity from this federal representation obligation.

**Authority:** The unanimous opinion identified above.

**Controlling explanation:** Reno v. Condon distinguishes regulation of a government's covered operations from the compulsory administration of others' conduct prohibited by Printz and Mack. Here Congress regulates its departmental employment arrangement through the designated administrator. Federal Maritime Commission requires examination of actual statutory function and relief. That inquiry identifies federal employment administration here, rather than relying solely on an administrative label or the absence of damages.

**Material precedent treatment:** Condon's function distinction is applied; Printz and Mack's anti-conscription rule is distinguished. NASA and NFFE supply bounded statutory analysis, and Feres remains confined to its different military tort setting.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Reno v. Condon:** Applied to the regulated function; its own-activity reasoning supports analysis of the identified federal employment arrangement.
- **Printz v. United States; Mack v. United States:** Distinguished because the representation duty governs the federal employer function rather than conscripting a state executive to administer regulation of unrelated persons.
- **NASA v. FLRA; NFFE v. Department of the Interior:** Applied to identify the statutory representation relationship and enforce actual limits on labor obligations.
- **Federal Maritime Commission v. South Carolina State Ports Authority:** Applied in requiring actual function and remedy analysis rather than immunity by title or procedural label.
- **Feres v. United States:** Distinguished; military tort liability supplies no repeal of the civilian representation provisions.

## Law After Decision

Federal civilian technician administration carries the labor obligations Congress attached to that function. The Guard's dual character must be assessed by the capacity and particular obligation at issue. Representation proceeds subject to the statute's genuine military and management exclusions, which govern later concrete bargaining disputes.
