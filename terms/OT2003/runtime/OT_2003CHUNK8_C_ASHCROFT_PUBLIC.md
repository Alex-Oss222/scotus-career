## Event

Ashcroft v. American Civil Liberties Union; No. 03-218. Argued March 2, 2004; decided June 29, 2004. On writ of certiorari to the Third Circuit, which again affirmed the preliminary injunction against the Child Online Protection Act after this Court's 2002 remand. The question is whether that protection should continue because the Government has not adequately justified the restriction against plausible, less restrictive filtering alternatives. The Government seeks dissolution; the challengers seek continued protection and final adjudication on the proper record.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated.

## Public Action

The judgment is affirmed and the case remanded for prompt final adjudication. The preliminary enforcement injunction continues.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed and remanded, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg.
**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia and Breyer.

The Attorney General remains bound by the existing injunction without an enforcement gap. It must prevent the demonstrated chilling and access burdens; a narrower order requires a supported showing that it would provide an effective cure. Modification requires a supported legal or factual basis. The opposing Justices would reverse the filtering-based preliminary determination and remand for lawful disposition of remaining preserved grounds.

## Holdings

### I. The Government must justify the complete restriction against concrete alternatives

**Holding and operative rule:** When a content-based restriction burdens protected adult Internet expression and a concrete, plausibly effective less restrictive alternative is identified, the Government must establish why that alternative's comparative inadequacy warrants the broader restriction in serving the compelling child-protection interest. At the preliminary stage, an injunction may remain when the supported record shows a likelihood that the Government cannot meet that burden and the other equitable requirements are satisfied; appellate review respects the district court's discretion while independently correcting legal error.

**Authority:** Part I of Ginsburg's opinion, joined by all nine Justices, supplies the common framework.

**Controlling explanation:** Reno v. American Civil Liberties Union protects adults' access to lawful Internet expression and requires workable safeguards against a criminal restriction's actual burdens. United States v. Playboy Entertainment Group places the comparative justification burden on the Government after a concrete lesser restraint is presented. Legislative predictions deserve serious consideration, but do not settle that inquiry. Neither a merely imagined alternative nor a demand for perfect filtering supplies the proper comparison.

The earlier Ashcroft decision requires the statute to be considered as a whole. COPA reaches knowing World Wide Web communications in interstate or foreign commerce, made for commercial purposes with knowledge of their character, available to persons under seventeen and harmful to minors. Its commercial category concerns a regular business course and profit objective, with knowing posting or solicitation; it requires neither actual profit nor a sole or principal business. That category does not turn protected literature into commercial advertising.

Harmful material is obscenity or material satisfying all three additional elements: taken as a whole, prurient appeal or pandering with respect to minors under contemporary community standards; specified patently offensive sexual depictions, descriptions or representations with respect to minors; and, taken as a whole, lack of serious literary, artistic, political or scientific value for minors. The content element includes actual or simulated sexual acts or contacts and lewd displays of genitals or post-pubescent female breasts. The community-standards language expressly appears in the prurience element; serious value remains objective under Pope v. Illinois.

The defenses, exclusions and privacy conditions stated below matter to practical adult access. Their availability reduces coverage and potential liability, but does not prove every reader has affordable, anonymous and effective access. Courts must evaluate their operation, rather than add missing offense limits or treat material's lack of value for minors as loss of adult protection.

**Operative statutory qualifications:** Section 231(b) excludes telecommunications carriers, Internet-access providers and information-location services to the extent they act in those capacities. It separately protects intermediaries transmitting, storing, retrieving, hosting, formatting or translating another's communication without selecting or altering its content; deletion consistent with §231(c) or §230 does not defeat that protection. Section 231(c)(1)'s alternative good-faith access defenses are credit card or debit account use, adult access code or adult personal identification number, an age-verifying digital certificate, or other reasonable measures feasible with available technology. Section 231(c)(2) separately protects good-faith implementation or restriction activity against a cause of action only if the activity violates no law carrying a criminal or civil penalty. Section 231(d) requires protection against unauthorized access to collected information and prior written or electronic consent for disclosure from the adult concerned or the parent or guardian of a person under seventeen; disclosure is permitted when necessary to communicate, to conduct legitimate related business activity, or under an authorizing court order. These provisions protect information without guaranteeing absolute anonymity.

### II. The present record supports continued protection and a prompt merits adjudication

**Holding and operative rule:** The district court did not abuse its discretion by preserving the injunction because the supported comparison shows a likelihood that the Government cannot justify COPA's adult-speech burdens against user-controlled filtering. The injunction continues while the district court promptly resolves final validity through the integrated statutory inquiry; supported findings remain unless genuinely new evidence or legal error warrants reconsideration, and challengers need not disprove every conceivable alternative.

**Authority:** Part II of Ginsburg's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg, controls by six direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** Filtering acts at the receiving end. It can address foreign-origin and non-Web material outside COPA's coverage while permitting adults to choose lawful reading without publisher-imposed verification or identification. COPA's defenses do not eliminate the record's expense, anonymity and access concerns, and its verification methods remain subject to circumvention.

The dissent correctly identifies the statute's narrower harmful-material category and its added protection for children whose parents do not activate filters. Those are relevant advantages. But a beneficial increment does not itself demonstrate why this broader adult burden is necessary. The Government's evidence does not adequately answer filtering's wider reach and reduced interference with adult choice. The Court neither assumes universal parental participation nor requires flawless filtering; it requires the actual comparative justification that Playboy assigns to the Government.

United States v. American Library Association concerns an assisted library service, distinct LSTA and E-rate disabling permissions, and effective adult access in that setting. Its statutory permission is not a finding that every request is honored; its constitutional protection concerns prompt whole-session disabling or an equally effective method without disclosure of protected reading or an approved reason, subject to reasonable technical, supervision and resource procedures. That decision establishes neither universal filter effectiveness nor justification for criminal restrictions on private publishers.

Preserving relief avoids the demonstrated chill while the court develops any material changes in date-appropriate evidence. The preliminary posture leaves final facial validity and supported particular applications for the merits record. Any severance must follow an actual statutory division, rather than create a new offense. The district court must set a prompt schedule, identify genuinely disputed updated facts and state the enforceable scope of its final decree.

## Precedent Treatment

- Ashcroft v. American Civil Liberties Union, 535 U.S. 564, is applied: rejection of community standards as an automatic ground of invalidity requires integrated review and preserves consideration of actual adult burdens.
- Reno v. American Civil Liberties Union is applied to protected adult Internet access, workable safeguards and genuine textual severance; COPA's different elements receive their own examination.
- United States v. Playboy Entertainment Group is applied to the Government's comparative justification burden and the need for concrete alternatives rather than perfect protection.
- Pope v. Illinois is applied to the objective serious-value inquiry; community approval does not supply that element.
- United States v. American Library Association is distinguished at its library-service, funding and effective-access scope. Its distinct statutory disabling provisions retain their own terms.

## Law After Decision

Courts considering a preliminary challenge to a content-based adult-speech restriction must compare the complete law with concrete lesser restraints under the Government's continuing justification burden. COPA remains enjoined on the present record. Genuine changes in evidence may be considered at final adjudication under that same rule; the preliminary judgment does not resolve final facial validity.

## Separate Writings

Justice Stevens, joined by Justice Ginsburg in the geographic-burden and criminal-chill discussion, joins the Court's opinion and adds that those features reinforce the need for preliminary protection. National Web speakers cannot select every local audience, so a restrictive community's standard can burden protected reading elsewhere. The whole-material and serious-value requirements narrow coverage, but value for minors does not exhaust the speech adults may receive. Reno's protection of adult access and the statutory inquiry required by the earlier Ashcroft decision make that practical burden material. Stevens separately continues to regard the geographic objection as independently sufficient and rejects insertion of a national standard into the enacted community language. Ginsburg's join is confined to the geographic concern within the complete statutory inquiry and the criminal-chill discussion.

Both emphasize the distinctive chill of a criminal publisher restriction. An affirmative defense may ultimately defeat liability while still exposing a speaker to the risk and cost of prosecution, encouraging silence before a court ever adjudicates the defense. The information-protection provisions and access options therefore do not eliminate the practical burden merely by existing in the statute. Their additional concern supports retaining the preliminary restraint while the complete statutory operation is adjudicated; it does not expand the Court's common filtering holding into a final declaration of facial invalidity.

Justice Breyer, joined by Justices O'Connor and Scalia in this comparative-application portion, agrees that protected adult speech is involved and that the Government bears the strict-scrutiny burden. They would reverse the determination that filtering establishes a sufficient likelihood of success to retain this injunction basis. Their disagreement concerns the supported comparison, not the governing standard.

COPA's conjunctive harmful-material requirements, commercial-coverage limits and alternative good-faith adult-access defenses materially reduce the burden compared with the broader restriction in Reno. The dissent accepts verification costs, anonymity concerns and the possibility of circumvention; the privacy provision does not erase those effects. It nevertheless finds the remaining burden sufficiently fitted to a protection filtering does not supply by itself: children whose parents do not obtain or activate household controls receive the benefit of COPA's default restriction. The comparative position stated in the Playboy dissent gives that activation gap substantial weight while recognizing that filters reach foreign and non-Web sources and preserve adult choice.

In these Justices' assessment, the narrower statutory category and access routes, considered together with that additional protection, carry the Government's burden at this preliminary stage. They would remand for lawful disposition of other preserved grounds. Breyer's conclusion further depends on the national-community construction stated below; O'Connor and Scalia join the comparative reasoning without adopting that construction.

Justice Breyer additionally requires the national adult community to supply the governing community standard. His earlier Ashcroft position reads the phrase in statutory context and in light of the serious difficulty caused when restrictive local standards govern nationally distributed speech. Different jury applications of one national standard differ from different substantive standards. That construction is necessary to his conclusion that the narrower offense and access defenses sufficiently answer the filtering comparison. O'Connor and Scalia do not join this construction.

## Procedure After Action

The preliminary injunction remains continuously effective. The district court must proceed promptly under the complete statutory and comparative inquiry, identify material disputed updated facts and specify the scope and any genuine severance in its final order. A supported change to relief must preserve an effective cure for demonstrated speech burdens.
