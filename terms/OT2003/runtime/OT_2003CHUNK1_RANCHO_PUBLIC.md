## Event

Rancho Viejo, LLC v. Norton; D.C. Circuit No. 01-5373; judgment below reported at 323 F.3d 1062. Merits decision, February 5, 2004, October Term 2003.

On merits submission for review of the D.C. Circuit's April 1, 2003 judgment, rehearing denied July 22, 2003. The question is whether the Commerce Clause authorizes application of the Endangered Species Act's wildlife-taking prohibition to a commercial housing development threatening a species found entirely within one State. The proposed 202-acre San Diego County development threatens the arroyo toad. The circuit sustained the application by treating the commercial development as the regulated activity. Rancho Viejo seeks reversal and relief against the application; the federal officials seek affirmance.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate at submission and decision.

## Public Action

The Court affirms the D.C. Circuit's judgment sustaining the challenged application against the Commerce Clause objection, on the necessary-inclusion ground of National Association of Home Builders v. Babbitt.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Disposition:** Affirmed, 9–0, against the Commerce Clause challenge. The D.C. Circuit's judgment sustaining the challenged application of the taking prohibition stands on the necessary-inclusion ground, not on the circuit's commercial-project ground.

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The judgment sustaining the application against Rancho Viejo's Commerce Clause objection remains in effect, and Rancho Viejo's requested relief against the application is denied. The Government may enforce the taking prohibition in this application subject to its actual elements and exceptions. Whether the proposed development will cause a prohibited taking remains governed by the statute's injury and causation requirements, and any section 10 permission or other independently pending statutory issue retains its ordinary procedural posture.

## Holdings

### I. A commercial project does not replace the statutory taking class

**Controlling proposition:** The commercial character of a housing project does not by itself establish Congress's authority to apply the Endangered Species Act's wildlife-taking prohibition. The Government must identify an eligible interstate mechanism fitted to the taking class Congress enacted, including the habitat-caused injury that class reaches.

**Authority:** Part I of Kennedy's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Those seven Justices directly join this holding and its explanation; Stevens and Souter join Parts II and III and the judgment but not this part.

**Controlling explanation:** National Association of Home Builders v. Babbitt governs this question. Section 9 prohibits taking protected wildlife without making a project's commercial character the defining condition of the prohibition. United States v. Lopez requires a constitutionally sufficient connection for that enacted class before aggregation can measure its effects.

The D.C. Circuit relied on Rancho Viejo's commercial housing development as the regulated activity. The development is commercial, and the contrary view correctly identifies habitat conversion itself, rather than an incidental purchase, as the injury-producing conduct. But Home Builders already considered and rejected that feature as an independently sufficient connection for the enacted taking class. Describing the challenge as applied to this project does not remove the need to identify the mechanism that connects the prohibition to interstate commerce. The Court applies that existing rule.

Babbitt v. Sweet Home Chapter of Communities for a Great Oregon supplies the statutory treatment of habitat-caused injury. It does not supply the separate constitutional connection. Wickard v. Filburn and Perez v. United States permit class-wide assessment within properly connected economic classes; under Home Builders they do not make this project's commercial character a substitute for the required connection.

### II. The taking prohibition, as applied to the arroyo toad, is an integral part of the Act's valid interstate scheme

**Controlling proposition:** Under National Association of Home Builders v. Babbitt, Congress may regulate intrastate conduct that is neither itself interstate commerce nor independently shown to have substantial interstate effects when inclusion of the regulated class is a reasonably adapted and integral means of executing an otherwise valid federal regulation of interstate commerce. The Endangered Species Act's integrated system regulating interstate exploitation, movement and commercial disposition of listed wildlife may include the unauthorized taking of listed wildlife as a means of preserving the populations and enforceability of that system, without separate proof that each species crosses state boundaries, participates in interstate commerce or would independently produce a substantial interstate commercial effect. The section 9(a)(1)(B) taking prohibition, as applied to the listed arroyo toad within that system, falls within Congress's Commerce and Necessary and Proper powers; Rancho Viejo has not shown that the application is outside the statutory class, that the Act's interstate regulation is not independently valid, or that the relationship is nominal or pretextual.

**Authority:** Parts II and III of Kennedy's opinion of the Court are joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine Justices directly join this holding, its explanation and the affirmance.

**Controlling explanation:** Home Builders supplies the rule and its unit of analysis. The Commerce Clause, with the Necessary and Proper Clause, lets Congress select means reasonably adapted to the effective execution of a valid regulation of commerce among the States; it does not require every included intrastate act to be independently regulable. The unit is the taking prohibition within the federal scheme, not each species or local controversy.

The Act regulates the importation, exportation, interstate transport, sale and commercial handling of listed wildlife, the possession and shipment of unlawfully taken wildlife, the taking of listed wildlife and permissible incidental taking. The taking prohibition protects the populations whose exploitation and interstate movement those provisions govern and keeps the provisions enforceable. Home Builders sustained it as applied to the Delhi Sands flower-loving fly, a species found in one State and absent from trade.

That holding governs this application. The challenged application concerns habitat modification that section 9(a)(1)(B) reaches, as Sweet Home construes it, when the modification actually kills or injures listed wildlife. The Act's valid interstate regulation, the covered class and the protective function connecting them appear from the statute's operation, so the toad's intrastate range and the absence of an established market in it do not defeat the application. Rancho Viejo identifies no feature placing this application outside the class, no defect in the Act's interstate regulation and nothing nominal or pretextual in the relationship. United States v. Holston applied necessary inclusion to the reproducible supply of a prohibited interstate image market; this application rests on the Act's own wildlife scheme, not on a transferred market analogy.

The rule keeps an enforceable distinction between the commerce power and a general police power. Congress cannot establish the relationship by declaring a national objective or assembling unrelated provisions into one statute, and biodiversity's general importance, speculative scientific or future economic value, tourism and development pressure do not supply it. Aggregation measures magnitude only after a constitutionally eligible connection exists. Because necessary inclusion establishes congressional power here, Home Builders's independent externality route need not be satisfied.

### III. The judgment sustaining the challenged application is affirmed

**Controlling proposition:** When a judgment sustaining an application of the taking prohibition rests on the insufficient commercial-project ground but Home Builders's necessary-inclusion rule sustains the same application on the statute's text and structure, the judgment is affirmed on that ground, and no Commerce Clause evidentiary remand is required. The D.C. Circuit's judgment sustaining application of section 9 to the arroyo toad against Rancho Viejo's Commerce Clause objection is affirmed.

**Authority:** The same nine-Justice Parts II and III control the disposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court reviews the judgment, not only its reasoning. The commercial ground below cannot sustain the application, but the necessary-inclusion ground does, and it rests on the Act's text and structure and the record already before the Court. A further hearing on the local toad would leave the constitutional question unsettled without bearing on the relationship that sustains the application. Rancho Viejo's requested relief against the application is therefore denied.

Whether the proposed development will cause a prohibited taking remains governed by Sweet Home's statutory injury, proximate-cause and foreseeability requirements. Any section 10 permission and any independently pending statutory or permitting issue retain their own statutory conditions and ordinary procedural posture.

**Application and remedy:** The D.C. Circuit's judgment sustaining the challenged application is affirmed. The Government may enforce the taking prohibition in this application subject to its actual elements and exceptions.

## Precedent Treatment

- **National Association of Home Builders v. Babbitt, June 8, 1998:** Reaffirmed and applied. Its enacted taking class and rejection of project commerce as independently sufficient govern Part I. Its necessary-inclusion rule sustains the taking prohibition as applied to the listed toad within the Act's integrated system, without species-by-species, border-crossing or independent-effects proof, subject to its outside-class, invalid-regime and pretext showings, its identification rule, independent judicial determination of the legal boundaries and respect for reasonable congressional judgments. Its independent externality route remains available but need not be satisfied.
- **United States v. Lopez, April 26, 1995:** Applied. The constitutional connection precedes magnitude, must fit the enacted class and must leave an enforceable boundary; neither project commerce nor insertion of an unexpressed commerce element supplies the required inquiry.
- **United States v. Holston, October 30, 2003:** Applied for the necessary-inclusion method and distinguished on its actual image-supply relationship. Its demonstrated mechanism supplies no presumed wildlife market or ecological spillover; this application rests on the Act's integrated wildlife scheme.
- **United States v. Hale, February 12, 1996:** Preserved. Its machinegun holding supplies a different example of necessary inclusion within an identified valid interstate regime; no machinegun-specific premise is imported into conservation law.
- **United States v. Kirk, February 14, 1998:** Preserved for a fitted prohibited-stock relationship without a universal individual-sale requirement. Its specific stock relationship is not imported into conservation law; the relationship here lies in the Act's own scheme.
- **Wickard v. Filburn, 317 U.S. 111:** Preserved for aggregation within a properly connected economic class; it does not displace Home Builders's enacted-class rule.
- **Perez v. United States, 402 U.S. 146:** Preserved for class-wide assessment of connected economic conduct without a separately substantial effect from each act; it does not create a missing connection through this project's commerce.
- **Babbitt v. Sweet Home Chapter of Communities for a Great Oregon, June 29, 1995:** Preserved as the separate statutory rule. Significant habitat modification must actually kill or injure protected wildlife through significant impairment of essential behavior, including breeding, feeding or sheltering, with proximate causation and ordinary foreseeability. Speculative harm and bare but-for linkage are insufficient; an omission requires an independent legal duty, and otherwise lawful development alone is no exemption. Those statutory conditions remain distinct from constitutional authority and from any section 10 permission.

## Law After Decision

Effective February 5, 2004, Home Builders's necessary-inclusion rule sustains the Endangered Species Act's section 9(a)(1)(B) taking prohibition as applied to listed wildlife within the Act's integrated system, including an application to a commercial project threatening a species found only in one State, without separate proof that the species crosses state boundaries, participates in interstate commerce or independently produces a substantial interstate commercial effect. A challenger may show that an application is outside the statutory class, that the Act's interstate regulation is not independently valid, or that the relationship is nominal or pretextual. A project's commercial character does not itself supply the connection for the enacted taking class, and the independent externality route remains available on its own requirements.

The commercial-project rationale cannot alone sustain this application; the necessary-inclusion ground sustains it, and the judgment below stands. This decision applies the existing doctrine without creating a new Commerce category.

## Separate Writings

**Stevens, concurring in part and concurring in the judgment, joined by Souter.** They join Parts II and III and the judgment. The Court's necessary-inclusion ground sustains this application of the taking prohibition, as they agreed in Home Builders. They would also affirm on a further ground: the activity directly producing the alleged habitat injury is commercial housing construction and land conversion. Sweet Home identifies that physical habitat alteration as the conduct reached by the taking prohibition. Their additional ground therefore looks to the actual economic application, rather than to the developer's collateral equipment purchases or a generalized claim that species preservation benefits the national economy.

Wickard and Perez permit class-wide regulation of connected economic activity, and Hodel v. Virginia Surface Mining & Reclamation Association permits regulation of economic land use for environmental ends. On that reasoning, the actual construction supplies a sufficient economic connection without a market in the toad or a newly found ecological spillover. They accept the continuing statutory requirements of actual habitat-caused injury and ordinary causation; their additional ground concerns the Commerce Clause challenge.

Home Builders's contrary enacted-class holding directly obstructs this ground, and they do not join Part I. Stevens and Souter would reconsider that holding to the extent it excludes reliance on the actual injury-producing economic activity in this application. They regard treating the prohibition's broader coverage as a reason to disregard this commercial application as a mistaken class inquiry. They neither claim that their earlier dissent already controls nor distinguish Home Builders through a new factual premise. Their ground is an additional reason for the same judgment; it would not make every noneconomic taking an economic activity.

## Procedure After Action

The D.C. Circuit's judgment sustaining the challenged application is affirmed, and the taking prohibition remains enforceable in that application subject to its actual elements and exceptions. Any section 10 permission and any independently pending statutory or permitting issue retain their ordinary procedural posture. Supreme Court merits review of the Commerce Clause challenge is complete.
