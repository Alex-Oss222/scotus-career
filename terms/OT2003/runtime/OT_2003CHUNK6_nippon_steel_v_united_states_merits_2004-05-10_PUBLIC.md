## Event

Nippon Steel Corp. v. United States, on review of 337 F.3d 1373 (Federal Circuit, August 8, 2003). Decided May 10, 2004. The question is whether section 1677e(b) requires intentional concealment, willfulness or bad faith before Commerce may use adverse inferences for failure to respond to the best of an exporter’s ability. The Federal Circuit restored Commerce’s partial adverse-facts determination and separately addressed starting-price currency methodology and a record-exclusion regulation.

## Holdings

### Best ability measures available capability and effort, without an intent prerequisite

**Holding and operative rule:** Under the governing version of 19 U.S.C. §1677e(b), Commerce may draw an adverse inference in selecting otherwise available facts when substantial evidence shows that a respondent failed to use the effort within its actual ability to comply with an information request. Intentional concealment, willfulness or bad faith is not an additional required element; Commerce must identify supported capability and inadequate effort, because a deficient response alone does not establish that separate predicate.

**Authority:** Scalia’s unanimous opinion, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, controls this rule, the statutory qualifications and the application below.

**Controlling explanation:** The enacted phrase measures performance against ability. It requires the maximum effort the party can make under its actual capabilities, including reasonable inquiry into available records, without demanding perfection or information genuinely unavailable despite adequate cooperation. Requiring intent would substitute good faith for the separate ability-and-effort condition Congress enacted. Nippon’s concern that an honest error should not produce automatic punishment is answered by that distinct evidentiary predicate and the mandatory information safeguards.

Commerce twice requested theoretical-to-actual-weight conversion information under the December 21, 1998 and January 25, 1999 deadlines. Nippon first called conversion unnecessary and then said the weights were unavailable. Its later explanation admitted that the answering staff had not sought factory records. Commerce found those records routinely maintained, readily available and not burdensome to produce; the later production supported that stated finding. The conclusion therefore rests on available capability and omitted inquiry, rather than lateness or eventual production alone. The accelerated investigation and separate production and sales databases do not compel rejection of that supported finding.

The Court independently construes the statutory words and reviews the agency’s actual determination on the whole record under section 1516a(b)(1)(B)(i). Allentown Mack requires testing evidence under the actual announced standard; Boston & Maine forbids substituting court-invented agency reasons. Those requirements are met by Commerce’s own explanation here.

**Precedent treatment:** Allentown Mack’s whole-record discipline and Boston & Maine’s actual-reason requirement are applied. Ventura preserves agency responsibility for genuinely unresolved findings and delegated choices; Commerce already made the reviewed finding and exercised the adverse-inference choice.

### The distinct information safeguards remain conditions of lawful use

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1677e(a) requires otherwise available facts when necessary information is absent or a party withholds requested information, misses the requested deadline or form, significantly impedes the proceeding, or supplies unverifiable information, subject to section 1677m(d); the deadline/form branch separately preserves section 1677m(c)(1) and (e). Section 1677e(b)’s supported noncooperation finding permits rather than compels an adverse inference, and section 1677e(c) requires practicable corroboration of secondary information from reasonably available independent sources.

**Authority:** The same unanimous opinion adopts these qualifications as part of the operative rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The provisions impose distinct commands. Under section 1677m(c)(1), a party promptly explaining inability to comply in the requested form and manner, with suggested alternatives, triggers consideration of its ability; the agency may modify requirements as needed to avoid an unreasonable burden. Subsection (c)(2) requires attention to difficulties, particularly those of small companies, and practicable assistance. Under subsection (d), the agency must promptly identify a response’s deficiency and, as practicable within investigation limits, permit explanation or correction. It may disregard an unsatisfactory or late further response subject to subsection (e).

Subsection (e) requires consideration of necessary information notwithstanding unmet requirements when all five conditions hold: submission by the established deadline, verifiability, sufficient completeness to provide a reliable basis, demonstrated best ability in providing information and meeting its requirements, and usability without undue difficulty. Subsection (f) separately requires, to the extent practicable, a written explanation for refusing information.

Nippon’s invocation of a general preverification submission opportunity does not replace the specific questionnaire deadlines. Entirely missing conversion data differ from a minor correction, and subsection (e) does not require acceptance merely because data arrive before verification. The repeated requests, available-records findings and actual explanation sustain the reviewed partial adverse-facts determination under these preserved requirements. The statute’s permission to disregard deficient material remains a permission, rather than a command to reject every correction.

**Precedent treatment:** The statutory text supplies these distinct conditions. The actual-authority and reasoned-exercise distinctions preserved in the Court’s administrative precedents remain applicable; the adverse-inference holding supplies no independent currency-valuation rule.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Allentown Mack Sales & Service, Inc. v. NLRB:** Applied to assess whole-record support under the agency’s actual governing standard.
- **National Railroad Passenger Corp. v. Boston & Maine Corp.:** Applied to Commerce’s own discernible reasons; the reviewing court supplies no substitute factual justification.
- **INS v. Ventura:** Preserves the agency’s responsibility for genuinely unresolved findings and delegated calculations. It does not require repetition of this completed adverse-inference decision, and it continues to govern work required by the surviving currency-methodology mandate.
- **United States v. Mead Corp. and United States v. Haggar Apparel Co.:** Their actual-authority and statutory-limit distinctions remain intact; independent construction of the best-ability words supplies the rule here.

## Law After Decision

Commerce may use an adverse inference for a supported avoidable failure to use available capability even without intentional misconduct. Missing information and failure to cooperate remain separate predicates, with permissive inference, practicable corroboration and the distinct section 1677m duties governing the resulting calculation. The presented record sustains the partial adverse-facts treatment. The separate currency methodology lies outside the submitted question and remains governed by the existing mandate.
