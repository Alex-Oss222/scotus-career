## Event

**Sabri v. United States, No. 03-44.** Argued March 3, 2004. Decided May 17, 2004, on review of the Eighth Circuit's reinstatement of the indictment, 326 F.3d 937. The question is whether Congress may prohibit bribery of a federally assisted entity's agents without requiring proof that each bribe affected particular federal dollars.

The indictment alleges three corrupt offers to a Minneapolis council member concerning development business, together with qualifying federal assistance to the city and development agency. The dismissal rested on a facial constitutional objection to the absence of a dollar-tracing element. The allegations remain for trial.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated in the decision.

## Public Action

Affirmed, 9–0; the indictment proceeds to trial.

**Affirmed, 9–0. Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The Eighth Circuit's judgment reinstating the indictment stands. The prosecution returns for trial under the enacted elements and independently preserved defenses.

## Holdings

### Congress may protect the actual assisted institution without tracing individual dollars

**Holding and operative rule:** The Spending and Necessary and Proper Clauses permit Congress to prohibit corrupt payments directed at the agents and substantial transactions of an entity receiving genuine federal assistance, without tracing the payment to particular federal dollars. Section 666's lack of a traced-dollar element does not make that assisted-entity bribery mechanism facially invalid; actual benefits, agency, covered business or transactions and corrupt intent remain necessary.

**Authority:** Kennedy's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; eight Justices directly adopt this rule. Thomas agrees with the judgment on the distinct ground described in his concurrence.

**Controlling explanation:** McCulloch v. Maryland permits appropriate means of carrying an enumerated power into execution. A bribe that corrupts an assisted institution's authorized agent can distort the institution's use of resources and its administration of federally supported work. That operational relationship permits preventive protection of the institution. The fungibility of its money reinforces the relationship but does not replace it. Salinas supplies the enacted offense's absence of a demonstrable federal-dollar effect requirement; Fischer supplies the separate requirement of actual programmatic assistance rather than ordinary commercial receipts.

The objection that a modest grant would authorize general federal policing overlooks the class Congress regulated: intentional corruption of actual recipients' agents in substantial recipient business. Geographic proximity to federal spending or receipt of ordinary procurement revenue is insufficient. Lopez and Morrison require an identified enumerated-power connection rather than generalized downstream consequences. Here the connection is protection of actual federal expenditure and its recipient institution, not an assertion that every local offense affects the national economy. Dole and Kansas govern conditions attached to a recipient's funding choice; they do not restrict Congress to a grant condition when it directly prohibits third-party bribery threatening an assisted institution.

The pleaded assistance, agency and development-business relationship permits rejection of this facial tracing objection. A materially different prosecution claimed to lack that institutional relationship requires its concrete facts and lies outside this pretrial record.

### Every enacted element and the compensation exception retain their force

**Holding and operative rule:** The Government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt corrupt giving, offering or agreement to give a thing of value, intent to influence or reward a covered agent, covered institutional business or a transaction or series involving at least $5,000, and more than $10,000 in qualifying federal benefits during the statutory one-year period. Section 666(c) preserves bona fide salary, wages, fees and other compensation paid, and expenses paid or reimbursed, in the usual course of business.

**Authority:** The same eight-Justice opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** The transaction-value threshold and the amount of the bribe are different requirements. The continuous one-year benefits period may begin up to twelve months before the offense or end up to twelve months after it and may straddle the offense. Fischer's genuine-assistance rule gives the benefits condition substantive work; a lawful reimbursement is distinct from the corrupt transaction charged. Salinas preserves the other statutory elements while rejecting traced federal loss as an addition. The constitutional ruling leaves those conditions for trial on the indictment.

**Material precedent treatment:** Salinas's statutory construction is applied and the presented constitutional question is now resolved. Fischer's assistance and compensation limits remain governing; McCulloch supplies the appropriate-means framework.

## Precedent Treatment

- **McCulloch v. Maryland, 4 Wheat. 316:** Applied to protection of an identified federal spending undertaking through appropriate preventive means.
- **Salinas v. United States:** Applied on statutory meaning; the constitutional question about the enacted no-tracing mechanism is resolved on the actual institution-and-agent relationship.
- **Fischer v. United States:** Applied to require genuine programmatic benefits and preserve the bona fide compensation and ordinary-course expense exception.
- **United States v. Lopez; United States v. Morrison:** Applied to require an actual enumerated-power connection; generalized economic consequences do not supply it.
- **South Dakota v. Dole; Kansas v. United States:** Distinguished as funding-condition decisions, while this prohibition directly regulates third-party bribery protecting an assisted institution.

## Law After Decision

Section 666's assisted-entity bribery prohibition has a sufficient Spending and Necessary and Proper connection without individual federal-dollar tracing. Prosecution remains tied to genuine assistance, the recipient's agent and business, substantial transaction value and corrupt intent. Salinas's rejection of an added effects element and Fischer's limits operate together.

## Separate Writings

**Thomas, concurring in the judgment.** Thomas concludes that the pleaded bribery of an agent administering the directly assisted institution's development business is plainly adapted to protecting that federal undertaking. The facial claim that an express dollar-tracing element is indispensable therefore fails. He requires an evident protective relationship, and declines to treat any rational association between incidental federal receipts and local integrity as sufficient.

His reasoning applies McCulloch's means-to-power inquiry at the narrower scope of this indictment. The identifiable-undertaking and genuine-functional-relationship analysis applied in Lopez and Rancho Viejo supports this direct protective application; it does not add a statutory nexus element or dispense with proof at trial. Salinas remains a construction of the enacted elements. Thomas agrees that the case may proceed while withholding assent from the Court's broader institutional rule for all covered transactions.

## Procedure After Action

The district court must permit the prosecution to proceed, instruct on the actual benefits, agency, transaction-value and corrupt-intent requirements, and apply §666(c)'s exception when implicated. The Government bears the criminal burden of proof. Independently preserved defenses receive ordinary adjudication.
