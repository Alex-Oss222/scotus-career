## Event

Raymond B. Yates, M.D., P.C. Profit Sharing Plan v. Hendon, No. 02-458; merits. Argued January 13, 2004; decided March 2, 2004. On certiorari to the Sixth Circuit. Yates, the working owner of a professional corporation, participated in its plan with genuine nonowner employees and repaid a plan loan shortly before bankruptcy. The court below denied participant status because of ownership. The question is whether ownership categorically prevents participation in an otherwise covered employee plan.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated.

## Public Action

The categorical owner-exclusion judgment is vacated and the case remanded.

## Holdings

**Controlling proposition:** A working owner may hold both employer and employee-beneficiary capacities and qualify as an ERISA participant in a pension plan that also covers one or more genuine employees other than the owner and spouse. The owner-only exclusion determines whether an employee plan exists; it does not categorically expel a working owner from an otherwise covered plan. The claimant must establish participant and covered-plan predicates and the independent requirements for any anti-alienation or bankruptcy protection claimed.

**Authority:** The Opinion of the Court is joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Scalia's concurrence in the judgment directly adopts the same operative coverage rule through the official agency interpretation, giving that rule eight direct supporters; he does not join the seven's independent statutory rationale. Thomas agrees that categorical ownership exclusion is wrong but would require an additional common-law classification inquiry.

**Controlling explanation:** ERISA's integrated provisions recognize overlapping capacities. Section 1108(c)(1) permits a fiduciary's participant benefit when computed and paid consistently with the plan as applied to all other participants and beneficiaries; subsection (c)(3) recognizes fiduciary service alongside service for a party in interest. Subsection (d) separately restricts owner-employee transactions. These provisions regulate the risks of dual capacities rather than declare them impossible.

The regulation likewise defines employee-plan coverage. Section 2510.3-3 contrasts owner-only arrangements with plans covering common-law employees as well as self-employed individuals, and confines its owner exclusion to that section's purpose. Its boundary remains effective without excluding an owner from an existing employee plan. Darden's common-law agency inquiry supplies a default when the statute otherwise leaves employee status unresolved; it does not displace ERISA's specific structure. Clackamas also requires attention to the particular statute, rather than treating ownership as a talisman.

Yates's plan covers genuine nonowner employees. His ownership therefore cannot sustain the categorical exclusion adopted below. Patterson v. Shumate protects a beneficial trust interest only when an enforceable transfer restriction satisfies the applicable conditions. The remand must determine the claimed protection of the repayment and the remaining plan, anti-alienation and bankruptcy questions, giving effect to the unchallenged preference determination and the independent conditions of the asserted protection.

## Precedent Treatment

Nationwide Mutual Insurance Co. v. Darden retains its default common-law relationship inquiry; the specific ERISA structure resolves the dual-capacity question first. Clackamas Gastroenterology Associates v. Wells retains its statute-specific classification rule and does not impose categorical exclusion here. Patterson v. Shumate retains its protection for a qualifying beneficial trust interest subject to an enforceable restriction; participant status does not itself establish every requirement of that protection.

## Law After Decision

Working ownership is compatible with participation in a plan covering genuine nonowner employees. Owner-only arrangements retain their distinct regulatory treatment. Courts must separately apply the requirements for plan coverage, participation, enforceable anti-alienation protection and the bankruptcy treatment of the actual transaction.

## Separate Writings

Scalia concurs in the judgment. The Department of Labor's official interpretation permitting working-owner participation is reasonable within ERISA's structure and governs under his approach to authoritative agency interpretation. That ground supplies the same operative coverage result without independently declaring the judicial construction the only permissible reading. He agrees that the remaining qualification and transaction questions must be decided separately.

Thomas concurs in the judgment setting aside categorical exclusion. In his view, provisions regulating benefits or transactions for some owner-employees do not establish that every working owner in the proposed category is an employee. Darden's common-law relationship inquiry remains necessary because those structural indications do not fully resolve classification. Ownership alone is insufficient to disqualify Yates, and corporate separateness may support employee status, but Thomas would require the lower court to decide that question first and then address the remaining statutory and bankruptcy conditions.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns for application of the participation holding and adjudication of the remaining claimed protection of the repayment. The established employee coverage and unchallenged preference finding are part of that posture.
