The operative instrument chain matters:

- Article Seventh of the 1785 Compact secured adjoining shore ownership, its benefits and the privilege of wharves and other improvements, subject to nonobstruction and noninjury of navigation. Its common-fishing entitlement and the separate hindrance/disturbance and nets/seines restrictions are distinct provisions.
- Article Eighth required mutual agreement for its specified fish-preservation, quarantine, channel, navigation and ballast regulations. That historical allocation informs the bargain; it is not an unlimited present-day mutual-consent rule for every regulatory subject.
- The 1877 Award, approved in every part by Congress in 1879, fixed Virginia's boundary at low water and reserved Virginia's use beyond that line necessary to full enjoyment of riparian ownership. That use may neither impede navigation nor otherwise interfere with Maryland's proper use. Congress preserved federal jurisdiction.
- The 1958 Compact, approved by Congress in 1962, replaced the former Compact after its prescribed effective-date condition. Article VII section 1 nevertheless preserved the existing shore-owner rights without diminution, enlargement or other alteration and gave full force to judicial constructions of the relevant 1785 provision. The preserved rights therefore survive through that savings clause and the Award; the entire 1785 Compact is not simply still independently operative.

Article VIII prohibits unilateral alteration of the new agreement. Congress's consent reserves federal rights and jurisdiction, its constitutional commerce power and its right to alter the consent resolution.

## Competing legal constructions

**V1 — reserved use free of unilateral licensing.** Maryland's riverbed title and Virginia's protected use beyond the boundary coexist. The Award names Virginia and reserves use essential to full enjoyment of its riparian ownership as part of an interstate settlement; the protected shore-owner rights extend to Virginia, its subdivisions and citizens exercising appurtenant rights. Conditioning those uses on Maryland's discretionary prior permission would make the express reservation subject to the other party's unilateral veto. The navigation and proper-use conditions specify real substantive limits, but do not allocate Maryland sole authority to determine Virginia's needs or make its permission the source of the right. The historically mutual regulation provisions reinforce the distinction between title and all-purpose unilateral control. The 1958 savings clause preserves, rather than recreates or enlarges, that allocation.

**M1 — protected use subject to compatible regulation.** The strongest contrary construction treats the rights as enforceable riparian entitlements on Maryland's side of a sovereign boundary, rather than a transfer of regulatory jurisdiction. The instruments speak of ownership, privileges and use, and expressly condition the use on navigation and Maryland's proper enjoyment; they do not expressly exempt it from Maryland's otherwise reasonable police-power administration. Protecting the entitlement against a prohibitory or incompatible permit decision can give the reservation effect without eliminating advance review of whether a proposed use satisfies its limits. Article Eighth's specific mutual-consent categories demonstrate that the parties knew how to allocate regulatory authority expressly. The 1958 savings clause preserves existing rights, so it does not resolve that original meaning by creating an exemption.

**Strongest objection to V1.** Private property rights ordinarily coexist with public regulation, and a licensing procedure need not be a veto if constrained by the superior compact right. The answer supporting V1 is the interstate character of the Award's express reservation to Virginia: its purpose is not just to create a revocable benefit within Maryland's unilateral licensing system. Maryland can enforce the retained limits through lawful interstate and federal channels, so immunity from its prior permit requirement does not erase those limits.

**Strongest objection to M1.** A permit regime that lets Maryland decide whether Virginia needs an improvement subordinates a sovereign reservation to the other State and can defeat the bargain in practice. The answer supporting M1 is that the entitlement is superior law constraining every permit decision: Maryland cannot withhold a protected reasonable use merely to suppress Virginia's growth or obtain bargaining power. Judicial protection against such interference remains available. The alternative supports compatible regulation, not the unrestricted discretionary regime Virginia describes.

**A1 — acquiescence not established.** Maryland has not shown that Virginia surrendered an instrument-secured exemption, if V1 correctly identifies one. New Jersey requires supported adverse exercise and knowledge or sufficiently open conduct supporting notice, with acquiescence; it does not turn routine cooperation into automatic displacement of a sovereign entitlement. Earlier permit applications are relevant, but their significance must be considered with official knowledge, protest and the first actual refusal. The supported course contains protests and contested control rather than a sufficiently established accepted surrender. No fixed prescriptive period, mandatory formal-protest rule or categorical bar to acquiring rights by prescription is created. New Jersey's precise territorial-acquisition burden is not mechanically converted into a new universal burden for every regulatory-right dispute.

The stronger opposing acquiescence inference combines repeated applications, receipt of permits, successive amendments and official knowledge into practical acceptance of Maryland's authority. Its weakness is that cooperating to obtain a project while contesting sovereign authority is not the same as conceding the claimed entitlement, and the supported protests prevent treating this record as an unbroken settled construction. A disposition based on some additional episode of accepted adverse control would require that actual episode's source; its absence from the extract is not evidence that no such document exists.

## Judgment form and operative limits

For the V1/A1 commitments, overrule Maryland's two exceptions, adopt the Master's recommendation on the controversy presented, and enter declaratory relief that Virginia, its subdivisions and citizens may exercise the protected withdrawals and appurtenant improvements without obtaining the challenged Maryland permits. The declaration must state that exercise remains limited by noninterference with navigation and Maryland's proper use, with federal rights and jurisdiction preserved. It resolves unilateral licensing authority, not the volume of every future withdrawal. Appropriate original-jurisdiction relief remains available to vindicate the interstate obligations; no unrequested quantitative decree is invented.

For Stevens and Kennedy, sustain the regulatory exception, reject the categorical no-permit declaration, and recognize Maryland's authority to conduct reasonable permitting that gives full effect to the federally protected riparian uses. They agree that the supplied history establishes no independent surrender of a right but reach a different construction of the right. Their account must explain that a protected use cannot be denied simply because Maryland would prefer less development in Virginia; the permit authority is bounded by the superior instruments. The eventual permit leaves no supplied request for this Court to adjudicate an independent project-specific damages or quantitative claim.

These are distinct constructions, not a broad/narrow logical-subset pair yielding a single rule. Shared acceptance of navigation and proper-use limits does not merge opposite positions on prior permitting. Stevens and Kennedy require a substantive separate-position explanation if their construction is not controlling; the other six require the controlling explanation to answer the regulation-versus-entitlement objection rather than rest on river ownership alone.
