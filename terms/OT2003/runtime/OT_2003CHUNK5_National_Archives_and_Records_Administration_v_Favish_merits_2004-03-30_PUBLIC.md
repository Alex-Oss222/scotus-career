## Event

**Case:** National Archives and Records Administration v. Favish, No. 02-954.
**Event:** Merits decision, March 30, 2004. Argued December 3, 2003.
**Route and judgment below:** On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, which required disclosure of four photographs following the district court's privacy and public-interest proceedings.
**Questions:** Whether FOIA Exemption 7(C) protects surviving relatives' privacy in death-scene images and what evidentiary foundation and governmental-transparency contribution are required for this misconduct-based disclosure demand.
**Posture:** The four images depict Vincent Foster's death scene. Relatives presented their own privacy evidence, and Favish presented documentary criticisms and image-specific hypotheses concerning the official investigation. The Office of Independent Counsel transferred the records to the National Archives on March 23, 2004, and the custodian was substituted before decision.

## Participation

Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the four-photograph disclosure judgment and remands for summary judgment permitting withholding of the protected depictions, with mandatory segregation of any reasonably separable nonexempt material.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 9-0.**
**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.
The judgment permits withholding of the protected material in the four disputed photographs. Complete withholding requires the ordinary determination that no reasonably segregable nonexempt material remains; any such material must be disclosed.

## Holdings

### I. Living-family privacy and the asserted public interest

**Holding and operative rule:** Exemption 7(C) permits withholding law-enforcement material whose disclosure could reasonably be expected to constitute an unwarranted invasion of surviving relatives' own privacy through dissemination of a close family member's death images. The agency must establish the law-enforcement and privacy predicates and ultimately justify withholding; when the asserted public benefit is exposing official misconduct or a meaningful investigative deficiency, the requester must identify a significant governmental-transparency interest, supply an objective foundation warranting a reasonable belief that such a deficiency might have occurred, and show a plausible connection between the requested material and that issue, without first proving misconduct itself.

**Authority:** Part I of Stevens's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, establishes the unanimous rule. All nine also join Part II's application below.

**Controlling explanation:** The relatives assert their own interest against intrusive public dissemination, supported by declarations describing distress and unwanted publicity. That interest fits personal privacy under 7(C) without inheritance of the deceased's rights or a family veto. Law-enforcement compilation is undisputed here. The statutory standard is 7(C)'s reasonable expectation of an unwarranted invasion, distinct from Exemption 6's clearly-unwarranted standard.

Ray requires concrete privacy and demonstrated incremental transparency. Bibles and Reporters Committee focus the public-interest inquiry on what disclosure contributes to understanding government conduct. A requester cannot give substantial public-interest weight to a bare accusation merely by requesting sensitive images. But requiring established wrongdoing would withhold evidence because the requester has not yet proved the very deficiency under investigation. A reasonable evidentiary foundation and plausible image-specific connection avoid that circular demand while permitting the actual balance. Other demonstrated transparency purposes receive their own analysis. The agency retains persuasion throughout.

FLRA preserves an interest in controlled official dissemination despite scattered public exposure. Unauthorized publication of a degraded gun-hand image therefore does not itself waive the family's remaining interest in official release of the original. FLRA also distinguishes permission to withhold under FOIA from a separate legal prohibition on disclosure.

**Precedent treatment:** Ray's privacy and incremental-value rule is extended to living relatives' death-image interests; Bibles and Reporters Committee supply the governmental-transparency inquiry. FLRA's dissemination and permission-versus-prohibition distinctions are applied.

### II. These four protected depictions may be withheld

**Holding and operative rule:** On this record, the demonstrated family intrusion outweighs the sufficiently supported incremental governmental-transparency value of the four disputed death-scene depictions. The custodian is entitled to summary judgment permitting withholding of their protected material under Exemption 7(C), subject to the ordinary segregability requirement stated in Part III.

**Authority:** Part II of Stevens's opinion, joined by all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Favish supplies documents and specific hypotheses. His gun-hand argument concerns thumb entrapment, alleged backspatter, the gun's location beneath the leg and the superior detail of an original over a leaked copy. His shoulder-image argument concerns blood transfer, possible later flow and an asserted investigative omission. Those are the closest connections between the requested images and claimed deficiencies, and the Court evaluates them as such.

The disclosed report addresses the thumb indentation and trigger position, laboratory residue, high-velocity spatter, transfer to the face and possible head movement while checking vital signs. It also exposes limitations, including possible contamination of trace evidence through common packaging of clothing. These explanations show what investigators examined; their official source does not make every inference conclusive. Favish's blood-sequence criticism and the possibility of a clearer independent visual assessment are considered against that disclosed work. On the supplied evidence, the additional contribution of these photographs to identifying a meaningful investigative failure remains insufficient to outweigh the concrete family intrusion.

Alleged changes to the Haut report, gun-identification statements, autopsy attendance, x-ray records and vehicle observations also question the investigation. Their relevance depends on the contribution of these four images: death-scene photographs do not resolve who attended the later autopsy, which version of a report was altered or which vehicle witnesses observed. The other two photographs have a more general asserted contribution. The governing balance thus permits withholding the protected depictions after consideration of the actual evidence, rather than from the number of official investigations or a presumption of official infallibility. The Court resolves the disclosure demand without adjudicating the underlying cause of death.

**Precedent treatment:** Ray and Bibles are applied to the actual additional agency transparency these records would provide; Center for National Security Studies preserves independent assessment and the agency's ultimate burden under the particular exemption.

### III. Reasonably segregable nonexempt material must be released

**Holding and operative rule:** Before complete withholding of a photograph is implemented, the agency must establish that no reasonably segregable nonexempt material remains; any such material must be disclosed after exempt portions are removed. Photographic form creates no exception, and the requester need not prove that a nonexempt portion would be useful or would independently advance the asserted theory.

**Authority:** Part III of Stevens's opinion, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies eight direct joins to the complete rule and its application. Stone-Zsela concurs in this component's judgment without joining its rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 552(b) makes disclosure of reasonably segregable nonexempt material mandatory, with the agency retaining its withholding burden. Center for National Security Studies requires that determination under the actual exemption and record. The conclusion that protected death depictions may be withheld does not establish that every part of every photograph is exempt. The agency and reviewing court must make the ordinary segregation determination before complete withholding. A crop that continues to depict protected remains retains the privacy analysis applicable to that content even if it omits the face. Favish's particular neck-only example concerns an additional photograph outside this four-image review.

**Precedent treatment:** Center for National Security Studies and Landano are applied to mandatory, actual segregation; the same duty applies to photographs as to other agency records.

## Precedent Treatment

United States Department of State v. Ray supplies concrete privacy and incremental-transparency balancing while preserving the possibility of a supported derivative inquiry. Bibles v. Oregon Natural Desert Association and Department of Justice v. Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press supply the governmental-conduct focus; Exemption 7(C)'s own threshold governs. Department of Defense v. Federal Labor Relations Authority preserves controlled official dissemination and the distinction between FOIA permission and an independent disclosure prohibition. Center for National Security Studies v. United States Department of Justice and United States Department of Justice v. Landano preserve agency persuasion and mandatory segregation under the applicable exemption.

## Law After Decision

Exemption 7(C) protects living relatives' demonstrated privacy against unwarranted disclosure of death images. A misconduct-based disclosure claim requires a reasonable evidentiary foundation and plausible connection to the requested material, assessed with the actual evidence and agency burden intact. The rule permits withholding of these protected depictions while requiring disclosure of reasonably segregable nonexempt portions; other demonstrated transparency purposes retain their own analysis.
