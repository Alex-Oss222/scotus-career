* **Citation or Docket:** No. 03-107; 541 U.S. 193.
* **October Term:** 2003.
* **Entry Stage and Event:** Supreme Court merits review, designated MERITS.
* **Simulated Event Date:** 2004-04-19
* **Question Presented:** Whether Congress may recognize and restore a tribe’s inherent criminal jurisdiction over nonmember Indians, so that a subsequent federal prosecution is not barred by double jeopardy.
* **Question Granted:** The question presented above; review is confined to the preserved issues and judgment identified here.
* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** Eighth Circuit en banc, treating the tribal prosecution as an exercise of delegated federal power and barring the subsequent federal prosecution.
* **Relief Requested:** The United States seeks reversal and permission to proceed on its federal assault charge.
* **Material Record:** Lara, an Indian belonging to another tribe, was prosecuted by the Spirit Lake Tribe for conduct involving an officer on the reservation and later faced a federal prosecution arising from the same incident. The statutory source of the tribal prosecution is decisive; the Court is not reviewing the tribal conviction itself.
* **Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** The preserved defense is double jeopardy. A due-process attack on the tribal proceeding is not established as an issue presented for decision.
* **Relevant Dates:** Congress amended the Indian Civil Rights Act after Duro v. Reina; the amendment was in force before the prosecutions.

### Issue 1: Restoration of inherent authority and separate sovereigns

* **Controlling Law:** the tribal cases require source-specific attention to sovereign authority and do not establish that all tribal power derives from federal delegation. No controlling decision resolves this statutory restoration.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** Identify the sovereign source of the prosecutorial power, keeping substantive jurisdiction, individual procedural rights and double jeopardy separate.
* **Other Governing Law:** Indian Commerce Clause, Necessary and Proper Clause, Fifth Amendment Double Jeopardy Clause, and 25 U.S.C. §1301(2). The post-Duro amendment explicitly recognizes inherent jurisdiction over all Indians; its response to that judicial rule must be acknowledged.
* **Material Other Authority:** United States v. Wheeler distinguishes inherent tribal power from federal delegation. Duro v. Reina supplies the displaced common-law limitation; its precise surviving scope must not be assumed to be a constitutional prohibition.
* **Existing Rule's Reach:** Separate sovereigns may prosecute offenses deriving from independent sovereign authority. Congress’s power to lift this restriction remains the necessary antecedent.
* **Open Point:** Whether the statutory amendment restores inherent authority or necessarily delegates federal prosecutorial power.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

* **Reasoning below:** The Eighth Circuit treated the additional tribal authority as federal in source, triggering the same-sovereign bar.
* **Petitioner or appellant:** Congress may alter a federal restriction on retained tribal sovereignty; the tribal offense therefore remains an act of another sovereign.
* **Respondent or appellee:** Congress cannot relabel power the judiciary has found absent; newly supplied federal authority cannot support successive prosecution as independent tribal power.
* **Alternative or unreached grounds:** Individual due-process or equal-protection objections to tribal jurisdiction require preserved claims and the necessary record; they do not automatically follow from resolving source.
* **Concessions and limiting principles:** No unverified oral-argument concession is used.
* **Solicitor General or material amicus position:** The United States presents the restoration-of-inherent-power theory and seeks reinstatement of the federal prosecution.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

**Plausible holding scope and consequences:** Treating the amendment as a valid restoration reverses the double-jeopardy dismissal and permits federal prosecution, without finding guilt. Treating it as delegation affirms the bar. A ruling limited to nonmember Indians on reservation conduct need not decide tribal prosecution of non-Indians or constitutional adequacy of a particular tribal trial.
