## Event

**Case:** ACLU of New Jersey v. County of Hudson, Appellate Division No. A-4100-01; 352 N.J. Super. 44, 799 A.2d 629.

**Event:** Merits decision, April 13, 2004. On writ of certiorari to the Superior Court of New Jersey, Appellate Division; submitted on the merits. The June 12, 2002 judgment reversed the order requiring public disclosure of federal detainee information. The question is whether authorized federal detainee-confidentiality rules displace conflicting state disclosure requirements in voluntarily accepted county custody arrangements and whether those arrangements commandeer county government.

The counties housed federal immigration detainees under voluntary paid agreements. The interim regulation stated an April 17, 2002 effective date and was published April 22, while the disclosure litigation was pending. The identical text was adopted as final on January 29, 2003. The common-law-access dismissal was not appealed and remains outside this review.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at submission and decision.

## Public Action

The Court affirms the appellate judgment sustaining nondisclosure of the information covered by the federal custody rule.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The reversal of the disclosure order remains in place for information within 8 C.F.R. §236.6. The ACLU’s request to invalidate that federal withholding ground or compel release of the covered information is denied. The unappealed common-law-access dismissal remains intact. The mandate returns the case for ordinary implementation of the affirmed judgment.

## Holdings

**Common authority:** The unanimous opinion of the Court supplies nine direct joins to every holding below. The separate notice-and-comment and effectiveness requirements within Holding II are each supported by all nine Justices.

### I. The enacted detention-and-records authority supports this confined confidentiality rule

**Holding and operative rule:** The immigration-administration, Service-records, implementing-regulation and paid nonfederal-custody provisions of 8 U.S.C. §1103(a)(1), (2), (3), (9)(A) and (c), as governing the promulgation, authorize federal control of public disclosure of detainee information acquired through the covered federal custody function. Section 236.6 validly channels that disclosure through applicable federal laws, regulations and executive orders; a voluntary contract alone supplies neither delegated authority nor a power over unrelated county records.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court begins with Congress’s assignment. Section 1103 combines authority over immigration administration and Service records with authority to arrange and pay for nonfederal housing, care and security of persons detained under federal law. Necessary implementing regulations may preserve control of information acquired through that federal function. The Commissioner acts through the Attorney General’s delegated authority under subsection (c). Subsection (a)(1)’s exceptions for powers assigned to the President, Secretary of State, State Department officers and diplomatic or consular officers remain part of that assignment.

Haggar requires the Court to enforce statutory boundaries before accepting a reasonable delegated choice. Mead requires actual authority to act with the force of law, exercised through the relevant mechanism. The combined custody-and-records provisions supply that authority here. Neither the agency’s importance nor its use of rulemaking form supplies the missing premise by itself. Nor does the Attorney General’s legal-ruling proviso displace independent judicial examination of the delegation.

The rule reaches public and private custodians housing, maintaining, serving or otherwise holding detainees on the Service’s behalf, and persons acquiring detainee information through an official or contractual relationship with those custodians. Its public-record restriction applies insofar as records contain the covered information. Federal disclosure law continues to govern release; the rule channels requests rather than deciding every request against disclosure. Detainees retain their ability to identify themselves, communicate and seek counsel under the applicable law. This confined operation answers the objection that contracting permits unlimited local secrecy: the legal source and the federal custody connection, rather than the contract alone, define the authority.

### II. The published interim reasons separately satisfy both APA good-cause requirements

**Holding and operative rule:** Except when a statute requires notice or a hearing, §553(b)(B) permits omission of advance notice and public procedure when the agency finds and briefly explains in the issued rule that such procedure is impracticable, unnecessary or contrary to the public interest. The interim notice’s identified risk of irreversible disclosures satisfies the public-interest ground here; independently, its published explanation establishes good cause under §553(d)(3) to dispense with the ordinary thirty-day period before effectiveness for the public-release demand presented in this case.

**Controlling explanation:** The two exceptions perform different work. The first permits the agency to act before public participation; the second concerns the period before a substantive rule takes effect. Satisfaction of one does not establish the other.

The interim notice explained that disclosure through dispersed custodians could expose investigative methods, sources, witnesses and the direction and scope of investigations. It identified possible intimidation, harm, retaliation against detainees and associates, and impaired cooperation during the continuing emergency. These are the agency’s published predictions, not findings that every detainee possesses sensitive information. They support immediate federal management of release under the governing federal disclosure law.

Advance public procedure would permit the identified irreversible disclosures before that control could operate. A further thirty-day effectiveness interval would independently allow the same loss and defeat the rule’s protective function. Those distinct consequences satisfy the separate statutory requirements.

The contrary argument rightly rejects a general emergency exception, particularly months after the attacks and for a rule covering immigration detainees beyond a single investigation. An inconvenient disclosure suit does not itself supply good cause. Here the justification is the particular risk of irreversible disclosure before the responsible federal authority can apply federal law. Litigation makes the timing concrete; it does not replace the statutory showing. The demanded relief is a future public release after promulgation, so the Court applies the exceptions to that demand while preserving the distinction between the stated April 17 date and April 22 publication.

### III. Regulation of information from voluntarily accepted federal custody does not commandeer the counties

**Holding and operative rule:** A valid federal confidentiality rule may govern information acquired through a county’s voluntarily accepted, federally paid immigration-custody operation without compelling the county to administer federal regulation against unrelated private persons. The rule’s obligation follows that custody function and its resulting information, and the personnel work needed for compliance does not convert it into prohibited conscription.

**Controlling explanation:** Condon distinguishes direct regulation of a State’s own handling of information from assigning it legislation or a regulatory program against others. Section 236.6 governs the counties’ handling of information from the federal custody work they accepted, and also governs private custodians and related information holders. Federal responsibility for disclosure remains identifiable.

New York prevents official assent from enlarging federal authority. The Court therefore resolves actual delegation and procedure independently of the counties’ agreements. Printz concerns affirmative assistance demanded of an unwilling office; its confined source-and-function rule does not make voluntary paid custody forbidden. Lomont requires genuine voluntariness and distinguishes it from a nominal option burdened by a federal command. The established voluntary entry into these paid custody arrangements supplies the relevant relationship here.

Environmental Defense Center requires examination of the actual regulated activity and legal choices. That inquiry identifies the counties’ own custody-information handling, rather than an assigned general regulatory program. Valid regulation of that accepted operation remains enforceable while it is performed. The arrangement’s label alone would be insufficient, but its actual function answers the structural objection. Ordinary compliance costs remain costs of the regulated operation.

### IV. The valid federal restriction controls the conflicting demand for public release

**Holding and operative rule:** When state disclosure law requires public release of information covered by this valid federal custody rule and federal law restricts that release to its own disclosure channels, the federal rule controls the actual conflict. The appellate reversal of the disclosure order is affirmed for the covered information; separable unprotected material remains governed by applicable law, and any coverage dispute must identify the information’s category and legal basis rather than rely on a blanket immigration label.

**Controlling explanation:** The state release command and the federal restriction cannot both be obeyed for the same covered information. Federal supremacy resolves that conflict only after the Court establishes lawful delegated authority and procedure. The contracted federal custody connection fixes the application’s scope.

The rule expressly includes pending disclosure requests. Here the demand seeks a future public release; applying the restriction to that release does not attach a new sanction to completed conduct. The trial order remained subject to appellate review and was reversed after promulgation. The requested disclosure thus receives an answer under the governing restriction, with the existing appellate reversal left in place.

Applying the coverage rule is ordinary implementation of that judgment. The counties must continue withholding covered information while processing separable unprotected material under applicable law. Separately available federal access channels remain available on their own terms.

## Precedent Treatment

- **United States v. Haggar Apparel Co. (April 21, 1999):** Applied to require actual delegated authority and independent enforcement of statutory boundaries before accepting a reasonable implementing choice.
- **United States v. Mead Corp. (June 18, 2001):** Applied to the statutory delegation and exercised rulemaking mechanism; the binding custody regulation differs from Mead’s transaction-specific, revisable classification letter.
- **Reno v. Condon (January 12, 2000):** Its distinction between regulation of the State’s own information handling and compulsory administration against others is applied to information from accepted federal custody.
- **New York v. United States (June 19, 1992):** Its rule that official assent cannot enlarge federal power is preserved and applied through the independent authority inquiry.
- **Printz v. United States / Mack v. United States (June 27, 1997):** Its compulsory-assistance setting is distinguished from the voluntarily accepted paid custody operation.
- **Lomont v. O’Neill (March 11, 2004):** Its insistence on an actual voluntary relationship informs the custody inquiry; its unconditional option to refuse applicant assistance does not invalidate lawful rules for an accepted operation’s information.
- **Environmental Defense Center v. EPA (March 25, 2004):** Its actual-function inquiry is applied to custody-information handling, and its treatment of ordinary own-operation compliance costs remains controlling.

## Law After Decision

The decision establishes the validity, against the presented delegation, interim APA and structural challenges, of federal control over public disclosure of information acquired through voluntarily accepted paid federal immigration custody. Contrary state disclosure requirements yield for that covered information. Each good-cause exception retains its own statutory predicate and published justification. The holding’s reach follows the custodial source and information category, while applicable federal disclosure channels and ordinary processing of separable unprotected material remain operative.
