## Event

Intel Corp. v. Advanced Micro Devices, Inc., No. 02-572, 542 U.S. 241. Argued April 20, 2004; decided June 21, 2004. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, 292 F.3d 664.

AMD complained to the European Commission about Intel and sought documents and testimony from American antitrust litigation. The district court denied authority to assist; the Ninth Circuit reversed that categorical ruling. The questions are whether AMD is an interested person, whether the contemplated Commission adjudication qualifies, whether foreign discoverability is required, and how statutory discretion operates.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision. Justice O’Connor took no part in the consideration or decision.

## Public Action

The judgment of the Court of Appeals is affirmed.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 7–1.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg.

**Opposing:** Breyer.

**Not participating:** O’Connor.

The application returns to the district court for a reasoned decision whether, and on what conditions, assistance should be provided.

## Opinion Topology

SCALIA, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, KENNEDY, SOUTER, THOMAS and GINSBURG, JJ., joined. BREYER, J., filed a dissenting opinion.

## Holdings

### I. Section 1782 permits this application for a contemplated adjudicative proceeding

**Holding and operative rule.** A district court may provide assistance under 28 U.S.C. §1782(a) upon an interested person’s application for evidence from a person residing or found in the district, for use in a reasonably contemplated proceeding before a foreign governmental body exercising adjudicative authority. The applicant need not await a pending or imminent adjudication; practical rights to submit evidence and obtain review may establish the required interest without formal litigant status.

**Authority.** The seven Justices joining the Court’s opinion support every holding and explanation in Parts I–III.

**Controlling explanation.** The enacted language reaches a foreign tribunal, and Congress removed the former restriction to pending judicial proceedings. That change permits assistance before an adjudication has begun, provided the expected proceeding rests on more than speculation. AMD can submit evidence and seek review of Commission action or inaction. Those rights give it a practical legal interest in the proceeding. The Commission’s ability to make a first-instance competition determination, subject to judicial review, supplies the adjudicative function relevant here. Its investigative directorate’s refusal to request the materials does not eliminate AMD’s separate statutory right to apply. The Commission’s combined functions warrant attention to the actual use of the evidence; they do not support categorical exclusion of its adjudicative work.

### II. Foreign discoverability and domestic analogues are not additional statutory prerequisites

**Holding and operative rule.** Section 1782 does not require the applicant to show that the evidence would be discoverable under foreign law or that an analogous domestic proceeding would permit the same discovery. Differences in national procedures do not alone establish an effort to evade foreign restrictions.

**Controlling explanation.** The statute states who may apply, where the evidence provider must be found and the proceeding for which assistance may be given. It adds an express protection for legally applicable privileges, but no foreign-discoverability or domestic-analogue condition. Requiring either would substitute an extra eligibility rule for Congress’s discretionary authorization. A foreign body’s practices, policies and views remain important when the district court decides whether assistance is appropriate. The dissent’s concern that discovery litigation itself can cause expense and regulatory interference is substantial, but supports a genuine discretionary assessment rather than categorical conditions absent from the statute. Neither a foreign body’s refusal to request evidence nor the absence of comparable foreign procedures is a statutory veto.

### III. Eligibility leaves a genuine, application-specific discretion

**Holding and operative rule.** An eligible application does not compel production. The district court must exercise reasoned discretion in light of the contemplated proceeding and requested assistance, considering the foreign body’s receptivity, the nature and character of its proceeding, access to evidence through a participant within that body’s reach, actual circumvention of foreign proof-gathering restrictions or policies, and undue intrusion or burden; no order may violate a legally applicable privilege.

**Controlling explanation.** The word permitting assistance leaves the district court responsible for the actual production decision. Evidence from a participant subject to the foreign tribunal’s authority may present a different need from evidence outside that authority. The court must consider the Commission’s position and distinguish rejection of a particular assistance mechanism from a legally operative restriction. It may narrow, condition or deny a request with reasons, and use appropriate confidentiality protection. These considerations guide discretion; they are not new cumulative eligibility elements. The application therefore returns for identification of the materials, their relevance to the reasonably contemplated proceeding, and the protections and burdens that the actual request presents.

## Precedent Treatment

F. Hoffmann-La Roche Ltd. v. Empagran S.A. supplies respect for foreign regulatory interests through the statute actually at issue. Its construction of antitrust coverage does not eliminate Congress’s affirmative authorization of foreign evidentiary assistance.

## Law After Decision

Interested complainants with practical participation and review rights may seek assistance for reasonably contemplated governmental adjudication. Neither foreign discoverability nor a domestic discovery analogue is a categorical condition. The district court retains responsibility for a proportionate order consistent with privileges, foreign receptivity, lawful restrictions and actual burdens.

## Separate Writings

Justice Breyer dissents. Where a foreign institution’s tribunal character is seriously doubtful, he would give substantial weight to its own description of its function. The Commission’s mixed prosecutorial and adjudicative role and its opposition to this use counsel against assistance. He would also deny a private request for discovery unavailable both abroad and in analogous domestic circumstances, absent a special need supported by the foreign or intergovernmental authorities whose cooperation the statute serves. He derives these limits from statutory purpose or, alternatively, supervisory authority. Because the expense and disruption begin with the discovery litigation itself, later trimming of production does not sufficiently answer his objection. He would reverse and direct dismissal of this application.

## Procedure After Action

The Ninth Circuit’s return to the district court remains effective. That court must decide the scope, conditions or denial of the particular requested assistance under the statute and the Court’s stated discretionary considerations.

## Source Notes

28 U.S.C. §1782(a)–(b); Advanced Micro Devices, Inc. v. Intel Corp., 292 F.3d 664 (9th Cir. 2002); F. Hoffmann-La Roche Ltd. v. Empagran S.A., No. 03-724, decided June 14, 2004. The Commission’s adjudicative function and AMD’s participation and review rights are material to this application.

## Event

Duckworth v. State Administration Board of Election Laws. Decided June 23, 2004. On review of the judgment of the United States Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit, 332 F.3d 769, affirming a single district judge’s dismissal of a challenge to Maryland’s 2002 congressional map.

Duckworth alleges that dividing Anne Arundel County among four unusually shaped districts intentionally impaired Republican participation and influence. The questions are whether a single judge could dismiss the preserved constitutional claim on ordinary pleading grounds without convening three judges, and which tribunal must assess the allegations under the governing partisan-districting rule. Only the Fourteenth Amendment claim was pursued on appeal.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated in the consideration and decision.

## Public Action

The judgment of the Court of Appeals is vacated, and the case is remanded for proceedings before the tribunal required by 28 U.S.C. §2284.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Vacated and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The district judge must request a three-judge court. That court must address the preserved claim and any permissible conforming amendment under ordinary procedure and the governing rule in Vieth v. Jubelirer.

## Opinion Topology

THOMAS, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O’CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, SOUTER, GINSBURG and BREYER, JJ., joined.

## Holdings

### I. Ordinary pleading weakness does not dispense with the statutory tribunal

**Holding and operative rule.** When a plaintiff presents a constitutional challenge to congressional apportionment within federal jurisdiction, §2284 requires a three-judge district court unless the constitutional claim is wholly insubstantial, obviously frivolous or clearly foreclosed. A single judge may not equate ordinary failure to state a claim with that exceptional category and enter a merits dismissal.

**Authority.** All nine Justices join the Court’s statutory holding and the directions applying existing law in Part II. That application does not reconsider or enlarge the constitutional holding of Vieth.

**Controlling explanation.** Section 2284 allocates covered apportionment litigation to three judges and expressly restricts a single judge’s authority to enter judgment on the merits. Goosby v. Osser distinguishes a wholly insubstantial constitutional claim from one that is merely doubtful or may fail under ordinary merits rules. Treating Rule 12(b)(6) insufficiency as the substantiality test erases that distinction and permits the single judge to exercise the very authority Congress assigned elsewhere. The earlier map’s unsuccessful merits litigation supplies no categorical pleading bar for the changed map. Duckworth’s allegation of purposeful impairment of Republican participation presents the kind of equal-protection claim recognized by Vieth. Whether his allegations and evidence satisfy that rule belongs to the designated tribunal.

### II. The panel applies Vieth’s actual rule and ordinary pleading procedure

**Holding and operative rule.** The required panel must apply Vieth’s partisan equal-treatment rule: identified political capacity, material impairment, prohibited purpose as a substantial motivating factor, and boundary-specific causation, supported by an independently lawful comparison and subject to the State’s same-decision defense. Irregular shape or electoral disadvantage alone does not establish those elements; ordinary notice pleading does not require merits proof, an expert model or a replacement map.

**Controlling explanation.** Vieth supplies the law for the preserved Fourteenth Amendment claim, and Martinez v. Bush illustrates its claim-specific application. Division among four districts and alleged purposeful degradation of political participation make this a substantial constitutional claim for tribunal allocation. The panel must permit the ordinary process for seeking conforming amendment and evaluate pleading, proof and any remedy under the actual rule. The Court’s reliance on that rule does not revive the Article I or independent First Amendment theories abandoned on appeal. The State continues lawful election administration subject to any separately justified order entered by the proper tribunal.

## Precedent Treatment

Goosby v. Osser is applied to distinguish constitutional insubstantiality from ordinary merits insufficiency. Vieth v. Jubelirer supplies the controlling partisan equal-treatment rule; its constitutional coalition and scope remain unchanged. Martinez v. Bush supplies the requirement to apply that rule to the particular claim and proof. Shaw v. Reno does not convert unusual district shape into a substitute for the injury required by the preserved partisan claim.

## Law After Decision

A single judge cannot bypass §2284 by using ordinary Rule 12(b)(6) weakness as constitutional insubstantiality. A substantial congressional-apportionment claim receives merits adjudication from the statutory panel, which applies the existing substantive elements, lawful comparison, ordinary proof rules and State defense.

## Event

Beard v. Banks, No. 02-1603, 542 U.S. 406. Argued February 24, 2004; decided June 24, 2004. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit, 316 F.3d 228.

After Horn v. Banks required consideration of Pennsylvania’s preserved nonretroactivity defense, the Third Circuit held the mitigation rule available and reinstated its grant of capital sentencing relief. The questions are whether the individual-juror mitigation rule was dictated when direct review became final on October 5, 1987, and whether the actual charge and form satisfy the constitutional and statutory grounds for relief. Pennsylvania adjudicated the merits in 1995; the operative federal petition is subject to amended §2254(d).

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The judgment is affirmed in part and vacated in part, and the case is remanded for the remaining sentence-effect inquiry.

## Opinion Topology

STONE-ZSELA, C. J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STEVENS, SOUTER, GINSBURG and BREYER, JJ., joined. STEVENS, J., filed a concurring opinion. O’CONNOR, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which SCALIA, KENNEDY and THOMAS, JJ., joined.

The five-Justice opinion applies the existing effect standard; Justice Stevens’s concurrence preserves his different preferred standard without withholding the remand directed by controlling law.

## Holdings

### I. An individual-juror mitigation veto is forbidden by law dictated before finality

**Holding and operative rule.** A capital instruction may not condition an individual juror’s use of supported mitigating evidence on unanimous agreement that the circumstance exists. Applying that prohibition to a judgment final after the effective-mitigation requirement was established does not announce a new rule merely because the barrier takes the form of a unanimity requirement.

**Authority.** The five Justices joining the Court’s opinion support Parts I–IV, including application of the existing effect rule in Part IV.

**Controlling explanation.** Lockett v. Ohio and Eddings v. Oklahoma forbid legal exclusion of relevant mitigation from the sentencing decision. A juror who accepts mitigating evidence but is legally forbidden to use it because another juror disagrees encounters the same functional exclusion. Graham v. Collins explains that an effective route for independent culpability mitigation was dictated before its 1984 finality. Its later decision confirms that earlier rule; it is not backdated as a new command. The dissent distinguishes usable information from the agreement required within a collective sentencer. That distinction cannot sustain a legal veto over evidence an individual sentencing juror accepts. The rule leaves the jury’s ultimate verdict requirements and each juror’s assessment of weight intact. Horn required the preserved Teague defense to be answered, and this comparison supplies the answer. Because the claim applies old law, the Court need not extend the watershed exception.

### II. The actual charge and form create the forbidden barrier

**Holding and operative rule.** A capital charge violates the effective-mitigation requirement when, read with the verdict form and trial context, it creates a reasonable likelihood that a juror cannot use supported mitigation without unanimous agreement. Banks’s combined charge and form create that likelihood.

**Controlling explanation.** The charge required death upon unanimous findings of an aggravating circumstance without mitigation, or aggravating circumstances outweighing mitigation, and directed life in other cases. It stated different proof burdens—beyond a reasonable doubt for aggravation and preponderance for mitigation—while repeatedly requiring agreement on findings and the ultimate verdict. The form then placed the circumstance findings under a common unanimity introduction. Nothing in that mechanism distinguished a juror’s individual ability to use mitigation. Different proof burdens do not explain a different voting rule. The jury’s acceptance of extreme mental or emotional disturbance does not establish that every juror could use the separate impaired-capacity and catchall categories. The reported polling supplied no cure. Boyde v. California requires reasonable likelihood, not a merely possible restrictive reading; Middleton v. McNeil requires the complete charge and context, not isolated wording. The combined mechanism meets that standard. The defect does not arise solely from absence of a particular sentence or from the lawful unanimity required for an ultimate death verdict.

### III. The state application was objectively unreasonable

**Holding and operative rule.** Under §2254(d)(1), the 1995 state adjudication unreasonably applied the clearly established mitigation rule to this combined charge and form. The petitioner satisfies the statutory merits gateway in addition to establishing the constitutional violation.

**Controlling explanation.** Williams v. Taylor makes unreasonable application an objective legal inquiry measured against this Court’s holdings at the state merits decision. Mills v. Maryland and McKoy v. North Carolina were available by 1995, as was Graham’s explanation of the effective-use requirement. The state analysis relied on previously accepted wording without confronting how the common unanimity requirement operated across the distinct mitigation findings. This is more than ordinary disagreement about an isolated ambiguity. The form and repeated instruction create the operative veto the governing rule forbids. Yarborough v. Alvarado preserves room for reasonable application of general rules but does not demand an earlier factual twin or turn judicial disagreement into the statutory test. The state resolution falls outside that permissible range. This statutory conclusion remains separate from the effect of the error on the death sentences.

### IV. Sentence relief requires the separate State-borne effect inquiry

**Holding and operative rule.** For this properly available original-charge mitigation restriction, Brecht’s governing rule, as applied in Penry v. Johnson, requires the State to prove beyond a reasonable doubt on the complete penalty record that the defect did not contribute to death. Failure requires conditional sentence relief permitting lawful capital resentencing within a reasonable court-fixed period or a lawful noncapital sentence.

**Controlling explanation.** Constitutional error and satisfaction of §2254(d)(1) permit the inquiry; neither resolves actual contribution. Penry expressly separates these steps for an original-charge mitigation restriction, and Mitchell v. Esparza rejects treating a defect as structural merely because the sentence is capital. The full penalty evidence must be assessed under the State’s burden. The instructional account establishes the legal mechanism but does not supply the complete evidentiary basis for deciding effect here. The court on return must conduct that confined inquiry while treating the old-rule, constitutional-error and statutory conclusions as settled. Brecht and Penry govern present federal review; their effect procedure is not used as a later substantive command at the 1995 state-law cutoff.

## Precedent Treatment

Lockett v. Ohio and Eddings v. Oklahoma are applied to a legal barrier preventing individual use of supported mitigation. Graham v. Collins confirms the prefinality force of that protection. Horn v. Banks is followed by resolving the preserved Teague defense; its reserved question is answered without changing its sequencing rule. O’Dell v. Netherland retains its distinct old-rule and confined watershed grounds.

Mills v. Maryland and McKoy v. North Carolina are applied to the unanimity mechanism. Boyde v. California and Middleton v. McNeil supply contextual reasonable-likelihood review. Williams v. Taylor and Yarborough v. Alvarado supply objective statutory review rather than factual identity or a count of disagreeing judges. Brecht v. Abrahamson and Penry v. Johnson govern the separate State-borne effect inquiry. Mitchell v. Esparza preserves the distinction between a capital instructional error and a structural defect.

## Law After Decision

The effective-mitigation rule already dictated before Banks’s 1987 finality forbids a unanimity veto on an individual juror’s use of supported mitigation. This actual instruction/form mechanism violates that rule, and the 1995 state rejection was objectively unreasonable. Those conclusions bind the remand; the complete penalty-record effect inquiry determines whether conditional sentence relief must issue.

## Separate Writings

Justice Stevens concurs. He applies the governing State-borne beyond-reasonable-doubt rule and joins the confined remand, while retaining his preferred inquiry into whether the error had substantial and injurious influence on the sentence. The Court’s existing rule, rather than a new endorsement of its burden, controls his present vote.

Justice O’Connor dissents, joined by Justices Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. Lockett and Eddings required an effective opportunity for the sentencer to use mitigation, but did not, in their view, dictate the later restriction on agreement among members of a collective sentencer. Graham’s effective-use holding must be respected without treating every subsequent jury-arrangement rule as already dictated in 1987. The individual-juror rule changes procedure rather than the conduct or persons subject to punishment, and does not produce the fundamental systemic accuracy change necessary for the watershed exception. They distinguish O’Dell’s misleading-parole-premise safeguard and Tyler’s invalid verdict-wide burden. They would reverse the relief under Teague and leave remaining independent claims to their own disposition. They do not independently decide the actual charge or statutory gateway.

If the State fails to establish harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt, the lower court must enter a conditional sentence order fixing a reasonable period for lawful resentencing, with a lawful noncapital sentence if the State does not provide the replacement proceeding.

## Event

Blakely v. Washington, No. 02-1632, 542 U.S. 296. Argued March 23, 2004; decided June 24, 2004. On writ of certiorari to the Washington Court of Appeals, 111 Wash. App. 851, 47 P.3d 149.

Blakely pleaded guilty to kidnapping. His admissions supported a binding standard-range maximum of 53 months. The judge imposed 90 months after finding deliberate cruelty, a fact neither admitted as an element nor found by a jury. The question is whether the higher outer maximum for the felony classification permits that judicial increase above the binding range.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The judgment sustaining the exceptional sentence is reversed. The 90-month sentence is vacated, and the case is remanded for lawful sentencing.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed; sentence vacated and case remanded, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg.

**Opposing:** O’Connor, Kennedy and Breyer.

The guilty plea and conviction remain intact. On the existing admissions, the court must sentence within the lawful standard range. Any further aggravated proceeding requires independent Washington authority and compliance with applicable charging, plea, waiver and double-jeopardy limits.

## Opinion Topology

SOUTER, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, SCALIA, THOMAS and GINSBURG, JJ., joined. O’CONNOR, KENNEDY and BREYER, JJ., each filed a dissenting opinion.

## Holdings

### I. The binding range identifies the punishment authorized by the plea or verdict

**Holding and operative rule.** A present-offense fact legally necessary to increase a binding sentencing ceiling or floor must be validly admitted as an element or proved to the jury beyond a reasonable doubt; a lawful jury waiver changes the factfinder while retaining that burden. A higher outer statutory maximum does not permit a judge to exceed the enforceable inner range supported by the verdict or valid admissions.

**Authority.** The six Justices joining the Court’s opinion support the rule, application and remedy in Parts I and II.

**Controlling explanation.** Apprendi v. New Jersey supplies the requirement of element protection for a fact necessary to authorize greater punishment. United States v. Angle already applies that protection to binding inner ranges, including mandatory constraints below a broader statutory maximum. Harris v. United States confirms that sentencing labels and the distinction between increasing a floor and increasing a ceiling do not remove the protection. The relevant question is what punishment the actual adjudication authorizes, not the largest term the statute could allow after additional facts are established. The dissenters’ structured-sentencing concerns address whether Angle should stand; they supply no distinction under its controlling rule. Judicial evaluation remains permissible within independently authorized punishment. Here the plea supports a maximum of 53 months, so the additional cruelty finding was necessary to authorize the 90-month term.

### II. The judicial cruelty finding cannot sustain this sentence

**Holding and operative rule.** An unadmitted and untried deliberate-cruelty finding cannot authorize the exceptional sentence imposed here. The sentence must be vacated, and lawful resentencing must begin from the range authorized by the actual plea; any further aggravation adjudication depends on independent state-law authority and valid procedures.

**Controlling explanation.** Notice that the general felony classification allowed greater exposure neither admits the aggravating element nor waives its proper adjudication. The judge’s conclusion about cruelty supplies the very finding the constitutional rule assigns elsewhere; repeating it cannot cure the omission. Nor does the asserted strength of the evidence establish an equivalent actual adjudication or a valid admission. Ring v. Arizona preserves the distinction between identifying a constitutional factfinding requirement and deciding an error’s consequences on the actual record. Nothing here permits retention of the 90-month sentence on a hypothetical replacement verdict. Resentencing must respect the plea and conviction that remain in force. If additional aggravated proceedings are sought, their state-law basis and the applicable charging, waiver and double-jeopardy limits must be established independently; the constitutional remand supplies no such authority.

## Precedent Treatment

Apprendi v. New Jersey is applied to the fact legally necessary for greater punishment and to the distinction between notice and valid admission. United States v. Angle directly governs the binding-inner-range question. Harris v. United States supplies the common treatment of present-offense facts raising binding floors and ceilings. United States v. Buckland preserves analysis of the punishment actually authorized, rather than assumed exposure. Ring v. Arizona retains its separate capital-eligibility and qualified remedy rules. The Court applies these decisions without changing their operative rules.

## Law After Decision

The existing element-protection rule governs materially equivalent binding sentencing schemes, including Washington’s exceptional-range mechanism. Judges retain discretion within punishment independently authorized by the actual verdict or valid admissions. The 53-month range supported by this plea cannot be enlarged to 90 months through the judge’s contested cruelty finding.

## Separate Writings

Justice O’Connor dissents. She would reconsider Angle’s treatment of binding sentencing ranges. In her view, rules structuring the judge’s choice within the outer punishment authorized for the offense differ from elements defining the offense itself. She would sustain the exceptional sentence on that understanding, while recognizing that Angle as it stands requires correction.

Justice Kennedy dissents. He retains his objection to making every binding inner-range constraint an element, emphasizing the role of legislatures and sentencing institutions in developing structured sentencing. His acceptance of the jury requirement at Ring’s capital-eligibility boundary does not resolve this different objection. He would reconsider the inner-range rule and affirm; his position does not depend on characterizing this application as an unexpected first extension of the rule.

Justice Breyer dissents. He would reconsider the broader Apprendi and Angle allocation because structured judicial sentencing can serve consistency and individualized judgment within the offense’s outer authorization. His objection reaches binding floors and ceilings alike. His capital-specific view about jury participation in choosing death does not establish support for the noncapital rule applied here. He would affirm on his proposed reconsideration of that rule.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns for lawful sentencing with the plea and conviction preserved. The sentencing court must identify the range authorized by the actual admissions and resolve any request for further aggravated adjudication under independent Washington authority and the applicable constitutional and plea limits.

Washington Court of Appeals, 111 Wash. App. 851, 47 P.3d 149; Apprendi v. New Jersey, No. 99-478, decided June 26, 2000; United States v. Angle, decided June 20, 2002; Harris v. United States, decided June 24, 2002; Ring v. Arizona, No. 01-488, decided June 24, 2002; United States v. Buckland, decided March 10, 2003.

## Event

## Holdings

### I. Extraordinary appellate relief and statutory enforcement have separate requirements

**Holding and operative rule.** Appellate mandamus under §1651 requires absence of an adequate alternative, a clear and indisputable entitlement, and a determination that issuance is appropriate in the circumstances. The plaintiffs’ separate §1361 theory requires a legally enforceable, sufficiently clear nondiscretionary duty and its other prerequisites; any APA claim requires an eligible agency defendant and satisfaction of that statute’s independent conditions.

**Authority.** Part I is joined by all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation.** Kerr v. United States District Court supplies the exceptional-remedy conditions and the importance of adequate protection in the district court. An appeal deadline does not mechanically govern a mandamus petition, although unreasonable delay and resulting prejudice may bear on equitable relief. Appellate mandamus is not a substitute for establishing the plaintiffs’ underlying enforcement route. The official’s title also supplies neither a categorical immunity nor an automatic interlocutory appeal. The inquiry addresses the function involved, the actual demands and their supported burdens. These distinctions preserve both independent judicial enforcement of federal law and the protections necessary for executive responsibility.

### II. Concrete institutional burdens need not await a document-by-document privilege confrontation

**Holding and operative rule.** A court considering extraordinary protection against compulsory civil discovery directed into presidential advisory work must assess demonstrated separation-of-powers burdens from the scope and operation of the demands. A formal assertion of executive privilege is not an invariant prerequisite to addressing those distinct burdens; particular claims of privilege retain their own requirements.

**Authority.** Part II is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer.

**Controlling explanation.** Clinton v. Jones requires accommodation of concrete interference with official responsibilities while rejecting immunity based simply on presidential status. Its unofficial-conduct setting does not itself decide this official advisory dispute, but the functional accommodation principle applies. United States v. Nixon rejects absolute insulation of presidential communications in the face of a demonstrated need for identified criminal evidence; it does not make broad civil discovery and a later privilege contest the sole permissible course. A demand may burden official deliberation through its breadth and the search it requires before individual documents can be contested. Confidential advice, once disclosed, cannot be restored to confidentiality. The appellate court therefore erred in treating the absence of a formal privilege confrontation as sufficient to avoid examining these burdens. The availability of narrower district-court remedies remains important, as the dissent urges, but must be evaluated as an actual alternative to the asserted intrusion rather than presumed adequate from the possibility of future privilege objections.

### III. Actual integration into collective advisory work can establish membership

**Holding and operative rule.** Under FACA’s definition and its exclusion for a body composed wholly of federal officers or employees, a private person may be a functional member when sustained participation integrates that person into an established body’s internal collective deliberative and advisory work. Formal appointment, voting, veto or final governmental decisionmaking authority is not indispensable; access, attendance, lobbying, consultation, influence or outside recommendations without that integration is insufficient.

**Authority.** Part III is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. These are direct joins to the statutory distinction, independent of the disagreement over appellate relief.

**Controlling explanation.** Public Citizen v. Department of Justice construes FACA with attention to ordinary executive consultation and serious constitutional concerns; it does not exempt every presidential advisory arrangement. Association of American Physicians & Surgeons v. Clinton persuasively distinguishes regular work functionally indistinguishable from membership from episodic advice. Relevant facts include regular internal meetings, continuing assigned responsibilities, participation in developing collective recommendations or drafting the group’s report, and work materially indistinguishable from that of recognized members. No single fact automatically establishes membership. An executive label cannot conclusively exclude an integrated private member, and allegations of influence cannot conclusively establish one. The distinction concerns an actual organized advisory body, not every person whose views officials hear. Here private integration remains disputed. The governing inquiry concerns participants’ functions; coverage and any final enforcement require factual and claim-specific adjudication through an available legal route.

### IV. The proper mandate is appellate reconsideration

**Holding and operative rule.** The Court of Appeals must reconsider the petition by applying all mandamus requirements to the actual structural burdens and available protective alternatives. Its former privilege-only approach cannot sustain the judgment; whether a writ should issue requires that complete inquiry.

**Authority.** Part IV is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy and Breyer.

**Controlling explanation.** The identified appellate error does not establish that every extraordinary-relief condition is satisfied. The court must consider the breadth and necessity of the demands, whether narrower inquiry and district-court supervision adequately protect the asserted interests, the clear-duty and enforcement limitations, and the appropriateness of intervention. Thomas and Scalia would order the writ now because the uncertain duty cannot support discovery that may deliver the ultimate disclosure sought. Those concerns require consideration, but the present record leaves the complete alternatives and entitlement inquiry for appellate adjudication. Any legally authorized further process must respect the Court’s functional-membership rule and the distinct protections for particular privileged materials.

## Precedent Treatment

Kerr v. United States District Court is applied to the full extraordinary-writ inquiry and adequate protective alternatives. Clinton v. Jones is extended through its concrete-accommodation principle to official advisory discovery while retaining its actual unofficial-conduct holding. United States v. Nixon retains qualified protection and the distinction between identified criminal evidence and broad civil demands. Nixon v. Fitzgerald retains its function-based official-act damages immunity, a different question from statutory-compliance inquiry.

Public Citizen v. Department of Justice supplies contextual statutory caution without a blanket presidential-advice exemption. The functional reasoning of Association of American Physicians & Surgeons v. Clinton is adopted to distinguish integrated members from outside consultants. The statutory inquiry must preserve the independent requirements of the asserted enforcement channel.

## Law After Decision

Concrete institutional discovery burdens must receive consideration under the full mandamus standard without an automatic privilege-first barrier. FACA membership turns on sustained integration into an actual advisory body’s collective work, rather than formal voting authority or private influence alone. The Court’s mandate requires appellate reconsideration under the complete extraordinary-relief standard.

## Separate Writings

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela concurs and would add binding stages to the remand. First, the district court must identify the FACA provision, defendants, alleged private participants and allegations of integration, and separately determine personal injury and an available enforcement route under ordinary pleading rules. A generalized allegation of private influence does not authorize a search through presidential deliberations. Akins v. FEC supports concrete injury from denial of particular statutorily protected information; Chamber of Commerce v. Reich supports qualified review of responsible implementing officers, not an unrestricted private action.

Second, initial discovery must concern membership, attendance, assigned responsibilities and actual collective participation. The court must prefer official rosters, calendars, declarations or other adequate nondeliberative evidence before seeking the substance of advice. A title or conclusory declaration cannot prevent appropriate testing of a genuinely disputed material fact.

Third, before enlarging the inquiry, the court must identify the remaining material dispute, explain necessity and assess an adequate less intrusive alternative. Operational facts and advice are analytically distinct, but no category is automatically unprivileged. Narrow requests, segregation, redaction and protective orders must address the actual sensitivity and burden. Justified in-camera inspection—review by the judge outside public disclosure—does not replace decisions about relevance, necessity or privilege.

Fourth, particular privileges and concrete objections must be resolved before protected disclosure. The party seeking protection must provide the particularity reasonably possible without defeating it. Plaintiffs must establish coverage and final-relief prerequisites; access, influence and resistance to disclosure establish neither. A formal privilege claim may be necessary to decide a privilege, but is not invariably necessary to correct an unnecessarily broad order. The Chief would require this sequence while leaving appellate mandamus subject to its own conditions. These mandatory steps do not receive another Justice’s join.

Justice Stevens concurs. Broad discovery can deliver the very disclosure sought as final relief before plaintiffs establish the sufficiently clear duty required by §1361. Discovery must relate to establishing the asserted statutory coverage. Substantive participation in the group’s recommendations or report can support membership, but repeated access alone cannot. These concerns support reconsideration rather than a direction to issue the writ immediately.

Justice Thomas, joined by Justice Scalia, concurs in Parts I–II and dissents from the remedial disposition. An uncertain claim of FACA coverage and an unestablished clear nondiscretionary duty cannot justify the broad compulsory inquiry into official advice allowed here. The possible later establishment of functional membership does not supply the missing present basis for §1361 discovery. They would direct issuance of the writ against the challenged district-court action.

Justice Ginsburg, joined by Justice Souter, concurs in Parts I and III and dissents from vacatur. Specific requests for narrower discovery and properly supported privilege protection remain available in the district court. The petitioners have not adequately used those alternatives; the court’s failure to narrow the demands on its own does not establish a present entitlement to extraordinary appellate intervention. They accept protection of executive functions and the functional-membership distinction but would affirm the mandamus denial.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns to the Court of Appeals for a complete mandamus determination. The appellate court must address the actual discovery demands, structural burdens, underlying enforcement requirements and adequate protective alternatives before deciding whether extraordinary relief is warranted.

## Event

Schriro v. Summerlin, No. 03-526, 542 U.S. 348. Argued April 19, 2004; decided June 24, 2004. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, sitting en banc, 341 F.3d 1082.

Summerlin’s murder judgment became final in 1984. The trial judge found a prior violent felony and exceptional heinousness, cruelty or depravity and imposed death under Arizona’s former scheme. His federal petition preceded AEDPA and remained pending when Ring v. Arizona was decided. The Ninth Circuit granted Ring-based sentencing relief. The question is whether the jury determination of an indispensable death-eligibility fact applies retroactively. Review excludes whether the prior-conviction aggravator independently established eligibility.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The judgment is vacated, and the case is remanded. Ring is available under the confined watershed exception, but the actual error and requirements for sentence relief must be adjudicated.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Vacated and remanded with the Ring claim available, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would reverse the Ring-based grant and require rejection of that claim under Teague.

The rejection of the retroactivity bar is sustained on the watershed ground. The lower court must determine the actual eligibility omission and apply the governing collateral effect standard before entering any conditional sentence relief. The murder conviction and independently reviewable claims remain distinct. The prior-conviction sufficiency question remains outside this Court’s grant.

## Holdings

### I. Ring changes procedure rather than the substantive reach of punishment

**Holding and operative rule.** Ring’s allocation to the jury of an otherwise required statutory aggravating-fact determination is a new procedural rule for a judgment final in 1984. It does not change the substantive aggravators, conduct or classes of persons legally subject to death, so the substantive-rule exception does not apply.

**Authority.** All nine Justices join Part I.

**Controlling explanation.** The aggravating circumstance was already necessary under Arizona law, and the State already required proof beyond a reasonable doubt. Ring changes who must determine that fact. Calling the fact an element explains its constitutional protection but does not itself remove conduct or a class of offenders from the State’s power to punish. Caspari and Horn require consideration of a preserved retroactivity defense before granting relief; classification is one step in that inquiry. Because the rule was not dictated at Summerlin’s 1984 finality, its availability depends on the separately preserved exception for watershed procedures essential to accuracy and fundamental fairness.

### II. The indispensable capital-eligibility determination satisfies the watershed exception

**Holding and operative rule.** In a jury-tried prosecution, Ring’s protection for the determination of a present-offense fact indispensable to authorizing death, subject to valid admission and lawful waiver requirements, satisfies both the exceptional accuracy and bedrock-procedure requirements for watershed application on collateral review. Qualifying claims may proceed under the legal regime otherwise governing the petition.

**Authority.** Part II is joined by Breyer, Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg.

**Controlling explanation.** The missing safeguard operates at the factual boundary between imprisonment and death. Independent collective scrutiny of the prosecution’s case is absent at the point where the irreversible punishment first becomes lawful. That deficiency creates an impermissibly large risk in this confined category, especially when an aggravator calls for evaluation of exceptional cruelty rather than a merely mechanical fact. A correct burden of proof does not itself supply that collective assessment. Jury determination of a punishment-authorizing fact also belongs to the basic structure of a fair criminal adjudication; making it effective at this boundary changes understanding of an indispensable safeguard.

O’Dell recognizes a confined watershed safeguard against a materially misleading capital-information premise, and Tyler recognizes the exceptional significance of an adjudication lacking the required verdict-wide reasonable-doubt safeguard. Neither already decides Ring, but both require examination of how a missing procedure operates. DeStefano’s refusal to reopen ordinary noncapital bench trials under an earlier retroactivity framework supplies substantial caution: judges can find facts reliably. It does not resolve this confined capital question under Teague’s two requirements. The Court’s conclusion rests on the withheld eligibility determination and its evaluative function, not death’s severity alone, presumed judicial incompetence or a general preference for greater mercy. Ultimate discretionary selection among already authorized punishments is distinct from the eligibility rule applied here.

### III. Availability does not establish an omission’s effect or authorize an immediate writ

**Holding and operative rule.** The lower court must determine whether a necessary death-eligibility finding was withheld from the jury and address the remaining prerequisites to relief on the actual record. For a properly reached, nonstructural eligibility omission, Brecht’s governing collateral rule places on the State the burden to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the omission did not contribute to the death sentence.

**Authority.** Part III is joined by the same five Justices. It directs the remaining adjudication under existing law; the separate writings preserve the differing views about the detailed actual-record method.

**Controlling explanation.** Ring separates the jury entitlement from an actual error’s remedial consequence, and Brecht supplies the collateral effect standard. Retroactivity neither establishes that an indispensable fact was omitted nor resolves contribution to the sentence. Lindh and Carriger preserve the former merits and factual-presumption regime for this pre-AEDPA petition; Slack separately governs the later appeal’s certificate. If the remaining requirements are met and the State fails its effect burden, conditional sentence relief must permit lawful correction under governing law. Further sentencing requires independent state authority and compliance with applicable constitutional limits. Whether the prior violent conviction independently supplied eligibility was excluded from review and must receive its own lawful treatment within the grant’s limits.

## Precedent Treatment

Ring v. Arizona is classified as procedural and its reserved collateral question is answered for indispensable present-offense death-eligibility findings. Its distinction between eligibility and ultimate selection, and its qualified actual-adjudication method, retain their existing force. O’Dell v. Netherland and Tyler v. Cain are extended through their confined accuracy-and-bedrock principles; neither exception holding is narrowed. DeStefano v. Woods is distinguished at the capital-eligibility boundary and under the later Teague framework.

Caspari v. Bohlen and Horn v. Banks supply the obligation to resolve the preserved retroactivity defense. Lindh v. Murphy and Carriger v. Stewart preserve the petition’s former-law merits regime, while Slack v. McDaniel separately governs appellate certification. Brecht v. Abrahamson, as applied in O’Neal v. McAninch, supplies the State-borne collateral effect standard. Sullivan v. Louisiana retains its distinct rule for the absence of a valid verdict-wide reasonable-doubt determination.

## Law After Decision

Ring’s indispensable present-offense death-eligibility factfinding protection is available on collateral review through a confined watershed application. The rule remains procedural, and ordinary finality rules continue outside that category. Each qualifying petitioner must establish the actual violation and satisfy the otherwise applicable conditions for relief; the State bears the governing beyond-reasonable-doubt effect burden for a properly reached nonstructural omission.

## Separate Writings

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela concurs, joined by Justices Stevens and Souter. Ring’s qualified actual-adjudication method requires attention to the actual instructions, the evidence they directed to the jury, necessary findings and material conflicts. Genuine equivalent jury findings can establish noncontribution but are not the exclusive route: reliable whole-record inferences must remain connected to the actual adjudication. New credibility findings, sufficient evidence for a hypothetical verdict and appellate reweighing that supplies a missing eligibility adjudication cannot substitute for that inquiry. The State’s burden under Brecht remains separate from the finding of retroactive availability.

Justice Ginsburg concurs. She joins the procedural classification, the confined watershed holding and the bounded remand, but retains her view that harmlessness for the omitted eligibility determination requires genuinely equivalent actual findings. A hypothetical jury verdict or the court’s assessment that the evidence was strong cannot supply them. Her narrower method does not turn retroactivity alone into an entitlement to sentence relief.

Justice Breyer concurs. His support rests on the jury’s independent collective role in reliable capital adjudication, including the community evaluation required by an aggravator such as exceptional cruelty. He joins the confined eligibility holding without adopting the general Apprendi theory for noncapital sentencing, and preserves his broader Eighth Amendment view of the jury’s role in choosing death without making that additional issue part of this decision.

Justice Scalia, joined by Justices O’Connor, Kennedy and Thomas, concurs in procedural classification and dissents from retroactivity. Constitutional allocation of authority on direct review does not establish the extraordinary accuracy showing needed to reopen final judgments. Arizona already required aggravating facts to be proved beyond reasonable doubt, and a competent judge’s findings are not shown to create the necessary impermissible accuracy risk merely because a jury is constitutionally required. The evaluative nature and capital importance of the finding do not, in their view, bridge that gap. O’Dell’s misleading-parole-premise protection and Tyler’s invalid verdict-wide burden remain distinct. They would reverse the Ring-based grant under Teague and preserve genuinely remaining independent claims.

The case returns for determination of the actual eligibility error and the remaining collateral requirements under the petition’s applicable former-law regime. Conditional sentence relief depends on those determinations and the State’s failure to establish the required noncontribution; further sentencing must have its own lawful authority.

## Event

Tennard v. Dretke, No. 02-10038, 542 U.S. 274. Argued March 22, 2004; decided June 24, 2004. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit, 317 F.3d 476.

Tennard introduced an IQ score of 67 at the capital penalty phase. The special issues addressed deliberateness and future dangerousness. The lower federal courts rejected his claim and denied a certificate of appealability under a heightened relevance screen requiring a uniquely severe permanent handicap and a causal connection to the crime. The questions are whether those requirements are lawful and whether the mitigation claim warrants an issue-specific appeal.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The judgment denying a certificate of appealability is reversed, and the case is remanded for an issue-specific appeal under the proper relevance and certification standards.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Scalia and Thomas.

The court of appeals must issue the appropriate certificate and adjudicate the preserved mitigation claim. The conviction and sentence remain subject to the ordinary merits and remedial process; certification alone supplies no sentencing writ.

## Opinion Topology

KENNEDY, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O’CONNOR, SOUTER, GINSBURG and BREYER, JJ., joined. SCALIA and THOMAS, JJ., each filed a dissenting opinion.

## Holdings

### I. Supported culpability mitigation does not require a special diagnosis or crime nexus

**Holding and operative rule.** Supported intellectual-functioning and other culpability mitigation cannot be excluded from constitutional consideration merely because the defendant has not proved a uniquely severe permanent impairment or a causal connection between the impairment and the offense. Evidence may bear on culpability independently of deliberateness or future danger; relevance requires neither a completed categorical disability diagnosis nor favorable weight from the sentencer.

**Authority.** The seven Justices joining the Court’s opinion support the relevance rule and the certificate holding in Part II.

**Controlling explanation.** Graham v. Collins rejects the permanent-handicap restriction and protects the independent culpability significance of supported mitigation. Johnson v. Texas preserves that significance even when some evidence also bears on future danger. Penry v. Johnson requires a coherent, authorized means for the jury to give the evidence effect beyond the factual special issues. Tennard’s low IQ score supplies a supported basis for a culpability argument, even though it does not itself establish adaptive limitations, developmental onset or the categorical disability addressed in Atkins v. Virginia. Evidence that might have an aggravating use under future danger can still have an independent mitigating use. The dissenters would reconsider this underlying entitlement because of its relationship to structured capital discretion. The Court adheres to the settled distinction: directing the jury’s factual decisions does not permit legal exclusion of supported mitigation bearing independently on culpability.

### II. This claim warrants an issue-specific certificate

**Holding and operative rule.** A certificate of appealability issues when the applicant makes a substantial showing that reasonable jurists could debate the constitutional claim under the habeas restrictions actually governing it. That threshold exceeds mere nonfrivolousness but does not require conclusive proof of constitutional error or statutory entitlement before the authorized appeal; Tennard satisfies it on the preserved effective-mitigation claim.

**Controlling explanation.** Slack v. McDaniel separates certification from the final merits, and Miller-El v. Cockrell requires meaningful debate about the claim as constrained by applicable habeas law. The IQ evidence and the submitted special issues make it reasonably debatable whether the jury had an effective route to use the evidence’s independent mitigating force. The unlawful heightened-relevance gate prevented the proper threshold assessment. On appeal the court must examine the complete charge and trial context, identify the relevant state merits adjudication and statutory limits, and resolve any properly preserved procedural bar. If it establishes a properly available original-charge mitigation defect, Brecht’s governing rule, as applied in Penry, places on the State the burden to show beyond a reasonable doubt on the complete penalty record that the error did not contribute to death. Failure then requires lawful conditional sentence correction. These are successive adjudicative questions; the supported threshold showing does not resolve their ultimate answers.

## Precedent Treatment

Graham v. Collins supplies the rejection of the uniquely severe permanent-handicap screen and protection for independent culpability mitigation. Johnson v. Texas preserves effective consideration beyond the factual special issues. Penry v. Johnson supplies the coherent-verdict requirement and separates the constitutional/statutory inquiry from effect review. Atkins v. Virginia concerns categorical intellectual disability and is distinguished from ordinary mitigation relevance.

Slack v. McDaniel and Miller-El v. Cockrell are applied to issue-specific certification under the applicable habeas restrictions. Brecht v. Abrahamson, as applied by Penry, governs any properly reached original-charge mitigation error’s separate State-borne effect inquiry.

## Law After Decision

Capital mitigation relevance cannot be screened through a uniquely severe permanent-handicap requirement or an offense-causation requirement. Supported low intellectual functioning can justify debate over the jury’s effective ability to use independent culpability mitigation. Courts must issue a certificate when that substantial debate exists under the governing habeas limits, and then separately adjudicate the complete merits and any remedy.

## Separate Writings

Justice Scalia dissents. He would reconsider the Penry, Graham and Johnson mitigation entitlement because he regards its demand for discretionary mitigation beyond the special issues as conflicting with the constitutional requirement to structure capital discretion. His position rejects the underlying entitlement rather than presenting the Fifth Circuit’s displaced heightened-relevance gate as current law. On that ground he would affirm denial of certification.

Justice Thomas dissents. He maintains his objection to extending a constitutional requirement of individualized mitigation beyond the structured capital decision and would deny stare-decisis force to that broader requirement. His disagreement is independent of any factual presumption or unmade finding about Tennard’s impairment. He would affirm the certificate denial because he would reconsider the constitutional premise of the claim.

## Procedure After Action

The court of appeals must issue an issue-specific certificate and decide the preserved mitigation claim under the correct relevance rule and applicable habeas conditions. If final adjudication establishes the requisite original-charge violation and the State fails its beyond-reasonable-doubt effect showing, conditional relief must provide a reasonable court-fixed period for lawful sentencing correction or a lawful noncapital sentence.
