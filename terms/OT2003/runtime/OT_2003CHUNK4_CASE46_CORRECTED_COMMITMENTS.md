## Common law and record boundaries

Article 17 requires an accident causing the injury aboard the aircraft or during embarking or disembarking. Saks, 470 U.S. 392, 405-406, defines the accident as an unexpected or unusual event or happening external to the passenger, applies that definition flexibly to all the circumstances, permits any qualifying causal link, and excludes an injury that results solely from the passenger's internal reaction to usual, normal, expected aircraft operation. An unusual injury is not enough. Fault and accident are different questions. Intentional conduct is not excluded merely because the actor intended it; Saks's examples include intentional attacks.

The current Zicherman holding, unanimous under O'Connor's opinion, keeps treaty liability conditions separate from domestic compensable losses. The current Tseng holding, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg, makes treaty conditions exclusive within the Article 17 field, preserves Saks unchanged, and does not decide the accident classification of the search there. Stevens dissented from that judgment; the slice supplies no additional ground from his separate writing, and none is invented. Tseng binds this case despite that dissent. The unavailability of an alternative local action cannot itself relax Article 17.

Chan, 490 U.S. 122, 127-135, supplies the binding prohibition against adding treaty consequences on policy grounds when the text does not supply them. Scalia authored that opinion; O'Connor and Kennedy joined. Stevens joined Brennan's concurrence, id. at 136-152, which examined context and history but rejected converting a private notice standard into a treaty condition. Chan does not classify this refusal and cannot mechanically settle the present fork.

The full sequence matters. The first response directed respondent to sit. The second refused reseating because the plane was said to be full and the attendant too busy. The third again asserted a full plane, proposed that respondent find another passenger to exchange seats without crew help, and denied assistance even after respondent proposed the cockpit or business class. Eleven passenger seats were empty, most in economy; 15 of 28 non-revenue passengers occupied economy seats farther from smoke. These facts establish neither entitlement to a particular seat nor the independent effectiveness of a particular hypothetical accommodation. The trial finding that the refusals were unusual in light of policy and practice is not converted into a treaty command to perform every requested service.

## Tested lawful paths

**Path A: affirm on the unusual-refusal event.** A repeated, explicit refusal of available assistance, after notice of a passenger's medical vulnerability and contrary to the established ordinary practice, can be the external unusual event in the causal chain. The event is the actual interaction and repeated responses, considered in context; injury is not made an accident merely because care could have prevented it. The principal objection is that calling a failure to act an event lets every negligent omission evade Saks. The limiting answer is that fault alone remains insufficient: the claimant must identify unusual external conduct and prove its causal contribution. The ordinary smoke and asthma do not foreclose another causal link. On the accepted findings, no new independent false-statement finding is necessary to this path.

**Path B: reject omission as the sufficient event; vacate and remand on the affirmative-statement alternative.** Article 17 still requires an event that occurred; unusualness and negligence cannot supply that requirement when the asserted cause is a service not performed. Repeating a refusal does not by itself transform nonperformance into an accident. The principal objection is that this divides a concrete interaction into artificial verbal and nonverbal pieces and disregards Saks's contextual causal-chain instruction. The answer is that the unusual-event and causal-link requirements are cumulative; flexible application cannot dispense with either. The affirmative statement that the aircraft was full is a potentially distinct event, because it conveys false information about available accommodation. It is not automatically causal merely because the record proves it false. This path therefore permits the lower court to consider that event's causal significance under the existing record and ordinary preservation rules, without inventing reliance, a new finding, or a new claim. It rejects immediate affirmance on that separate theory and also rejects a categorical direction to enter judgment for the carrier while that supported alternative remains for consideration.

**Excluded shortcuts.** No liability merely for breach of airline policy, every medical emergency, every refusal, every intentional act, or smoking aboard an aircraft. No assumption that the false statement caused the death. No automatic revival of a local tort claim after a treaty failure. No new damages category or alteration of an existing damages award independently of the liability disposition. The possible affirmative-statement theory is an analytical alternative developed from supplied facts, not a claim that counsel actually advanced or preserved that distinct theory.

## Justice-specific commitments

### Stevens

**Judgment and rule:** Affirm. Join Path A: this unusual series of express refusals after notice of the medical problem is an external event within Saks and may supply the necessary causal link. The affirmed liability rests on the accepted unusual-refusal findings and their causal role, not solely on asthma, smoke, or negligent service.

**Own authorities and two-path comparison:** Stevens joined Saks, including its flexible application and any-link rule. His Chan concurrence join supports reading the actual interaction in context while retaining a genuine treaty condition; it also bars treating company practice as self-executing treaty liability. His Zicherman join keeps damages separate. Path B's strongest point is that unmet expectations alone cannot turn ordinary carriage into an accident. Stevens accepts that restriction but rejects applying it to erase the actual repeated responses and their causal role. Tseng's controlling exclusivity rule is accepted here; his earlier dissent is not used to supply an alternative remedy.

**Affirmative statement, remedy, and joins:** The false full-plane statements reinforce the characterization of the whole interaction; they are not an independently sufficient finding of deceptive causation. Join affirmance without a new trial. Would not join an opinion equating fault with accident or deciding the separate misinformation theory on invented reliance. No separate writing is necessary if those limits remain explicit.

### O'Connor

**Judgment and rule:** Vacate and remand. Reject refusal of assistance, treated simply as nonperformance, as a sufficient accident; retain the independently testable false-full-plane event for lower-court consideration under Path B.

**Own authorities and two-path comparison:** O'Connor's Saks opinion deliberately distinguishes an accident causing injury from the injury and from a mere occurrence, while allowing any qualifying external link. Her Chan join enforces distinctions in the treaty's chosen conditions rather than creating consequences from related standards. Her current Zicherman authorship and Tseng opinion preserve the liability gate independently of damages and remedial sympathy. Path A's strongest answer is that express responses actually occurred and their unusualness is established. The commitment nevertheless treats words conveying a refusal as insufficient when their only asserted causal significance is that assistance was not supplied; ordinary-service expectations cannot substitute for an event. This does not reject all consequences of speech or require an accident to be unintended by the attendant.

**Affirmative statement, remedy, and joins:** The false statement about full occupancy is affirmative information and may stand differently if it actually obstructed an available response. Its separate causal role is not established in this packet. Would join remand to consider that ground if properly available on the existing record, not outright affirmance on it and not a direction to dismiss every possible treaty ground. Would not join a categorical no-omission exception covering every failure in aircraft operation. A separate writing would explain the event/nonperformance distinction and why the affirmative-statement alternative warrants remand.

### Scalia

**Judgment and rule:** Vacate and remand. Join Path B: an accident must be an event, and a refusal whose causal content is solely failure to furnish service does not satisfy that requirement merely because it is unusual.

**Own authorities and two-path comparison:** Scalia's Chan opinion makes the text's distinctions decisive and refuses to import a liability consequence from a related obligation. His Zicherman and Tseng joins retain the Convention's conditions even where another law would provide damages or a remedy. Saks's any-link rule is fully accepted, but each asserted link must itself qualify. Path A's strongest objection is that a spoken refusal is a happening in ordinary language and that Saks demands context. Scalia's commitment distinguishes the speech act from the withheld assistance: if the causal theory identifies only aid not rendered, the external happening has not been shown to be the injury-causing accident. That objection does not foreclose a false affirmative representation with its own causal effect.

**Affirmative statement, remedy, and joins:** The inaccurate full-plane representation can be analyzed as an affirmative event. A remand is needed to determine its legally available and factually supported causal role; the existence of vacant seats alone proves neither reliance nor prevention of successful assistance. Would join O'Connor's stated ground at that level of generality, including remand, without adopting a general immunity for all omissions. Would not join affirmance based solely on breach of industry practice, nor grant the carrier final judgment before the independent-statement alternative is addressed. No foreign-law consensus is asserted.

### Kennedy

**Judgment and rule:** Affirm. Join Path A on the actual sequence of unusual external responses and its established causal contribution.

**Own authorities and two-path comparison:** Kennedy's Chan join requires fidelity to the treaty condition rather than a judicial addition to it. His Zicherman and Tseng joins preserve the accident gate. The competing Path B objection is substantial because calling every nonresponse an event would empty that gate. Here the combination of repeated express refusals, a stated lack of capacity contrary to actual availability, and the identified departure from ordinary practice supplies a concrete interaction; applying Saks to that interaction adds no general duty to Article 17. Neither Chan's ticket distinction nor Tseng's exclusivity rule requires treating that interaction as nonexistent.

**Affirmative statement, remedy, and joins:** Join the causal-chain holding without requiring the external event to be the sole or final physiological cause. The occupancy statements are part of the interaction, not an independently adjudicated deception claim. Affirm the liability judgment; no remand is needed on a separate ground once the existing refusal ground is sufficient. Withhold any proposition that policy violations alone establish accidents or that a passenger's subjective surprise supplies unusualness.

### Souter

**Judgment and rule:** Affirm. Join Path A, applying Saks to the supported circumstances as a whole while maintaining separate accident and causation requirements.

**Own authorities and two-path comparison:** Souter's Zicherman and Tseng joins preserve the treaty's actual liability conditions and Saks's flexible rule. Those joins supply constraints, not a preexisting answer about omissions. Path B properly insists that some event must have occurred; the stronger application here is that the attendant's repeated explicit responses to a disclosed problem were events, and the lower court's supported characterization as unusual is pertinent without becoming conclusive on the legal question. The accepted causal connection of that interaction is enough under Saks even though ordinary smoke also contributed.

**Affirmative statement, remedy, and joins:** The full-plane statements make a categorical non-event description less persuasive, but do not authorize a new causal finding about reliance. Join affirmance on the unusual-refusal ground and decline a remand unnecessary to that sufficient basis. A narrower opinion limited to these supported refusals is acceptable; an opinion establishing general service-negligence liability or resolving unpresented damage rules is not.

### Thomas

**Judgment and rule:** Affirm. Join Path A because a deliberate external response can be an unusual event within Article 17; deliberateness is not an exception to Saks.

**Own authorities and two-path comparison:** Thomas's Zicherman join and full Associate joins in Tseng retain distinct treaty conditions and reject domestic law as a substitute. His Tseng join also accepts that intentional touching alone does not establish Article 25 misconduct; this prevents conflating an intended act, an Article 17 accident, and willful misconduct. Those are separate inquiries. Path B's strongest textual point is that an omission is not a happening. Thomas's commitment answers it at the level of this record: express repeated responses to requests occurred outside the passenger; they were found unusual and causally significant. No treaty text supplied here excludes such a response because it communicates a refusal. Saks's intentional-event examples reinforce that classification without creating a negligence test.

**Affirmative statement, remedy, and joins:** The false occupancy statements support considering the actual responses rather than an abstract failure to act. They do not independently establish causal reliance, Article 25 misconduct, or changed liability limits. Join affirmance without reopening damages or making a finding on those matters. Withhold broad language treating all omissions as accidents or intention as sufficient to overcome a treaty limitation.

### Ginsburg

**Judgment and rule:** Affirm. Join Path A: the established unusual conduct of the carrier's attendant is an external link distinct from the passenger's internal asthma response and from ordinary cabin smoke.

**Own authorities and two-path comparison:** Ginsburg's Zicherman join and Tseng joins preserve uniform liability conditions while leaving domestic loss categories in their proper field. They do not authorize a court to widen Article 17 merely because Tseng forecloses local damages. Path B's strongest argument is that an airline's failure to prevent harm is not automatically a treaty accident. Ginsburg accepts that rule but distinguishes the repeated, explicit, factually unusual interaction shown here. The flexible Saks inquiry is satisfied by the supported interaction, not by substituting compensation policy or a general standard of reasonable care.

**Affirmative statement, remedy, and joins:** Join the external-link and causal-chain propositions and affirm on the trial-supported refusal theory. The false occupancy statements are relevant circumstances; their independent causal effect remains unadjudicated because the accepted ground suffices. Would not join a rule making every ordinary illness compensable, dispensing with proof of causation, or treating foreign uniformity as established from unread judgments. No separate writing is necessary if these limits remain.
