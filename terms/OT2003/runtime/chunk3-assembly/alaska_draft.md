## Controlling holdings

**Authority for both holdings:** {{AUTHOR}}'s opinion, joined by {{OTHER_JOINERS}}: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer supply six direct votes for both rules, their explanations and application.

**1. EPA may enforce the substantive BACT requirement within the State's reasoned decisional range.** Sections 113(a)(5) and 167 authorize EPA to prevent construction that fails the Clean Air Act's prevention-of-significant-deterioration requirements, including section 165(a)(4)'s best-available-control-technology requirement. EPA may intervene when a state BACT determination lacks a reasoned basis under section 169(3)'s maximum-achievable-reduction standard and its energy, environmental, economic and other-cost factors, but may not replace a supported state choice merely because it prefers another permissible technology.

The Court reads the state assignment and federal enforcement provisions together. The permitting authority makes the case-specific determination, while the separately enacted federal construction provisions enforce substantive compliance. A permit bearing the BACT label cannot conclusively establish compliance with the requirement it must satisfy. EPA must identify and demonstrate the actual defect; the State retains the permissible range of reasoned choice. That limit answers the objection that federal enforcement turns EPA into an unrestricted appellate permitting authority. State review does not displace the federal enforcement channels Congress supplied.

Mead and Christensen distinguish statutory authority from an agency's persuasive advice. Walton and Heimmermann require an actual grant and its authorized exercise. The Court independently identifies the enforcement power in this Act; EPA's experience may inform persuasion but creates no power of its own. Bragg likewise preserves a retained federal command after state primacy without converting every discretionary state judgment into a nondiscretionary federal duty.

**2. EPA reasonably identified the defect in Alaska's explanation.** Review under 5 U.S.C. §706(2)(A) sustains EPA's orders when the administrative record supports its conclusion that the State's BACT explanation falls outside the reasoned statutory range. Alaska's abandoned offset rationale and unsupported economic reversal meet that standard here; the operative federal orders are sustained as modified.

Alaska initially found selective catalytic reduction technically and economically feasible. It then abandoned the offset theory supporting a different choice and invoked costs and competitiveness while acknowledging that it lacked the information necessary to assess those consequences. A rural-utility analogy and the mine's regional importance did not establish the asserted effect on this mine. The defect is the missing connection in the State's actual reasoning. Costs remain a statutory consideration, and the top-down method was Alaska's chosen method rather than a universal statutory command.

EPA's path is reasonably discernible from its orders and explanatory correspondence. The court reviews that path rather than supplying reasons or selecting technology. The State's acknowledged information gap does not automatically require one control technology whenever a financial statement is absent. The required reasoned explanation preserves the State's role while giving the substantive federal requirement effect.

## Public Projection

## Event

**Alaska Department of Environmental Conservation v. EPA, No. 02-658.** Argued October 8, 2003; decided January 21, 2004. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 298 F.3d 814. The questions are whether the Clean Air Act authorizes EPA to prevent construction when a state BACT determination lacks a reasoned statutory basis and whether EPA lawfully exercised that authority for the Red Dog Mine's MG-17 generator. Alaska initially identified SCR as feasible, then approved a different choice on an offset rationale and later economic reasoning.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated in argument and decision.

## Public Action

The judgment of the Court of Appeals is affirmed.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 6–3. Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. **Opposing:** Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

The operative EPA construction restrictions remain effective according to their actual modified terms, including the March allowance for summer-dependent construction; the withdrawn December direction is not revived. The July 16, 2003 permit already selects SCR as BACT, subject to a decision in Alaska's favor in this case. Affirmance leaves that condition unsatisfied, so the permit's SCR term remains operative. A reasoned reconsideration through ordinary lawful agency procedures remains available; the judgment requires no fresh permit proceeding.

## Precedent Treatment

- **United States v. Mead and Christensen v. Harris County:** Applied to distinguish the independent identification of statutory power from persuasive agency reasoning.
- **Barnhart v. Walton and Heimmermann v. First Union Mortgage Corp.:** Applied for actual delegation and authorized exercise; expertise and uncertainty do not confer power.
- **Bragg v. West Virginia Coal Association:** Applied for the survival of an identified federal permitting command after state primacy; its nondiscretionary-duty holding does not establish federal review of every state judgment.
- **INS v. Ventura:** Its allocation of initial administrative judgment remains effective; judicial review identifies the statutory defect while the responsible agency makes any subsequent BACT determination.

## Law After Decision

EPA may enforce the requirement that a state BACT determination have a reasoned statutory basis. The State retains its case-specific weighing within the statutory range, and federal intervention requires an identified, record-supported deficiency. This application sustains the actual modified orders and leaves the existing conditional permit operative under its own terms.

## Separate Writings

**{{DISSENT_AUTHOR}}, dissenting, joined by {{DISSENT_JOINERS}}.** The Act specifically assigns BACT's case-specific weighing to the state permitting authority. The dissent reads the federal prevention provisions to enforce required permits, required consideration and independently specified federal conditions, but not to create a federal merits-review channel over the completed discretionary balance. A reasonableness limit describes how review would operate without establishing that Congress authorized EPA to conduct it. Weak state reasons therefore cannot themselves create that power. The dissent would reverse the federal interference on the allocation ground, giving effect to the July permit's Alaska-success condition according to its terms; it does not need to choose a superior technology.

## Procedure After Action

The mandate leaves the reviewed federal enforcement result in force as actually modified. The operator must comply with operative construction limits and the existing July permit. Any later state reconsideration must supply a reasoned BACT determination under all statutory factors, and EPA must assess that determination within the bounded enforcement authority stated above.
