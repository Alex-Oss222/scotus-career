## Event

**Doe v. Chao, No. 02-1377.** Argued December 3, 2003; decided February 24, 2004. Review of the Fourth Circuit's denial of the Privacy Act's$1,000 minimum without proof of actual damages.

The Department of Labor used and disclosed Social Security numbers in administering benefits claims. The district court accepted Doe's uncontroverted account of distress and awarded the statutory minimum. Only Doe seeks review of the monetary prerequisite.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The judgment is affirmed.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 6–3. Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. **Opposing:** Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The denial of Doe's monetary recovery remains in force. The separate prospective order retains its existing effect.

## Opinion Topology

Scalia delivered the opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. Ginsburg files a dissenting opinion, joined by Stevens and Breyer. The six-Justice common rationale rests on the enacted remedy's text and structure.

## Holdings

**Actual damages are a prerequisite to the Privacy Act's minimum monetary recovery.** A claimant seeking damages under 5 U.S.C. section 552a(g)(4) must establish actual damages caused by the qualifying intentional or willful violation before receiving the statutory minimum of $1,000. The adverse effect that permits suit under section 552a(g)(1)(D) does not by itself satisfy that additional monetary condition.

**Authority:** Scalia's opinion, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, controls the prerequisite, its application and affirmance.

The statute first identifies the conduct and culpability that permit a monetary claim and then specifies actual damages as the recoverable loss. The minimum follows that authorization and applies to a person entitled to recovery under it. Reading the proviso as an unconditional alternative to actual damages would detach entitlement from the immediately preceding payment condition. The opening liability language establishes the class that may invoke the remedy; it does not erase the limits Congress places on that remedy.

The contrary construction has force because a fixed minimum can compensate harms difficult to price. But Congress tied this minimum to an existing entitlement to recovery, rather than making every qualifying adverse effect independently sufficient for payment. The Court must enforce that distinction within the monetary authorization Congress enacted. The express monetary authorization is applied within its enacted terms.

J. Roderick MacArthur Foundation and Lance E. Lindblom v. Federal Bureau of Investigation distinguishes a personal adverse effect from final monetary entitlement and reserved this very question. Doe's accepted distress and the unchallenged violation and culpability establish the predicates assumed here; they do not resolve the separate actual-damages prerequisite. Because Doe seeks the minimum without the additional showing required by the judgment under review, the denial stands. The complete definition of actual damages, including compensable emotional loss and its required proof, lies outside the granted question and remains open.

## Precedent Treatment

**J. Roderick MacArthur Foundation and Lance E. Lindblom v. Federal Bureau of Investigation:** Reaffirmed at its personal-adverse-effect scope. Its reserved minimum-recovery question is resolved by requiring actual damages before the minimum becomes available; its adverse-effect holding is not converted into a monetary award.

## Law After Decision

A Privacy Act plaintiff must distinguish the violation, culpability and adverse-effect predicates from the actual-damages condition for monetary recovery. Once qualifying actual damages are established, the statutory minimum governs the amount recoverable when those damages are smaller. The complete definition and proof of actual damages remain outside this decision's granted scope.

## Separate Writings

**Ginsburg, joined by Stevens and Breyer, dissenting.** They would restore Doe's $1,000 award. In their reading, the opening liability command identifies the persons entitled to recovery once an intentional or willful violation causes a genuine adverse effect, and the proviso supplies an independent minimum for those persons. The actual-damages language permits larger compensation for proved loss rather than imposing a further threshold on the minimum.

They distinguish a fixed amount expressly enacted by Congress from unlimited presumed general damages. The intentional-or-willful requirement and genuine adverse effect restrict recovery and answer the concern that a harmless technical violation would produce an award. Breyer's emphasis on that culpability limit is part of the shared dissent. MacArthur left this monetary issue open; its separation of injury from ultimate recovery does not, for the dissenters, decide which reading of the proviso is correct.

## Procedure After Action

The Fourth Circuit's monetary judgment stands. The separate prospective obligations continue under their existing terms.
