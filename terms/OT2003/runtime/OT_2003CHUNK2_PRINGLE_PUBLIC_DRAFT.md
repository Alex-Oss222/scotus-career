## Event

Maryland v. Pringle, No. 02-809; 540 U.S. 366. Argued November 3, 2003; decided December 15, 2003. On writ of certiorari to the Court of Appeals of Maryland, 370 Md. 525, 805 A.2d 1016. The question is whether the known combination of packaged cocaine, cash and shared travel supplied probable cause to arrest Pringle, the front-seat passenger. Police stopped the small private car for speeding at about 3:16 a.m.; the driver consented to a search. Officers found $763 in rolled cash in the glove compartment and five cocaine baggies concealed behind the rear-seat armrest. The three occupants supplied no ownership information. Maryland seeks reversal of the arrest-based suppression ruling; Pringle seeks exclusion of his ensuing confession and relief from the conviction.

## Participation

Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The arrest-based suppression judgment is reversed and the case remanded.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The lawful arrest supplies no ground to suppress the ensuing confession as the product of an unlawful seizure. The state court must implement that correction and decide any independently preserved admissibility or trial issue before settling the conviction’s ultimate status.

## Holdings

### I. Personal probable cause can rest on a supported common-possession inference

**Controlling proposition:** Officers may arrest a vehicle occupant for possession when facts known at arrest, considered together, establish a fair probability that the occupant knowingly exercises sole or joint dominion or control over the contraband. The Government must identify the particular facts supporting that common-possession inference; mere presence, a general passenger presumption or silence alone is insufficient, while exclusive ownership need not be established.

**Authority:** The complete Court opinion has nine direct joins and controls both holdings.

**Controlling explanation:** Maryland possession law permits individual or joint knowing control. The Fourth Amendment requires a practical probability of that participation, rather than trial proof or a division of probability among three possible exclusive owners. Ornelas requires independent review of the legal conclusion with proper protection for historical facts and grounded inferences. Wardlow and Valentine require the actual connection to wrongdoing; they do not permit a category or association label to supply it.

Ybarra protects a customer against suspicion arising merely from presence among strangers in a public establishment. Di Re requires attention to affirmative information identifying another participant. Both preserve individualized cause, without forbidding a supported inference of joint possession. Houghton’s detached-container rule governs the scope of an already justified automobile search; it does not presume passenger guilt or a criminal enterprise and supplies no personal arrest cause here. The search’s validity and the arrest’s justification therefore remain separate inquiries.

### II. The combined circumstances connected Pringle to the cocaine

**Controlling proposition:** The five separately packaged cocaine baggies accessible within the common passenger compartment, associated rolled cash, small private automobile and shared early-morning travel together supplied probable cause that Pringle participated in sole or joint possession. This conclusion rests on the contemporaneous combination, including the drugs’ concealed location, rather than his later confession.

**Authority:** The same unanimous opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** The cocaine was concealed behind the upright rear armrest, not observed in Pringle’s sight or found on his person. The state court’s strongest objection—that an innocent passenger could travel without knowing of hidden drugs—is therefore substantial. Its majority and concurrence did not confuse probable cause with proof beyond a reasonable doubt. But the known packaged cocaine, its accessibility in the common passenger space, the cash directly before the front passenger and the common journey supported the practical inference of shared knowing possession. The possibility of an unwitting passenger did not make that inference unreasonable.

Cash and the late hour alone would establish neither a crime nor participation. The occupants’ failure to identify an owner adds no independent badge of guilt and imposes no duty to confess; it means only that reliable information identifying a different exclusive possessor did not displace the otherwise supported joint inference. That differs from Di Re’s affirmative identification and Ybarra’s incidental public-place association. Houghton supplies no shortcut around this independent assessment. Pringle’s later confession and account of the others’ knowledge cannot supply facts the officer lacked at arrest. The supported common-possession combination resolves this arrest, while preserving the requirement of an actual personal connection in materially different circumstances.

## Precedent Treatment

- Ornelas v. United States: applied to independent legal review of probable cause, with historical findings and grounded factual inferences retaining their proper treatment.
- Illinois v. Wardlow and United States v. Valentine: applied to the requirement of a supported personal connection to wrongdoing; the actual cocaine and common-transport evidence meet that requirement here under the higher probable-cause threshold.
- Ybarra v. Illinois: distinguished because this supported shared-transport inference differs from incidental presence among customers in a public establishment.
- United States v. Di Re: distinguished because officers lacked its affirmative information identifying a different transaction participant; joint possession remains distinct from exclusive ownership.
- Wyoming v. Houghton: its object-based detached-container search rule remains separate from personal arrest cause and supplies no presumption of a common criminal enterprise.
- United States v. Banks: its contemporaneous, particularized warrant-execution analysis remains within the distinct home-entry and reasonable-suspicion setting.

## Law After Decision

A supported common-possession inference can establish probable cause for more than one occupant without proof of exclusive ownership. Courts must identify the contemporaneous personal connection in the complete circumstances. The combination here justifies Pringle’s arrest, while the vehicle-search rule and independently preserved confession-admissibility requirements retain their separate force.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns to the Maryland courts for proceedings consistent with lawful arrest. Arrest-based derivative exclusion must be removed. Any genuinely preserved independent voluntariness, waiver, suppression or trial issue must be resolved under its own governing law; the probable-cause ruling does not itself establish a valid waiver.
