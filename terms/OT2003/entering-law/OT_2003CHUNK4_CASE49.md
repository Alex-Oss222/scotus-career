#### FDIC v. Meyer, 510 U.S. 471

**Docket or dockets:** No. 92-741.

**Decided:** 1994-02-23.

##### FTCA exclusivity does not encompass this federal constitutional claim

**Holding:** Section 2679(a) makes FTCA relief exclusive as against an agency's sue-and-be-sued authority only for claims cognizable under the whole of §1346(b), including its private-person and state-law requirements. Meyer's federal constitutional tort is outside that category, so §2679(a) does not bar this suit in the agency's own name; this statutory conclusion neither supplies a cause of action nor guarantees recovery.

**Proposition-level authority:** The statutory-exclusivity portion of Thomas's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg. All nine adopt this separate threshold holding.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The §1346(b) category requires a claim against the United States for money damages for property injury or loss, personal injury or death caused by an employee's negligent or wrongful act or omission within employment scope, under circumstances in which a private person would be liable under the law of the place. That last requirement means state substantive law; §2679(b)(1)'s differently worded employee rule is not imported.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Richards v. United States, 369 U.S. 1: applies the FTCA's state-law source and private-person limitation.
- Miree v. DeKalb County, 433 U.S. 25: applies state law as the substantive source under the law-of-the-place provision.
- Loeffler v. Frank, 486 U.S. 549: preserves FTCA coordination while specifying that §2679(a) refers to cognizability under §1346(b), not everything described as tortious.
- United States v. Smith, 499 U.S. 160: distinguishes employee exclusivity under §2679(b)(1) and its treatment of the foreign-country exception from the differently worded agency provision.
- Hubsch v. United States, 338 U.S. 440: preserves cognizability despite failure of proof on an element; distinguishes a claim that never falls within §1346(b)'s category.

##### Former FSLIC's sue-and-be-sued clause waives immunity

**Holding:** Former 12 U.S.C. §1725(c)(4) waives FSLIC's sovereign immunity for this constitutional tort claim, even though a private entity ordinarily would not bear that constitutional duty. An implied restriction on its broad sue-and-be-sued authority requires a clear showing that the suit is inconsistent with the statutory or constitutional scheme, that restriction is necessary to avoid grave interference with governmental functions, or that Congress plainly intended the clause in a narrow sense; FDIC establishes none here.

**Proposition-level authority:** The waiver portion of Thomas's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg. Nine Justices adopt the specific waiver and its stated limits.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- FHA v. Burr, 309 U.S. 242: applies liberal construction and all three grounds for a clearly established implied restriction; mere absence of private constitutional liability does not establish one.
- Loeffler v. Frank, 486 U.S. 549: applies the broad waiver rule while keeping separate statutory limits distinct.
- Franchise Tax Board v. United States Postal Service, 467 U.S. 512: preserves private-business liability as support for the incidents of suit recognized there, without turning it into an outer boundary for every waiver.
- United States v. Nordic Village, February 25, 1992: preserves the requirement to identify the particular sovereign consent; the ambiguous bankruptcy provision there does not narrow this express agency clause.

##### Waiver does not create a direct agency Bivens action

**Holding:** A waiver of sovereign immunity removes a defense to suit but does not itself authorize constitutional damages; Bivens does not supply Meyer a direct damages cause of action against FSLIC. Extending an individual-officer action to the federal agency would create a distinct defendant and public obligation, rather than merely provide relief within an existing agency action.

**Proposition-level authority:** The cause-of-action portion of Thomas's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg. All nine adopt this independently sufficient ground for rejecting the agency award.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- United States v. Mitchell, 463 U.S. 206: applies the analytical separation of sovereign consent and a substantive source authorizing relief.
- Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents, 403 U.S. 388: retains its individual-officer action and declines extension directly to a federal agency.
- Davis v. Passman, 442 U.S. 228: preserves the individual employment-discrimination action; it does not supply an agency defendant here.
- Carlson v. Green, 446 U.S. 14: preserves individual-officer liability and its deterrent rationale without making every useful compensatory arrangement an authorized agency action.
- Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools, February 26, 1992: preserves appropriate relief within an established private action; distinguishes creation of a new action and defendant.

**Independent alternative holding:** Even assuming that a direct agency remedy could advance the compensatory and deterrent purposes of Bivens, the resulting expansion of direct federal financial liability and redesign of the remedial structure counsel against judicial creation of that remedy. Those institutional considerations independently require rejection of this implied agency damages action; the Court does not find that Meyer received an adequate substitute remedy.

**Proposition-level authority for alternative holding:** The independent fiscal-and-remedial portion of Thomas's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg. Nine Justices adopt this independently sufficient alternative ground.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The $130,000 agency award cannot stand for want of an agency cause. Pattullo's separate immunity judgment is outside review and remains undisturbed. The Court does not decide Meyer's property entitlement, the underlying due-process violation, entitlement under another source, a universal federal waiver, or elimination of existing individual Bivens remedies. Former §1725(c)(4), rather than a silently substituted successor clause, governs this 1982 agency claim. Section 2679(b)(1)'s separate employee rule is not rewritten.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents, 403 U.S. 388: applies its special-factors limitation to the proposed direct public liability, independently of the individual-defendant distinction.
- United States v. Standard Oil Co. of California, 332 U.S. 301: applies the concern against judicial determination of a new federal fiscal obligation.
- Bush v. Lucas, 462 U.S. 367: applies institutional restraint concerning a new damages remedy; does not find that Bush's civil-service scheme governed or compensated Meyer.
- Schweiker v. Chilicky, 487 U.S. 412: applies caution about remedy creation despite incomplete compensation, without importing its Social Security system as Meyer's actual remedy.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The agency damages judgment is reversed and the $130,000 award cannot stand for want of a direct agency cause. Pattullo's separate, unreviewed immunity judgment remains undisturbed. No constitutional merits finding or new damages trial is ordered.

### Agency FTCA exclusivity and sue-and-be-sued immunity

**Governing question:** How FTCA agency exclusivity interacts with an express sue-and-be-sued clause.

**Current rule:** Section 2679(a) makes FTCA relief exclusive against agency sue-and-be-sued authority only for claims cognizable under the whole of §1346(b), including its private-person and state-law requirements. Former §1725(c)(4)’s broad FSLIC clause waives immunity for such a claim; an implied restriction requires a clear showing of inconsistency with the statutory or constitutional scheme, necessity to avoid grave interference with governmental functions, or plain congressional intent to use the clause narrowly. Waiver does not supply a substantive cause of action or establish recovery.

### No direct federal-agency Bivens damages action

**Governing question:** Whether sovereign consent itself permits constitutional damages against a federal agency.

**Current rule:** A waiver removes sovereign immunity but does not create constitutional damages liability. Bivens’s individual-officer action does not extend directly to a federal agency: that extension would create a new defendant and public obligation. Independently, even assuming compensation and deterrence benefits, the expansion of direct federal fiscal liability and redesign of the remedial structure counsel against judicial creation of that agency action. Existing individual Bivens, Davis and Carlson actions and genuinely authorized statutory remedies retain their own scope; no adequate substitute remedy is presumed.

## Earlier current-term public authority: verizon_communications_v_trinko_merits_2004-01-13

**Effective:** 2004-01-13. **Authority source:** [verizon_communications_v_trinko_merits_2004-01-13.md](../records/verizon_communications_v_trinko_merits_2004-01-13.md), Public Projection only.

## Event

**Verizon Communications Inc. v. Law Offices of Curtis V. Trinko, LLP, No. 02-682.** Decided January 13, 2004, on merits submission. The Court reviews the Second Circuit's reinstatement of an antitrust complaint alleging discriminatory performance of an incumbent telephone carrier's network-access obligations. The questions concern independent monopoly-maintenance conduct, the customer's entitlement to damages, and its request for prospective relief.

Trinko is an AT&T customer. AT&T obtains local-network access from Verizon. The complaint alleges that Verizon degraded rival-customer order processing to discourage rival patronage. Federal and state regulators investigated access failures and imposed remedial measures and financial consequences. The complaint seeks damages and an injunction providing rivals access on terms at least as favorable as Verizon's own. The case comes at the pleading stage.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated in submission and decision.

## Public Action

The judgment is reversed in part and vacated in part, and the case is remanded.

The requested access-parity decree is not authorized by the regulatory duty alone. The lower courts must evaluate the existing prospective claim under the rules below and deny relief if its offense, threatened-injury or remedial requirements fail. Communications-statute and state-law claims retain their existing status outside this antitrust review.

## Holdings

**1. Regulatory access duties and independent monopolization remain separate.** A regulated firm's breach of an access duty violates Sherman Act section 2 only when the plaintiff independently establishes relevant-market monopoly power, unlawful acquisition or maintenance through an exclusionary competitive mechanism, and causally connected antitrust injury. Deliberate discriminatory degradation of rival-customer access may qualify when it maintains monopoly power and lacks an adequate business justification; regulatory breach, rival injury or exclusionary purpose alone supplies no substitute for those elements.

**Authority:** Part I of Stevens's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The saving clause preserves existing antitrust law without enlarging its duties. CSU v. Xerox requires an actual input-exclusion inquiry while giving statutory entitlements and legitimate business explanations substantial force. That separation extends to this regulated setting. The complaint identifies selective impairment of rival-customer orders as the alleged means of making competing service ineffective. Courts must examine that mechanism under ordinary pleading and proof requirements, rather than infer liability from a regulatory finding or end the inquiry because access originated in a regulatory command.

Aspen Skiing illustrates unjustified exclusion; a prior voluntary course of dealing is probative rather than an exclusive element. Kodak requires attention to actual market constraints and conduct. NYNEX distinguishes a regulatory wrong from harm to competition. Technical requirements, investment incentives, efficiency and operational explanations therefore remain material to whether the alleged discrimination constitutes unlawful maintenance. Those requirements answer the contention that the Court has simply converted compulsory assistance into an antitrust supply duty.

A freestanding leveraging label cannot avoid the offense's elements. If an attempt theory is pursued, Spectrum Sports requires anticompetitive conduct, specific intent and a dangerous probability of monopolization in the relevant market. The plaintiff bears the ordinary civil burden. The lower courts must assess the existing allegations and any amendment properly permitted under ordinary rules; an insufficient theory is subject to dismissal through the ordinary procedures.

**2. The existing damages theory is insufficiently direct.** A customer claiming damages from interference with its carrier must satisfy Associated General Contractors by establishing a sufficiently direct personal business-or-property injury, assessed through the causal relationship, intervening injury, more direct claimants, duplicate recovery and workable allocation. Trinko's existing theory places its service loss downstream of the carriers' impaired access and service provision, so the damages claim must be dismissed.

**Authority:** Part II of Stevens's opinion, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Thomas.

Steamfitters applies the directness inquiry to the actual chain of injury rather than a plaintiff's status. The carriers suffer the access impairment and are positioned to seek damages for it. This complaint's consequential customer loss presents the overlapping recovery and allocation concerns that the directness requirement addresses. Alleging that the strategy targeted rival patronage does not itself eliminate the intervening carrier injury.

McCready permits recovery when a plaintiff's own deprivation is the instrument of the exclusionary scheme. The present theory instead derives the customer's service loss from impairment of its carrier's access. The distinction answers the proposed targeted-customer analogy without making noncompetitor or consumer status conclusive. This statutory damages conclusion remains distinct from Article III and from the requirements governing an injunction.

**3. Prospective relief requires its own injury and remedy inquiry.** Section 16 permits relief upon a significant personal threat of injury of the kind the antitrust laws protect against, arising from what makes an impending, continuing or likely recurring violation unlawful. A damages-directness defect based on duplicate recovery, allocation or a more direct damages claimant does not by itself defeat that prospective claim; the plaintiff must establish the independent offense, its personal threat and the ordinary equitable grounds for a lawful, effective decree.

**Authority:** Part III of Stevens's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

Cargill distinguishes threatened antitrust injury from completed business-or-property loss and the additional allocation concerns attending damages. The alleged systematic impairment of rival service warrants an independent examination of the threat to this existing rival-carrier customer. The actual regulatory measures bear on whether a threat persists and whether additional relief is necessary. Their existence establishes neither complete redress nor entitlement to another decree.

The requested access parity cannot follow merely from telecommunications law's sharing duty. Any injunction must target established antitrust harm, respect lawful access terms consistently with the cure, and have a necessary, workable scope. A narrower effective remedy may be considered when supported by the existing claim and ordinary equity. If the independent offense, personal prospective threat or lawful effective remedy is missing, relief must be denied. These requirements resolve the governing legal questions and define the lower courts' remaining application work.

## Precedent Treatment

- **CSU v. Xerox:** Its separation of statutory entitlements, independent exclusion and substantial justifications is extended to regulated network-input conduct; it supplies no general right to assistance.
- **Eastman Kodak Co. v. Image Technical Services:** Applied for actual market and conduct analysis rather than categorical economic assumptions.
- **Aspen Skiing Co. v. Aspen Highlands Skiing Corp.:** Preserved as an example of unjustified exclusion; its prior-dealing evidence is not made an exclusive gateway.
- **NYNEX Corp. v. Discon:** Applied to distinguish regulatory misconduct and rival injury from the competitive harm the offense requires.
- **Spectrum Sports v. McQuillan:** Applied to preserve the independent conduct, intent and dangerous-probability elements of any attempt theory.
- **Associated General Contractors v. Carpenters and the Steamfitters decision:** Applied to dismiss this consequential damages theory on actual causal directness, claimant priority, duplication and allocation.
- **Blue Shield of Virginia v. McCready:** Distinguished because the present damages theory rests on service loss consequent on the carrier's access impairment, rather than a direct deprivation serving as the exclusionary instrument.
- **Cargill, Inc. v. Monfort of Colorado, Inc.:** Applied independently to threatened antitrust injury and the different operation of prospective relief; damages allocation does not decide section 16.

## Law After Decision

Regulated input conduct receives the ordinary monopoly-maintenance inquiry, with substantial attention to legitimate legal and operational explanations. Regulatory noncompliance neither proves that offense nor immunizes it. The present damages theory fails the separate directness requirement, while the prospective claim returns for the independent offense, personal-threat and equitable-remedy inquiry. Access parity requires support as a lawful antitrust remedy for established harm, rather than simply as a regulatory obligation.

## Separate Writings

**Scalia, dissenting in part, joined by O'Connor and Thomas.** The alleged customer diversion is the consequence of deficient compelled assistance, rather than an independently sufficient antitrust wrong. CSU preserves the requirement of such a wrong and the absence of a general duty to assist rivals; NYNEX separates regulatory misconduct from injury to the competitive process. Exclusionary purpose and a sharing command cannot supply the antitrust dealing obligation the complaint assumes. The dissent would dismiss the prospective claim for that conduct defect. It accepts Cargill's distinct equity standard and does not treat the damages ruling as a substitute for the missing offense analysis. All three join the Court's damages holding.

**Ginsburg, dissenting in part, joined by Stone-Zsela, Kennedy and Breyer.** The alleged impairment of end-user orders may itself be the instrument by which Verizon discourages rival patronage. McCready therefore supplies a potentially closer causal model than the remote expense chain in Steamfitters. The Court should remand for assessment of Trinko's actual personal loss, intervening steps, overlap with carrier recovery and workable allocation under Associated General Contractors. The dissent would allow any amendment properly permitted by ordinary rules and dismiss an insufficient theory, while allowing a sufficiently direct independent claim to proceed. It does not find the damages predicates established. All four join the conduct and prospective-relief holdings.

## Procedure After Action

The lower courts must dismiss the existing damages theory and assess the prospective claim under Parts I and III. That work includes the alleged market and exclusion mechanism, substantial justifications, a significant personal prospective threat, the actual protection supplied by regulatory measures, and the necessity and scope of any effective antitrust remedy. Ordinary pleading and amendment rules govern; a failure of a required predicate permits dismissal or denial at the appropriate stage. Other statutory and state-law claims retain their separate procedural status.
