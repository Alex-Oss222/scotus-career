### Robertson v. Seattle Audubon Society, No. 90-1596

Merits decision, March 25, 1992

Argued December 2, 1991. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1). The question is whether §318(b)(6)(A) changes applicable law or impermissibly dictates judicial results under unchanged law. Sixteen Forest Service sales offered during fiscal year 1990 remain enjoined and supply a live controversy; the Bureau of Land Management portion appears moot and is not an independent basis for merits relief.

Environmental groups challenged Forest Service and BLM timber activity under MBTA, NEPA, NFMA, FLPMA and OCLA. The fiscal-year 1990 Northwest Timber Compromise both required sales and imposed new protected-area limits. Section 318(b)(6)(A) treated compliance with (b)(3) and (b)(5) as meeting the statutory requirements identified by the pending cases’ captions. Individual-sale compliance remained for judicial determination; some additional BLM areas were to be designated after enactment. The Ninth Circuit held that Congress directed results under unchanged law and could not amend substantive law in an appropriations measure.

The Court reverses the Ninth Circuit’s judgment and remands, 9–0, for application of the new statutory criteria. Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas support that disposition of the Article III challenge to §318(b)(6)(A). No particular timber sale is declared compliant. Thomas delivers the unanimous Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter.

#### Substitution of governing law

Section 318(b)(6)(A) changes the statutory law applicable to the identified timber disputes by making compliance with the new restrictions sufficient to meet the specified old requirements, while leaving actual compliance for courts to decide. That clear substantive amendment is effective although enacted in an appropriations statute and does not violate Article III on the ground decided below.

This holding and explanation control in Thomas’s unanimous Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter.

The Court holds that Congress supplied new legal standards and left their application to the judiciary. Before the Compromise, the challenges depended on the identified environmental requirements; afterward, compliance with the two new provisions could satisfy those obligations. Simpson explains why the specific enactment qualifies the more general laws. No provision finds that a particular timber sale meets the new restrictions, and the contemplated later designation of protected areas confirms that judicial application remains necessary. The case captions identify the requirements affected; they do not themselves decide facts. Will permits Congress to amend substantive law clearly in an appropriations act, while TVA’s caution against implied repeal does not defeat this express substitution. Because the premise of the circuit’s Klein ruling fails, the Court need not define Klein’s full reach. It reserves the unraised challenge to narrowly targeted legislation and leaves actual sale compliance for remand.

United States v. Klein, 13 Wall. 128, is distinguished on the ground decided below; its full constitutional reach is not resolved.

Pennsylvania v. Wheeling & Belmont Bridge Co., 18 How. 421, is consistent with adjudication under changed law; no broad targeted-legislation holding is drawn from it.

Simpson v. United States, 435 U.S. 6, is applied: specific provisions qualify general ones.

United States v. Will, 449 U.S. 200, is applied: clear substantive amendments may appear in appropriations legislation.

TVA v. Hill, 437 U.S. 153, is distinguished: the amendment here is express rather than an inferred repeal.

NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp., 301 U.S. 1, is preserved: a possible constitutionally valid construction reinforces, but does not replace, the enacted meaning.

The specified statutory requirements are modified for the Compromise’s defined geographical, temporal and contract scope. The decision establishes no broad exception to Article III, no constitutional environmental entitlement and no finding that any sale complies. The unraised challenge to legislation narrowly targeting pending litigation, Klein’s full reach and final-judgment reopening remain undecided.

The courts apply the new criteria to the still-live sale disputes. Fiscal-year limits and continued application to covered sales contracts remain. The decision resolves Supreme Court review on the stated terms; lower-court proceedings do not imply retained Supreme Court supervision.

**End of entry: Robertson v. Seattle Audubon Society, merits decision, March 25, 1992.**

### Graham v. Collins, No. 91-7580

Merits decision, January 25, 1993

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 14, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the Fifth Circuit sitting en banc, 950 F.2d 1009, affirming denial of federal habeas relief. The questions are whether Teague bars Graham’s claim and whether Texas’s three submitted special issues permitted effective use of youth, background and positive-character mitigation. This decision and Herrera v. Collins are issued on the same day, without a sequence between them.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses denial of sentencing relief on the youth and background grounds and remands for a conditional writ directed to the death sentence. The murder conviction remains undisturbed.

| Component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Teague and the youth/background sentencing claim | Reverse denial of relief, 5–4 | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter | White, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Conditional writ: Texas must provide an adequate new sentencing proceeding within a reasonable district-court period or replace the death sentence with a lawful noncapital sentence |
| Positive character as a separately sufficient ground for relief | No independent relief on this component, nine supporting disposition on different grounds | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | No opposing votes | Youth/background relief remains; no separate additional remedy. Stone-Zsela supports this component in the judgment only |

The new sentencer must have an effective route for all relevant mitigation, but no particular weight or favorable sentence is dictated. No guilt retrial or release from independent lawful custody is ordered.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Parts I–III and V | Souter | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor | Reverse denial of youth/background sentencing relief and order a conditional writ | Teague, youth, background and conditional sentencing relief; five join these propositions in full |
| Part IV, positive-character application | Souter | Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor | Deny independent relief on this component | Four find no independent material culpability use requiring further relief on this record; no fifth rationale join |
| Concurrence | Stevens | Author alone | Joins Souter throughout | Individual mitigation complements safeguards against arbitrary and discriminatory sentencing |
| Dissent | White | Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Would affirm under Teague | No new-rule exception applies on their view |
| Separate dissent | Thomas | Author alone | Also joins White | Would reject Penry’s broader mitigation requirement and preserve reasonable legislative structuring |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The youth and background claims apply law settled before finality

**Controlling proposition:** Teague does not bar Graham's claim that Texas's submitted sentencing questions prevented effective use of his youth and unstable background as material mitigation independent of deliberateness or future danger. Jurek's conditional approval, Lockett and Eddings dictated that functional requirement before his conviction became final on September 10, 1984; no new procedural-rule exception is created.

**Authority:** Parts I–III and V of Souter's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens and O’Connor; five Justices adopt this prefinality-law holding, its application to youth and background, and the conditional sentencing remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that applying an existing protection to these facts does not announce a new rule. Teague and Butler protect reasonable reliance on law at finality; they do not require an earlier case with identical evidence. Jurek approved the Texas scheme on the understanding that relevant mitigation could receive effective consideration. Lockett rejected legal barriers to mitigating aspects of character, record and offense, and Eddings expressly protected youth and disturbed background. Those decisions preceded Graham's final judgment. Penry later applied the same duty when the special issues failed to express a material mitigating judgment; it did not create that duty. The State's reliance on Jurek is substantial, but facial approval subject to effective mitigation cannot authorize its material exclusion in a particular application. Franklin's treatment of prison conduct and Saffle's rejection of unrestricted sympathy do not negate the preexisting requirement. Stringer confirms that a later application need not create new law merely because judges disagree. The Court reaches these claims without invoking either Teague exception.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S. 288, and Butler v. McKellar, 494 U.S. 407 — applied; the relevant result must be dictated at finality, not merely supported by a later plausible extension.
- Jurek v. Texas, 428 U.S. 262 — preserved as qualified approval; it does not immunize every application of the special issues.
- Lockett v. Ohio, 438 U.S. 586, and Eddings v. Oklahoma, 455 U.S. 104 — applied as the pre-1984 effective-mitigation rule.
- Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302 — applied as recognition of that existing rule, not as law inserted retroactively into 1984.
- Stringer v. Black, March 9, 1992 — applied at its existing Teague scope; disagreement alone does not establish novelty.
- Franklin v. Lynaugh, 487 U.S. 164, and Saffle v. Parks, 494 U.S. 484 — distinguished; evidence whose mitigating use fits the questions, and unrestricted sympathy, differ from material culpability mitigation excluded by them.

##### Youth requires an effective route for its independent mitigating force

**Controlling proposition:** When the record supports a material mitigating judgment about culpability that the authorized sentencing answers cannot express, a reasonable likelihood that the submitted mechanism prevents that use requires an effective corrective instruction or procedure. Graham's youth at seventeen has that independent significance notwithstanding deliberate conduct or predicted future danger, and the three submitted special issues provide no adequate route for it here.

**Authority:** Parts II and V of Souter's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens and O’Connor; five Justices adopt this independently sufficient ground for sentencing relief and its conditional remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a prediction of future violence does not answer every question about a young offender's culpability. Eddings recognizes immaturity, inexperience and undeveloped judgment as material mitigation. A juror could accept those considerations while finding that Graham deliberately killed and might remain dangerous. Jurek's approval therefore does not end the inquiry: the permitted answers must actually allow the material mitigating judgment. The provocation question supplies no route for youth's independent significance either. Texas correctly observes that youth may predict rehabilitation, and the jury could consider that prediction. But permitting one material use does not authorize exclusion of another. Penry confirms this distinction between admitting evidence and allowing its mitigating effect. Boyde's reasonable-likelihood formulation assesses the submitted mechanism; the substantive duty remains the prefinality rule. The Court requires an effective sentencing vehicle, not favorable weight, acquittal or an unrestricted mercy question. It announces no categorical prohibition on executing all seventeen-year-old offenders and does not decide the constitutionality of Texas's later statute.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Eddings v. Oklahoma, 455 U.S. 104 — applied to youth's independent culpability significance; jurors may assign weight but cannot be legally prevented from giving it effect.
- Jurek v. Texas, 428 U.S. 262 — its conditional framework remains; this defective application does not establish facial invalidity.
- Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302 — applied beyond a permanent-handicap limitation; a mitigating trait need not have only aggravating relevance to future danger to require an effective vehicle.
- Boyde v. California, 494 U.S. 370 — applied as the reasonable-likelihood method of assessing instructional operation, not as the source of a new substantive rule at 1984 finality.

##### The submitted background evidence also requires effective consideration

**Controlling proposition:** A capital sentencing system may not prevent a jury from acting on the material culpability significance it finds in a defendant's disturbed upbringing merely because that evidence also bears on future dangerousness or lacks a uniquely severe permanent handicap. The evidence of Graham's mother's recurring mental illness and hospitalizations and his shifting family care supports such a mitigating judgment, which the submitted special issues cannot adequately express; this independently requires the same sentencing remedy.

**Authority:** Parts III and V of Souter's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens and O’Connor; five Justices adopt this independent background-evidence ground and the conditional sentencing remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the sentencing jury must have a lawful way to act on the material mitigating significance of this upbringing. Eddings protects consideration of disturbed family background, particularly for a young defendant; it does not demand proof of a uniquely severe permanent handicap. Graham's grandmother described his mother's repeated hospitalization and his movement between family caregivers. The jury could find that history reduced his culpability without denying that the killing was deliberate or that future violence was probable. The provocation question does not express that judgment. The State argues that the favorable testimony about his behavior shows no lasting harm and that the evidence can support rehabilitation. Those arguments properly address weight, but cannot establish that every material mitigating use fits the authorized answers. Penry requires an effective means of expressing the supported culpability judgment; admission alone is insufficient. The Court neither finds a psychiatric disability nor requires jurors to infer a particular causal effect. It requires a lawful opportunity for sentencing assessment and leaves the weight to the new sentencer.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Lockett v. Ohio, 438 U.S. 586 — applied to relevant background proffered as a basis for less than death.
- Eddings v. Oklahoma, 455 U.S. 104 — applied; disturbed upbringing cannot be excluded from mitigating effect as a matter of law.
- Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302 — applied; neither a uniquely severe permanent handicap nor complete absence of relevance to the special issues is an exclusive trigger.

##### Positive character as an independent ground

**Judgment without controlling rationale:** The separate contention that Graham's household assistance, religious observance and financial support of his children independently require additional sentencing relief is rejected by all nine Justices, on different grounds. Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor and Souter regard its asserted independent culpability significance as insubstantial on this record and its future-behavior use as available under the second issue; White, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would bar the requested mitigation relief under Teague. Stone-Zsela joins the denial of this independent component in the judgment only. These grounds do not yield a common general rule or a Marks subset; the youth and background holdings independently require sentencing relief.

**Limits:** No universal rule about positive character evidence or its value apart from the asserted culpability and future-danger uses is decided. The jury remains free to hear constitutionally relevant evidence at the new sentencing proceeding. Neither denial of this independent ground nor the sentencing remedy disturbs the murder conviction.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

No precedent is overruled. Jurek's qualified approval and the prefinality Lockett/Eddings rule are applied. Penry is not confined to uniquely severe permanent disabilities or evidence wholly lacking mitigating relevance to the special issues. Franklin's prison-conduct example and Saffle's unrestricted-sympathy claim remain distinct. Teague retains its dictated-by-prefinality-law requirement and exceptions; neither exception supplies this judgment. Sawyer's gateway and Stringer's separate weighing-cure law are unchanged.

Effective January 25, 1993, Texas's former special-issue mechanism is unconstitutional as applied to the material culpability significance of Graham's youth and disturbed background, and relief on those grounds is not new law under Teague. Lower courts must ask whether supported material mitigating use can actually receive effect, using the reasonable-likelihood instruction inquiry; mere admission or some relevance to future danger is insufficient. This is no categorical fourth-question requirement, entitlement to favorable weight, facial invalidation, or juvenile-execution prohibition. The positive-character component yields no new general controlling rule.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

White, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, would affirm because reasonable jurists in 1984 could read Jurek, Lockett and Eddings as allowing Texas to channel this mitigation through deliberateness and future dangerousness. In their view a new instruction requirement is not dictated by prefinality law; it neither removes a class of conduct or persons from punishment nor supplies a watershed accuracy procedure, so neither Teague exception saves it. They distinguish Penry's disabling evidence from Graham's evidence supporting rehabilitation and do not adopt the Court's as-applied merits rule.

Thomas also writes separately. He regards Penry's expansive account of mitigating effect as inconsistent with Furman's requirement that capital discretion be structured against arbitrariness and discrimination. He would permit reasonable state definitions of relevance and sentencing criteria while allowing defendants to present constitutionally relevant information. He would overrule Penry to that extent; the Court does not do so. His view is separate from White's sufficient Teague ground.

Stevens concurs while joining the controlling opinion. He regards narrowing eligibility, avoiding vague aggravators, proportionality limits and individualized mitigation as complementary protections. Requiring a sentencer to act on relevant individual circumstances reduces resort to irrelevant considerations such as race; it does not demand an unreasoned preference. His discussion responds to Thomas without creating an additional test or changing the conditional remedy.

Souter's Part IV, joined by Blackmun, Stevens and O’Connor, explains the limited character-evidence disposition. Ordinary chores, religious observance and support for children can inform future dangerousness through the second special issue. On the asserted theory here, their separate bearing on culpability for this offense is insubstantial. The four do not decide the value of such traits apart from the submitted culpability/future-danger arguments. Stone-Zsela joins only the denial of independent relief on that component.

#### Source Notes

The [official report, 506 U.S. 461](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep506/usrep506461/usrep506461.pdf), and [full en banc judgment, 950 F.2d 1009](https://static.case.law/f2d/950/html/1009-01.html), including the separate opinions, support the identity, dates, finality, submitted evidence, statutory questions and prior proceedings. The lower judgment reproduces the applicable statute and the three submitted questions. No quotation from the Court’s decision is supplied. The complete independent trial appendix is unavailable in the supplied source support; the decision uses the judicial account and makes no new factual findings about psychological injury.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court merits review is complete. The case returns through the Fifth Circuit to the District Court for a conditional habeas order. Texas has a reasonable period fixed by that court to provide a sentencing proceeding that permits effective consideration of the relevant mitigation or to replace death with a lawful noncapital sentence. The conviction and independent lawful custody remain; no particular instruction text, sentencing weight, immediate release or later filing is prescribed.

**End of entry: Graham v. Collins, merits decision, January 25, 1993.**

### Smith v. United States (Antarctica), No. 91-1538

Merits decision, March 8, 1993

#### Chronology and Posture

507 U.S. 197. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 953 F.2d 1116. Argued December 7, 1992; decided March 8, 1993. The question is whether the Federal Tort Claims Act (FTCA) foreign-country exception bars this claim arising from alleged negligence in Antarctica despite the absence of a recognized foreign sovereign. Sandra Jean Smith seeks a wrongful-death remedy for her husband's death after falling into a crevasse during a recreational hike from McMurdo Station. All alleged negligent acts or omissions occurred in Antarctica; no domestic-headquarters theory is asserted. Smith resides in Oregon and filed there, so residence-based venue is available under § 1402(b). The district court dismissed for want of subject-matter jurisdiction; the Ninth Circuit affirmed on the territorial exception and alternatively the absence of the asserted sovereign consent. Its prior opinion at 932 F.2d 791 was withdrawn; 953 F.2d 1116 is the judgment reviewed. Review is under 28 U.S.C. § 1254(1).

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participate at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Court affirms the Ninth Circuit judgment. The FTCA claim remains dismissed under the foreign-country exception; no factual negligence or damages question is adjudicated.

| Component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| FTCA Antarctic wrongful-death claim | Affirmed, 8–1 | Stone, White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Stevens | Dismissal under § 2680(k) stands; no negligence or damages relief. |

The Court does not dismiss for lack of Oregon venue. It leaves any independently authorized remedy to its own statutory or constitutional source.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing or portion | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Exact scope |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court: territorial exception and place-of-law structure | White | Stone, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Eight affirm | The complete controlling holding below; valid Oregon venue acknowledged |
| Opinion of the Court: additional extraterritorial-presumption discussion | White | Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Seven affirm | Confirmatory interpretive reason for this FTCA Antarctic claim; no absolute all-offshore bar |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Stone | No other Justice | Affirms and joins statutory portion only | Rejects the proposed domiciliary-law substitution under the enacted structure; does not join additional presumption discussion |
| Dissent | Stevens | No other Justice | Would reverse/remand | Exception confined to foreign sovereign territory, domestic conflicts route available, maritime history against absolute territorial premise |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The FTCA does not authorize this Antarctic tort action

**Controlling proposition:** The foreign-country exception in § 2680(k), read with the FTCA's enacted liability and place-of-law provisions, excludes this claim based entirely on acts or omissions in Antarctica even though no other sovereign is recognized there. Sections 1346(b) and 2674 do not authorize substituting the claimant's Oregon law for the law of the Antarctic place of the alleged negligence merely because she resides in Oregon; her valid residence venue does not supply the missing substantive consent.

**Authority:** The statutory portion of White's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: eight Justices adopt this holding. Stevens dissents.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the territorial exception must be read within the liability scheme Congress enacted. Spelar explains the exception's territorial setting and the refusal to expose the United States to liability based on foreign law; it does not make foreign sovereignty the only permissible meaning of country. Richards requires the whole law of the place where the negligent act occurred. Here every alleged act occurred in Antarctica, which has no local civil tort system furnishing that statutory starting point. Oregon law might govern some private dispute, but neither the claimant's residence nor ordinary forum conflicts reasoning changes Congress's place-based direction. The Court does not regard the proposed domestic rule as nonexistent; it rejects the statutory authority to substitute that rule here. Kubrick requires fidelity to Congress's waiver, without either enlarging or narrowing it by judicial preference. Oregon venue is available, and possible venue difficulties for other plaintiffs are context only. The holding concerns this FTCA claim and decides neither negligence nor an independently authorized remedy.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Spelar, 338 U.S. 217 (1949): its territorial understanding of the exception is applied to Antarctica; its foreign-sovereignty facts do not fix an exclusive definition requiring another recognized sovereign.
- Richards v. United States, 369 U.S. 1 (1962): applied; the whole law of the place of the negligent act controls the statutory choice, and the claimant's residence does not authorize a different starting point.
- United States v. Kubrick, 444 U.S. 111 (1979): applied to respect the intended scope of the FTCA waiver without judicial expansion or contraction.
- Beattie v. United States, 756 F.2d 91 (D.C. Cir. 1984): its conclusion permitting the Antarctic FTCA action is disapproved; the territorial/choice-of-law objection in Scalia's dissent is consistent with the adopted statutory construction.
- United States v. Nordic Village, February 25, 1992: preserved at its scope concerning clear consent to affirmative bankruptcy monetary relief; it does not independently decide this different express waiver.
- Molzof v. United States, January 14, 1992: preserved; its construction of punitive damages and independent local-law entitlement do not establish Antarctic territorial coverage.

#### Current-Law Effect and Limits

Effective March 8, 1993, § 2680(k) excludes the Antarctic claim on the stated statutory ground. No blanket rule defeats every offshore FTCA, constitutional or other statutory channel. White's additional discussion, joined by Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, treats the presumption described in EEOC v. Arabian American Oil Co., 499 U.S. 244, as confirmation where Congress has supplied no indication of Antarctic coverage; the presumption is not adopted as an independently sufficient universal territorial rule. Stone joins none of that additional discussion. The FTCA's maritime history and separate statutory waivers remain to be addressed according to their own terms; this case resolves none of them.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stone concurs in the statutory portion and the judgment. Oregon residence supplies venue, and Oregon tort law is a possible proposed rule rather than a factual impossibility. The defect is that the FTCA selects the whole law where the negligent acts occurred and supplies no direction to substitute the claimant's domiciliary law for this Antarctic claim. Stone accepts the territorial reading through Spelar and that enacted structure. He does not join extraterritoriality as an additional interpretive reason, emphasizing that the maritime history prevents using offshore location alone as an absolute bar.

Stevens dissents. He reads foreign country to mean territory under another sovereign and regards Spelar's avoidance of foreign-law liability as the reason for that exception. Antarctica does not present that problem. Yellow Cab's broad-waiver approach and the private-person language of § 2674 support asking whether a private defendant would be liable under ordinary conflicts principles, which can point to Oregon law for its resident's claim. He invokes United Continental Tuna's account of maritime claims to reject an exclusively domestic understanding of the original FTCA waiver. A place without its own sovereign can still be subject to legal duties. Because Smith has Oregon venue, hypothetical nonresident plaintiffs' venue difficulties cannot justify foreclosing her suit. He would reverse and permit the claim to proceed under an appropriate legal rule, without adjudging negligence or damages.

The [official U.S. Reports text, 507 U.S. 197–217](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep507/usrep507197/usrep507197.pdf) and [complete Ninth Circuit judgment and separate writing, 953 F.2d 1116](https://static.case.law/f2d/953/cases/1116-01.json) support the docket, dates, Antarctic acts, residence venue, withdrawn earlier opinion, dissenting conflicts analysis and judgment reviewed. The enacted provisions and judicial accounts support the limited choice-of-law question; no complete underlying evidentiary record is claimed. No verbatim decisional quotation is presented.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court merits review is complete; the dismissal stands. There is no Supreme Court remand, negligence determination or damages award. The separate firearm-use Smith case is not part of this action.

**End of entry: Smith v. United States (Antarctica), Merits decision, March 8, 1993.**

### Plaut v. Spendthrift Farm, Inc., No. 93-1121

Merits decision, April 18, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 30, 1994; decided April 18, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit, reviewing its August 3, 1993 judgment, 1 F.3d 1487. The question is whether §27A(b) may require reopening this final, unappealed private federal securities dismissal.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas; Justice Breyer in the judgment only.
**Opposing:** Justices Stevens, Souter, and Ginsburg.

The Court affirms the judgment of the Sixth Circuit, which affirmed the district court's refusal to reopen the final August 13, 1991 dismissal under §27A(b). That dismissal remains final. Section 27A(b) is unconstitutional to the extent it requires the district court to vacate that final judgment and reinstate the same litigation, and the February 11, 1992 reinstatement motion was properly denied. No determination of the underlying securities claims is made; no finding of fraud, liability, or damages is entered.

#### Opinion Topology

STONE-ZSELA, C. J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, and THOMAS, JJ., joined. BREYER, J., filed an opinion concurring in the judgment. STEVENS, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which SOUTER and GINSBURG, JJ., joined.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Opinion of the Court | Chief Justice Stone-Zsela | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas | Affirmance | Entire opinion, including the Article III finality rule, its application to the August 13, 1991 dismissal, its qualifications, and its limits. |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Breyer | No other Justice | Affirmance | No part of the Court's opinion; rests on the closed, identifiable class of final judgments §27A(b) reopens without general prospective application. |
| Dissent | Stevens | Souter and Ginsburg | Would reverse and remand for reinstatement | Entire dissent; no part of the Court's opinion. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Congress may not, solely by changing the applicable law, require an Article III court to reopen a final private judgment

**Controlling proposition:** Article III protects the conclusive disposition of a private case by a federal court once its judgment has become final through the ordinary judicial process. Congress may amend substantive or procedural law, prescribe expressly retrospective rules for pending litigation, and authorize judicial relief through constitutionally permissible procedures. It may not, solely by changing the law previously applicable to a completed private action, require an Article III court to vacate that final judgment and reinstate the same litigation. Section 27A(b) is unconstitutional to that extent.

**Authority:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas. Those five direct rationale joins control every proposition, qualification, and limit stated here. Breyer concurs only in the judgment, on a narrower ground, and joins no proposition; Stevens, Souter, and Ginsburg dissent. No combination of separate rationales is necessary.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that finality is itself an Article III protection. It does not disappear merely because Congress leaves the underlying merits undecided. Morgan Stanley's affirmance by an equally divided Court established no precedent, so the question is one of first impression. Robertson permits Congress to change the law governing pending cases and left the final-judgment question open. Klein and McCullough supply the governing principle: Congress may not prescribe the results of cases or legislatively revise completed adjudications. Petitioners' action was not pending. It was dismissed as time barred on August 13, 1991, and the dismissal became final without appeal before §27A(b)'s December 19, 1991 enactment. Section 27A(b) does not change the law for litigation still pending; it commands a court to vacate that completed judgment and reinstate the same litigation because the law that produced the dismissal has since changed. The February 11, 1992 motion sought exactly that vacatur and reinstatement. The principal contrary argument is that §27A(b) dictates no finding or liability, because courts would independently determine eligibility and then decide the securities claims, defenses, and remedies. The statute's objective class and retained adjudication answer the contention that Congress selected parties for liability, but not the finality objection: the judicial act Congress sets aside is the completed dismissal itself, and later independent adjudication cannot restore its conclusiveness. Because §27A(b) expressly directs reinstatement, Landgraf's and Rivers's rules of statutory construction cannot avoid the constitutional question. The rule is confined to legislative commands of this kind. Congress may amend substantive or procedural law, prescribe expressly retrospective rules for pending litigation, and authorize judicial relief through constitutionally permissible procedures. A court's reopening on valid Rule 60(b) grounds is judicial action under general rules, not legislative displacement of the judgment. Section 27A(b) is therefore unconstitutional to the extent it requires reopening this dismissal; the reinstatement motion was properly denied, and the Sixth Circuit's judgment is affirmed. The Court makes no determination of the underlying securities claims.

**Qualifications:** The rule leaves existing law in place. Congress may change the law while a case remains pending. It may expressly make a new limitations rule applicable to pending cases, subject to other constitutional limits. It may change the law governing the future operation of an injunction within the applicable remedial framework, as Wheeling Bridge illustrates. It may not require judges to adopt particular factual findings or impose predetermined liability in any case; Klein and McCullough forbid that legislative adjudication. Relief from a final judgment through constitutionally permissible judicial procedures, including reopening on valid Rule 60(b) grounds, remains available.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court does not decide §27A(a), the application of §27A(b) to litigation still pending on direct review, any due-process question, or severability beyond withholding this application. It does not hold that every statute affecting a final judgment is invalid, decide every form of later judicial reopening, or address the Government's waiver of its own defenses. Fraud, reliance, causation, liability, damages, and ordinary merits defenses are not adjudicated; the affirmance establishes none of them.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Morgan Stanley & Co. v. Pacific Mutual Life Insurance Co., May 23, 1994: its affirmance by an equally divided Court, 4–4, issued no opinion and established no precedent; it is neither applied nor distinguished, and the Article III question is decided as one of first impression.
- Robertson v. Seattle Audubon Society, March 25, 1992: answers, for completed private judgments, the final-judgment question Robertson left open; its rule that Congress may change the law governing pending cases remains intact.
- United States v. Klein, 80 U.S. (13 Wall.) 128: applies its bar on legislative prescription of judicial results; it continues to forbid legislatively dictated findings or outcomes in any case.
- McCullough v. Virginia, 172 U.S. 102: applies its protection of completed judicial determinations against legislative displacement; it supports the finality rule without being extended to every statute that affects a judgment.
- James B. Beam Distilling Co. v. Georgia, 501 U.S. 529: its finality limit on judicial retroactivity is consistent with, but does not itself decide, the limit on legislative reopening; its force is unchanged.
- Pennsylvania v. Wheeling & Belmont Bridge Co., 59 U.S. (18 How.) 421: distinguishes new law governing the future operation of continuing equitable relief within the applicable remedial framework; that prospective setting does not authorize vacating a completed private judgment.
- United States v. Sioux Nation of Indians, 448 U.S. 371: distinguishes the Government's waiver of its own defense; it does not concern a command to reopen a private defendant's final judgment.
- Chase Securities Corp. v. Donaldson, 325 U.S. 304: remains a due-process revival authority only; it supplies no answer to the Article III question.
- Lampf, Pleva, Lipkind, Prupis & Petigrow v. Gilbertson, 501 U.S. 350: supplies the limitations rule under which the August 13, 1991 dismissal was entered and to which §27A responds; its force is unchanged.
- Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244, and Rivers v. Roadway Express, Inc., 511 U.S. 298: retained; their presumption against statutory retroactivity does not avoid the question because §27A(b) expressly directs reinstatement, and Congress's power to prescribe expressly retrospective rules for pending litigation remains subject to other constitutional limits.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Morgan Stanley's affirmance by an equally divided Court established no precedent and is neither applied nor distinguished; the Court decides the question as one of first impression. Klein and McCullough are applied: Congress may not prescribe findings or results or legislatively revise completed adjudications. Robertson's reserved final-judgment question is answered for completed private judgments, and its pending-case rule remains intact. Wheeling Bridge is distinguished as concerning the prospective operation of continuing equitable relief, and Sioux Nation as concerning the Government's waiver of its own defense. Beam's finality limit on judicial retroactivity is consistent with the rule but does not itself decide it. Chase Securities remains a due-process revival authority only. Lampf supplies the limitations rule that produced the dismissal and to which §27A responds. Landgraf and Rivers are retained. No precedent is overruled.

Article III protects the conclusive disposition of a private case by a federal court once its judgment has become final through the ordinary judicial process. Congress may not, solely by changing the law previously applicable to a completed private action, require an Article III court to vacate that final judgment and reinstate the same litigation, and later independent judicial determination of eligibility and the merits does not save such a command. Section 27A(b) is unconstitutional to that extent and cannot require a court to reinstate an action whose private judgment had become final through the ordinary judicial process. Congress may still change the law while a case remains pending, expressly make a new limitations rule applicable to pending cases subject to other constitutional limits, and change the law governing the future operation of an injunction within the applicable remedial framework; it may not require judges to adopt particular findings or impose predetermined liability. Relief from a final judgment through constitutionally permissible judicial procedures, including valid Rule 60(b) grounds, remains available. Section 27A(a), litigation still pending on direct review, due process, and severability beyond withholding this application remain undecided. Breyer's closed-class ground and the dissent's statutory-entitlement position are noncontrolling.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Breyer concurs in the judgment and joins no part of the Court's opinion. Section 27A(b) reopens final judgments in a closed, identifiable class of cases and has no general prospective application. In his view, that combination of retroactivity, a closed class, and finality makes the command operate as a legislative disposition of particular decided cases, and it violates the separation of powers. He examines the judicial function the command displaces rather than adopting a categorical rule: he declines the Court's broader holding that finality alone bars the command and expresses no view on a reopening provision of general prospective application. He agrees that the dismissal remains final and the motion was properly denied, and he decides nothing on §27A(a), due process, fraud, liability, or damages.

Justice Stevens dissents, joined by Justices Souter and Ginsburg. Section 27A(b) creates a new legal entitlement to adjudication for an objectively defined class of actions dismissed under Lampf's limitations rule. Courts independently determine whether its conditions are satisfied and then decide the securities claims, defenses, and remedies. Congress makes no finding of fraud, reliance, causation, or damages and leaves application of the rule to judges, so the statute does not perform adjudication, and the completed limitations dismissal does not bar the remedy Congress enacted. Securities limitations policy and its temporal scope belong to Congress. An express statutory source of post-dismissal judicial authority differs from reopening by judicial retroactivity alone, which Beam forecloses, although such a source does not by itself validate every reopening command. In the dissent's view, finality alone cannot defeat an entitlement that leaves every finding and the question of liability to courts. The dissent claims no general legislative power to reopen completed merits judgments and would leave Klein and McCullough intact. The dissenters would reverse and remand for reinstatement under §27A(b) and further judicial proceedings on the securities claims, without deciding §27A(a), independent due-process claims, fraud, liability, or damages.

#### Source Notes

The [Sixth Circuit opinion and Keith concurrence, 1 F.3d 1487](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/001/1.F3d.1487.92-5591.html) supply the filing, final dismissal, timely motion, §27A(b) conditions, and lower-court grounds. The Court's [Morgan Stanley judgment, May 23, 1994](../../OT1993/output/OT_1993CHUNK5.md), 511 U.S. 658, records the affirmance by an equally divided Court and its stated alignment; it issued no opinion and established no precedent. These sources establish no finding of fraud, liability, or damages.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The judgment of the Sixth Circuit is affirmed. The August 13, 1991 dismissal remains final, and the February 11, 1992 reinstatement motion was properly denied. Section 27A(b) is unconstitutional to the extent it requires the district court to reopen that dismissal. Supreme Court review is complete; no remand is ordered, and no determination of the underlying securities claims is made.

### O'Dell v. Netherland, No. 96-6867

Merits decision, June 19, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

O'Dell v. Netherland, No. 96-6867. Argued March 18, 1997; decided June 19, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the Fourth Circuit, which sitting en banc on September 5, 1996 reversed the District Court's Simmons ruling under Teague, 95 F.3d 1214. O'Dell's judgment became final on October 3, 1988, after the Virginia Supreme Court's January 15, 1988 judgment. The granted question is whether Simmons's parole-ineligibility protection is available on habeas review of that final capital judgment.

Virginia argued future dangerousness, and the trial court refused the requested parole-ineligibility instruction and an attempted inquiry whether life meant life without parole. The jury found both future dangerousness and vileness and selected death. It also heard O'Dell describe sixteen years on an earlier parole violation and state that, at his age, he would never get out. The District Court's sentencing relief was expressly conditioned on proof of actual legal parole ineligibility; that predicate was not conclusively established by the order.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Holdings

##### I. The opportunity to answer this future-danger case was dictated at finality

**Holding and operative rule:** When the State relies on future dangerousness to seek death and the only lawful noncapital sentence actually excludes ordinary parole, the defendant must have an effective opportunity to give the jury accurate information about that legal ineligibility. Gardner and Skipper dictated that limited protection before O'Dell's October 3, 1988 finality; Teague therefore does not bar the claim, although eligibility, violation and relief still require their own predicates.

**Authority:** Part I of Stevens's opinion, directly adopted by Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. These five adopt the old-rule holding; Stone-Zsela does not join it.

**Controlling explanation:** Gardner protects the opportunity to deny or explain material information supporting a death sentence. Skipper expressly protects a response to the State's future-danger argument, independently of its additional mitigation rationale. Fixed legal ineligibility answers that argument by identifying the actual alternative to death. This concrete protected function, not a general appeal to fairness, supplies the dictated application.

The strongest objection distinguishes historical evidence about the defendant from legal information about postsentencing confinement. The distinction does not justify withholding the accurate response when the State makes future danger material. Ramos permits truthful information about commutation; it does not authorize exclusion of the ordinary-parole answer. Caldwell forbids misleading diminution of the sentencing jury's responsibility, not accurate explanation of its lawful choices.

Simmons states the present protection and its limits: accurate defense presentation or an adequate instruction may suffice, no fixed script or immunity from clemency is required, and the State may prove danger within prison. Graham, Gray and Lambrix require functional comparison without inserting their later decisions into the law existing in 1988. Gilmore and Caspari still permit genuinely new procedural extensions after that comparison. No rule that every capital protection is old, or that every defendant is entitled to release predictions, follows.

##### II. Independently, the confined protection satisfies the watershed exception if treated as new

**Independent alternative holding and operative rule:** Even if the parole-information protection were classified as new, Teague's watershed exception permits its application where future dangerousness is an operative basis for choosing death, the only lawful noncapital alternative excludes ordinary parole, and the defendant is denied an effective opportunity to correct the materially misleading release premise through accurate defense presentation or an adequate instruction. The rule establishes retroactive availability in that confined category, not that O'Dell has proved those predicates or is presently entitled to sentencing relief.

**Authority:** Part II of Stevens's opinion, directly adopted by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Five adopt this independently sufficient alternative. The four Associates also join Part I; Stone-Zsela actually regards the rule as new. O'Connor takes no position on this alternative.

**Controlling explanation:** Teague's exception, as applied in Sawyer, Gilmore and Caspari, requires a basic safeguard of accuracy and fundamental fairness, not merely an important procedural improvement. The confined conjunction here meets that demanding requirement. Death is sought on a prediction of future danger while the defendant lacks an effective means to explain the legal confinement alternative that directly answers the release premise. That loss corrupts the adversarial choice between the two lawful sentences. Gideon supplies the model of a bedrock safeguard for a fair adversarial proceeding; Crane and Gardner identify the basic importance of an effective chance to answer the case against the defendant. The analogy concerns that function, not equivalence between every evidentiary rule and the right to counsel.

The objection that this is only a helpful instruction understates the holding. A refused instruction alone is insufficient: an accurate alternative defense presentation may satisfy Simmons. Nor does the protection's limited incidence diminish its basic role where the conjunction exists. The exception covers no general class of capital-sentencing rules, no case without a future-danger ground, no legally available ordinary parole or practical release prediction, and no case with an effective corrective opportunity. It does not make the error structural, displace a separate effect inquiry, or abolish the substantive/new-procedure distinction.

##### III. Retroactivity does not by itself establish a present sentencing writ

**Holding and operative rule:** The Fourth Circuit's Teague disposition must be vacated with that bar unavailable under the preceding holdings, but an operative sentencing writ requires actual legal parole ineligibility, denial of Simmons's effective opportunity, the applicable error consequence, and resolution of genuinely applicable unresolved habeas conditions. The District Court's conditional order is not automatically reinstated, and the conviction and unrelated claims remain undisturbed.

**Authority:** Part III of Stevens's opinion, directly adopted by Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. This five-Justice coalition supplies the formal vacatur and bounded-remand mandate; Stone-Zsela seeks the different conditional-restoration direction stated separately.

**Controlling explanation:** Simmons permits accurate defense presentation as well as an adequate instruction. The refused instruction therefore does not establish every element of violation. O'Dell's remarks about sixteen years on a prior parole violation and never leaving prison differ from accurate legal information about this conviction's sole alternative, but remain part of the complete presentation. The verdict's vileness finding and evidence of an earlier prison murder do not automatically cure an influence on sentence selection. Prison dangerousness remains a lawful State argument, without creating a categorical exception to the parole-information protection.

Brecht settled the harmlessness standard for preserved constitutional trial error properly reached on federal habeas, and O'Neal applies that rule to its identified personal-intent claim. Gray found the capital preparation injury harmful under that rule and under Kotteakos alike. Any necessary effect inquiry here proceeds under that governing standard, within its stated limits; no general prisoner-prejudice rule is assumed. The Court makes no present harm finding.

Gray also preserves genuinely applicable unadjudicated statutory conditions, each with its own temporal and substantive predicates; the Court invents no condition, waiver or satisfaction. If an available violation ultimately warrants relief, Virginia may provide a constitutionally adequate new penalty proceeding or a lawful noncapital disposition. No new factual hearing, reinstated or expired deadline, release or execution order follows from this remand.

#### Precedent Treatment

#### Law After Decision

The confined Simmons opportunity-to-answer claim is available against the Teague defense through two independently majority-supported grounds: it was dictated at finality, and, even if new, it satisfies the watershed exception on Part II's exact conjunction. The alternative does not label Simmons new as Court law, extend watershed treatment to sentencing procedures generally or make relief automatic. #### Separate Writings

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** He joins the confined watershed holding in Part II and the rejection of the Teague bar, but concludes that Simmons was not dictated in 1988 and therefore does not join Part I. He would reverse and restore the District Court's conditional sentencing-relief framework. Restoration would still require proof of legal ineligibility, denial of an effective opportunity through accurate presentation or an adequate instruction, and the applicable error analysis. Neither instructional refusal alone nor the retroactivity holding proves entitlement. He would leave guilt undisturbed and permit Virginia a compliant new penalty proceeding or lawful noncapital sentence if the conditions warrant relief.

**Justice Thomas, joined by Justices Scalia and Kennedy, dissenting.** They would affirm under Teague. Gardner concerned undisclosed adverse information and Skipper excluded historical evidence; neither compelled in 1988 a legal-information obligation concerning the postsentencing regime. Ramos's state latitude and Caldwell's distinct responsibility rule support that substantive distinction. The rule may validly govern cases where it is available without having been dictated at finality. It regulates selection of an otherwise authorized penalty, so it is not substantive; its defined rebuttal protection also lacks the systemic character required for a watershed procedure. The dissenters preserve both exceptions but find neither satisfied. They decide no actual parole status, violation, harm, statutory condition or prison-danger exception.

### Reno v. American Civil Liberties Union, No. 96-511

Merits decision, June 26, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 19, 1997; decided June 26, 1997. On direct appeal from a three-judge District Court for the Eastern District of Pennsylvania under §561 of the Telecommunications Act of 1996. The question is whether the CDA's indecency and patently-offensive restrictions are facially invalid under the First Amendment because they are content based, vague, substantially overbroad and insufficiently tailored to protecting minors.

The Act was enacted February 8, 1996. The district court's preliminary-injunction order was dated June 11 and entered June 12, 1996; its June 28 clarification preserved June 12 as the appeal-clock entry date. The plaintiffs demonstrated present speech restrictions and practical compliance burdens. The record describes diverse lawful Internet expression and age-verification costs and barriers, especially for noncommercial and small speakers.

Section 223(a)(1)(B) reaches knowing creation or solicitation and initiation of transmission by a telecommunications device of an obscene or indecent communication, knowing its recipient is under eighteen, regardless of who placed the call or initiated the communication. The enacted definition of telecommunications device excludes an interactive computer service. Section (d)(1)(A) separately reaches knowing use of an interactive computer service to send to specified persons under eighteen; (d)(1)(B) reaches display available to a person under eighteen. Their content category covers contextual depictions or descriptions of sexual or excretory activities or organs in terms patently offensive under contemporary community standards. The corresponding facility offenses require knowing permission and intent that the facility be used for the prohibited activity. Violations carry fines, imprisonment of up to two years, or both.

Section (e)(1) protects mere access or connection to a facility, system or network outside the provider's control, including incidental functions that do not create content. Section (e)(2) denies that defense to a conspirator with an entity actively creating or knowingly distributing prohibited communications, or one knowingly advertising them; the conspirator need not personally create content. Section (e)(3) separately denies it for access to an offending network the provider owns or controls. Employer liability under (e)(4) requires within-scope employee or agent conduct plus knowing authorization or ratification, or reckless disregard. Section (e)(5) provides alternative defenses: good-faith reasonable, effective and appropriate measures under the circumstances to restrict minors' access, or restricted access requiring a verified credit card, debit account, adult access code or adult personal identification number. Under (e)(6), the FCC may describe suitable measures; it may not approve, sanction, compel or enforce their use or endorse particular products. Their use is evidence of good faith. Interactive services do not thereby become common or telecommunications carriers.

Section (f)(1) protects lawful good-faith implementation of defenses or access restrictions against suit. Section (f)(2) bars inconsistent state or local liability for the identified commercial, library and higher-education activities, while allowing complementary regulation confined to intrastate services without inconsistent interstate duties and preserving regulation of conduct outside this section. Section (g) preserves other federal laws. Section (h)'s device definition adds no broadcast-licensee or cable-operator obligations under other indecency provisions; its access-software definition covers content-neutral filtering, selection and transmission tools that do not create or provide the communication's content. These distinct savings provisions supply no absent narrow content definition.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**Authority for both holdings:** The eight-Justice opinion of the Court identified above directly adopts the following propositions, explanations, limits and remedy. O'Connor's agreement with part of the judgment does not join this opinion.

##### I. The challenged content restrictions violate the First Amendment

**Holding and operative rule:** A content-based criminal restriction on expression through a general-purpose communications network is facially invalid when uncertain and substantially overbroad categories suppress substantial protected adult speech and the Government fails to establish narrow tailoring in light of practical, materially less restrictive means of limiting minors' access. On this Internet record, §223(a)(1)(B)'s indecency prohibition and §223(d)(1)'s patently-offensive transmission and display prohibitions, together with their corresponding facility-permitting offenses, fail that requirement.

**Controlling explanation:** Sable recognizes the compelling interest in protecting children but forbids reducing adults to children's speech when less restrictive means are available. Ginsberg sustained a confined commercial restriction on material obscene for minors, including a value limitation absent here. Pacifica's scarcity, intrusiveness and broadcast setting do not describe this medium; ACT/Pacifica requires independent protection of adult access even there. Turner concerns content-neutral carriage, not this direct selection of prohibited content. Denver Area's compulsory-blocking holding supplies the practical adult-access analogy, without extending its fractured permission judgments into a universal rule.

The criminal categories reach serious discussion of health, sexuality, art, politics and personal experience. Uncertainty about content and community standards chills speech; scienter cannot tell a speaker which protected discussion is criminal. Knowledge of a minor or reference to specified recipients does not prevent the prohibition from reaching conversations also intended for adults. Other communication channels do not cure exclusion from this one.

The defenses do not remove those burdens. Many small and noncommercial speakers lack feasible age verification; costs, loss of anonymity and exclusion of lawful adults persist. Proposed tagging does not establish the effective protection the defense requires on the present record. User-controlled filtering and precise regulation of unprotected material offer materially less restrictive approaches. The Government has not justified this broad criminal suppression in light of those alternatives. The Court does not find every age screen invalid or all future controls ineffective. Vagueness contributes to the First Amendment holding; no independent Fifth Amendment holding is needed.

##### II. Textual severance preserves obscenity law; judicial rewriting cannot save the remaining restrictions

**Holding and operative rule:** The discrete words imposing indecency liability in §223(a)(1)(B) are severable from its obscenity prohibition. The Court affirms the clarified preliminary injunction against that indecency branch and its corresponding §223(a)(2) liability, and against §223(d)(1)–(2); the general severability provision does not authorize creating a new minor-only offense or inserting a value exception absent from the enacted text.

**Controlling explanation:** The word choice permits removal of the indecency branch without disabling the distinct obscenity rule. The proposed further construction would instead change the offenses' substantive coverage. Knowledge and audience limits do not provide the missing narrow content definition, and a serious-value or harmful-to-minors exception cannot be supplied by judicial legislation. The statutory defenses preserve their own terms but do not establish workable protection for the record's diverse speakers. The challenge's varied activities and substantial protected reach make a selective judicial reconstruction inappropriate. O'Connor would retain knowing transmissions solely to minors; the Court does not adopt her conclusion that minors' protected-speech overbreadth is insubstantial or her proposed application severance.

The ruling does not hold that Congress can never regulate protected Internet indecency, decide the proposed blanket ban on such messages to seventeen-year-olds or the role of parental approval, or resolve the separate question of extraterritorial efficacy and scope. It does not require one constitutional classification for every Internet service. Independently lawful obscenity, child sexual-abuse material, true-threat, fraud, unlawful-solicitation and direct-harmful-contact provisions remain governed by their own predicates; they do not save these overbroad restrictions. A future precisely drawn rule using effective, nonexclusionary age assurance remains open.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Sable, Butler and Bolger preserve adult access despite the compelling interest in protecting minors; child protection does not alone justify suppressing adult expression.
- Ginsberg's commercial restriction on material obscene for minors is distinguished because the CDA reaches broader protected categories without its value limitation.
- Pacifica retains its contextual broadcast force and supplies no reduced Internet protection.
- ACT/Pacifica's independent review of content-selected restrictions and adult access remains intact; the Internet record is separately examined.
- Turner retains the distinction between content-neutral carriage and content discrimination, with independent scrutiny of the evidentiary fit.
- Denver Area's compulsory-segregation holding is applied by analogy to practical adult-access burdens; its separate permission judgments create no universal Internet rule.
- Broadrick supplies the substantial-overbreadth framework; the enacted breadth cannot be repaired by an unavailable narrowing construction. No precedent is overruled.

#### Law After Decision

The challenged indecency and patently-offensive restrictions cannot be enforced at the clarified scope. Content-based criminal regulation of this medium must protect lawful adult expression and satisfy narrow tailoring on the actual record. Obscenity and other independently unprotected conduct retain their own legal treatment. Future, precisely drawn regulation and genuinely effective nonexclusionary age controls remain open; no general Internet-service classification or freestanding Fifth Amendment rule is adopted.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**O'Connor, concurring in the judgment in part and dissenting in part.** Butler, Sable and Bolger protect adult access; Ginsberg permits genuine restrictions confined to minors. The Internet presently lacks generally usable identity gates, so the display offense and mixed adult/minor conversations cannot be saved by treating the network as divided into adult and child zones. She agrees with the injunction to that extent and with preservation of independent obscenity enforcement.

She would retain the transmission provisions only where the initiating adult knows all recipients are minors, with corresponding facility liability confined to those underlying communications. She construes §223(d)(1)(A) to require knowledge of minority, relying on its wording and DeBartolo's constitutional-avoidance principle. Such a communication has no other adult audience whose access is lost. Brockett's application-severability principle and the statutory severability provision support preserving that application. Although the CDA's content categories are broader than Ginsberg's obscenity-for-minors rule, she finds no showing under Broadrick that the additional protected minor speech is substantial relative to the provisions' legitimate sweep. She does not deny minors all First Amendment protection or foreclose individual vagueness challenges. Her view relies on current technology, not predicted improvements. No other Justice joins her separate writing.

Pub. The clarification did not restart the June 12 appeal clock. The Court adds no damages, new deadline, mandated technology or permanent decree.

### Abebe-Jira v. Negewo, No. 95-8873

Merits decision, January 14, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

Submitted for merits consideration January 14, 1997; decided January 14, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the Eleventh Circuit, No. 93-9133, 72 F.3d 844, which affirmed a bench-trial damages judgment. Review concerns whether §1350 supports judicial recognition of this civil action, whether compensatory and punitive remedies are authorized, and whether the political-question doctrine bars individual claims for official torture abroad. No earlier oral argument is stated.

#### Judgment

The aggregate award remains $1,500,000. Scalia and Thomas would reverse both components and remand because they reject the asserted action. All nine separately agree on statutory jurisdiction and the absence of a categorical political-question bar. The judgment decides categorical authority on the proved torture ground; it adds no TVPA claim, damages allocation or amount assessment.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stevens | O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer | Affirmance of both damages components | Parts I–V in full |

#### Holdings and Controlling Reasons

##### I. Jurisdiction does not itself establish a damages action

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1350 supplies district-court jurisdiction over an alien's civil tort action alleging a violation of the law of nations or a United States treaty; these substantial allegations of official torture fall within that jurisdiction. Jurisdiction alone neither supplies a domestic damages action nor establishes liability, sovereign-immunity waiver or authority for every asserted international claim.

**Authority:** Part I of Stevens's Opinion of the Court; all nine Justices join.

**Controlling explanation:** The statute identifies the forum and category of controversy. Whether domestic law provides the asserted action is a separate question. Suter requires attention to the source of an enforceable right and action, and Meyer distinguishes federal adjudicative authority and consent from substantive liability. Those distinctions do not make a substantial source-of-action dispute a reason to deny jurisdiction over this pleaded category. The plaintiffs are aliens alleging torts violating the established prohibition of official torture. Their allegations establish the statutory kind of controversy; findings and governing law must separately support relief. No sovereign is made a defendant and no immunity issue is decided.

##### II. A confined received action for personally committed official torture

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1350's reception of judicial adjudication of law-of-nations torts, read in its common-law setting, supports a confined domestic civil action against an individual for deliberate participation in or personal supervision of official torture. The actionable norm forbids severe physical or mental suffering intentionally inflicted under official authority for interrogation, punishment or intimidation; international law supplies that specific obligatory prohibition, while the received domestic action supplies authority to enforce it.

**Authority:** Part II of Stevens's opinion; the seven-Justice coalition identified in the opinion table joins. Scalia and Thomas do not join.

**Controlling explanation:** Universal condemnation alone is not domestic remedial authority, and the jurisdictional label alone cannot resolve the action question. Congress expressly provided adjudication of law-of-nations torts against the common-law background in which such wrongs received judicial remedies. That particular statutory function supports this determinate, established personal-tort action. Filartiga supplies persuasive evidence of judicial recognition of the official-torture prohibition and its civil enforcement. Central Bank's confined received-action reasoning and Musick, Peeler's treatment of incidents of an established action offer analogies; neither itself creates an action under §1350. Suter's administrative-plan provisions supply no contrary rule for this received tort, and O'Melveny rejected a new receiver preference rather than this source-specific construction. The contrary argument correctly separates jurisdiction from liability but gives insufficient effect to the statute's particular reception of tort adjudication. The supported trial findings establish Negewo's direct participation and personal supervision concerning Abebe-Jira and Taye, and personal supervision of some torture concerning Demissie. Official title alone is insufficient. The Court sustains the judgment on torture, without adopting a general Lincoln Mills or legislative-history ground, and announces no action for every customary norm, private nonstate violence, corporate liability, absent-superior liability or every cruel, inhuman or degrading act.

##### III. Compensation for proved injury within that action

**Holding and operative rule:** The recognized action permits compensatory damages for proved injury caused by the individual's qualifying official torture. The established trial findings support categorical compensatory authority for each plaintiff, without substituting allegations or official position for personal responsibility and causation.

**Authority:** Part III of Stevens's opinion; the same seven-Justice coalition joins the compensatory holding.

**Controlling explanation:** Traditional tort reparation compensates an injured person for loss caused by the defendant's wrongful conduct. Franklin preserves appropriate relief within a cognizable action, while requiring the action to be established first. Part II supplies that separate source here. The bench-trial findings establish the personal torture ground; this is not a damages award based solely on the complaint. The source objection therefore does not defeat compensation once the confined action is recognized. Each plaintiff's $200,000 compensatory award remains in place against the granted categorical objection. The Court neither enlarges the judgment nor adjudicates an independent challenge to amount or trial allocation.

##### IV. Punitive authority requires an independently justified aggravated-tort remedy

**Holding and operative rule:** Within this established action for intentionally inflicted official torture, traditional common-law remedies permit punitive damages for malicious, willful or equivalently reckless aggravated wrongdoing; punitive relief is permitted, not required in every case. The punitive culpability standard does not dilute the underlying action's intentional-official-torture predicate, and the proved deliberate personal torture supports punitive availability here while amount and lawful assessment remain distinct questions.

**Authority:** Part IV of Stevens's opinion; the six Justices joining it in full directly adopt the complete punitive-culpability formulation. Stone-Zsela joins punitive authority and its application to proved deliberate personal torture, but not equivalently reckless aggravation. Seven therefore directly support the deliberate-torture ground sustaining the judgment. Scalia and Thomas reject relief because they reject the action's source.

**Controlling explanation:** Punishment cannot be inferred from compensation alone. Arms distinguishes compensation from the traditional punitive response to aggravated personal wrongdoing. Filartiga's remedial decision supplies a persuasive application to official torture. The domestic action's common-law remedial character and that independently supported aggravated-tort remedy authorize punishment here; Franklin expressly left punitive authority open and does not answer this question. The concern that punitive relief has penal and international consequences warrants attention to the source, culpability and remedy, but does not eliminate an ordinary remedy for this established intentional personal tort. No sovereign or population is punished and no criminal conviction is entered. The Court does not import Filartiga's default, its assessment of a foreign prosecution, its choice-of-law findings or its dollar amounts. Each plaintiff's $300,000 punitive award survives the categorical authority objection. TXO and BMW retain their actual constraints and disagreements: this grant supplies no independent amount challenge, wealth finding, assessment formula, required ratio or certification of every possible calculation. The Court authorizes no universal punitive remedy for international wrongs.

##### V. The foreign setting does not make this individual tort a political question

**Holding and operative rule:** Adjudication of these individual official-torture claims is not categorically barred as a political question because it applies a determinate prohibition to personal conduct without deciding the legitimacy of Ethiopia's government, directing United States diplomacy or resolving a constitutionally committed war-powers dispute. A concrete immunity defense or contrary political-branch determination would require its own consideration; none is presented or decided here.

**Authority:** Part V of Stevens's opinion; all nine Justices join. Scalia and Thomas's agreement supplies neither an action nor their support for the damages judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** Baker requires a concrete constitutional commitment or obstacle to judicial decision, not merely a foreign setting. Montana and Nixon preserve that issue-specific inquiry. Courts can decide personal conduct under an established torture prohibition and apply ordinary remedial law without choosing a foreign government or supervising diplomacy. Sanchez-Espinoza concerned United States foreign-policy operations and domestic sovereign immunity; its express distinction of suits against foreign officials defeats using it as a categorical prohibition here. The Court does not decide a new immunity question or an unpresented defense. The absence of a political-question bar does not answer the independent source-of-action and remedy inquiries.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Suter v. Artist M.: its separation of enforceable right, action and relief remains; its administrative-plan ruling does not foreclose this source-specific received action.
- FDIC v. Meyer: jurisdiction and consent remain distinct from a substantive action; no sovereign-immunity rule is enlarged.
- O'Melveny & Myers v. FDIC: the rejection of a new receiver preference is distinguished from §1350's particular common-law function.
- Central Bank of Denver v. First Interstate Bank and Musick, Peeler & Garrett v. Employers Insurance: their confined received-action reasoning supports analogy, without importing securities liability or independently establishing an ATS action.
- Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools: appropriate remedies presuppose an action; compensation is supported at that scope, and punitive authority is independently decided here.
- Milwaukee & St. Paul Railway Co. v. Arms: its traditional distinction between compensation and punitive relief for aggravated wrongdoing informs the punitive rule.
- TXO Production Corp. v. Alliance Resources and BMW of North America v. Gore: their actual procedural and substantive punitive limits remain unchanged; no new amount-review rule is adopted.
- Baker v. Carr, United States Department of Commerce v. Montana and Nixon v. United States: their concrete political-question inquiry is applied without a foreign-affairs category bar.
- Filartiga's Second Circuit and damages decisions supply persuasive support for this established official-torture action and its remedies. Sanchez-Espinoza's domestic-immunity and United States foreign-policy setting is distinguished, consistently with its express distinction of foreign-official suits. These lower-court decisions are not treated as prior Supreme Court holdings.

#### Law After Decision

Section 1350's forum grant remains distinct from an actionable norm, a domestic action and the permitted relief. The Court recognizes a confined received action for proved personal official torture, with compensatory relief and independently justified punitive authority. It does not make every customary-international-law norm privately enforceable, impose liability from official title, adjudicate a sovereign's immunity or authorize general supervision of foreign policy. The underlying tort still requires intentional infliction under official authority; the punitive aggravation formulation does not create a cause for merely reckless conduct.

#### Separate Writings

Justice Scalia, joined by Justice Thomas, concurs in Parts I and V and dissents from the damages judgment. They accept the statutory forum and reject a categorical political-question bar, but find no sufficient congressional authority for the asserted domestic damages action. The prohibition of torture does not itself authorize a federal cause of action. A forum for independently actionable law-of-nations torts does not necessarily authorize judicial recognition of every later norm; the common-law setting and lower-court practice are the strongest affirmative arguments, but do not establish the needed source for these Justices.

Suter requires an actual source of private liability; O'Melveny rejects the manufacture of a federal rule from general federal interests; Meyer keeps jurisdiction and substantive action distinct. Franklin presupposed a cognizable action, and their concurrence there relied on a narrower enacted-remedies ground. Central Bank's binding received-securities holding remains confined to its established setting, and Musick, Peeler concerns incidents of already recognized liability. Neither supplies the missing ATS premise. Sanchez-Espinoza's domestic-immunity result is not a categorical bar to foreign-official suits. Scalia and Thomas would reverse the damages judgment and remand on the absence-of-action ground, rejecting both compensation and punishment through that ground. They announce no categorical objection to punitive damages in an otherwise authorized intentional-tort action, do not approve torture, and do not reach an unpleaded TVPA claim, limitations or appointed-counsel question.

The findings and individual awards are reported in the [Eleventh Circuit opinion, 72 F.3d 844](https://openjurist.org/72/f3d/844/abebe-jira-v-negewo), also reproduced in [the complete judicial text](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-11th-circuit/1056244.html). The reported record does not provide every trial allocation or punitive calculation; no such missing detail is decided on the categorical questions before the Court. Filartiga v. Peña-Irala, 630 F.2d 876 and 577 F. Supp. 860, supports the specific tort and remedial analysis; Arms, 91 U.S. 489, supplies the traditional punitive distinction. January 14 is the submission and decision date; no separate earlier oral argument is established.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Eleventh Circuit judgment remains in force. No remand, new trial, reallocation or recalculation is directed. The ungranted limitations and appointed-counsel questions are not reopened; ordinary jurisdictional, personal-responsibility and amount-of-damages limits remain.

### Kadic v. Karadzic; Doe v. Karadzic, Second Circuit Nos. 94-9035 and 94-9069

Merits decision, April 21, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

The included domestic-source question is distinct from jurisdiction. Ultimate liability and amount of relief are not presented.

Karadzic seeks restoration of dismissal. The two plaintiff groups seek continuation on allegations of killing, rape, forced prostitution and impregnation, torture, and a personally planned or directed campaign against protected ethnic or religious groups. #### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. Jurisdiction and the domestic action are separate questions

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1350 gives the district court jurisdiction over these substantial allegations by aliens of torts violating the law of nations; it imposes no universal official-action requirement on norms that bind private individuals. A federal forum, an applicable international norm, a domestic civil action, this defendant's responsibility, and authority for the requested remedy remain separate requirements.

**Authority:** Part I of Ginsburg's opinion, joined by all eight other Justices, controls. Unless a different coalition is specified below, Parts IV–VI have that same nine-Justice support; Parts II–III have the seven-Justice coalition identified in the opinion table.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court applies Abebe-Jira's separation of jurisdiction from liability. The complaints allege international wrongs, rather than merely an ordinary domestic assault involving foreign persons. Their asserted source of civil liability presents a substantive question within that jurisdiction. A norm's personal scope must be determined from that norm: an official-action requirement attached to one prohibition cannot be transferred to another that expressly binds individuals. Suter supplies the distinction between an enforceable obligation and an action; neither that distinction nor an eventual failure on the merits defeats jurisdiction over this pleaded category. The allegations do not establish that a plaintiff has proved every required element.

##### II. A confined civil action reaches personally committed or directed genocide

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1350's reception of law-of-nations tort adjudication supports a domestic civil action against an individual for personally perpetrating, planning, or directing genocide, whether or not that individual acted under state authority. The plaintiff must establish intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial, or religious group as such, through killing members, causing serious bodily or mental harm, deliberately imposing conditions calculated to bring about the group's physical destruction in whole or in part, imposing measures intended to prevent births, or forcibly transferring children to another group, together with the defendant's qualifying responsibility, causation, and a lawful basis for relief.

**Authority:** Part II of Ginsburg's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, and Breyer: seven direct joins. Scalia and Thomas accept the norm's private coverage but reject this additional domestic action.

**Controlling explanation:** Abebe-Jira recognizes the particular statutory reception of a determinate personal tort while reserving private violence. The Court now resolves that reservation for genocide. Article IV of the Genocide Convention expressly subjects private individuals to its prohibition, and Article II supplies the defined destructive acts and group-destruction intent. These provisions identify the obligatory norm; the distinct domestic source is the received-action construction of §1350. The Convention and the criminal implementation chapter are not themselves treated as civil causes. Section 1092's disclaimer of an enforceable substantive or procedural right created by that criminal chapter remains fully effective. It does not repeal this independently received action, and the criminal statute's territorial and nationality limits govern prosecution rather than silently amending §1350. The contrary argument correctly demands a domestic source but gives insufficient effect to the particular reception function already recognized in Abebe-Jira. The alleged personal planning and direction of a campaign against protected groups suffice to defeat categorical dismissal. Ethnic cleansing, rape, forced pregnancy, and murder must still satisfy the defined act and specific intent; their labels prove neither. No universal action for every international norm or liability from command title follows.

##### III. The pleaded core war crimes do not require a recognized state actor

**Holding and operative rule:** The same confined received domestic action reaches personally perpetrated, planned, or directed deliberate killing, torture, and grave sexual violence against protected persons as core war crimes, without requiring that the defendant act for a diplomatically recognized state. Each claim requires the relevant armed conflict, protected victim, prohibited act, connection between the act and the conflict, personal responsibility, causation, and independently supported relief; the Court recognizes no civil action for every breach of a treaty provision.

**Authority:** Part III of Ginsburg's opinion, joined by the same seven Justices as Part II. Scalia and Thomas accept the individual international obligations but reject the additional received civil action.

**Operative norm and limits:** Common Article 3 binds each party to a noninternational armed conflict occurring in the territory of a High Contracting Party. It protects persons taking no active part in hostilities, including members of armed forces who have laid down their arms and persons out of combat through sickness, wounds, detention, or any other cause. It requires humane treatment without adverse distinction based on race, colour, religion or faith, sex, birth, wealth, or similar criteria. At any time and place it prohibits, against those persons, violence to life and person, especially murder, mutilation, cruel treatment and torture; hostage-taking; outrages upon personal dignity, especially humiliating and degrading treatment; and sentences or executions without a previous judgment by a regularly constituted court affording indispensable judicial guarantees. Its further commands and permissions remain distinct: the wounded and sick must be collected and cared for; an impartial humanitarian body may offer services; the parties should endeavour to put other Convention provisions into force through special agreements; application does not affect the parties' legal status. The civil holding concerns the pleaded core wrongs, not automatic enforcement of every one of these clauses.

**Controlling explanation:** Common Article 3 applies to each party to the defined conflict. A categorical recognized-state requirement would defeat that individual protection. Abebe-Jira's particular reception rationale supplies the domestic source; neither the existence of a treaty obligation nor the seriousness of an alleged injury alone creates a civil action. Yamashita confirms individual duties in war, but its military adjudication does not supply this domestic civil action or decide an absent commander's liability. The complaints' allegations of personal planning and direction permit proceedings on the defined claims. They do not establish protected status, the conflict connection, any defendant's responsibility, or damages. The Court does not determine Protocol II's customary status, decide alternative international-conflict theories, adopt a comprehensive command-responsibility rule, or transplant Central Bank's securities-assistance standard. These reservations preserve questions unnecessary to the pleaded deliberate-conduct ground.

##### IV. Official torture and the enacted TVPA route retain their separate conditions

**Holding and operative rule:** Abebe-Jira's action remains available for deliberate personal participation in or supervision of severe physical or mental suffering intentionally inflicted under official authority for interrogation, punishment, or intimidation; ordinary private torture or killing outside a proved genocide or war-crime setting requires an independently authorized route. The Torture Victim Protection Act separately authorizes damages against an individual who, under actual or apparent authority or color of law of a foreign nation, subjects an individual to the statute's defined torture or extrajudicial killing, subject to its beneficiaries, exhaustion requirement, ten-year limit, definitions, and exclusions stated here.

**Authority:** Part IV of Ginsburg's opinion has nine direct joins. Scalia and Thomas apply the controlling official-torture holding without withdrawing their disagreement with its source; they independently accept the TVPA's enacted action.

**Statutory conditions:** Torture damages run to the injured individual; killing damages run to the individual's legal representative or a person entitled to claim in a wrongful-death action. The court shall decline to hear a claim when adequate and available remedies in the place where the conduct occurred have not been exhausted. Suit must commence within ten years after the cause arose. Extrajudicial killing means a deliberated killing not authorized by a previous judgment of a regularly constituted court affording indispensable judicial guarantees; it excludes a killing lawfully carried out under foreign national authority under international law. Torture requires intentionally inflicted severe physical or mental pain or suffering against a person in the offender's custody or physical control, excluding pain arising only from, inherent in, or incidental to lawful sanctions. Its purposes include obtaining information or a confession from the victim or a third person; punishing the victim for an act the victim or a third person committed or is suspected of committing; intimidating or coercing the victim or a third person; and discrimination of any kind. Mental pain means prolonged mental harm caused by intentional or threatened severe physical pain; administration, application, or threatened use of mind-altering substances or procedures calculated to disrupt profoundly the senses or personality; threatened imminent death; or a threat that another person imminently will suffer death, severe physical pain or suffering, or those substances or procedures.

**Controlling explanation:** An enacted cause remains distinct from a received action. The TVPA permits these claims to be considered under its actual words; neither a generalized allegation of cruelty nor inability to identify a better forum establishes its statutory conditions. Its definitions do not automatically rewrite every element of Abebe-Jira's separate action. Where torture or killing also satisfies the distinct genocide or war-crime norm, Parts II–III govern that additional route; ordinary private violence acquires no federal cause merely from its gravity. Section 1331 supplies federal-question jurisdiction for the enacted TVPA claim, but the Court does not decide whether it supplies a universal customary-law action. Nor is a further independent ATS action for ordinary official execution necessary to the disposition. The absence of a finding about exhaustion, timeliness, or a defined element requires claim-specific adjudication, not assumed compliance or forfeiture.

##### V. Nonrecognition alone does not defeat an adequately alleged official connection

**Holding and operative rule:** Diplomatic nonrecognition does not categorically foreclose proof of governmental attributes or concerted action with an existing foreign state where the applicable norm requires official action. The TVPA still requires actual or apparent authority or color of law attributable to a foreign nation; a claimed title or mere semblance of authority is insufficient by itself.

**Authority:** Part V of Ginsburg's opinion, joined by all other Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** The district court's blanket approach failed to address the alternative allegations of concerted action with Yugoslavia and Serbia. The plaintiffs may also attempt to establish the alleged government's population, territory, governmental organization, and capacity for relations. Allowing proof does not diplomatically recognize a government or establish its attributes. Lugar supplies a useful analogy concerning joint action with government, but does not transfer every §1983 rule into international law or the TVPA. The operative source must establish the necessary official connection in each claim. Neither an alleged presidency nor the Court's rejection of categorical dismissal supplies that proof.

##### VI. The asserted foreign-affairs objection does not require dismissal

**Holding and operative rule:** These defined claims against an individual are not categorically nonjusticiable merely because they arise during a foreign civil war or concern a political leader. Dismissal as a political question requires an identified constitutional commitment, absence of manageable judicial standards, or other concrete obstacle under Baker; none is established by this record.

**Authority:** Part VI of Ginsburg's opinion, joined by all other Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Baker requires examination of the issue the court must actually decide. The Court can apply defined personal-tort elements without selecting Bosnia's government, fixing borders, or directing negotiations. Abebe-Jira applies that distinction to foreign individual misconduct, while Nixon concerns a particular Senate impeachment procedure and supplies no categorical foreign-affairs bar. The published United States position expressly disclaims a political-question obstacle; it reinforces, rather than replaces, the independent judicial inquiry. No present executive recognition or immunity determination in this defendant's favor is established. Act of state was not asserted below and is not decided. The absence of an established suitable alternative forum on this record neither creates a worldwide forum duty nor resolves any properly supported later forum contention.

##### VII. Invitee status supplies no general immunity; particular service questions remain

**Holding and operative rule:** The United Nations Headquarters Agreement gives these invitees no general immunity from suit or from the reported service outside the headquarters district: §9's consent requirement for service inside that district, §15's privileges for designated member-state representatives, and §11's transit protections do not establish that asserted immunity. Kadic's court-authorized delivery through a security officer and admitted receipt permit continuation; Doe's intercepted hotel-lobby delivery, disputed distance, and remaining service issues require their own adjudication.

**Authority:** Part VII's rejection of general invitee immunity is joined by all nine. Its plaintiff-specific service application is joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer; Stone-Zsela agrees with that disposition without joining this additional service rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** The Agreement differentiates the headquarters district, designated representatives, and travel to the United Nations. It does not convert every invitee into a diplomat immune from civil proceedings. The State Department letter reports no invitee immunity. A narrowly presented question about direct airport-to-headquarters transit remains open because the reported service circumstances do not present it. The two plaintiffs' delivery records cannot be collapsed: acknowledged receipt under an alternate-service order in Kadic differs from the contested delivery in Doe. Rejection of a general immunity defense does not adjudicate every ordinary service or personal-jurisdiction objection. No general exception to sovereign or diplomatic immunity for international torts is announced.

##### VIII. Affirmance restores proceedings, not liability or an award

**Holding and operative rule:** The Second Circuit's reversal of the blanket dismissal is affirmed, and both actions return for claim-specific service, factual, liability, and remedial adjudication under the surviving federal routes. Supplemental claims may receive renewed consideration because federal claims survive, but neither an exercise of supplemental jurisdiction nor any state-law judgment follows automatically.

**Authority:** The affirmance and full scope of surviving actions have seven judgment votes, with Scalia and Thomas dissenting only as to the new actions in Parts II–III. All nine agree on the ordinary proof and remedial limits; all eight Associates join the supplemental-claim rationale, while Stone-Zsela joins that disposition only.

**Controlling explanation:** The pleaded conduct suffices to defeat the categorical grounds on which dismissal rested. Compensation still requires an authorized action, personal responsibility, causation, and proved loss. Abebe-Jira's punitive rule retains its established scope for proved official torture and is not automatically extended to every additional category. Franklin's appropriate-relief principle presupposes an action and left punitive authority open. Any additional relief needs its own legal source and predicate facts. The Court awards no damages, punishment, fees, class certification, or injunction, and authorizes no prospective supervision of a foreign government. #### Holdings and Controlling Reasons

##### I. The hearing restriction requires applicant-attributed failure

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 2254(e)(2) is triggered by deficient diligence or greater fault attributable to the applicant or counsel in developing the claim's state-court factual basis; an incomplete record alone is insufficient. When triggered, the applicant must show either a previously unavailable new constitutional rule made retroactive by this Court on collateral review or a factual predicate previously undiscoverable through due diligence, and must also show by clear and convincing evidence that, but for constitutional error, no reasonable factfinder would have found guilt of the underlying offense.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join Part I of Justice Ginsburg's opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** Diligence measures reasonable effort against the information and procedures then available, not investigative success. Counsel must pursue identified material leads and seek the development a claim needs. A generic request is neither always sufficient nor always deficient, and counsel need not discover every public document without an informative lead. Attributing failure to the applicant gives the opening condition work independent of the statute's restrictive exceptions. Those exceptions do not transform state-prevented diligent development into applicant fault.

The opposite deliberate-bypass-only interpretation would preserve the former rule despite Congress's new restriction on deficient diligence. Keeney retained Townsend's deliberate-bypass qualification and did not make ordinary negligence a former-law hearing bar. The new statute changes that consequence where it applies; it does not ratify a former cause-and-prejudice rule. Lindh requires attention to the provision's temporal reach, which includes this post-enactment application. Keeney continues in its former-law domain, while Coleman independently governs actual state claim defaults. No freestanding miscarriage exception substitutes for the enacted cumulative requirements.

##### II. The known psychiatric report triggers the statutory bar

**Holding and operative rule:** Counsel's failure to pursue an identified psychiatric report through reasonably available means, after receiving a transcript repeatedly referring to it, constitutes deficient diligence here. Williams's conceded inability to satisfy section 2254(e)(2)(B) therefore forbids the requested hearing on that report claim.

**Authority:** Part II of Justice Ginsburg's opinion is joined by all seven other Associate Justices and controls with eight votes.

**Controlling explanation:** The report was in Cruse's court file, and the transcript supplied a concrete lead. The broad request for psychological examinations, refused without a court order, did not exhaust reasonably available pursuit of that identified document. The allegation of a Brady violation does not establish diligent investigation or material suppression merely because disclosure was disputed. No bad-faith finding is necessary, and none is made. The concession concerning the cumulative innocence requirement resolves the hearing prohibition without deciding every alternative exception. This is a hearing-bar affirmance, not a new Brady merits decision or finding of guilt, and this claim's neglect is not imputed to the two relationship claims.

##### III. Unknown relationships do not establish a neglected available lead

**Holding and operative rule:** The applicant did not fail to develop the juror-relationship and prosecutor-nondisclosure claims where counsel lacked a lead to the concealed relationships, reasonably sought investigative assistance, and the State opposed and the court denied that assistance. The public existence of a divorce record does not by itself establish deficient diligence, so section 2254(e)(2) does not bar factual development of these claims.

**Authority:** All eight Associate Justices join Part III of Justice Ginsburg's opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** A diligent lawyer need not check every juror's entire public-record history despite answers supplying no reason to discover the undisclosed marriage and prior representation. The reasonable-effort rule does not excuse ignored suspicious answers or known relationships; none is established here. The argument that a broad investigator request can never suffice would require counsel to identify the very omitted relationship before obtaining assistance to investigate the omission. Denied assistance matters to available means, without establishing that the State caused every record gap.

The two claims share an investigative history but retain distinct merits elements. Woodson's claimed lack of recollection, Stinnett's affidavit and the limited witness testimony are not resolved findings of honesty, dishonesty, bias or harmlessness. Removal of this statutory bar neither compels immaterial factfinding nor guarantees a writ. An independently sufficient lawful ground may resolve a claim without an unnecessary hearing; otherwise the lower courts must apply the remaining hearing and merits requirements.

##### IV. Cause is established only for the specified concealed-fact nonpresentation

**Holding and operative rule:** The concealed relationships, lack of an informative lead, reasonable unsuccessful investigative efforts and later unavailability of state relief establish cause insofar as default consists of failing to present these two relationship-based claims in the Virginia proceeding. That conclusion does not excuse a different default or establish prejudice; the appropriate prejudice standard and its application remain for the lower courts.

**Authority:** Part IV of Justice Ginsburg's opinion has the support of all eight Associates. Stone-Zsela agrees with rejection of the lower no-cause ground without joining this reasoning.

**Controlling explanation:** Coleman preserves an independent state-default inquiry, but the same absence of a reasonably available lead that defeats the diligence inference defeats the lower no-cause inference for this specific nonpresentation. Lack of a state remedy alone is not cause, and an incomplete factual record alone is not an adequate independent state procedural ground. The result follows from the combined supported circumstances, not an assumption of constitutional misconduct or an exemption for every newly discovered claim.

The applicable ordinary hearing rules, exhaustion, section 2254(e)(1)'s factual presumption and clear-and-convincing rebuttal, and section 2254(d)'s restriction where a state merits adjudication actually occurred remain distinct. Weeks preserves that actual merits prerequisite; a short state explanation does not eliminate it. The statute permits, but does not command, denial of an unexhausted claim on the merits; unavailable or ineffective state process and express State-counsel waiver retain their separate conditions. No such route awards relief automatically here.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Keeney and Townsend retain their deliberate-bypass former-law hearing rule. The later statute changes the consequence of deficient diligence in applications it governs; the Court does not rewrite Keeney's prior holding.
- Lindh is applied for provision-specific temporal reach; this post-enactment application is governed by section 2254(e)(2).
- Coleman remains controlling for an actual adequate and independent state claim default; hearing eligibility does not eliminate it.
- Vasquez retains the distinction between supplementing an exhausted claim and fundamentally altering it into a new claim.
- Weeks preserves the actual state-merits predicate and independent statutory conditions for relief; no general construction of section 2254(d)(1) beyond that holding is adopted here.
- Brady supplies the alleged disclosure duty, but neither that allegation nor the hearing ruling establishes suppression or materiality on the report claim.

#### Law After Decision

Applicant-attributed deficient diligence triggers section 2254(e)(2)'s restrictive, cumulative exceptions. Diligent efforts defeated by unavailable information or state process do not. The report hearing remains barred; the two relationship claims return without the rejected diligence and specified no-cause barriers. Ultimate bias, misconduct, prejudice, ordinary hearing entitlement and habeas relief remain unresolved. The separate plea-agreement merits disposition is not revived.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela agrees that reasonable and timely efforts appropriate to available information prevent an applicant-attributed failure, and that strategic inaction or unexplained neglect remains subject to the statute. He would vacate and remand the diligence and hearing questions on all three claims rather than finally classify their development here. The district court should permit the development needed to determine diligence and the material juror-bias, nondisclosure and materiality issues, with a hearing conditioned on the required diligence and remaining legal requirements. Keeney distinguishes a lost opportunity from deliberate bypass, but the applicable AEDPA text controls. The writing neither grants a writ nor removes other hearing and merits restrictions. Stone-Zsela adds no separate cause ground.

### Williams v. Taylor (Terry Williams), No. 98-8384

Merits decision, April 18, 2000

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 4, 1999, following the April 5 grant. On certiorari to the Fourth Circuit, 163 F.3d 860, Nos. 98-14 and 98-16. This is the capital-sentencing ineffective-assistance case, distinct from Michael Wayne Williams's factual-development case.

The questions concern section 2254(d)(1)'s contrary-to and unreasonable-application clauses, use of Fretwell in applying Strickland, and whether Virginia's unanimity requirement demands a probability that all twelve jurors would favor life. The June 6, 1997 state merits judgment rejected sentencing relief; the federal district court granted it April 7, 1998; the Fourth Circuit reversed that grant December 18, 1998 and affirmed dismissal of independent claims. The December 12, 1997 federal application is governed by amended section 2254(d). The 1988 state petition does not change that federal filing date. The Warden retains the adequate-performance defense as well as lack of prejudice.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

**Reversed on the granted sentencing claim and remanded for conditional relief, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Scalia and Thomas.

Virginia must provide a constitutionally adequate new sentencing proceeding within a reasonable period to be fixed by the lower federal court, or replace the death sentence with a lawful noncapital sentence. The convictions and independent ungranted cross-appeal rulings remain undisturbed. No acquittal, immediate release, life-without-parole direction, new collateral evidentiary hearing, expert appointment or execution/stay action is ordered.

Neither four-Justice account of the exclusive general allocation between the clauses commands a majority. The common rules and the independently supported grounds below control at their stated scope. No controlling rationale is constructed by combining those competing general accounts.

#### Holdings and Controlling Reasons

##### I. Section 2254(d)(1) has independent clauses and objective legal limits

**Holding and operative rule:** For a claim adjudicated on its merits in state court, the contrary-to and unreasonable-application clauses perform independent work: conflict with this Court's governing legal rule can satisfy the first without factual identity, and an opposite result on materially indistinguishable facts also qualifies. Unreasonable application is an objective legal inquiry, not a count of judges; clearly established federal law consists of this Court's holdings at the relevant state merits decision, and a governing rule such as Strickland is not excluded merely because it is general.

**Authority:** Part I of Justice Breyer's opinion, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg, supplies eight direct votes for these common propositions. It does not resolve the competing exclusive general allocations between the clauses.

**Controlling explanation:** The statute states alternatives. Requiring both identical law and identical facts for every contrary-to determination would deny relief even when a state court adopted a rule directly inconsistent with governing law. Requiring agreement from every actual reasonable jurist would turn objective review into a poll and let the existence of an adverse decision establish its own reasonableness. The Fourth Circuit's formulations cannot operate as those categorical barriers.

Strickland prescribes a governing rule for ineffective assistance. Its generality leaves room for differing reasonable applications; it does not make the rule unavailable as clearly established law. The relevant date is the June 6, 1997 state merits adjudication. Supreme Court holdings then in force supply the source, not later holdings, dicta or circuit precedent.

Lindh and Carriger preserve their identified pre-enactment applications; Williams filed federally after enactment. Weeks requires statutory eligibility independently of a constitutional-error finding. Section 2254(a)'s unlawful-custody condition, (d)(2)'s factual ground and (e)(1)'s factual presumption and clear-and-convincing rebuttal retain their distinct tasks. The legal question receives no imported clear-and-convincing burden. No new hearing or section (e)(2) determination follows, and the Court does not invalidate section (d) under Article III or the Suspension Clause. Neither exclusive general construction of the two clauses obtains majority support beyond the shared propositions stated here.

##### II. Lawful mitigation receives ordinary prejudice review under Virginia's unanimity rule

**Holding and operative rule:** In an ordinary sentencing ineffective-assistance claim, Strickland requires a reasonable probability of a different lawful result, sufficient to undermine confidence, assessed on the full supported aggravating and mitigating record; Fretwell adds no separate unfairness requirement. Because Virginia requires unanimity for death and directs life if the penalty jury cannot agree, a reasonable probability that one conscientious juror would decline death suffices to change the legally available outcome; a bare possible holdout or idiosyncratic mercy does not.

**Authority:** Part I, with the same eight-Associate coalition, directly adopts these rules. The seven-Justice Part II applies them in finding the actual constitutional violation.

**Controlling explanation:** Strickland evaluates reasonable professional assistance and outcome confidence rather than demanding proof that a different sentence was more likely than not. Fretwell's limited unavailable-benefit rule concerns a displaced substantive entitlement that governing law does not recognize where no other fair or reliable protection was lost. Its controlling opinion expressly preserves ordinary probability in the usual case. Lockett, Eddings and Skipper recognize lawful consideration of relevant mitigation; lost investigation of that material is not the forbidden substantive advantage addressed by Fretwell.

Virginia Code section 19.2-264.4(E) makes disagreement about death consequential: the penalty jury is dismissed and life imposed. Twelve votes for life are therefore unnecessary. Strickland nevertheless assumes reasonable conscientious decisionmakers applying law. It requires probability, not speculation that a particularly lenient juror might disregard the evidence. The same rule permits disagreement here about the weight of the full record without altering the unanimity consequence.

##### III. The state prejudice application falls outside the range permitted by governing law

**Holding and operative rule:** A state court unreasonably applies Strickland when its prejudice inquiry materially truncates the supported mitigation or weighs it in an objectively indefensible way; mere disagreement among reasonable jurists does not satisfy section 2254(d)(1). The actual treatment of Williams's documentary deprivation, functional limitations and structured-setting evidence, considered with the complete sentencing record, is objectively unreasonable and independently satisfies the statutory condition for review of his constitutional entitlement.

**Authority:** Part II of Breyer's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg, supplies seven direct votes. The four Justices favoring broader governing-rule review independently join this actual objective-unreasonableness conclusion; their broader construction is unnecessary to it.

**Controlling explanation:** The state court recited ordinary probability as well as Fretwell's fairness language. A mistaken additional gloss does not automatically eliminate an independent correct ground. The Court therefore examines that alternative rather than granting relief simply because Fretwell was cited.

The actual discussion largely reduced the omitted case to favorable impressions of relatives and acquaintances. It did not adequately assess the different documentary account of hunger, neglect, alcoholism, violence and removal from home, the fuller educational and functional history, and concrete evidence of functioning in structured settings. That evidence is not merely another favorable character opinion. It supplies an explanation and a response to future danger that the sparse trial presentation lacked.

The Court includes the severe aggravation, existing mitigation, adverse juvenile entries, later family improvement, jail misconduct and possible parole. It assumes neither that every proffer was credited nor that every juvenile entry would necessarily be admitted. The defect is legal evaluation of the supported total picture, not a new factual finding under section (d)(2) or disregard of section (e)(1).

This is more than a preferred federal weighing of a reasonably evaluated record. The qualitative omissions make the state assessment fall outside the range Strickland permits. The generality of Strickland leaves a range; it does not shelter an assessment that materially truncates what competent representation would have supplied. Satisfaction of that statutory condition remains distinct from the actual performance and prejudice findings that follow. No unproved diagnosis or disputed additional expert prediction is necessary.

##### IV. The preserved adequate-performance defense fails on the actual investigation

**Holding and operative rule:** Counsel must reasonably investigate available mitigation or make a reasonable, informed decision that further investigation is unnecessary; a later assertion of strategy does not excuse an uninformed failure to investigate identified records. Williams's counsel performed deficiently by failing to obtain the available youth records and develop the supported mitigation, independently of tactical decisions about particular cumulative witnesses.

**Authority:** Part II's same seven-Justice coalition adopts the performance rule and actual deficiency finding. Scalia and Thomas assume deficiency without deciding it; their votes do not support a performance holding.

**Controlling explanation:** Strickland assesses counsel's conduct from the time of representation. Sentencing preparation began about a week before trial. Lead counsel did not seek youth records because of a mistaken belief that state law prevented access and acknowledged that their known contents would have been used. Requests to Williams and relatives for helpful information, initially yielding a stable-home account, did not reasonably end investigation of readily identifiable records and leads.

Counsel consulted mental-health professionals and curtailed additional character testimony after damaging cross-examination. Those were real professional acts. An informed decision to limit cumulative witnesses, however, does not explain an uninformed failure to learn the documented childhood and functional history. Investigation would permit a reasoned choice about benefit, adverse information and presentation; the Court does not require automatic introduction of every record or witness.

The Warden preserved performance as an alternative ground, and the state and appellate courts merely assumed deficiency. The Court rejects that defense on the supported investigation failure rather than treating those assumptions as concessions. Counsel's alleged illness, separate expert-access claims and the necessity of individual wife, daughter or Elliott testimony are not independent grounds. The existing developed record permits this conclusion without a new hearing.

##### V. The complete sentencing picture establishes actual prejudice and sentence-only relief

**Holding and operative rule:** When the supported evidence competent mitigation investigation would have produced is considered with the actual aggravation and defense evidence, there is a reasonable probability that at least one conscientious Virginia juror would have declined death. Williams therefore establishes both Strickland requirements and is entitled to a conditional writ requiring constitutionally adequate resentencing within a reasonable period or a lawful noncapital sentence, while his convictions and independent ungranted claims remain undisturbed.

**Authority:** Part II's seven-Justice coalition adopts actual prejudice and the stated remedy, independently of any broader general construction of section 2254(d)(1).

**Controlling explanation:** The Court gives full weight to the brutal murder, prior offenses, recent parole, vehicle thefts, attacks on other elderly victims, jail arson, violent urges and two experts' future-danger predictions. It also accounts for the character testimony, limited intellectual evidence and mercy argument based on voluntary confession that counsel actually presented. Neither side's case is reduced to one fact.

The omitted childhood history materially changes that picture: severe parental alcoholism, hunger and neglect, filthy conditions, parental imprisonment, removal, beatings and fear. A later improved family account qualifies but does not erase early deprivation. Limited functioning and special education add context beyond the limited intellectual testimony already heard. Supported conduct, training and relationships in structured settings and prison commendations provide concrete information rather than uninformed praise.

The expanded history is not wholly favorable. Fourteen described juvenile offenses and repeated commitments support the State's persistent-criminality argument; the Court considers that risk without assuming every entry necessarily admissible. Property offenses and commitments have real weight without duplicating the severe violence already heard. Good custodial conduct neither erases jail arson nor establishes lifelong confinement or guaranteed nonviolence. Possible parole and the State's society-wide dangerousness argument remain part of the assessment.

The Court relies on no fetal-alcohol diagnosis, disputed new prognosis or claim that every proposed witness testified live or was credited. The trial judge's later recommendation provides a reasoned evaluation by someone familiar with the trial, not a controlling personal preference. The supported documentary and conduct evidence, considered with the properly supported relationships and actual trial case, independently undermines confidence in death. The reasonable probability concerns a lawful conscientious decision, not extraordinary leniency. Having resolved both statutory eligibility and the actual constitutional violation, the Court orders conditional sentence relief rather than an unnecessary framework-only or evidentiary remand.

##### VI. The forbidden additional Fretwell requirement also supplies a contrary-rule ground

**Holding and operative rule:** A state merits rejection that requires additional fundamental unfairness beyond ordinary reasonable-probability prejudice for this lawful-mitigation claim conflicts with Strickland and Fretwell's express limitation and is contrary to clearly established Supreme Court law. On this record the state court's nominal ordinary-probability discussion does not furnish an adequate independent ground, so this contrary-rule conclusion supplies a separate statutory basis for the same constitutional review and sentence relief.

**Authority:** Part III of Breyer's opinion, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg, supplies six direct votes for this alternative gateway ground. It does not establish either group's exclusive general allocation of the statutory clauses.

**Controlling explanation:** Fretwell's narrow controlling rule was in force before Virginia's June 1997 merits decision. It does not withhold ordinary probability review from legally relevant mitigation or require a separate fairness showing in a usual ineffective-assistance case. Lockett, Eddings and Skipper establish why this mitigation concerns a lawful sentencing protection rather than a displaced substantive advantage.

The Court does not treat the state court's citation or vocabulary as automatically dispositive. The asserted independent probability ground receives the whole-record examination stated in Part II. Its materially narrowed treatment of the omitted case fails to supply an adequate alternative under the governing rule. That actual contrary-rule error satisfies the first statutory clause without requiring a previous Supreme Court case with this identical evidentiary collection. The separate constitutional performance and prejudice findings, rather than gateway error alone, justify the writ.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Neither exclusive general allocation between contrary-to and unreasonable-application review has five votes. No universal rule concerning unreasonable extension or refusal to extend law into a different context is adopted. Sections 2254(d)(2), (e)(1) and (e)(2) retain separate conditions; no new factual-review or hearing holding is entered. The Court decides no facial Article III or Suspension Clause challenge, disputed diagnosis, independent expert claim, guilt-phase error, or future sentence.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Strickland is applied to professionally reasonable investigation and ordinary reasonable-probability prejudice on the entire supported sentencing record; neither deficiency alone nor additional mitigation automatically establishes prejudice.
- Fretwell is applied according to its already controlling limitation: the lost benefit of a rejected substantive rule is distinct from ordinary lawful-mitigation prejudice. No additional unfairness requirement is permitted in this usual claim.
- Lockett is applied to the legal relevance of individual mitigation; it does not require indiscriminate presentation of every item.
- Eddings is applied to lawful consideration of mitigating background; it does not assign that evidence automatic dispositive weight.
- Skipper is applied to the relevance of supported adjustment to confinement; it supplies no finding of lifelong confinement or future nonviolence.
- Lindh and Carriger retain the application-date limits of their respective pre-enactment holdings; this later federal filing falls outside those premises.
- Weeks is applied to the independence of statutory eligibility, actual constitutional error and remedy; its reserved comprehensive statutory construction is resolved only to the scope of the present majority propositions.
- Smith v. Robbins is distinguished at its missing appellate-assistance function; it does not presume prejudice from defective sentencing investigation.
- Flores-Ortega is distinguished at its causal loss of an appeal; contextual performance remains required, and the absence of a probable-appellate-success prerequisite there does not remove actual sentencing-prejudice review here.

#### Law After Decision

Section 2254(d)(1) requires independent attention to its two clauses, the Supreme Court source and temporal limit, and an objective legal inquiry without a judicial poll. The actual whole-record application here independently satisfies unreasonable-application review and, with the separate deficiency and prejudice findings, requires sentence relief. ### United States v. Playboy Entertainment Group, Inc., No. 98-1682

Merits decision, May 22, 2000

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 30, 1999. Direct appeal from the three-judge District of Delaware's December 28, 1998 judgment, 30 F. Supp. 2d 702. The question is whether §505 of the Telecommunications Act of 1996's complete-scrambling or time-channeling requirement satisfies the First Amendment in regulating the protected adult programming at issue. The lower court declared §505 unconstitutional and enjoined enforcement; it did not reach equal protection or vagueness.

**Separate statutory commands.** Section 505, codified at 47 U.S.C. §561, applies when a multichannel video programming distributor provides sexually explicit adult or other indecent programming on a channel primarily dedicated to sexually oriented programming. Subsection (a) requires complete scrambling or blocking of the channel's audio and video so that a nonsubscriber to the channel or programming does not receive it. Until compliance, subsection (b) requires withholding during FCC-determined hours when significant numbers of children are likely to watch. The FCC permits transmission from 10 p.m. to 6 a.m. Subsection (c) defines scrambling as signal rearrangement preventing understandable viewing or hearing; it creates no additional FCC compliance determination. The amendment took effect March 9, 1996. The Act's separate direct-review provision numbered §561 is not this codified section.

Section 504, codified at §560, requires a cable operator, upon a subscriber's request and without charge to that subscriber, to fully scramble or block each channel's audio and video so that a nonsubscriber does not receive it. It has no independent indecency or sexually oriented channel trigger. Subsection (b) supplies the same scrambling definition. This command took effect February 8, 1996. Subscriber-selected blocking and §505's default/time restriction are distinct; compliance with one does not automatically satisfy the other. Free service to the subscriber does not mean zero operator equipment or installation cost. Section 504 itself contains no notice program.

**Signal bleed and the proposed alternative.** The challenged nonobscene programming is protected for adults. Analog scrambling may leave understandable audio or pictures when a subscriber who has not purchased the premium service tunes to its scrambled channel; a child may tune without parental knowledge. Reception is not found to occur irrespective of channel selection. Traps used with the identified scrambling systems, channel-mapping converters and digital transmission can prevent bleed, but no universal device reliability or completed digital conversion is found.

The alternative is household-request blocking with adequate notice identifying the affected channels, understandable audio/video bleed, possible child exposure without parental knowledge, free blocking and the telephone request method. It includes repeated notice at reasonable intervals and special mailed notice to prior blocking recipients after the adult channel changes. Billing inserts, preview channels and advertisements elsewhere are examples, not cumulative compulsory media. No precise interval, installation deadline, repair protocol or automatic replacement rule is established.

The lower court found sufficient risk to susceptible minors notwithstanding weaknesses in exposure measurements and clinical inferences. Potential-exposure estimates do not measure actual exposed children; anecdotes establish incidents without a population rate. The Government's deposition-supported contentions concerning weeks-long fulfillment, device failure and channel changes remain material, but establish no universal failure or delay rate. Neither system is shown to protect every determined minor.

#### Holdings

##### I. Congress's content-selected cable restriction requires strict scrutiny

**Holding and operative rule:** A governmental restriction selecting protected adult cable programming by its sexual content must satisfy strict scrutiny: it must be narrowly tailored to a compelling interest, with the Government bearing the ultimate burden of justifying its means. When a concrete, plausibly effective and less speech-restrictive arrangement is presented, the Government must establish why its comparative inadequacy warrants the broader adult burden.

**Authority:** Part I of Ginsburg's Opinion of the Court, joined by all eight other Justices; nine direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** The law selects speech by content, not merely the technical operation of cable service. Protecting susceptible children, assisting parents and safeguarding privacy at home are substantial and compelling concerns in this setting; they do not themselves establish necessity. ACT/Pacifica requires independent review of means while preserving broadcasting's actual characteristics. Denver likewise subjects Congress's compulsory content restrictions to review despite distinct permissions for private editorial choice. Reno protects adult access and requires attention to the medium rather than automatic transfer of broadcast assumptions.

Turner's intermediate standard for content-neutral carriage does not govern this rule. Legislative predictions receive serious consideration, but the constitutional burden remains with the Government. Scientific certainty, quantified harm to every child and a perfect alternative are not required. Conversely, an alternative's mere existence on paper does not demonstrate practical effectiveness. The comparison must address the concrete household arrangement, the actual speech burden and supported implementation concerns. The Court does not convert the lower opinion's separate wording about Playboy demonstrating efficacy into an ultimate burden to prove that the broader governmental restraint is unnecessary.

##### II. The Government has not justified this adult burden against the noticed household alternative

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 505 cannot impose its complete-blocking or sixteen-hour time-channeling burden on the protected programming at issue because the Government has not established that household-request blocking with adequate notice is comparatively inadequate enough to justify that burden. This is a developed-record failure of necessity, not a finding that adequate notice already occurred, that household blocking is flawless, or that every narrower child-protection measure is invalid.

**Authority:** Part II of Ginsburg's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; seven direct joins. Scalia and Breyer dissent from this application.

**Controlling explanation:** Time channeling substantially burdens willing adult audiences, including viewers who ordinarily watch during the restricted hours. An operator's formal ability to choose complete blocking does not restore that access where the practical choice is the timing restriction. The narrower channel trigger and retained nighttime period limit the burden but do not eliminate it.

The actual alternative combines complete household blocking with adequate recurring notice and channel-change notice. It is technically concrete and can protect concerned parents while leaving other adults' access intact. The record does not establish the comparative inadequacy needed to suppress protected programming for most of the day. Low device distribution during a period of unproved notice is not evidence that informed parents rejected protection or that a properly publicized arrangement failed.

The Government's strongest objection concerns children whose parents miss, misunderstand or disregard notice and households encountering delay, malfunction or changed channels. Those risks matter. The Court accepts the susceptible-minor risk finding and does not demand protection of every determined minor. But the supported activation difference and implementation concerns do not establish that this broader restraint is necessary. The cost calculations show real feasibility issues, not measured post-notice demand or inevitable failure; the Court assumes neither free operator installation nor guaranteed profitability.

ACT/Pacifica rejects an inadequately justified broad schedule without making all media identical. Denver identifies the practical burden of compulsory machinery rather than validating every household device. Here a completed trial permits final assessment; the Government receives no new opportunity to justify the same restraint. The conclusion rests on the burden of comparative justification, not on judicial creation of a notice regime, speculative digital conversion or denial that children can encounter harmful material. Obscenity and evidence-supported narrower measures remain outside the holding.

##### III. Affirmance preserves the injunction without creating a judicial notice program

**Holding and operative rule:** The declaration and injunction against §505 are affirmed at the adjudicated scope, while §504's distinct requested-blocking command remains effective. The affirmance creates no Supreme Court notice order, statutory publicity command or new authority over nonparty operators, and leaves unreviewed collateral lower relief only at its actual scope and procedural status.

**Authority:** Part III of Ginsburg's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; seven direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** The constitutional failure concerns §505's broader restraint. It does not require abolition of household blocking, judicial selection of replacement hours or a new technical design. Targeted blocking, notice and improved scrambling remain available within lawful authority, without a ruling on every future arrangement.

The lower opinion states that it will require Playboy to secure notice through contractual arrangements and expressly lacks jurisdiction to command the nonparty operators directly. The record establishes no later formal implementation order, completed contract changes or delivered notices. The Court does not convert that announcement into an enacted §504 duty or adopt a new controlling notice-power rule. No independent challenge to that collateral direction is decided. Its actual lower-court scope and procedural status remain unaltered, and the constitutional holding does not depend on inventing notice authority.

The lower court's observation that inadequate notice would undermine the alternative does not create automatic restoration of §505 or a new enforcement procedure. The Court directs no new study or proof-repair remand and resolves neither equal protection nor vagueness. Ordinary obscenity enforcement and supported narrower regulation retain their lawful boundaries.

### Miller v. French / United States v. French, Nos. 99-224 and 99-582

Plenary merits decision, June 19, 2000

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 18, 2000. On writs of certiorari to the Seventh Circuit, reviewing French v. Duckworth, 178 F.3d 437 (1999), which sustained an injunction blocking the PLRA automatic stay.

The questions are whether §3626(e) preserves ordinary equitable authority to stop its automatic stay, whether mandatory operation is categorically inconsistent with Article III, and how a supported inability-to-adjudicate application is treated. The Pendleton decree followed adjudication in material part. Indiana moved for termination June 5, 1997; the District Court entered temporary protection July 3 and a preliminary injunction July 11. The United States defended the statute on appeal and sought review, proposing retained equitable authority and alternatively defending mandatory operation. Current prison conditions and the termination merits are outside this appeal.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Nos. 99-224 and 99-582: Reversed and remanded, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg.

**Opposing:** Stevens and Breyer, who would vacate and remand under a construction preserving exceptional equitable authority.

The categorical Article III invalidation cannot sustain the blocking injunction. The remand permits a supported inability-to-adjudicate objection under Article III as well as due process to be presented and decided; it neither automatically continues the blocking order nor denies consideration of a properly supported constitutional claim. No prison-compliance finding or termination of the underlying decree is entered.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship and scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | Souter | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg | Six-Justice mandatory statutory construction, Holding I |
| Opinion of the Court, Part II | Souter | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg | Seven-Justice bounded Article III rule and operative remand, Holdings II–III |
| Dissent | Breyer | Stevens | Would vacate and remand in both dockets under retained exceptional equitable authority |

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela joins Part II and the judgment, not Part I. Stevens and Breyer agree that valid prospective law may change and that actual termination requires judicial findings, but do not join the lead opinion's mandatory construction or constitutional disposition. Direct majorities control every holding; no combination under Marks is required.

#### Holdings

##### I. The automatic stay displaces ordinary equitable continuation

**Holding and operative rule:** For the presented motion under 18 U.S.C. §3626(b)(2), subsection (e) automatically stays covered prospective relief beginning on the thirtieth day after filing and ending when the court enters a final order resolving the motion; the court may postpone the starting date for good cause by no more than sixty days, but general calendar congestion is not good cause. Beyond that express postponement, ordinary equitable authority does not permit the court to suspend the statutory stay.

**Authority:** Part I of Justice Souter's opinion of the Court has six direct rationale votes: O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela joins the judgment without joining this statutory-construction part; Stevens and Breyer dissent from the construction.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court reads the stay, postponement and appeal provisions together. The enacted command makes the motion operate as a stay; the express postponement both limits its length and excludes general calendar congestion. Preserving the same decree through ordinary injunction balancing beyond those limits would defeat that structure. The United States argues that traditional equitable authority survives because extraordinary injunction requirements differ from good cause and subsection (e)(4) expressly addresses orders blocking the stay. But that subsection makes such orders appealable as refusals to dissolve or modify an injunction under 28 U.S.C. §1292(a)(1), regardless of their label or preliminary or final character; it supplies review, not additional substantive authority to block the stay. The express subsection (e)(3) postponement is excluded from that special appeal treatment. Constitutional avoidance cannot replace this detailed statutory direction with a competing scheme. Subsection (e)(1)'s prompt-disposition duty and mandamus remedy remain. The retained 180-day timing language for motions under other law does not govern the immediate-termination motion presented here; its broader interaction with the amended under-subsection-(b) opening is not decided. Section 3626(a)(2)'s separate ninety-day expiration for newly entered preliminary relief does not turn this existing permanent decree into a preliminary order.

##### II. Congress may change interim prospective law without deciding the case

**Holding and operative rule:** The automatic stay is not categorically unconstitutional under Article III merely because it temporarily suspends prospective relief before final adjudication of the termination motion. Congress may change the law governing future enforcement while leaving the court to decide the new law's factual and legal predicates; this holding does not resolve an application that actually deprives the court of a meaningful opportunity to adjudicate.

**Authority:** Part II of Justice Souter's opinion of the Court is joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg, supplying seven direct votes for this bounded holding and the remand in Holding III.

**Controlling explanation:** Robertson distinguishes changing governing law from prescribing a judicial answer under unchanged law; Klein preserves the independent judicial task. Wheeling Bridge and Agostini distinguish future operation of an injunction from completed adjudication. Rouse and Hadix apply that distinction to conditional prison-relief termination. Here the interim enforcement rule itself changes prospective law. A party's motion triggers the legislated consequence; the party does not thereby establish lawful prison conditions or defeat the findings required to retain relief. Although the Pendleton decree followed adjudication in material part, the stay neither contradicts those past findings nor cancels accrued damages or completed lawful effects. Plaut protects a private judgment that has become final through the ordinary judicial process from legislatively compelled vacatur and reinstatement of the same litigation, but it expressly leaves Congress free to change the law governing the future operation of continuing injunctive relief; the automatic stay of prospective relief falls outside its rule. The prisoners correctly distinguish termination after findings from suspension before findings. That distinction requires attention to an actual denial of adjudication, but it does not make every interim prospective rule legislative adjudication. The Court rejects the Seventh Circuit's categorical structural ruling without declaring this timetable sufficient in every complex case or deciding a supported inability-to-adjudicate claim against the prisoners.

##### III. The remand preserves a genuine adjudication of supported application objections

**Holding and operative rule:** In each docket, the Seventh Circuit's categorical invalidation is reversed and the case remanded, permitting a properly supported claim that the statutory timing, including available postponement, actually denies a meaningful opportunity to adjudicate to be presented and decided under Article III as well as any applicable due-process protection. No such violation is presumed, no feasibility hearing is required merely because time has elapsed, and no ordinary equitable exception or automatic continuation of the existing blocking injunction follows.

**Authority:** Part II of Justice Souter's opinion of the Court; the seven Justices named for Holding II directly join this operative remand and its limits.

**Controlling explanation:** Benjamin requires a real opportunity to offer material current proof when it is necessary for statutory adjudication; Gavin and Hadix require the unperformed termination inquiry without making a new hearing universal. The reported postponements of the termination hearing establish neither constitutional impossibility nor prison compliance, present violations or deliberate delay. They therefore neither compel an evidentiary hearing about timing nor foreclose a supported application objection. On remand, such an objection must receive genuine consideration under ordinary procedure; this judgment does not restrict it to due process or decide the appropriate remedy in advance. The court must also decide the actual termination conditions: the movant must establish that covered relief was entered without substantive necessity, narrowness and least-intrusion findings, and sufficient findings require no ritual wording. Under amended §3626(b)(3), relief shall not terminate if written, record-based findings establish that it remains necessary to correct a current and ongoing federal-right violation, extends no further than necessary, is narrowly drawn and is the least intrusive necessary means. Each retained duty requires those findings; obsolete relief may not survive through disregard of the new law. This appeal neither decides those merits nor terminates the decree. Hadix's distinct pending automatic-stay appeal is not adjudicated here.

- **Robertson v. Seattle Audubon Society:** Applied to the distinction between changed governing law and legislative adjudication under unchanged law; its reserved questions are not converted into general permission to control judgments.
- **United States v. Klein:** Its protection of independent adjudication remains; no legislative finding of prison compliance is authorized.
- **Pennsylvania v. Wheeling & Belmont Bridge Co.:** Applied to the distinct prospective operation of an injunction, without extending it to every completed judgment.
- **Agostini v. Felton:** Its distinction between a continuing injunction and completed adjudication supports examination of the current governing law; its Rule 60(b)(5) setting does not itself decide the automatic-stay issue.
- **Morgan Stanley & Co. v. Pacific Mutual Life Insurance Co.:** Its affirmance by an equally divided Court sets no precedent and supplies no rule for this case.
- **Plaut v. Spendthrift Farm, Inc.:** Its protection of completed private final judgments against legislatively compelled reopening remains; its express preservation of changed law governing the future operation of continuing injunctive relief places this interim prospective stay outside that rule.
- **Inmates of Suffolk County Jail v. Rouse:** Applied to lawful changed prospective standards, actual findings and preservation of completed effects; its rejection of speculative compulsory inquiry remains.
- **Benjamin v. Jacobson:** Applied to genuine opportunity for necessary current proof; its documented refusal of proof is not presumed from this hearing calendar.

- **Hadix v. Johnson:** Applied to independent adjudication of amended termination conditions; its separate automatic-stay appeal remains distinct.
- **Plyler v. Moore:** Its original-text conditional termination rule and separate statutory route remain; the amended current-and-ongoing conjunction is not retroactively written into its holding.
- **Lewis v. Casey:** Its scope and remedial-findings safeguards remain; the existing decree receives actual substantive examination rather than a ritual-words test.
- **Rufo v. Inmates of Suffolk County Jail:** Its independent significant-change and tailored-modification route remains; it adds no prerequisite to the statutory termination route.

#### Law After Decision

Section 3626(e)'s stay is mandatory beyond its bounded good-cause postponement; ordinary equitable continuation cannot displace it. Changing interim prospective relief is not categorically legislative adjudication, but a supported claim that the timing actually forecloses meaningful judicial determination remains open under Article III and due process. The decision does not decide every application or authorize retention of obsolete relief. The amended termination findings and meaningful consideration of necessary current proof remain obligatory.

The distinction between consent decrees and private settlements, the exclusion of fact-or-duration habeas proceedings, the compensatory-damages boundary and state-court relief solely under state law remain unchanged. Earlier agreed or otherwise lawful modification and termination remain available under their independent statutory provisions. A new preliminary injunction's ninety-day rule is distinct from the challenged statutory suspension of this permanent decree.

#### Separate Writings

**Justice Breyer, dissenting, joined by Justice Stevens.** They would vacate the Seventh Circuit's judgment in both dockets and remand under a construction preserving exceptional equitable protection. Congress ordinarily leaves the courts' equitable power intact, and subsection (e)(4)'s provision for appeal from a blocking order supports a reading that allows a genuinely exceptional injunction. The sixty-day good-cause postponement and traditional injunction authority require different showings and can coexist without making the automatic stay meaningless. An applicant would have to establish irreparable injury, likelihood of defeating termination under the amended law, inability to resolve the merits before the stay operates, and favorable traditional balancing of the parties' and public interests. No present finding establishes those predicates. Their reading avoids the difficult Article III timing question while requiring the actual current-and-ongoing-violation and remedial-scope findings. Plaut's finality rule reaches completed private judgments, not the future operation of continuing relief under changed law; Benjamin, Gavin and Hadix require meaningful consideration of material current proof. They do not make the old decree immutable or endorse the Seventh Circuit's categorical constitutional rule. On remand they would permit consideration of appropriately supported temporary protection, without directing indefinite continuation or finding current prison conditions unlawful. #### Holdings

##### Psychiatric claim: the statutory ground resolves this application

**Holding and operative rule.** Section 2254(d)(1) does not permit relief on this psychiatric claim: the state court's distinction between a defense-requested earlier competency report used in cross-examining a defense mental-condition expert and the compelled, uncounseled capital examination in Estelle v. Smith is neither contrary to nor an objectively unreasonable application of then-established federal law. The adverse psychiatric certificate disposition is affirmed without deciding a general direct-review admissibility or privilege-waiver rule.

**Authority.** Part I of the signed opinion by Souter is joined by all nine Justices. Its sufficient statutory ground controls; it makes no alternative finding about actual sentencing effect.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the statutory limitation must be applied to the actual examination setting. Williams v. Taylor supplies distinct contrary-to and unreasonable-application clauses and an objective inquiry. Estelle v. Smith protects against the compelled capital examination in that case; Buchanan v. Kentucky distinguishes a defense-initiated evaluation and responsive use. The Peebles report originated in defense counsel's request for a competency examination in an unrelated 1977 prosecution. Dr. Price reviewed it among fourteen reports while testifying for Penry about mental condition. Cross-examination elicited its dangerousness statement over objection, and the prosecutor repeated the statement in closing. Those uses present a substantial privilege concern, but they do not eliminate the materially different origin and use that supported the state court's distinction.

The applicable constitutional-law cutoff is the state merits decision of February 22, 1995; rehearing was denied June 7, 1995, and no later merits adjudication changing that cutoff is established. A preferred broader exclusion rule on direct review cannot replace the statutory standard. The Court adopts no universal subject-matter waiver, no rule admitting every report whenever mental condition is discussed, and no adverse finding based on absence of an objection. The statutory ground suffices; no general direct-review privilege decision or psychiatric harmlessness determination is necessary.

##### The mitigation charge failed to provide an effective lawful verdict

**Holding and operative rule.** When supported capital mitigation can reduce culpability independently of the factual special issues, the jury must have an authorized, coherent way to give that judgment effect in a life verdict. An instruction allowing a negative answer for mitigation does not satisfy that requirement when the unchanged verdict form requires the jury to certify reasonable doubt about a factual proposition it may believe proved; on this complete charge and trial context there is a reasonable likelihood of unconstitutional restriction, and the state's acceptance was an objectively unreasonable application of the established rule.

**Authority.** Part II has the seven direct joins identified in Opinion Topology. Scalia and Thomas dissent from both the constitutional application and the unreasonable-application conclusion. No independent contrary-to holding is adopted on this component.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that admitting mitigation is insufficient when the verdict mechanism cannot lawfully express its effect. Lockett v. Ohio and Eddings v. Oklahoma protect relevant culpability mitigation. Penry v. Lynaugh requires an effective vehicle for the evidence at issue; Graham v. Collins and Johnson v. Texas establish before the 1995 state adjudication that this vehicle cannot require a false factual answer. Boyde v. California requires a reasonable likelihood of restriction in the complete charge and trial, not a mere possible misunderstanding.

The jury could believe intellectual impairment and childhood abuse reduced culpability while still finding deliberateness, future dangerousness, and lack of reasonable provocation. The supplement permitted a negative answer if life fit that culpability judgment. But the attached form still required a negative answer to certify that at least ten jurors had reasonable doubt about the factual issue. An affirmative answer required unanimity beyond reasonable doubt. The conflict therefore concerned the lawful meaning of the signed answer, not simply awareness that life was possible.

Each eventual juror received a largely similar supplemental instruction and a judicial explanation during selection; counsel also explained its operation, and the defense renewed the explanation at penalty closing. Those circumstances favor the State and must be considered. They instructed jurors how to answer negatively without reconciling the final factual certification. The interval and intervening trial stages do not prove forgetting, and the prosecution's emphasis on oath, evidence, and law is not a separately imposed mitigation prohibition. The final packet nevertheless retained the conflict. Its acceptance exceeded the reasonable range under the pre-1995 effective-vehicle rule. Neither favored weight, a prescribed fourth question, nor a categorical disability exemption is required. The form's ten-juror language is considered as part of the conflict; no separate unanimity claim is decided.

##### Brecht's State-borne harmless-error rule governs the remaining effect inquiry

**Holding and operative rule.** For a preserved original-charge restriction of this kind on supported capital culpability mitigation, the State must establish beyond a reasonable doubt, on the complete penalty record, that the constitutional defect did not contribute to the death sentence. If the State cannot make that showing, conditional sentence relief must allow a reasonable period for constitutionally adequate capital resentencing or a lawful noncapital sentence; guilt remains unaffected.

**Authority.** Part III has the five direct joins identified in Opinion Topology. Stevens and Kennedy join the corrective return but reject this effect standard; Scalia and Thomas oppose the mitigation judgment and state a different effect position conditionally. This is a majority holding, not a synthesis of those separate positions under Marks.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court applies the general rule established in Brecht v. Abrahamson: when a federal habeas court properly reaches a preserved constitutional trial error, the State must establish beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute to the verdict or sentence, unless another controlling rule treats the defect as structural or the right carries its own prejudice element. Calderon v. Coleman applied that rule to commutation misinformation, and Weeks v. Angelone directed effect review of its response error under the same rule. The contradictory original-charge mechanism here falls within it; no category-specific extension is needed. Applied here, the rule protects reliable selection of death after an established denial of a lawful mitigation route and assigns uncertainty about the error's contribution to the State.

The State may rely on the entire penalty record, including testimony, instructions, and the clarifying explanations. The established error alone does not prove actual contribution or require automatic relief. Conversely, affirmative answers to the special issues do not establish what an unconflicted jury would have done with independent culpability mitigation. The reported excerpts do not permit a completed whole-record determination by this Court. The lower court must undertake that inquiry under the adopted standard.

Brecht's rule governs the federal habeas effect inquiry; it does not supply the clearly established constitutional law for the section 2254(d)(1) gateway at the 1995 cutoff, create a new retroactivity exception, or displace its exceptions for structural defects and rights carrying their own prejudice element. The constitutional gateway has already been satisfied through the pre-1995 mitigation rule. No structural-error classification or presumption that the error actually contributed is adopted.

##### Certificate review, corrective return, and the limit on immediate relief

**Holding and operative rule.** The Court may review the Fifth Circuit's judicial denial of a certificate of appealability under section 1254(1). The established mitigation violation and unreasonable application require reversal of that component of the certificate disposition and an issue-specific appellate return; they do not themselves constitute a present conditional writ or immediate sentence vacatur before the remaining effect inquiry.

**Authority.** Part I's reviewability framework has nine joins. Part II's mitigation certificate eligibility and corrective return have the seven direct joins identified above. Part III's five-Justice application of Brecht's general rule binds the effect inquiry on remand.

**Controlling explanation.** Hohn v. United States permits review of the judicial certificate denial. Slack v. McDaniel keeps the substantial-showing threshold distinct from ultimate entitlement to relief and requires issue-specific treatment. The psychiatric statutory disposition remains adverse. The mitigation claim satisfies certification and the constitutional and section 2254(d)(1) questions are resolved in Penry's favor. Those conclusions bind the return; further proceedings are not an invitation to relitigate the decided gateway. But the certificate is not itself a writ, and the record does not establish actual harmlessness or harmfulness. The Court therefore returns the matter for the issue-specific appeal and full-record effect review. Conditional sentence correction follows if the State fails the adopted standard; immediate resentencing, release, or a fixed deadline is not ordered now.

### Ashcroft v. American Civil Liberties Union, No. 00-1293

Merits decision, May 13, 2002

#### Chronology and Posture

**Citation:** 535 U.S. 564.

Argued November 28, 2001; decided May 13, 2002. On writ of certiorari to the Third Circuit under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1), reviewing 217 F.3d 162. The Third Circuit affirmed the February 1, 1999 preliminary injunction against the Child Online Protection Act on the ground that its community-standards feature independently made the statute facially invalid. The question is whether that feature alone supports the appellate disposition; other grounds for the preliminary injunction were not adjudicated by that rationale.

COPA, 47 U.S.C. §231, reaches knowing commercial World Wide Web communications, made with knowledge of the material's character in interstate or foreign commerce, available to any person under seventeen and containing material harmful to minors. Commercial purpose requires a regular business course with a profit objective and knowing posting or solicitation of the material; actual profit and a sole or principal business are unnecessary. This statutory category does not make protected expression commercial advertising. Harmful material includes obscenity or material satisfying all three requirements: taken as a whole and with respect to minors, prurient appeal or pandering judged by the average person applying contemporary community standards; patently offensive depictions, descriptions or representations of sexual acts or contacts, actual or simulated and including normal or perverted acts, or lewd displays of genitals or post-pubescent female breasts, with respect to minors; and, taken as a whole, lack of serious literary, artistic, political or scientific value for minors. The community-standards phrase appears expressly in the prurience clause.

Subsection (a) permits a fine up to $50,000, imprisonment up to six months, or both; intentional violations carry an additional fine up to $50,000 per violation, and the separate civil penalty is also up to $50,000 per violation. For the latter two, each day is a separate violation. Subsection (b)(1)–(3) excludes a telecommunications carrier to the extent it provides telecommunications service, an Internet-access-service provider in that capacity, and an information-location service in that capacity. Subsection (b)(4) separately protects intermediaries transmitting, storing, retrieving, hosting, formatting or translating another's communication without selecting or altering its content; deletion consistent with subsection (c) or §230 does not defeat that paragraph's protection. Subsection (c)(1) supplies a good-faith access-restriction defense through a credit card, debit account, adult access code or adult personal identification number, an age-verifying digital certificate, or other reasonable measures feasible with available technology. Subsection (c)(2) separately protects good-faith implementation or restriction activity against a cause of action only when that activity violates no law carrying a criminal or civil penalty. Subsection (d) separately requires protection against unauthorized access to collected information and prior written or electronic consent for disclosure, from the adult concerned or the parent or guardian of a person under seventeen. Disclosure is permitted when necessary to communicate, to conduct a legitimate related business activity, or under an authorizing court order.

The preliminary findings include speakers' inability to exclude Web visitors geographically and verification burdens affecting expense, anonymity and adult access. Those findings remain part of the inquiry; the Court does not substitute assumed future technology for the existing record.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Community standards alone do not establish COPA's facial invalidity

**Holding and operative rule.** COPA's use of contemporary community standards does not, standing alone, establish facial invalidity: constitutional review must assess the complete offense, its limiting elements and defenses, and its actual burden on protected expression together. This rejection of automatic invalidity supplies neither a presumption that COPA survives nor a holding that varying local standards are constitutional in every application to the Web.

**Authority.** Part II of Kennedy's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg, supplies seven direct votes for this confined rule and explanation. Part III's vacatur, remand and preservation of the injunction also receive Breyer's join. The Court adopts no national-community construction and uses no narrowest-grounds aggregation.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the First Amendment inquiry concerns the enacted restriction as a whole. Reno protects adult Internet expression against substantially overbroad criminal restrictions; it does not turn every reference to community standards in a different law into an independent constitutional defect. COPA contains commercial-coverage, whole-material, prurience, specified-content and serious-value limits that must do their actual legal work. Miller supplies the obscenity framework, and Pope separates serious value from local acceptance. Their relevance does not establish that material lacking serious value for minors is unprotected for adults.

The challengers' strongest objection rests on the existing finding that Web speakers cannot exclude visitors geographically: a restrictive community may consequently burden expression nationwide. The Court preserves that fact and the verification burdens on cost, anonymity and access. But geographic variation alone does not determine the protected reach of all the statutory requirements operating together. Nor does the presence of limiting elements establish that their combined operation satisfies the Constitution. Substantial overbreadth requires examination of protected expression in relation to the statute's legitimate reach, not isolation of one phrase.

Reno and Playboy continue to require justification of the actual protected-adult-speech burden, including consideration of concrete, plausibly effective, less restrictive means. The statutory commercial-purpose label does not reduce literary or other protected expression to advertising. Each defense, provider exclusion and privacy provision retains its own conditions. Free Speech Coalition distinguishes ordinary construction from judicial addition of a new offense limitation. The Court therefore neither mandates a national standard nor finally approves local standards. The complete constitutional challenge and unresolved tailoring grounds return because the reviewed appellate rationale did not decide them. The preliminary injunction remains while that work proceeds.

**Precedent treatment.** Reno's adult-access holding remains controlling and its categorical extension to any community-standards phrase is rejected; Miller and Pope supply distinct obscenity and objective-value principles; Playboy preserves the governmental justification burden; Free Speech Coalition preserves substantial-overbreadth review and the boundary against judicial rewriting. No precedent is overruled.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Reno v. American Civil Liberties Union, 521 U.S. 844: applied to protected adult access and practical scrutiny; distinguished as to COPA's different enacted elements. Its invalidation of the CDA's broader restrictions remains effective.
- Miller v. California, 413 U.S. 15: applied for the conjunctive obscenity framework; its acceptance of community standards does not itself resolve COPA's effect on protected adult speech.
- Pope v. Illinois, 481 U.S. 497: applied to keep serious value independent of local community acceptance; the objective-value inquiry is not a complete answer to adult access.
- Hamling v. United States, 418 U.S. 87, and Sable Communications of California, Inc. v. Their addressed-distribution settings do not erase the geographic findings here.
- United States v. Playboy Entertainment Group, Inc., 529 U.S. 803: applied for the Government's ultimate justification burden and examination of concrete, plausibly effective, less speech-restrictive alternatives; no perfect alternative or prescribed legislative-record format is required.
- Ashcroft v. Free Speech Coalition, 535 U.S. 234, and Broadrick v. Oklahoma, 413 U.S. 601: applied to substantial protected reach relative to legitimate scope; the former also preserves the difference between ordinary interpretation and adding absent offense limitations.

#### Law After Decision

Lower courts must evaluate COPA's actual statutory reach and adult-speech burden as a whole. The presence of community standards alone cannot sustain the reviewed categorical facial rationale, and the presence of other elements alone cannot establish ultimate validity. Neither a national standard nor categorical approval of local standards becomes controlling law. The existing adult-speech, substantial-overbreadth and justification rules remain operative. Full statutory validity, the factual adequacy of verification and the comparative effectiveness of alternative protections remain for the returned proceeding because the appellate rationale did not adjudicate them.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**O'Connor, concurring.** She joins the confined holding because the present showing does not identify sufficient otherwise protected material made unlawful solely through community variation once prurience and serious value are considered. Pope's objective-value inquiry and Sable's distinction between an adult ban and confined protection matter, but the Web's inability to select recipients prevents treating conventional mail and telephone distribution as conclusive. She affirmatively regards a national standard as constitutionally permissible and necessary for reasonable Internet obscenity regulation. Her judgment neither forecloses an adequately supported facial challenge nor denies later as-applied relief.

**Thomas, concurring, joined by Scalia.** The prurience and objective serious-value requirements materially narrow the relevant category compared with the CDA. Miller, Pope, Hamling and Sable support rejecting community variation as a sufficient objection to this statutory category; the existing showing does not establish substantial variation in the covered material. This broader explanation would reject the community-standards objection more fully than the Court's common ground. It does not adopt a national-standard amendment, resolve COPA's other constitutional defects, or treat statutory commercial purpose as permission to suppress protected adult literature. They join continued interim protection while the other injunction grounds are considered.

**Souter, concurring, joined by Kennedy and Ginsburg.** The extent of protected expression cannot be measured until the statute's reach is determined. Commercial coverage, the relevant whole work or communication, the different ages within the under-seventeen category and venue consequences affect the relationship between legitimate regulation and protected expression. Reno, Playboy and Free Speech Coalition require attention to actual adult access, not an assumption that value for minors cures the adult burden. These Justices expressly preserve the community-standards challenge as part of the integrated inquiry: after construction, geographic variation may still prove decisive. Their join does not approve local standards as constitutionally harmless, depend on hypothetical geographic controls, or adopt a national standard.

**Breyer, concurring in part and in the judgment.** He joins the statutory background and disposition but reads community standards to mean the national adult community. The ordinary phrase in context, its legislative explanation and avoidance of serious constitutional difficulty support that construction in his view. A local standard would give restrictive communities practical control over protected speech distributed nationally. Variation among juries applying one national standard is different from variation among the governing substantive standards themselves. This construction is necessary to his agreement, not a merely optional possibility. It does not resolve the other COPA objections, and he agrees that the injunction should continue.

**Stevens, dissenting.** He would affirm the preliminary injunction on the community-standards ground. His objections in Smith and Sable, together with Reno's adult-access rule, apply to the established inability of national Web speakers to avoid restrictive local audiences. Serious value for minors does not define all expression adults may constitutionally receive. Narrowing the commercial category or the whole-work unit may reduce absolute coverage without curing substantial overbreadth relative to legitimate applications. Verification burdens do not erase that injury. He rejects insertion of a national standard into language with an established community-based meaning. His position preserves the legitimate interest in protecting children and ordinary restrictions on unprotected material; it does not require invalidating every future Internet safeguard.

The District Court must enter judgment rejecting the facial claims and remove the injunction founded on them. An adult denied effective access through an actual library policy may seek prospective declaratory and injunctive relief against a legally responsible governmental defendant under an available enforcement cause of action, on proof of that library's operation and injury; a proved violation requires that the access policy provide prompt, effective access by lawful means rather than an automatic prohibition of all filtering or termination of the library's Internet service. Stevens would retain adult-access protection and return the decree for fitting to the distinct programs, lawful separability, and independently operable minor-use provisions.

#### Holdings

##### I. Lawful disabling and the distinct funding conditions

**Holding and operative rule:** The statutes permit disabling for bona fide research or other lawful purposes, which are alternative grounds; an adult may request an entire unfiltered session for lawful reading without identifying sites or explaining a purpose. E-rate's disabling clause expressly applies during adult use, whereas LSTA's clause contains no adult-use limitation. This is statutory permission, not a command that every request be granted or a factual finding that effective access exists at every library.

**Authority:** Part I of the Opinion of the Court, directly joined by all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** The text permits an authorized person to disable the technology protection measure for lawful purposes. Ordinary lawful reading fits that term without a patron's explaining a research project or identifying a particular blocked page. The Government's representation accords with that permissible reading. It narrows the claimed statutory burden but does not establish what every library actually does. The express adult-use language in §254(h)(6)(D) cannot be inserted into §9134(f)(3), and permission cannot be rewritten as a mandatory-access guarantee. The Court assesses the facial claim under this construction and the conditions' actual scope.

**Program boundaries:** LSTA's identified computer-purchase and direct Internet-cost restriction applies to a covered library that does not receive the specified discounted services. E-rate's condition excludes libraries receiving discounted services solely for purposes other than Internet access, Internet service, or internal connections. The required technology covers Internet-access computers; obscenity and child pornography are the adult categories, with the additional harmful-to-minors category for users under seventeen. Harmful-to-minors material must satisfy all three statutory elements: taken as a whole and with respect to minors, prurient appeal in nudity, sex, or excretion; a patently offensive depiction, description, or representation, with respect to suitability for minors, of actual or simulated sexual acts or contact, normal or perverted sexual acts, or lewd exhibition of genitals; and, taken as a whole, lack of serious literary, artistic, political, or scientific value as to minors. The child-pornography cross-reference operates subject to Free Speech Coalition's existing protection of nonobscene material made without actual-child exploitation; incorporation does not restore its invalidated protected coverage.

**Operative statutory consequences — LSTA:** Under §9134(f)(5), when the Director has reason to believe a recipient is substantially failing to comply with the Internet-safety subsection, the Director may withhold further payments, seek compliance through a cease-and-desist complaint, or enter a compliance agreement. Those remedies are exclusive for that failure; the Director may not recover funds and must end withholding upon determining, by certification or other appropriate evidence, that the failure is cured. Under §9134(f)(6), invalidity of a provision leaves the remainder of that subsection unaffected.

**Operative statutory consequences — E-rate:** Under §254(h)(6)(F), knowing failure to comply with annual certification-submission guidelines makes a library ineligible for discounted services or funding in their place; submission of the missing certification restores discounted-service eligibility. Knowing failure to ensure that computer use complies with the certification requires reimbursement of all funds and discounts received under §254(h) for the certification's covered period. A library may cure that operational failure by bringing computer use into compliance; submission to the Commission of a certification or other appropriate evidence of cure restores discounted-service eligibility.

##### II. Effective adult access and the facial speech and spending challenges

**Holding and operative rule:** Congress may condition the specified E-rate and LSTA assistance on reasonable filtering measures designed to restrict obscenity, child pornography and material harmful to minors. When a publicly operated library accepts covered assistance and provides general Internet access to adult patrons, however, it may not use the required filtering system to impose a continuing, unjustified prohibition on an adult's access to constitutionally protected material. An adult seeking lawful protected material must have an effective means of obtaining unfiltered access, through prompt whole-session disabling or an equally effective method, without being required to identify the subject of the reading, disclose a viewpoint, or demonstrate an official-approved reason for seeking the material. Reasonable procedures addressing actual technical limitations, lawful supervision and ordinary resource constraints remain permissible. A nominal request process that routinely denies access, imposes material delay without adequate justification, or requires disclosure of the content of protected reading does not satisfy this constitutional requirement. The statutory disabling provisions permit compliance with this constitutional obligation. Neither provision commands libraries to maintain filtering for adults when disabling is necessary to provide lawful protected access. Because the enacted federal funding conditions admit constitutionally permissible operation, and the existing facial record does not establish that they necessarily or substantially compel unlawful exclusion, the statute is not facially invalid. The Constitution does not guarantee public provision of Internet access, require a library to acquire every information source, or forbid otherwise lawful rules concerning illegal material and resource administration.

**Funding conditions:** The covered funding conditions do not facially require unconstitutional conduct because the statutory disabling provisions permit effective protection of adult access to constitutionally protected material. Congress may not, however, condition covered assistance on a recipient's refusal to provide the adult-access protection required by the First Amendment. The federal funding authorities may not treat lawful adult disabling, authorized by the statute and constitutionally necessary in the covered circumstances, as a violation of the funding condition. A recipient that employs filtering in a constitutionally permissible manner may receive funds subject to ordinary eligibility and compliance requirements. An actual policy that unlawfully obstructs protected adult expression remains subject to appropriately fitted relief. The existence of a lawful funding program does not immunize a separate unconstitutional library practice.

**Authority:** Part II of the Opinion of the Court. Stone-Zsela, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer directly join every proposition of this Part and the mandate; O'Connor, Scalia, and Thomas join only the judgment of reversal. The Part's force does not depend on combining different broader explanations under Marks.

**Controlling explanation:** Reno protects lawful adult Internet expression, and Denver requires attention to real access burdens rather than a nominal alternative. Forbes and Finley preserve genuine selection of what an institution elects to provide; ordinary selection of books and databases differs from filtering an existing general-purpose Internet connection. A filter that blocks protected material has not selected that work on its merits; it may have excluded it inaccurately or through an overinclusive category. Congress may require filtering as the initial configuration. But the government cannot rely on the availability of disabling while leaving officials unrestricted discretion to deny it whenever disabling is necessary to avoid unlawful exclusion. Effective release through the authority Congress supplied is therefore a constitutional safeguard, not merely a factor in weighing the burden. A temporary malfunction followed by prompt repair differs from a continuing, discretionary prohibition on lawful reading.

Broadrick and Hicks require a supported comparison of substantial unconstitutional reach with legitimate operation, not reliance on one conceivable lawful implementation. Part I's construction permits every covered library to provide the required access, and the record of substantial overblocking and practical concerns does not establish that the conditions necessarily or substantially compel unlawful exclusion. The concurrence in the judgment would rest on the definition of the assisted service. Selection explains why filtering may be the starting point; it does not justify a continuing, unjustified bar on protected reading already available through the service the library has opened. The dissent would treat the compulsory default itself as the injury requiring facial relief. Because adults must receive prompt, effective whole-session access without explaining themselves, the default does not by itself exclude them from protected reading, and an actual denial is remedied where it occurs.

Dole retains the bar on conditions inducing unconstitutional conduct. These conditions do not facially require such conduct, because the disabling provisions permit compliance; but Congress may not condition covered assistance on refusal to provide the access the First Amendment requires. Both facial grounds therefore fail.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Broadrick v. Oklahoma and Virginia v. Hicks supply substantial-overbreadth review of the actual challenged operation, distinct from standing and from an every-application requirement.
- Reno v. American Civil Liberties Union is applied to protect lawful adult Internet expression within the general-purpose Internet service a library has opened; its criminal restriction differs from the present funding and disabling arrangement.
- Denver Area Educational Telecommunications Consortium v. FCC is applied to distinguish genuine, effective adult access from a mechanism imposing substantial or unjustified burdens on adult expression; it retains its rejection of the integral compulsory delayed-access system and its distinction from ordinary voluntary requests.
- South Dakota v. Dole is applied: Congress may not condition covered assistance on a recipient's refusal to provide the adult-access protection the First Amendment requires, and it retains the prohibition on inducing unconstitutional conduct.
- Arkansas Educational Television Commission v. Forbes and National Endowment for the Arts v. Finley are preserved for ordinary selective programming and funding decisions; they do not confer immunity for every government-imposed restriction on access to protected expression already available through an opened service.
- Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of the University of Virginia, Legal Services Corp. v. Velazquez, and Center for Reproductive Law and Policy v. Bush retain protection of the independent expression within their actual benefit and program settings.
- Ashcroft v. Free Speech Coalition continues to govern the protected reach of the incorporated child-pornography definitions.

#### Law After Decision

The two statutory funding conditions remain applicable according to their distinct terms, including lawful disabling, while the facial injunction is removed. A publicly operated library that accepts covered assistance and offers general Internet access to adults may filter as an initial configuration, but an adult seeking lawful protected material must have prompt whole-session disabling or an equally effective method, without identifying the subject, disclosing a viewpoint or giving an approved reason. Reasonable technical, supervisory and resource procedures remain permissible; a nominal process that routinely denies access, materially delays it without adequate justification, or requires disclosure of the reading does not satisfy the requirement. Funding authorities may not treat lawful adult disabling as noncompliance. Actual denial, material delay or compelled disclosure is remedied by as-applied prospective relief on a supported record. The independently published service and scrutiny explanations retain their stated, different coalitions.

#### Separate Writings

**Kennedy, concurring.** Kennedy joins the opinion of the Court and writes to state the practical ground on which the facial question turns for him. Prompt whole-session disabling without explanation, which the Court requires, leaves no substantial adult-access burden. The record establishes overblocking and practical concerns but does not establish a significant burden on that lawful election sufficient for the facial decree. The difference from Denver's compulsory access machinery is material. Whether the election works is shown in the library where it is invoked: actual denial, material delay, disclosure requirements, or another substantial obstruction would call for review of the practice that produces it.

**Breyer, concurring.** The constitutional inquiry should examine this program's actual burden and means. Protection against unlawful images and minors' exposure are serious interests; imperfect filters impose burdens, but the lawful whole-session request matters to their extent. On this record the request burden does not produce disproportionate harm sufficient to invalidate the conditions facially. This contextual scrutiny is more demanding than ordinary rational-basis review and does not rest on rigid forum classification. Materially obstructive local practices remain separately reviewable.

**O'Connor, concurring in part and concurring in the judgment, joined by Scalia and Thomas.** These Justices join Part I and the judgment. They consider the actual service Congress assists together with the lawful adult election. Libraries exercise selection in providing a usable resource, and Forbes and Finley protect genuine institutional selection within their respective programs. Filtering aimed at unlawful material and protection of minors, coupled with permitted explanation-free adult access, fits this assisted service sufficiently to defeat the facial challenge. That ground decides the case without a general constitutional prescription for adult disabling; concrete obstruction can be reviewed on the record of an actual practice. Their ground addresses this library design; funding terminology does not create immunity for material unconstitutional administration or penalties on independent expression.

**Stevens, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** Local librarians' selection differs from a nationwide condition compelling filters across an otherwise opened Internet resource. The default barrier burdens readers and authors whose lawful material the filters remove. Permitting a request does not eliminate the burden of asking or the discretion surrounding release. Reno, Denver, Velazquez, and Center for Reproductive Law require attention to that actual private-expression burden. Stevens would retain protection for adults and remand to fit the decree to the distinct statutory provisions and independently lawful minor protections.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns to the three-judge District Court for entry of judgment rejecting the facial claims and removal of the injunction resting on them. The separate statutory funding and compliance provisions then govern within their own terms, administered consistently with the adult-access requirement. A claim that an actual library policy denies, materially delays or conditions adult access receives as-applied prospective review on proof of that library's operation and injury.
