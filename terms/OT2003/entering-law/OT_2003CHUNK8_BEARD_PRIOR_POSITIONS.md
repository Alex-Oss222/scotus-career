### Graham v. Collins, No. 91-7580

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Parts I–III and V | Souter | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor | Reverse denial of youth/background sentencing relief and order a conditional writ | Teague, youth, background and conditional sentencing relief; five join these propositions in full |
| Part IV, positive-character application | Souter | Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor | Deny independent relief on this component | Four find no independent material culpability use requiring further relief on this record; no fifth rationale join |
| Concurrence | Stevens | Author alone | Joins Souter throughout | Individual mitigation complements safeguards against arbitrary and discriminatory sentencing |
| Dissent | White | Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Would affirm under Teague | No new-rule exception applies on their view |
| Separate dissent | Thomas | Author alone | Also joins White | Would reject Penry’s broader mitigation requirement and preserve reasonable legislative structuring |



#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

White, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, would affirm because reasonable jurists in 1984 could read Jurek, Lockett and Eddings as allowing Texas to channel this mitigation through deliberateness and future dangerousness. In their view a new instruction requirement is not dictated by prefinality law; it neither removes a class of conduct or persons from punishment nor supplies a watershed accuracy procedure, so neither Teague exception saves it. They distinguish Penry's disabling evidence from Graham's evidence supporting rehabilitation and do not adopt the Court's as-applied merits rule.

Thomas also writes separately. He regards Penry's expansive account of mitigating effect as inconsistent with Furman's requirement that capital discretion be structured against arbitrariness and discrimination. He would permit reasonable state definitions of relevance and sentencing criteria while allowing defendants to present constitutionally relevant information. He would overrule Penry to that extent; the Court does not do so. His view is separate from White's sufficient Teague ground.

Stevens concurs while joining the controlling opinion. He regards narrowing eligibility, avoiding vague aggravators, proportionality limits and individualized mitigation as complementary protections. Requiring a sentencer to act on relevant individual circumstances reduces resort to irrelevant considerations such as race; it does not demand an unreasoned preference. His discussion responds to Thomas without creating an additional test or changing the conditional remedy.

Souter's Part IV, joined by Blackmun, Stevens and O’Connor, explains the limited character-evidence disposition. Ordinary chores, religious observance and support for children can inform future dangerousness through the second special issue. On the asserted theory here, their separate bearing on culpability for this offense is insubstantial. The four do not decide the value of such traits apart from the submitted culpability/future-danger arguments. Stone-Zsela joins only the denial of independent relief on that component.





### O'Dell v. Netherland, No. 96-6867

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | Stevens | O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer | Reject Teague bar | Meaningful-response protection was dictated at 1988 finality |
| Same opinion, Part II | Stevens | Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer | Independently rejects Teague bar | Even if new, the precisely confined protection qualifies under the watershed exception |
| Same opinion, Part III | Stevens | O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer | Formal vacatur and bounded remand | No automatic reinstatement; actual predicates, effect and genuine unresolved habeas conditions remain |
| Concurrence in part and dissent in part | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Joins Part II and rejection of the bar; seeks different formal relief | Finds a new rule; would reverse and restore the conditional sentencing-relief framework, with eligibility, effective opportunity and error analysis preserved |
| Dissent | Thomas | Scalia and Kennedy | Would affirm | New procedural rule satisfying neither contemporary Teague exception; no merits or harm finding |

Part I and Part III have five direct joins: Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Part II has five different direct joins: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. O'Connor does not join the watershed alternative. Stone-Zsela does not join the old-rule holding or the exact formal mandate. The two retroactivity grounds are independently sufficient holdings; they are not a synthesis of incompatible rationales.



#### Separate Writings

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** He joins the confined watershed holding in Part II and the rejection of the Teague bar, but concludes that Simmons was not dictated in 1988 and therefore does not join Part I. The new procedure qualifies, in his view, only when the State's future-danger case, actual legal parole ineligibility and loss of an effective means to correct the release premise combine to distort the sentencing choice. He would reverse and restore the District Court's conditional sentencing-relief framework. Restoration would still require proof of legal ineligibility, denial of an effective opportunity through accurate presentation or an adequate instruction, and the applicable error analysis. Neither instructional refusal alone nor the retroactivity holding proves entitlement. He would leave guilt undisturbed and permit Virginia a compliant new penalty proceeding or lawful noncapital sentence if the conditions warrant relief.

**Justice Thomas, joined by Justices Scalia and Kennedy, dissenting.** They would affirm under Teague. Gardner concerned undisclosed adverse information and Skipper excluded historical evidence; neither compelled in 1988 a legal-information obligation concerning the postsentencing regime. Ramos's state latitude and Caldwell's distinct responsibility rule support that substantive distinction. The rule may validly govern cases where it is available without having been dictated at finality. It regulates selection of an otherwise authorized penalty, so it is not substantive; its defined rebuttal protection also lacks the systemic character required for a watershed procedure. The dissenters preserve both exceptions but find neither satisfied. They decide no actual parole status, violation, harm, statutory condition or prison-danger exception.





### Williams v. Taylor (Michael Wayne Williams), No. 99-6615

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | Every other Associate Justice throughout; Stone-Zsela in Part I | Part I has nine votes; Parts II–IV have eight | Part I: applicant-attributed failure. Part II: report-hearing bar. Part III: relationship-claim diligence. Part IV: specified cause ruling. |
| Opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part | Stone-Zsela |  | Opposes report affirmance; supports further proceedings on the relationship claims while withholding final diligence findings | Joins Part I; seeks further diligence inquiry on all three claims. Agrees with rejection of the separate no-cause ground without joining its reasoning. |



#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela agrees that reasonable and timely efforts appropriate to available information prevent an applicant-attributed failure, and that strategic inaction or unexplained neglect remains subject to the statute. He would vacate and remand the diligence and hearing questions on all three claims rather than finally classify their development here. The district court should permit the development needed to determine diligence and the material juror-bias, nondisclosure and materiality issues, with a hearing conditioned on the required diligence and remaining legal requirements. Keeney distinguishes a lost opportunity from deliberate bypass, but the applicable AEDPA text controls. The writing neither grants a writ nor removes other hearing and merits restrictions. Stone-Zsela adds no separate cause ground.





### Williams v. Taylor (Terry Williams), No. 98-8384

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | Breyer | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | Shared rules; Scalia and Thomas oppose relief | Common statutory and sentencing propositions |
| Opinion of the Court, Part II | Breyer | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg | Seven-Justice reversal and conditional relief | Independently sufficient unreasonable application, actual deficiency and prejudice, and remedy |
| Opinion of the Court, Part III | Breyer | Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg | Alternative statutory ground supporting relief | Contrary-rule gateway and rejection of the asserted independent ordinary-probability ground |
| Concurrence | Souter | Stevens, Ginsburg, Breyer | Joins all lead parts and relief | Broader governing-rule construction |
| Separate opinion on statutory construction | O'Connor | Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | O'Connor and Kennedy join relief; Scalia and Thomas oppose | Distinct-clause general construction; all four join Part I |
| Dissent from application and relief | Scalia | Thomas | Would affirm denial of sentencing relief | Reasonable state application and independently absent actual prejudice; assumes performance |

Neither four-Justice account of the exclusive general allocation between the clauses commands a majority. The common rules and the independently supported grounds below control at their stated scope. No controlling rationale is constructed by combining those competing general accounts.



#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Souter concurs, joined by Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer. Where this Court has prescribed the rule governing the category of claim, the contrary-to clause permits independent application of that rule rather than demanding duplication of the precedent's facts. Strickland prescribes the performance and prejudice inquiry. The unreasonable-application clause retains work where existing holdings require derivation or extension beyond an already prescribed rule. The concurrence limits its approach to a governing rule for the category, not any abstract constitutional principle. It answers the concern about making the second clause redundant through that distinction. All four also join the independently sufficient conclusion that this particular state application is objectively unreasonable even under the more restrictive view; relief does not depend on adopting their broader construction. This exclusive allocation receives four votes.

Justice O'Connor writes separately on statutory construction, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. Contrary-to review reaches a conflicting governing legal rule or the opposite result on materially indistinguishable facts. Where the state court identifies the correct rule and applies it to different facts, unreasonable-application review requires objective unreasonableness distinct from mere error; independent federal judgment assesses that legal standard rather than deferring to a state label or polling judges. The governing rule's generality affects its range of reasonable applications without immunizing all applications. The writing objects that treating every incorrect application of a prescribed rule as contrary-to would largely consume the second clause. It imposes no clear-and-convincing burden on legal error and requires no identical Supreme Court facts for every contrary-rule claim. O'Connor and Kennedy join the actual gateway, constitutional violation and relief; Scalia and Thomas disagree with those applications. This exclusive general allocation also receives four votes.

Justice Scalia dissents from application and relief, joined by Thomas. Both accept the Court's common statutory rules, narrow Fretwell limit, lawful mitigation principle and Virginia unanimity consequence. They read the state court's ordinary-probability conclusion, consideration of juvenile history and discussion of severe aggravation as an independent application of the correct rule. The abbreviated treatment of witnesses does not establish legal exclusion of every document or commendation. The expanded mitigation has substantial explanatory value, but its mixed juvenile history, existing intellectual and character evidence, serious misconduct in custody, repeated violence and possible parole permit the State's conclusion about future danger. The trial judge's recommendation and fuller childhood account do not compel decisive weight.

The dissent finds the state application at least reasonable and, independently, finds no actual reasonable probability that a conscientious juror would decline death on the complete record. The latter ground defeats constitutional relief even if a forbidden additional framework requirement opens the statutory gateway. It does not require twelve votes for life, rely on an improper Fretwell fairness supplement or hold counsel's performance adequate; deficiency remains assumed. Because prejudice is independently absent in its view, the dissent would affirm without a framework-only remand or new factual development.





### Penry v. Johnson, No. 00-6677

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | Souter | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | Affirms psychiatric component; states reviewability framework | Statutory psychiatric ground; no actual-effect ruling |
| Opinion of the Court, Part II | Souter | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer | Reverses mitigation component and remands | Constitutional impairment, unreasonable application, certificate eligibility, corrective return |
| Opinion of the Court, Part III | Souter | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer | Governs effect inquiry on remand | Brecht's general rule and conditional remedy |
| Concurrence in Parts I and II and in the judgment | Stevens | Kennedy | Supports corrective return | Whole-record substantial influence with grave doubt against harmlessness; does not join Part III |
| Concurrence in Part I and dissent from mitigation disposition | Scalia | Thomas | Would affirm mitigation denial | No constitutional restriction and independently reasonable state application; distinct conditional effect position |



#### Separate Writings

**Stevens, concurring in Parts I and II and in the judgment, joined by Kennedy.** They agree that the psychiatric claim fails under section 2254(d)(1), that the mitigation mechanism was unconstitutional and objectively unreasonable, and that the adverse mitigation certificate disposition must be reversed. Adhering to their Brecht dissents, they do not join Part III's application of Brecht's general rule. As in their Coleman and Weeks positions, they would ask on the whole record whether the error substantially influenced the death sentence, with genuine grave doubt preventing a harmlessness conclusion. Their approach demands meaningful effect review but is not Chapman's beyond-reasonable-doubt requirement and is not a rule that the prisoner loses whenever the evidence is evenly balanced. They make no present finding of effect and support a return for the remaining inquiry. Their different effect methodology is noncontrolling.

**Scalia, concurring in Part I and dissenting from the mitigation disposition, joined by Thomas.** They accept the psychiatric statutory resolution but read the supplemental permission as a lawful specific exception to the ordinary factual directions. The judge's explanations to eventual jurors and the renewed defense closing strengthen that reading of the whole charge. On this view, the negative answer has a mitigation meaning supplied by the supplement, so the charge does not create a reasonable likelihood of exclusion. Independently, the state court's application remains within the objective reasonable range under Williams. They apply Graham and Johnson as binding but distinguish the inadequate mechanisms those cases addressed. Their prior Buchanan and Weeks adequacy positions support this whole-context reading. They would sustain denial of the mitigation certificate and relief. Only if error requiring effect review were assumed would they, adhering to their Brecht dissents, require petitioner-proved substantial injurious influence; evenly balanced proof would be insufficient. That conditional view supplies neither a vote to remand nor an actual finding that this error was harmless.
