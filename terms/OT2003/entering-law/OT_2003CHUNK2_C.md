## Standards and Tests selection: Concrete personal interests and procedural citizen-suit standing

### Concrete personal interests and procedural citizen-suit standing

**Governing question:** Article III standing for a citizen-suit plaintiff alleging a deprivation of procedure, including ESA §11(g) enforcement.

**Current rule:** A plaintiff invoking federal jurisdiction must establish a concrete and particularized, actual or imminent injury; a causal connection fairly traceable to the defendant's challenged conduct rather than to independent third parties' own decisions; and a likelihood, not mere speculation, that the requested relief will redress the injury. Authorization for any person to sue does not supply standing for only the public's undifferentiated interest in lawful administration. A procedural right must protect the plaintiff's own threatened concrete interest; when that connection exists, the plaintiff need not prove that the required procedure will certainly alter the ultimate agency decision. Aesthetic, recreational or professional wildlife interests may qualify, but past visits and indefinite return intentions without concrete plans or another supported basis for imminent renewed exposure do not establish threatened interference with personal use. At summary judgment the plaintiff must support challenged standing elements with specific facts by affidavit or other evidence, with favorable reasonable inferences available; allegations cannot replace missing evidence.

Supported curtailment of an identified person's local recreational or aesthetic use because of reasonable concern about ongoing discharges can establish personal injury without separate proof of environmental damage. An unexplained subjective fear or interest in statutory compliance alone is insufficient; a permit violation does not injure everyone downstream.

**Authority by component:**
- Stage-specific evidentiary requirement and existing injury, traceability and redressability elements: Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, “1. Article III elements must be supported at the litigation stage”, June 12, 1992; Scalia Part II, joined by White, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas (five).
- Prospective personal-use boundary: Lujan, “2. Indefinite return intentions and general species interests do not establish threatened personal injury here,” June 12, 1992; Scalia Part III-A, same five-Justice opinion; Stone separately supports the record-specific conclusion.
- Citizen-suit and procedural-right boundary: Lujan, “3. Citizen-suit authorization does not dispense with a threatened concrete interest,” June 12, 1992; Scalia Part IV, same five Justices.

- Concrete local lost use without a separate environmental-damage prerequisite: Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw, Public Projection / Holdings / Concrete interference with members' own river use supplies injury, January 12, 2000; O'Connor with Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer (seven).

**Present operation effective:** Laidlaw, January 12, 2000, for the lost-local-use component; Lujan's standing elements, procedural-interest limits and stage-appropriate proof retain their June 12, 1992 operation.

**Exceptions and limits:** No purchased-ticket or particular-document requirement is adopted. The redressability application to absent agencies, binding force of the challenged regulation upon those agencies, effect of particular funding changes and ESA §7(a)(2)'s foreign reach are not controlling holdings.

**Holdings navigation:** Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555 (1992) — Standing and personal injury.

## Standards and Tests selection: Individual legislator standing for institutional dilution

### Individual legislator standing for institutional dilution

**Governing question:** Whether individual legislators may challenge abstract dilution of institutional legislative power after fully effective votes.

**Current rule:** Members whose votes were counted and given full legal effect lack Article III injury on a claim of abstract institutional-power dilution or altered force of future votes. Express statutory permission to sue may remove prudential restrictions but cannot supply personal concrete injury. Losing a fully effective vote differs from complete nullification of votes sufficient to determine an outcome or a personal deprivation of office or pay.

**Authority by component:**
- Personal/institutional boundary and statutory authorization: Raines, Raines v. Byrd — I. These individual legislators lack a concrete personal injury, Scalia with Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas and Ginsburg (six), 1997-06-26.

**Present operation effective:** Raines, 1997-06-26.

**Exceptions and limits:** No universal ban on official or legislator litigation, legislative-exhaustion requirement or categorical rule against an authorized institutional suit is adopted. Concrete cancellation injury and complete vote nullification retain their distinct Article III questions.

**Holdings navigation:** Raines v. Byrd (1997-06-26) — Federal Courts / Legislator standing.

## Standards and Tests selection: Present civil obligations and pre-enforcement ripeness

### Present civil obligations and pre-enforcement ripeness

**Governing question:** Whether a legal duty can create injury without an express statutory or criminal penalty.

**Current rule:** A presently applicable legal obligation or supported threatened civil enforcement may burden conduct and establish Article III injury even without an express statutory or criminal penalty. The claimant must establish concrete personal application, traceability and redressability. Ripeness separately examines fitness and hardship: a predominantly legal question alone does not prove hardship, but possible later civil enforcement does not categorically require postponing review of a presently burdensome duty.

**Authority by component:**

- Compelled participation: injury and ripeness: Lee v. Harcleroad, 1998-01-20; Stevens Part III-A with all seven other Associates; Part III-B’s remand with O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Present operation effective:** 1998-01-20.

**Exceptions and limits:** Conditional statutory duties, an asserted obligation or an abstract fear do not themselves establish present application. The rule creates no presumption of provider standing, statutory duty, violation or automatic hearing.

**Related current rules:** Concrete personal interests and procedural citizen-suit standing supplies the ordinary injury and litigation-stage proof requirements.

**Holdings navigation:** Lee v. Harcleroad — Pre-enforcement injury and ripeness.

## Standards and Tests selection: Permissive FEC advisory opinions before pre-enforcement review

### Permissive FEC advisory opinions before pre-enforcement review

**Governing question:** Whether §437f requires an advisory-opinion request before challenging an effective FEC regulation.

**Current rule:** An association whose recurring issue communications have actually ceased because of a regulation’s credible application may seek pre-enforcement review of its legal reach. Section 437f’s advisory-opinion procedure is permissive, not a compulsory exhaustion route for that challenge, and it cannot yield an agency determination invalidating the governing rule.

**Authority by component:**

**Present operation effective:** 1998-09-14.

**Exceptions and limits:** The holding supplies no universal exemption from independently required administrative exhaustion.

## Standards and Tests selection: Intermediary contribution limits and meaningful association

### Intermediary contribution limits and meaningful association

**Governing question:** Fit of limits on contributions received by political committees and political funds.

**Current rule:** Contributions remain distinct from independent expenditures. A broadly restrictive ceiling on contributions received by political committees or funds must preserve meaningful collective political association and fit an identified corruption or circumvention mechanism. Realistic routes around valid candidate limits may be regulated; an insufficiently fitted ceiling cannot be sustained merely by invoking circumvention. The invalidation of the reviewed $100 aggregate annual ceiling establishes no permanent constitutional dollar minimum. Annual duration, actual recipient function, per-recipient versus aggregate operation, amount and demonstrated effect on collective advocacy must be considered together. Other available committees and aggregate political success do not alone cure a burden on the chosen association.

**Authority by component:**

- This $100 intermediary-contribution ceiling lacks sufficient fit: This $100 intermediary-contribution ceiling lacks sufficient fit, 1995-05-15. Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, Part III, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: nine direct votes, at this stated functional application.

- Russell v. Burris — Canonical Decision Record, Holdings, “The ordinary-PAC receipt limit survives on this operational record”, 1999-04-28; O’Connor Parts II–IV with Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Part III supplies the actual-channel and operational considerations.

**Present operation effective:** 1995-05-15; the component authorities identified above.

**Exceptions and limits:** The reviewed Day recipient class and its exclusions remain as originally stated. Russell supplies no general approval of a $200 intermediary receipt ceiling, no permanent dollar floor, no immunity for sham or materially different structures and no unlimited candidate-contribution authority. Genuine independence and actual candidate-conduit status retain their separate requirements.

**Holdings navigation:** This $100 intermediary-contribution ceiling lacks sufficient fit. [Russell v. Burris — The ordinary-PAC receipt limit survives on this operational record](HOLDINGS.md#the-ordinary-pac-receipt-limit-survives-on-this-operational-record).

**Current wording authority:** Russell, April 28, 1999, applies Day’s meaningful-pooling and actual-fit rule to a demonstrated per-recipient candidate-conduit setting without adopting a general dollar safe harbor.

**Related current rules:** Candidate contribution limits and anticorruption fit states the distinct direct-candidate and exact dispersed-donor classification rules.

## Standards and Tests selection: Nonprofit political spending outside a literal safe harbor

### Nonprofit political spending outside a literal safe harbor

**Governing question:** Independent expenditures by a political nonprofit with incidental business activity and insignificant corporate funding.

**Current rule:** Failure to meet a statutory safe harbor because of incidental business activity or absence of a formal policy against significant corporate contributions does not alone defeat MCFL protection. A voluntary political organization whose resources reflect political support rather than commercial accumulation, whose structure avoids economic claimants and corporate conduits, and whose actual business income and corporate funding are minimal and insignificant may receive as-applied protection. Material commercial accumulation, economic claimants or corporate or union conduit funding requires a different assessment. This independent-expenditure protection does not require a categorical ideological-nonprofit exemption from a restriction on direct candidate contributions out of the general corporate treasury.

**Authority by component:**

- MCCL receives protection on its actual nonprofit characteristics: MCCL receives protection on its actual nonprofit characteristics, 1995-05-15. Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, Part IV, joined by all eight Associates: nine direct votes on this as-applied holding. No general overruling or endorsement beyond Austin's surviving scope is adopted.

- Stengel Holding 3: direct candidate gifts distinguished from protected independent spending: Kentucky Right to Life, Inc. v. Stengel — controlling Holdings, 1998-09-14.

**Present operation effective:** 1998-09-14, for the new component; earlier component authorities retain their stated dates.

**Exceptions and limits:** Section 211B.15(15)’s safe harbor requires no business activity, no economic shareholders or claimants, no establishment by a business corporation or union, and a policy against significant contributions from them. Missing the literal checklist does not itself establish the constitutional regulatory predicate. This does not overrule Austin in its materially different corporate setting or immunize future changes in nonprofit operations. Stengel separately rejects the claimed categorical exemption for general-treasury direct candidate contributions. Day’s responsive-financing judgment has no controlling five-Justice framework and supplies none of this rule.

**Holdings navigation:** MCCL receives protection on its actual nonprofit characteristics. Kentucky Right to Life, Inc. v. Stengel — Moot amended election claims and equitable judgment treatment; Kentucky Right to Life, Inc. v. Stengel — Corporate gifts, paid identification and distinct contribution limits.

## Standards and Tests selection: Independent political-party campaign expression and candidate connections

### Independent political-party campaign expression and candidate connections

**Governing question:** Application of the Senate party-expenditure ceiling and the alternative candidate-contribution theory to a genuinely independent party advertisement.

**Current rule:** Section 441a(d)(3)'s Senate-election expenditure ceiling cannot be applied to a party's own genuinely independent campaign advertisement merely because the speaker is an official political party. Party identity or a continuing electoral relationship also cannot replace §441a(a)(7)(B)(i)'s required cooperation, consultation, concert, request or suggestion by a candidate, authorized political committee or agent for the asserted contribution theory. Actual independence defeats that theory; realistic corruption and circumvention concerns do not alone establish the statutory candidate connection.

Expenditures actually coordinated with a federal candidate may be limited when the government proves an objectively grounded risk of circumventing valid candidate-contribution limits and a restriction closely drawn to that risk while preserving meaningful party association and effective advocacy. The presented Senate-election party ceiling meets that standard against the categorical facial challenge; party identity, policy agreement, endorsement and ordinary affiliation alone prove neither coordination nor corruption. Ordinary access, equality of influence and dislike of expensive campaigns supply no independent justification. Independent spending cannot automatically cure burdens on joint party expression.

The actual candidate connection remains necessary. Section 441a(a)(7)(B)(i) requires cooperation, consultation or concert with, or a request or suggestion from, the candidate, an authorized committee or an agent. The separate republication and conduit provisions retain their different predicates, including section (a)(8)'s attribution to the original source and source-and-recipient reporting. The same-party transfer exception supplies no categorical constitutional immunity for candidate-connected expenditures. Section (d)(1)'s permission for the identified national and State party committees, including subordinate State committees, is subject to (d)(2) and (3). The Senate and single-Representative-State allowance is the greater of two cents per voting-age resident or $20,000, with section (c)'s annual price-index adjustment; section (d)(3)(B)'s separate indexed $10,000 amount concerns other House, Delegate and Resident Commissioner races. Only the Senate counterclaim's constitutional category is decided.

**Authority by component:**

- Independent-expression protection: Independent party expression; distinct contribution predicate: The alternative contribution theory, 1996-06-26; O’Connor Part I, joined by Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer (six).

- Coordinated-party facial standard and preservation of independent-expression protection: Canonical Decision Record `FEC_v_Colorado_Republican_Federal_Campaign_Committee_merits_2001-06-25.md`, Public Projection / Holdings / I. Actual coordinated party spending may be limited to prevent demonstrated circumvention; II. The facial invalidation is reversed while the independent-advertisement judgment remains intact, 2001-06-25; six direct joins.

**Present operation effective:** FEC v. Colorado Republican, June 25, 2001, for the coordinated-party facial standard; Colorado Republican, June 26, 1996, retains independent-expression protection.

**Exceptions and limits:** The general contribution/expenditure distinction survives. Transfers between national, State, district, local and subordinate committees of the same party retain their exemption from §441a(a)(1)–(2); candidate-material republication under §441a(a)(7)(B)(ii) and earmarking or intermediary direction under §441a(a)(8) have different predicates. The rule creates no immunity for candidate cash, genuine conduits, sham independence or coordinated expression.

**Open question:** A particular coordinated communication may present a distinct expressive application requiring its own constitutional characterization. The facial ruling supplies no universal amount, approval of materially different ceilings or automatic validation of all coordinated communications, other contribution categories, or soft-money practices. The record does not establish the range of potentially protected applications needed for a broader facial-overbreadth ruling.

**Holdings navigation:** [FEC v. Colorado Republican Federal Campaign Committee — Actually coordinated political-party expenditures](HOLDINGS.md#fec-v-colorado-republican-federal-campaign-committee).

## Standards and Tests selection: Candidate financing, political-committee thresholds and membership exclusions

### Candidate financing, political-committee thresholds and membership exclusions

**Governing question:** Whether predominantly issue-oriented groups are exempt from FECA political-committee duties for otherwise covered candidate financing.

**Current rule:** Buckley and MCFL supply no categorical organization-wide major-purpose exemption for genuinely covered candidate financing. Section 431(4)(A) reaches a committee, club, association or other group receiving contributions aggregating over $1,000 in a calendar year or making expenditures aggregating over $1,000 in a calendar year, subject to actual definitions, exclusions and constitutional limits. Establish membership, covered transactions, actual candidate connection and thresholds before imposing duties. Where a contribution theory requires cooperation, consultation, concert, request or suggestion, ideological alignment, lobbying or partisan identity alone is insufficient. Independent expression retains its protection.

**Authority by component:**
- No categorical major-purpose exemption: Akins, Akins v. FEC — III. Major purpose is not a categorical exemption for otherwise covered candidate financing, Souter Part III with Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer (five for both candidate-cash/conduit and broader covered candidate-financing subparts; Kennedy joins only the former), 1997-06-25.
- Distinct exclusions and reporting conditions: Akins, Akins v. FEC — IV. Membership exclusions and their distinct reporting proviso govern the remand, seven-Justice Part IV, 1997-06-25.

**Present operation effective:** Akins, 1997-06-25.

**Controlling construction:** Section 431(9)(B)(iii) excludes membership-organization or corporate communications to members, stockholders, or executive or administrative personnel when the organization is not primarily organized to influence federal nomination or election. Its separate proviso requires reporting direct costs of communications expressly advocating a clearly identified candidate's election or defeat when costs exceed $2,000 per election, except communications primarily about other subjects, under §434(a)(4)(A)(i) and, for a general election, (ii). Section 441b(b)(2) separately excludes corporate communications on any subject to stockholders and executive or administrative personnel and their families, and labor communications to members and their families; its nonpartisan registration/get-out-the-vote exclusion covers those respective recipients. Its separate-fund exclusion covers establishment, administration and solicitation for a political separate segregated fund by a corporation, labor or membership organization, cooperative or corporation without capital stock. Its loan exception concerns ordinary-course national or State bank loans under banking law and regulations. Sections 431(8)(B)(vi) and (9)(B)(v) cross-reference activity allowed by §441b without importing the $2,000 proviso.

**Open question:** Particular organizational or donor-disclosure burdens retain supported constitutional challenges. The construction supplies no automatic membership, transaction, threshold or liability finding.

**Holdings navigation:** Akins v. FEC (1997-06-25) — III. Major purpose is not a categorical exemption for otherwise covered candidate financing; Akins v. FEC (1997-06-25) — IV. Membership exclusions and their distinct reporting proviso govern the remand.

## Standards and Tests selection: Candidate inquiries and independently edited voter guides

### Candidate inquiries and independently edited voter guides

**Governing question:** Whether asking candidates about issues or votes itself constitutes candidate financing under §441b.

**Current rule:** A publisher’s mere inquiry to a candidate about positions or recorded votes cannot itself be prohibited as a contribution or candidate-directed expenditure. The required cooperation, consultation, concert, request or suggestion concerns campaign spending. General rulemaking and enforcement authority does not justify treating every independently edited guide involving inquiry as a prohibited contribution or imposing compulsory written questions and replies and equal space and prominence on that premise.

**Authority by component:**

- Clifton Holdings 1–2: expenditure connection and the bounded §114.4(c)(4)–(5) construction: Clifton v. Federal Election Commission — controlling Holdings, 1998-09-14.

**Present operation effective:** 1998-09-14.

**Exceptions and limits:** Actual coordination concerning an expenditure retains its statutory predicates. Unfinished electioneering-message clauses, severability and nonprofit protection based on actual funding characteristics remain unresolved; no blanket constitutional ban on contact safeguards is adopted. Staged inquiries supplying campaign work and paid political advertisements disguised as independent guides are not protected by this rule. The decision does not foreclose specifically fitted contact safeguards or accurate disclosure under separate lawful authority; government-authored guides and conditions on properly defined public resources remain distinct.

**Holdings navigation:** Clifton v. Federal Election Commission — Independent voter guides and candidate inquiries.

## Standards and Tests selection: Express advocacy through limited context under §100.22(b)

### Express advocacy through limited context under §100.22(b)

**Governing question:** When a communication lacking a listed electoral slogan is express advocacy under the FEC rule and §441b.

**Current rule:** Paragraph (b) requires all of the following: assessment of the communication as a whole, only limited reference to external events, an unmistakable and unambiguous electoral portion suggestive of only one meaning, and no reasonable difference over whether it encourages election or defeat of a clearly identified candidate rather than another action. A reasonable nonelectoral interpretation excludes coverage. Within these cumulative restrictions, limited context does not exceed §441b merely because the communication lacks a listed slogan.

**Authority by component:**

- Maine Right to Life Holdings 2–3: five-Justice statutory construction: Federal Election Commission v. Maine Right to Life Committee, Inc. — controlling Holdings, 1998-09-14.

**Present operation effective:** 1998-09-14.

**Exceptions and limits:** Constitutional vagueness and particular applications remain open. The construction changes neither paragraph (a), distinct contributions, membership exclusions nor protection based on a nonprofit’s actual characteristics.

**Holdings navigation:** Federal Election Commission v. Maine Right to Life Committee, Inc. — Pre-enforcement campaign-finance review and optional advisory opinions; Federal Election Commission v. Maine Right to Life Committee, Inc. — Contextual express advocacy and statutory limits.

## Standards and Tests selection: Candidate contribution limits and anticorruption fit

### Candidate contribution limits and anticorruption fit

**Governing question:** What justifies direct candidate-contribution limits and differentiated source allowances.

**Current rule:** Candidate contribution limits must be closely drawn to a sufficiently important interest in preventing corruption or its appearance while preserving meaningful political association. No completed bribe is necessary, and equality of political influence is not an independent justification. Amount and inflation comparisons establish no permanent constitutional boundary; actual campaign scale, fundraising burden and cumulative restrictions matter. Under the adjudicated Arkansas classification, a registered committee receiving contributions exclusively from individuals and no more than $25 cumulatively from any individual during a calendar year may receive a $2,500 per-candidate, per-election contribution allowance while ordinary sources face lower ceilings. The actual dispersed-donor structure and its anticorruption fit, rather than a small-donor label, support the distinction.

The same contribution standard permits the presented viewpoint-neutral prohibition of direct business-corporation treasury gifts to federal candidates when the government justifies the direct-recipient and commercial-source safeguard while preserving voluntary human political channels. Neither corporate form, concentrated wealth, ordinary access nor nominal availability of a separate fund alone supplies close fit. Materially different party-financing channels do not require permission for direct treasury transfers where the direct-control risk remains justified; underinclusion must still receive a real fit inquiry. Independent expenditures, materially different organizations and supported operational burdens retain their own analysis.

**Authority by component:**

- Direct-candidate interest and close fit: Russell v. Burris — Canonical Decision Record, Holdings, “Contribution limits require actual anticorruption fit; two candidate categories fail here”, April 28, 1999; O’Connor Part I, all nine Justices.
- Exact dispersed-donor classification: Russell — Holdings, “The exact small-donor qualification supports a different candidate allowance”, April 28, 1999; O’Connor Part IV with Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

- Business-treasury candidate restriction and distinct soft-money comparison: Canonical Decision Record `Mariani_v_United_States_merits_2000-12-12.md`, Public Projection / Holdings / II. The presented direct corporate-treasury restriction is constitutionally permissible; III. The demonstrated soft-money practices do not invalidate the direct-transfer rule, 2000-12-12; six direct joins.

**Present operation effective:** Mariani, December 12, 2000, for the business-treasury source component; Russell, April 28, 1999, retains the earlier general and dispersed-donor rules.

**Exceptions and limits:** Office-specific dollar applications establish no general floor or safe harbor. Every small-donor qualification remains necessary to the adjudicated distinction; no unlimited candidate-giving right, immunity for sham structures or automatic validity of materially different classifications follows. A failed categorical challenge does not resolve a genuinely different office-specific application.

**Related current rules:** Intermediary contribution limits and meaningful association separately governs committee receipt restrictions and actual candidate-conduit fit.

**Holdings navigation:** [Mariani v. United States — Corporate contributions and true-source attribution](HOLDINGS.md#mariani-v-united-states-1).

## Standards and Tests selection: Issue advocacy and definite campaign-finance coverage

### Issue advocacy and definite campaign-finance coverage

**Governing question:** When issue discussion may trigger comprehensive political-committee duties or a prohibition on campaign expenditures.

**Current rule:** A State may not impose a full registration, recordkeeping and continuing-reporting committee regime on an issue organization lacking a major electoral purpose merely because its communications discuss candidates or incidentally affect elections, without a determinate electoral-financing boundary. A prohibition on expenditures likewise may not reach genuine independent issue advocacy merely through a general political-purpose or election-influence formula; it requires a sufficiently definite electoral-advocacy boundary. A nonbinding narrowing assurance does not supply that boundary.

**Authority by component:**

- Comprehensive committee regime and incidental-purpose coverage: North Carolina Right to Life, Inc. v. Bartlett — Incidental influence on elections cannot trigger this full political-committee regime, May 1, 2000.
- Definite expenditure boundary and protection of genuine issue discussion: Bartlett — The expenditure provisions lack a sufficiently definite electoral-advocacy boundary, May 1, 2000. Souter’s unanimous opinion controls both components.

**Present operation effective:** Bartlett, May 1, 2000.

**Exceptions and limits:** This creates no organization-wide major-purpose exemption for genuinely covered candidate financing. Distinct contribution, coordinated-spending, sponsor-disclosure, candidate and party disclosure, nonprofit-status, and major-electoral-purpose committee rules retain their actual predicates. The Court requires no canonical list of electoral words and decides neither every contextual test nor the exact guide’s status under a different sufficiently definite rule.

**Related current rules:** Candidate financing, political-committee thresholds and membership exclusions states Akins’s actual-financing rule. Express advocacy through limited context under §100.22(b) retains Maine Right to Life’s separate statutory construction and reserved constitutional question; its federal thresholds and exclusions are not imported into state law.

## Standards and Tests selection: Bounded responsive grants within a voluntary public-financing option

### Bounded responsive grants within a voluntary public-financing option

**Governing question:** Responsive public grants and the combined burdens of a capped public option and private contribution limits.

**Current rule:** A support-qualified, capped public-financing option may supplement an enrolled candidate in response to an opponent's candidate-financing measure or qualifying independent advocacy when the demonstrated programmatic connection and actual operation create neither an independent legal restraint nor substantial practical suppression of lawful opposing activity. A voluntary label alone cannot answer the burden: participation conditions, the finite grant, adjustments and the practical private route must be examined. Independent advocacy measured by a grant formula does not thereby become a coordinated contribution.

Lawful components can combine into unconstitutional coercion. A meaningful private campaigning alternative defeats that additional claim, but public funding cannot cure an independently unconstitutional private restriction. Competitive disadvantage and an attractive subsidy are not alone compulsion; formal permission to remain private is insufficient if substantial practical suppression is established. The permission for Maine's two triggers depends on the complete bounded mechanism below, not a categorical exemption for matching subsidies.

**Authority by component:**

- Two responsive triggers with actual program connection and nonsuppression limits: Canonical Decision Record `Daggett_v_Commission_on_Governmental_Ethics_merits_2000-12-11.md`, Public Projection / Holdings / Both bounded responsive-grant triggers survive the presented challenge, 2000-12-11; Part II; five direct joins.
- Cumulative burden and meaningful-private-route inquiry: Canonical Decision Record `Daggett_v_Commission_on_Governmental_Ethics_merits_2000-12-11.md`, Public Projection / Holdings / The meaningful private alternative defeats the additional combination claim, 2000-12-11; Part III; six direct joins.

**Present operation effective:** 2000-12-11.

**Statutory conditions:** The legislative ceiling is per election; the individual candidate's own and spouse's contributions retain §1015(1)'s exception. That exception does not remove separate restrictions accepted by a certified public participant. A legislative participant must file the declaration, obtain ballot qualification and compliant seed funding, and collect qualifying $5 contributions from at least 150 registered district voters for a Senate race or 50 for a House race. Contributions go to the fund by check or money order during the qualifying period with the candidate's knowledge and approval, an identifying receipt, and no exchanged value. Seed money is limited to $100 per individual, including candidate or family funds, and aggregate legislative amounts of $1,500 for Senate or $500 for House, subject to Commission rule authority. Unspent seed money transfers to the fund at certification. Later private contributions require specific Commission authorization.

Contested-election initial grants use the corresponding office's average expenditures in contested races in the prior two applicable elections, with the initial 2000 amount reduced by 25 percent; inadequate data permits use of the most recent applicable elections. An uncontested primary uses the corresponding uncontested average or the contested average if lower. No grant issues for an uncontested general election.

The matching measure is the greater of expenditures or obligations and funds raised or borrowed, alone or with qualifying independent expenditures reported under §1019. Once that measure exceeds the applicable initial distribution, the Commission pays the opposing certified candidate the reported excess. Supplements stop at twice the relevant contested-primary or contested-general initial distribution, making the ordinary combined maximum three times that distribution. The regulatory deduction reduces matching payments for independent express advocacy favoring the participant or opposing the private opponent after that opponent exceeds the initial distribution; it never reduces the initial grant. No unpresented multiple-opponent formula is decided.

Section 1125(13) forbids spending beyond available fund revenue. If revenue cannot meet the initial or matching distributions, the Commission may authorize certified candidates to accept and spend private contributions up to the applicable funding amounts, reduced by seed money and subject to the legislative $250 per-donor-per-election ceiling and Commission rules. This is conditional permission, not a mandatory release or unlimited financing right.


**Exceptions and limits:** No general dollar safe harbor, universal matching-grant permission, mandatory empirical study or candidate-disappearance prerequisite follows. Day's distinct judgment remains effective without converting its four-Justice rationale into law. Unchallenged administrative details in the stated scheme receive no independent constitutional holding.

**Holdings navigation:** [Daggett v. Commission on Governmental Ethics & Election Practices — Contributions and responsive public financing](HOLDINGS.md#daggett-v-commission-on-governmental-ethics--election-practices).

## Standards and Tests selection: Truthful source attribution in candidate contributions

### Truthful source attribution in candidate contributions

**Governing question:** False-name contributions, knowing permission to use a name, and knowing acceptance in covered candidate-finance transactions.

**Current rule:** Congress may protect truthful donor attribution and prevent evasion by prohibiting contributions in another person's name, knowing permission to use one's name for such a contribution, and knowing acceptance of such a contribution. This independent attribution justification does not depend on the validity of the particular corporate-source restriction. Regulation of different financing channels does not confer a right to substitute a false donor.

The first clause of section 441f contains no express knowledge term; the second and third do. That distinction establishes neither strict criminal liability nor a construction of all criminal-enforcement elements. ##### What evidence of Article III injury, causation and redressability is required at summary judgment

**Holding:** A plaintiff invoking federal jurisdiction must establish a concrete and particularized, actual or imminent injury, a causal connection fairly traceable to the defendant rather than independent third parties, and a likelihood that the requested relief will redress the injury. **Limits and questions not reached:** The general redressability element controls. ##### The rule's cumulative restrictions preserve the statutory express-advocacy boundary

**Holding:** Under 2 U.S.C. §441b, 11 C.F.R. §100.22(b) does not exceed the statute merely because it permits limited context rather than an exclusive list of electoral slogans. Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas dissent from the statutory disposition and do not join this construction.

The lower court itself recognized that MCFL included a marginally less direct explicit appeal; the disagreement is whether paragraph (b)'s contextual route necessarily crosses the narrowed statute's boundary. Limited context may inform the communication's meaning, but cannot displace a reasonable call to issue debate or some other action.
