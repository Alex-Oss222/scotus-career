#### Borden Ranch Partnership v. Army Corps of Engineers, 537 U.S. 99

**Docket or dockets:** No. 01-1243.
**Decided:** December 16, 2002.

##### Actual mechanized conveyance and deposit satisfy the discharge elements

**Holding:** Equipment that excavates, conveys and actively deposits dredged soil or other statutory pollutants into jurisdictional waters makes an addition from a point source even when the material was not imported from offsite. Mere machinery movement, in-place disturbance or hydrological alteration without a proved conveyance and deposit does not satisfy those elements.

**Proposition-level authority:** Parts I–II and Part III's application in the opinion of O'Connor are joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, including the author; those six direct joins control the liability holdings and their application.

**Material application:** The trial found fully engaged shanks actively conveying and depositing soil, rock, sand and biological material, including channel filling or mounding. Liability rests on that statutory act, not hydrological injury alone or a machine's presence near water. Agricultural stormwater runoff remains excluded.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Jurisdiction over the surviving drainages and swales is conceded. The vernal-pool component stays excluded. The holding does not treat every incidental fallback as active deposit; later guidance and the 2002 regulatory revision provide no retroactive liability ground.

##### Recapture requires the purpose and effects specified for the water area

**Holding:** Section 1344(f)(2) requires a permit for a discharge incidental to an activity whose purpose is to bring a water area into a use to which it was not previously subject, where flow or circulation may be impaired or the waters' reach reduced, even if normal-farming protection otherwise applies. An actual discharge, the purpose of a new use of the water area, and the specified possible physical consequence are separate conditions; a crop change, ranch-wide agricultural label or accidental passage alone does not establish them.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part II of O'Connor's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; six Justices.

**Material application:** Even assuming normal farming or plowing protection under section 1344(f)(1)(A), the proved deposits were incidental to purposeful conversion of the water areas toward orchards and vineyards, with the requisite flow impairment. Prior ranch-wide grazing did not establish cultivation of each drainage. Directions to raise the shanks did not authorize engaged ripping.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No independent regulatory plowing exclusion is decided. An accidental pass alone does not establish conversion purpose, but the statute adds no separate intent-to-pollute element to each pass once its connection to a qualifying conversion activity is proved. Nondischarge cultivation, natural soil movement and farming within an operative exemption are unaffected.

##### Distinct proved discharges may be counted separately within a day

**Holding:** Under §1319(d), each distinct lawfully established discharge may constitute a separate violation within the same day; a single project does not merge separate discharges into one daily violation. The applicable maximum here is $25,000 per day for each violation, and the assessment must consider seriousness, economic benefit, violation history, good-faith efforts, economic impact and justice; a pass lacking a proved discharge cannot be counted.

**Proposition-level authority:** All eight participating Justices directly join Part III's statutory counting rule. Only Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer join its application sustaining the drainage count and the resulting judgment; Scalia and Thomas require a new lawful addition inquiry before any count can stand.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm the surviving drainage and swale liability and discharge-counting application, 6–2, while preserving exclusion of the pool counts and the penalty-recalculation remand. Kennedy did not participate. Each count requires a proved discharge; the maximum is a ceiling, not a mandatory penalty. No replacement amount or automatic restoration duty is set. Restoration depends on the operative decree and authorized findings.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- PUD No. 1 of Jefferson County v. Washington Department of Ecology: applies the need for a discharge trigger before broader activity controls; its distinct certification authority is not an independent liability source here.
- Babbitt v. Sweet Home Chapter of Communities for a Great Oregon: applies the insistence on an actual statutory act and causal conditions; habitat or water damage alone cannot replace them.
- Solid Waste Agency of Northern Cook County v. Army Corps of Engineers: preserves its confined rejection of jurisdiction resting solely on migratory-bird use and its requirement of enacted authority; it does not decide the conceded surviving waters or the addition issue.
- United States v. Riverside Bayview Homes: its adjacent-wetland coverage remains intact; this case supplies no broader geographic definition.
- MCI Telecommunications Corp. v. AT&T: preserves the requirement that agency action remain within the statutory grant; purpose or administration cannot manufacture an absent element.

#### Arkansas v. Oklahoma, 503 U.S. 91 (1992)

**Docket or dockets:** Nos. 90-1262 and 90-1266.
**Decided:** February 26, 1992.
**Companion or consolidated matters:** *EPA v. Oklahoma*, No. 90-1266, consolidated with *Arkansas*; both concern EPA’s federal discharge permit for Fayetteville.

##### Whether EPA may condition this upstream federal permit on compliance with downstream state water-quality standards

**Holding:** The Clean Water Act authorizes EPA, in issuing this federal permit under §402(a), to require compliance with applicable downstream state water-quality standards through its valid regulations. A downstream State’s limited direct role in permitting does not restrict that federal authority; whether the Act itself commands identical compliance in every permitting setting remains open.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stevens’s opinion, joined by the other eight Justices; unanimous statutory-authority holding.

**Material application:** Section 402(a)(2) authorizes permit conditions, §301(b)(1)(C) identifies attainment of applicable standards, and 40 C.F.R. §122.4(d) reasonably implements that scheme through assurance of affected-state compliance. Limits on Oklahoma’s independent authority do not negate EPA’s delegated power.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court decides neither a universal statutory command governing every federal or state permit nor an independent source-state tort remedy. Technical expertise alone creates no authority.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Reverse in both dockets and sustain EPA’s permit under its existing compliance, study and modification conditions. No new numerical limit or order concerning the separate White River discharge is entered.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Milwaukee v. Illinois*, 451 U.S. 304, retains the Act’s displacement of federal nuisance common law. *International Paper Co. v. Ouellette*, 479 U.S. 481, is distinguished: limits on affected-state common-law control and direct permitting participation do not restrict this authorized federal condition. *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, applies within Congress’s delegation. *Lechmere, Inc. v. NLRB*, decided January 27, 1992, is preserved: deference operates within controlling statutory precedent, and no contrary construction is displaced.

##### Whether existing downstream degradation categorically prohibits a new discharge

**Holding:** The Clean Water Act does not categorically forbid a new discharge merely because its effluent will reach waters already failing a water-quality standard, irrespective of whether it will cause a detectable violation under the applicable permit standards. Courts may not substitute that categorical prohibition for the Act’s actual permitting and pollution-reduction scheme.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stevens’s opinion, joined by the other eight Justices; unanimous rejection of the court of appeals’ independent statutory ground.

**Material application:** Section 303(d) provides a framework for pollution reduction, and §402(h) addresses additional pollutants accepted by a treatment plant violating its own permit; neither supplies the broader downstream prohibition the Tenth Circuit imposed. EPA applied the no-detectable-violation approach to this discharge.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Existing degradation remains relevant under the actual governing standards. No detectable violation is excused and no later permit condition is decided on facts outside the record.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Remove the categorical already-degraded-waters bar and sustain the permit under the existing conditions in both dockets.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Milwaukee v. Illinois*, 451 U.S. 304, describes a comprehensive statutory program; it does not authorize courts to add a categorical prohibition.

For *Arkansas v. Oklahoma*, Nos. 90-1262 and 90-1266, decided February 26, 1992, on construction of the incorporated downstream standards and judicial review of EPA’s findings, see Administrative Law, “Federal permit interpretation and review.”

### Active discharge and conversion of protected waters

**Governing question:** When mechanized relocation of pollutants into jurisdictional waters requires a permit and how distinct discharges are counted.

**Current rule:** Active equipment-mediated excavation, conveyance and deposition of dredged soil or other statutory pollutants into jurisdictional waters can constitute an addition from a point source without an offsite source. Machinery movement, in-place disturbance and hydrological harm alone cannot replace proof of conveyance and deposit. Even where normal-farming protection otherwise applies, §1344(f)(2) requires a permit when a discharge is incidental to an activity intended to bring the water area into a previously absent use and flow or circulation may be impaired or the waters' reach reduced. The discharge, new-use purpose and specified possible effect must each be established; a crop change, a general agricultural label or accidental passage alone is insufficient. No separate intent to pollute is required for each discharge incidental to the qualifying conversion activity.

Under §1319(d), distinct proved discharges may count as separate violations within one day; one project does not merge them. Apply the conduct-date maximum and consider seriousness, economic benefit, violation history, good-faith efforts, economic impact and justice when fixing the amount. A pass without a proved discharge cannot be counted.

**Authority by component:**

- Canonical Decision Record `Borden-Ranch-Partnership-v-Army-Corps-of-Engineers_merits_2002-12-16.md`, Public Projection / Holdings / I. Actual mechanized conveyance and deposit satisfy the discharge elements; II. Recapture requires the purpose and effects specified for the water area; 2002-12-16. Six direct joins support these components.
- Canonical Decision Record `Borden-Ranch-Partnership-v-Army-Corps-of-Engineers_merits_2002-12-16.md`, Public Projection / Holdings / III. Distinct proved discharges may be counted separately within a day; 2002-12-16. All eight participating Justices adopt the counting rule.

**Present operation effective:** Borden Ranch, December 16, 2002.

**Exceptions and limits:** Ordinary non-discharge cultivation, natural soil movement and activities remaining within an operative exemption are excluded. The decision does not equate every incidental fallback with an addition or decide jurisdiction over waters conceded to be covered. Its $25,000-per-day-for-each-violation ceiling applies to the conduct dates adjudicated; it does not supply the ceiling for a different statutory period.

### Downstream water-quality standards in federal discharge permitting

**Governing question:** Whether and how EPA may apply downstream state standards in an interstate federal discharge permit, including where receiving waters are already degraded.

**Current rule:** The Clean Water Act authorizes EPA under §402(a) and its valid regulations to require compliance with applicable downstream state water-quality standards; limits on the downstream State's own direct authority do not restrict that federal delegation. The Act does not categorically forbid a new discharge merely because effluent will reach already noncompliant waters irrespective of whether the discharge causes a detectable violation under applicable permit standards. For the federal interstate permit setting decided in *Arkansas*, federally approved downstream standards incorporated by valid regulation operate as federal permitting law, and EPA's reasonable, consistently held construction receives substantial deference. EPA permissibly construed Oklahoma's no-degradation requirement to prohibit an actual detectable or measurable violation, rather than every infinitesimal theoretical effect. This construction preserves enforceable standards and supplies no general tolerance for pollution labeled trivial.

**Authority by component:**

- Delegated downstream-protection authority: Arkansas v. Oklahoma — EPA may require downstream standards in this federal permit, February 26, 1992.
- Rejection of an already-degraded-waters categorical bar: Arkansas — Existing degradation is not a categorical statutory prohibition, February 26, 1992.
- Incorporated standards and permissible detectable-violation construction: Arkansas — Reasonable construction of the incorporated downstream standards, February 26, 1992. Stevens's unanimous opinion controls these components.
- Distinct administrative proof and unchanged judicial-review standards needed to apply that construction: Arkansas — Supported findings govern review of this permit, February 26, 1992; the same unanimous opinion.

**Present operation effective:** *Arkansas v. Oklahoma*, February 26, 1992.

**Exceptions and limits:** The no-detectable-violation showing in this permitting proceeding uses an administrative preponderance burden; reviewing courts apply whole-record substantial-evidence review rather than reweighing merely permissible alternative findings. Those standards retain their distinct functions. Existing degradation matters where the actual governing standards make it relevant; detectable violations, unreliable findings, and failure to apply valid standards remain reviewable.

**Open question:** The Court does not hold that Congress requires identical compliance in every federal or state permitting setting, construe state law for unrelated purposes, or decide independent source-state remedies.

**Holdings navigation:** Arkansas v. Oklahoma, 503 U.S. 91 (1992) — Federal permit interpretation and review; Arkansas v. Oklahoma, 503 U.S. 91 (1992) — Interstate water-pollution permits.

## Holdings

### I. The Act and valid regulations reach this connected system

**Controlling proposition:** The Clean Water Act and the Corps’ tributary and adjacent-wetland regulations cover these wetlands adjoining the demonstrated feeder system into navigable waters. The statutory program delegates authority exercised through binding regulations; the permissible inclusion of this connected system and the responsible Corps construction of tributaries as its feeder branches receive their respective controlling weight within statutory and regulatory limits.

**Authority:** Part I’s coverage conclusion has nine joins. Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer supply eight direct joins to the complete delegated-choice and regulatory-interpretation rationale; Stone-Zsela’s join is confined to the coverage conclusion.

**Controlling explanation:** Sections 1311, 1344 and 1362 establish the discharge prohibition, dredged-or-fill permit program and definition extending beyond navigability in fact. Section 1344 assigns the program to the Secretary of the Army acting through the Chief of Engineers, with structured permit, guideline, general-permit and state-administration provisions. Haggar and Mead require that actual assignment and its exercise before deference; Barnhart applies the same sequence in a different statutory program. Ambiguity or expertise alone does not confer authority.

The Corps reasonably includes this connected tributary system and its conceded adjacent wetlands under section 328.3(a)(1), (5) and (7). The latter provision covers wetlands adjacent to the identified waters other than waters themselves wetlands. Riverside Bayview preserves adjacent-wetland coverage. SWANCC’s bird-use-only isolated-pond limit does not impose the owners’ proposed direct-feeder boundary on this supported connection.

The distinct question about the regulation’s meaning receives Auer’s fair-and-considered-interpretation rule. Tributaries reasonably includes feeder branches reaching navigable water through intermediate streams; the responsible Corps construction is consistent with that text. Earlier primary, secondary and tertiary usage confirms the meaning. A former headwaters coverage cutoff did not establish that more remote branches were linguistically never tributaries. The direct-feeder alternative is plausible but not compelled. Neither form of deference proves hydrological facts, permits interpretive amendment or displaces the Government’s ordinary civil burdens. The actual route and conceded adjacency supply the application here, rather than environmental importance alone.

### II. Protection of the receiving navigable channel supports this application

**Controlling proposition:** Congress may prevent pollutant effects in an identified navigable waterway by regulating the established discharge class in its demonstrated tributary system and adjacent wetlands. The Government must support that connection and mechanism; once established, the channel ground requires neither an independently substantial effect from this individual fill nor tracing a particular particle into the receiving river.

**Authority:** Part II, joined by all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Lopez preserves direct protection of interstate channels and requires a real connection before aggregate magnitude can do work. Rio Grande Dam, Appalachian Electric Power, Oklahoma ex rel. Phillips and Grand River Dam support protection of navigable waters through relevant nonnavigable reaches; Caminetti recognizes protection against harmful channel uses. The power is not confined to clearing obstructions from boat passage.

The agreed drainage route connects the roadside ditch and adjacent wetlands to the Wicomico. The Corps supplied evidence that discharges into the connected tributaries and adjacent wetlands substantially affect receiving-water quality; the owners did not contend that the reported class effect was overstated. The approximately eight-mile route to the navigable river, intermediate ponds and dams, and disputed flow consistency therefore require attention but do not erase this supported connection. A particular parcel’s small contribution does not defeat prevention directed to that connected class.

The owners’ strongest objection is that remote drainage can turn channel protection into general land-use power. The answer is the established receiving channel, actual route and supported discharge mechanism, rather than a presumption that every ditch connects or national environmental concern. Home Builders retains a separate externality route. The externality route requires identification of the regulated class; a concrete cross-border ecological, commercial or comparable interstate effect; why that effect is substantial rather than speculative or merely nationally interesting; why the affected States cannot reasonably internalize or control it through ordinary territorial authority; and the reasonable fit of the federal prohibition to that effect. Drainage alone does not establish those conditions. The supported channel ground fully sustains this application.

### III. The root-zone indicator answers the preserved Manual objection

**Controlling proposition:** Section 328.3(b) requires inundation or saturation by surface or groundwater at a frequency and duration sufficient to support, and under normal circumstances actually supporting, a prevalence of vegetation typically adapted to saturated soils. Within that complete definition, the Manual’s express visual indicator of saturation in a major part of the root zone, usually within twelve inches, defeats the asserted requirement of saturation to the surface; the Corps’ determination is sustained on this preserved interpretation issue.

**Authority:** Part III, joined by all eight Associate Justices. Stone-Zsela concurs in affirmance of this component without adding a ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The 1987 Manual applies vegetation, hydric-soil and hydrology criteria, with recorded water data ranked above field indicators. Its specific root-zone indicator explains how saturation can have the overriding effect on vegetation relevant to wetland hydrology. The owners’ twenty-two-well monitoring argument relied on inadequate saturation to the surface. Reading the general surface description with the express indicator answers their contention that the Corps contradicted the Manual. Vegetation and hydric soil are undisputed, and the described challenge establishes no contrary root-zone measurement requiring this Court to resolve a factual conflict.

Auer permits the responsible, text-consistent regulatory explanation; it does not allow a scientific manual to amend the regulation or make one observation conclusive. Frequency, duration, normal circumstances and the actual vegetation criterion remain essential. Genuinely contrary higher-ranked data must retain their proper weight. The claim that the Manual itself is inconsistent with the regulation was not presented. Affirmance thus resolves the asserted misuse of its language while preserving the complete regulatory conditions.

### IV. Refilling reasonably restores the established violation’s effects

**Controlling proposition:** A restoration order must bear an equitable relationship to the actual violation, be practically achievable and serve restoration of the affected environment. The district court did not abuse its discretion by requiring refilling and previolation elevation rather than removal of the sidecast soil elsewhere, because the proposed removal would preserve the unlawful project’s drainage benefit and risk further ecological harm.

**Authority:** Part IV’s complete rationale has the eight Associate joins. All nine support the operative restoration mandate; Stone-Zsela’s agreement is confined to that operative remedial scope.

**Controlling explanation:** The owners emphasize that digging alone was not the established discharge and that hauling the soil to nonwetland land could have avoided a violation initially. But an option that would have avoided a violation before the work does not necessarily restore the affected wetlands afterward. The actual findings explain both the retained drainage benefit and the risk of further harm from the proposed removal. Those concrete reasons connect the ordered refilling to the previolation condition.

Cumberland Farms supplies persuasive considerations of environmental benefit, feasibility and equitable relation rather than a compulsory checklist. The Act’s restoration objective supports the properly bounded choice; it does not establish a separate prohibition on excavation or authority over unrelated land use. The existing order remains tied to this discharge and its effects, and ordinary enforcement must address any supported feasibility or compliance issue on its evidence.
