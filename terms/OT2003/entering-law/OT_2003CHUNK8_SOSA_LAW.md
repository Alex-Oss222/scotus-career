#### Abebe-Jira v. Negewo

**Docket or dockets:** No. 95-8873; Eleventh Circuit No. 93-9133.

**Decided:** January 14, 1997.

For the other questions in Abebe-Jira v. Negewo, No. 95-8873; Eleventh Circuit No. 93-9133, decided January 14, 1997, see Federal Courts - Law-of-nations jurisdiction and political questions (Jurisdiction does not itself establish a damages action; The foreign setting does not make this individual tort a political question).

##### A confined received action for personally committed official torture

**Holding:** Section 1350's reception of judicial adjudication of law-of-nations torts, read in its common-law setting, supports a confined domestic civil action against an individual for deliberate participation in or personal supervision of official torture. The actionable norm forbids severe physical or mental suffering intentionally inflicted under official authority for interrogation, punishment or intimidation; international law supplies that specific obligatory prohibition, while the received domestic action supplies authority to enforce it.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stevens's Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; seven Justices.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The actionable norm is confined to intentional official torture and personal participation or supervision. No action is adopted for every customary norm, private violence, corporate liability, absent-superior responsibility or every cruel, inhuman or degrading act. Official title alone is insufficient.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Suter and O'Melveny are distinguished at their source-specific limits; Central Bank and Musick, Peeler supply confined received-action analogies, not existing ATS holdings. Filartiga is persuasive lower-court authority for this established personal tort.

**Later-authority backlinks:** Kadic v. Karadzic; Doe v. Karadzic, April 21, 1997, resolves the reserved private-wrong question for defined personally perpetrated, planned or directed genocide and core war crimes; Abebe-Jira's official-torture action and remedy scope remain unchanged.

##### Compensation for proved injury within that action

**Holding:** The recognized action permits compensatory damages for proved injury caused by the individual's qualifying official torture. The established trial findings support categorical compensatory authority for each plaintiff, without substituting allegations or official position for personal responsibility and causation.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stevens's Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; seven Justices.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The grant concerns categorical compensatory authority, not an independent challenge to amount or allocation.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Franklin's action/remedy distinction is applied without turning its Title IX holding into an independent ATS cause or an amount-of-damages ruling.

##### Punitive authority requires an independently justified aggravated-tort remedy

**Holding:** Within this established action for intentionally inflicted official torture, traditional common-law remedies permit punitive damages for malicious, willful or equivalently reckless aggravated wrongdoing; punitive relief is permitted, not required in every case. The punitive culpability standard does not dilute the underlying action's intentional-official-torture predicate, and the proved deliberate personal torture supports punitive availability here while amount and lawful assessment remain distinct questions.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stevens's Part IV, joined in full by O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; six Justices support the complete culpability formulation. Stone-Zsela also joins punitive authority and its application to deliberate personal torture, producing seven joins at that scope, but not the equivalently reckless aggravation formulation.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Punitive aggravation does not relax the underlying intentional-torture requirement. No mandatory punitive award, universal international-tort remedy, wealth finding, ratio or amount-review formula is adopted; Franklin's punitive reservation and TXO/BMW constraints remain.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Arms supplies the traditional distinction between reparation and punishment for aggravated wrongdoing; Franklin's punitive reservation remains intact and is not treated as authorization. TXO and BMW remain controlling at their existing procedural and substantive scopes; no new excessiveness test is announced.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm both damages components for each plaintiff, 7-2. Each retains $200,000 compensatory and $300,000 punitive damages, $1.5 million in total, against the granted categorical objections; no amount reassessment or new TVPA claim is adjudicated.




#### Kadic v. Karadzic; Doe v. Karadzic

**Docket or dockets:** Second Circuit Nos. 94-9035 and 94-9069.
**Decided:** 1997-04-21.

For jurisdiction, political questions, invitee immunity and the remand limits, see Federal Courts — Law-of-nations jurisdiction and political questions, Kadic and Doe, April 21, 1997, these dockets.

##### A confined civil action reaches personally committed or directed genocide

**Holding:** Section 1350's reception of law-of-nations tort adjudication supports a domestic civil action against an individual for personally perpetrating, planning, or directing genocide, whether or not that individual acted under state authority. The plaintiff must establish intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial, or religious group as such, through killing members, causing serious bodily or mental harm, deliberately imposing conditions calculated to bring about the group's physical destruction in whole or in part, imposing measures intended to prevent births, or forcibly transferring children to another group, together with the defendant's qualifying responsibility, causation, and a lawful basis for relief.

**Proposition-level authority:** Ginsburg's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer; seven direct joins. Scalia and Thomas reject the additional domestic action.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Genocide Convention identifies the norm; neither it nor the criminal implementation chapter independently creates this civil action. Section 1092's no-right disclaimer for that chapter remains effective. Labels such as ethnic cleansing, murder or rape do not establish the defined acts, specific intent or personal responsibility; command title alone is insufficient.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Abebe-Jira's confined reception rationale is extended to this defined private wrong, resolving that reservation without changing its official-torture holding. Atherton and O'Melveny still reject common law based merely on a general federal interest; Central Bank is an analogy for bounded development, not an imported assistance rule.

##### The pleaded core war crimes do not require a recognized state actor

**Holding:** The same confined received domestic action reaches personally perpetrated, planned, or directed deliberate killing, torture, and grave sexual violence against protected persons as core war crimes, without requiring that the defendant act for a diplomatically recognized state. Each claim requires the relevant armed conflict, protected victim, prohibited act, connection between the act and the conflict, personal responsibility, causation, and independently supported relief; the Court recognizes no civil action for every breach of a treaty provision.

**Proposition-level authority:** Ginsburg's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer; seven direct joins. Scalia and Thomas reject the additional domestic action.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Common Article 3 binds each party to a noninternational armed conflict occurring in the territory of a High Contracting Party. It protects persons taking no active part in hostilities, including members of armed forces who have laid down their arms and persons out of combat through sickness, wounds, detention, or any other cause. It requires humane treatment without adverse distinction based on race, colour, religion or faith, sex, birth, wealth, or similar criteria. At any time and place it prohibits, against those persons, violence to life and person, especially murder, mutilation, cruel treatment and torture; hostage-taking; outrages upon personal dignity, especially humiliating and degrading treatment; and sentences or executions without a previous judgment by a regularly constituted court affording indispensable judicial guarantees. Its further commands and permissions remain distinct: the wounded and sick must be collected and cared for; an impartial humanitarian body may offer services; the parties should endeavour to put other Convention provisions into force through special agreements; application does not affect the parties' legal status. The civil holding concerns the pleaded core wrongs, not automatic enforcement of every one of these clauses. The Court does not determine Protocol II's customary status, alternative international-conflict theories or comprehensive command responsibility.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Abebe-Jira supplies the confined domestic reception source. Yamashita supports individual wartime obligations but supplies neither this civil action nor a decision on absent-superior responsibility; Central Bank's securities standard is not transferred.

##### Official torture and the enacted TVPA route retain their separate conditions

**Holding:** Abebe-Jira's action remains available for deliberate personal participation in or supervision of severe physical or mental suffering intentionally inflicted under official authority for interrogation, punishment, or intimidation; ordinary private torture or killing outside a proved genocide or war-crime setting requires an independently authorized route. The Torture Victim Protection Act separately authorizes damages against an individual who, under actual or apparent authority or color of law of a foreign nation, subjects an individual to the statute's defined torture or extrajudicial killing, subject to its beneficiaries, exhaustion requirement, ten-year limit, definitions, and exclusions stated here.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Ginsburg), unanimous at this proposition.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Torture damages run to the injured individual; killing damages run to the individual's legal representative or a person entitled to claim in a wrongful-death action. The court shall decline to hear a claim when adequate and available remedies in the place where the conduct occurred have not been exhausted. Suit must commence within ten years after the cause arose. Extrajudicial killing means a deliberated killing not authorized by a previous judgment of a regularly constituted court affording indispensable judicial guarantees; it excludes a killing lawfully carried out under foreign national authority under international law. Torture requires intentionally inflicted severe physical or mental pain or suffering against a person in the offender's custody or physical control, excluding pain arising only from, inherent in, or incidental to lawful sanctions. Its purposes include obtaining information or a confession from the victim or a third person; punishing the victim for an act the victim or a third person committed or is suspected of committing; intimidating or coercing the victim or a third person; and discrimination of any kind. Mental pain means prolonged mental harm caused by intentional or threatened severe physical pain; administration, application, or threatened use of mind-altering substances or procedures calculated to disrupt profoundly the senses or personality; threatened imminent death; or a threat that another person imminently will suffer death, severe physical pain or suffering, or those substances or procedures. The TVPA does not rewrite every element of Abebe-Jira's separate action. No universal §1331 customary-law action or additional ATS action for ordinary official execution is decided.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Abebe-Jira's official-torture action and remedy limits remain intact; Suter preserves the distinctions among obligation, action and relief.

##### Nonrecognition alone does not defeat an adequately alleged official connection

**Holding:** Diplomatic nonrecognition does not categorically foreclose proof of governmental attributes or concerted action with an existing foreign state where the applicable norm requires official action. The TVPA still requires actual or apparent authority or color of law attributable to a foreign nation; a claimed title or mere semblance of authority is insufficient by itself.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Ginsburg), unanimous at this proposition.

**Material application:** The allegations permit proof of governmental attributes or concerted action with Yugoslavia or Serbia; neither a claimed presidency nor rejection of categorical dismissal establishes the connection.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Lugar supplies a joint-government-action analogy without importing the whole §1983 attribution framework. Abebe-Jira's official-authority requirement continues for its own norm.

**Holding:** Section 1350 supplies district-court jurisdiction over an alien's civil tort action alleging a violation of the law of nations or a United States treaty; these substantial allegations of official torture fall within that jurisdiction. **Holding:** Adjudication of these individual official-torture claims is not categorically barred as a political question because it applies a determinate prohibition to personal conduct without deciding the legitimacy of Ethiopia's government, directing United States diplomacy or resolving a constitutionally committed war-powers dispute. A concrete immunity defense or contrary political-branch determination would require its own consideration; none is supplied or decided here.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stevens's Part V, unanimous; the jurisdictional and political-question agreements of Scalia and Thomas do not establish agreement on the action or damages.

#### Kadic v. Karadzic; Doe v. Karadzic

**Docket or dockets:** Second Circuit Nos. 94-9035 and 94-9069.
**Decided:** 1997-04-21.

**Companion or consolidated matters:** The consolidated review preserves each plaintiff group's distinct service circumstances. For the domestic causes of action, see Civil Rights — Law-of-nations personal tort actions, Kadic and Doe, April 21, 1997, these dockets.

##### Jurisdiction and the domestic action are separate questions

**Holding:** Section 1350 gives the district court jurisdiction over these substantial allegations by aliens of torts violating the law of nations; it imposes no universal official-action requirement on norms that bind private individuals. A federal forum, an applicable international norm, a domestic civil action, this defendant's responsibility, and authority for the requested remedy remain separate requirements.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Ginsburg), unanimous at this proposition.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Abebe-Jira's jurisdiction/action distinction and Suter's separation of obligation from action remain controlling.

##### The asserted foreign-affairs objection does not require dismissal

**Holding:** These defined claims against an individual are not categorically nonjusticiable merely because they arise during a foreign civil war or concern a political leader. Dismissal as a political question requires an identified constitutional commitment, absence of manageable judicial standards, or other concrete obstacle under Baker; none is established by this record.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Ginsburg), unanimous at this proposition.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The decision neither recognizes a foreign government nor establishes a worldwide forum duty. Act of state was not asserted below and is not decided; independently supported immunity and forum objections remain separate.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Baker and Abebe-Jira require an issue-specific political-question inquiry. Nixon's Senate impeachment-procedure holding supplies no categorical foreign-affairs exemption.

##### Invitee status supplies no general immunity; particular service questions remain

**Holding:** The United Nations Headquarters Agreement gives these invitees no general immunity from suit or from the reported service outside the headquarters district: §9's consent requirement for service inside that district, §15's privileges for designated member-state representatives, and §11's transit protections do not establish that asserted immunity. Kadic's court-authorized delivery through a security officer and admitted receipt permit continuation; Doe's intercepted hotel-lobby delivery, disputed distance, and remaining service issues require their own adjudication.

**Proposition-level authority:** Ginsburg's Part VII: unanimous rejection of general invitee immunity; eight Associates join the plaintiff-specific service rationale, with Stone-Zsela agreeing only in disposition.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Direct airport-to-headquarters transit is reserved. Acknowledged alternate-service receipt in Kadic does not decide contested delivery in Doe. No general exception to sovereign or diplomatic immunity for international torts is created.

##### Affirmance restores proceedings, not liability or an award

**Holding:** The Second Circuit's reversal of the blanket dismissal is affirmed, and both actions return for claim-specific service, factual, liability, and remedial adjudication under the surviving federal routes. Supplemental claims may receive renewed consideration because federal claims survive, but neither an exercise of supplemental jurisdiction nor any state-law judgment follows automatically.

**Proposition-level authority:** Seven Justices—Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer—support all surviving actions. All nine support ordinary proof and remedy limits; the eight Associates join the supplemental-claim rationale, while Stone-Zsela agrees only in that disposition.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No liability or award follows from the allegations. Abebe-Jira's punitive rule remains confined to proved official torture and is not automatically extended to genocide or war crimes; additional relief requires an independent legal source and predicate facts. Franklin's appropriate-relief rule presupposes an action and preserves its punitive reservation.




#### Smith v. United States (Antarctica), 507 U.S. 197 (1993)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 91-1538.

**Decided:** March 8, 1993.

##### The FTCA does not authorize this Antarctic tort action

**Holding:** The foreign-country exception in § 2680(k), read with the FTCA's enacted liability and place-of-law provisions, excludes this claim based entirely on acts or omissions in Antarctica even though no other sovereign is recognized there. Sections 1346(b) and 2674 do not authorize substituting the claimant's Oregon law for the law of the Antarctic place of the alleged negligence merely because she resides in Oregon; her valid residence venue does not supply the missing substantive consent.

**Proposition-level authority:** The statutory portion of White's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: eight Justices adopt this holding. Stevens dissents.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- United States v. Spelar, 338 U.S. 217 (1949): its territorial understanding of the exception is applied to Antarctica; its foreign-sovereignty facts do not fix an exclusive definition requiring another recognized sovereign.
- Richards v. United States, 369 U.S. 1 (1962): applied; the whole law of the place of the negligent act controls the statutory choice, and the claimant's residence does not authorize a different starting point.
- United States v. Kubrick, 444 U.S. 111 (1979): applied to respect the intended scope of the FTCA waiver without judicial expansion or contraction.
- Beattie v. United States, 756 F.2d 91 (D.C.Cir.1984): its conclusion permitting the Antarctic FTCA action is disapproved; the territorial/choice-of-law objection in Scalia's dissent is consistent with the adopted statutory construction.
- United States v. Nordic Village, February 25, 1992: preserved at its actual scope concerning clear consent to affirmative bankruptcy monetary relief; it does not independently decide this different express waiver.
- Molzof v. United States, January 14, 1992: preserved; its construction of punitive damages and independent local-law entitlement do not establish Antarctic territorial coverage.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The claim concerns acts wholly in Antarctica. No blanket rule forecloses every offshore FTCA, constitutional or independently authorized remedy. Extraterritoriality discussion is confirmatory, not an independently sufficient universal rule; maritime and other waivers remain distinct.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm dismissal under the foreign-country exception; no negligence determination or damages award.




#### United States v. Alvarez-Machain, 504 U.S. 655 (1992)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 91-712.
**Decided:** June 15, 1992.

##### Whether the Treaty’s allocation of surrender decisions to the requested State is operative law

**Holding:** The United States–Mexico Extradition Treaty is law of the United States under Article VI, and courts give its terms operative effect by reading them for their text, their context, the parties’ reciprocal obligations, their practice and the function each provision performs. Article 9 allocates to the requested State the decision whether to surrender its own nationals and, if it declines, to submit the case to its own prosecuting authorities. Courts neither manufacture restrictions the parties did not adopt nor read the Treaty to let one party nullify a decision it expressly reserves to the other.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part I of Stone-Zsela's opinion of the Court, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter; five direct joins. White, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas dissent from the 5–4 affirmance.

**Material application:** Article 9 reserves to Mexico the decision whether to surrender its national or submit the case to its own prosecutors. The Treaty's silence about abduction does not deprive that reservation of operative content.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The decision does not authorize abduction, require extradition for every international acquisition of custody, adjudicate guilt, or decide a constitutional, due-process, outrageous-conduct or customary-international-law bar to trial. Captures by private persons not attributable to the Government, voluntary surrender, lawful deportation and consensual transfers are judged under their own law, and Ker–Frisbie remains the default outside the stated predicates. No general private damages action under treaties is created, and courts do not supervise diplomacy. The independently rejected outrageous-conduct theory is not reconsidered, and no separate Verdugo-Urquidez docket is adjudicated.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The Ninth Circuit's judgment sustaining the treaty bar to trial, the discharge and the repatriation order is affirmed, 5–4; the prosecution founded on the custody obtained through the seizure ends. The district order had discharged respondent and ordered repatriation without separately dismissing the indictment; the affirmance creates no district dismissal order, dismissal with prejudice, acquittal or permanent immunity, and extradition or another transfer Mexico authorizes remains available.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Air France v. Saks, 470 U.S. 392 (1985), and Valentine v. United States ex rel. Neidecker, 299 U.S. 5 (1936): applied; interpretation begins with the Treaty’s terms read in context, and no obligation is supplied that the parties did not adopt.
- Chan v. Korean Air Lines, Ltd., 490 U.S. 122 (1989): applied as a limit; courts do not insert a missing sanction from purpose alone.
- United States v. Rauscher, 119 U.S. 407 (1886): applied; an obligation necessarily implied by a treaty’s text and structure is enforceable law.

##### Whether the United States–Mexico Extradition Treaty bars prosecution following this officially sponsored, protested abduction

**Holding:** Where a treaty allocates to the other party the decision whether to surrender a person, the United States may not circumvent that allocation through a forcible seizure attributable to it in the other party’s territory, without that party’s consent and over its formal objection, and then prosecute the person on the custody so obtained. The bar applies only when five predicates are met: (1) the Treaty allocates the surrender decision, as Article 9 does for a party’s nationals; (2) the seizure is attributable to the United States Government; (3) it occurs in the other party’s territory; (4) that party did not consent and formally objected; and (5) the prosecution depends on custody obtained through that conduct. Extradition is not the exclusive lawful means of acquiring custody: voluntary surrender, lawful deportation, consensual transfers and conduct of private persons not attributable to the Government are judged under their own law. Each predicate is met on this record.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part II of Stone-Zsela's opinion of the Court, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter; five direct joins. White, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas dissent from the 5–4 affirmance.

**Material application:** Persons paid to act at DEA behest seized Alvarez-Machain in Guadalajara and brought him to the United States; official responsibility does not depend on whether regular DEA personnel performed the seizure. Mexico did not consent, formally protested and consistently demanded his return. No extradition proceeding under the Treaty produced the transfer, and the prosecution depends on the custody so obtained.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The decision does not authorize abduction, require extradition for every international acquisition of custody, adjudicate guilt, or decide a constitutional, due-process, outrageous-conduct or customary-international-law bar to trial. Captures by private persons not attributable to the Government, voluntary surrender, lawful deportation and consensual transfers are judged under their own law, and Ker–Frisbie remains the default outside the stated predicates. No general private damages action under treaties is created, and courts do not supervise diplomacy. The independently rejected outrageous-conduct theory is not reconsidered, and no separate Verdugo-Urquidez docket is adjudicated.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The Ninth Circuit's judgment sustaining the treaty bar to trial, the discharge and the repatriation order is affirmed, 5–4; the prosecution founded on the custody obtained through the seizure ends. The district order had discharged respondent and ordered repatriation without separately dismissing the indictment; the affirmance creates no district dismissal order, dismissal with prejudice, acquittal or permanent immunity, and extradition or another transfer Mexico authorizes remains available.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Ker v. Illinois, 119 U.S. 436 (1886): ordinary rule retained; distinguished, because the kidnapper there acted without governmental authority and no government circumvented an applicable treaty’s allocation. To the extent Ker could be read to govern such a circumvention, it is narrowed to that boundary; it is not treated as affirmative authorization to seize.
- Frisbie v. Collins, 342 U.S. 519 (1952): preserved; it involved no treaty, and its distinction between unlawful capture and the power to conduct a lawful trial remains the default outside the stated predicates.
- United States v. Rauscher, 119 U.S. 407 (1886): extended from limits that follow an actual surrender to the surrender decision the Treaty reserves before any surrender.
- Cook v. United States, 288 U.S. 102 (1933): applied; a treaty limit on the Government’s own power of seizure binds courts, and the Government may not rely on what it obtained by exceeding it.
- Chan v. Korean Air Lines, Ltd., 490 U.S. 122 (1989): distinguished; no sanction is inserted from purpose alone, because the limit follows from the allocation Article 9 makes and is confined to its five predicates.

##### Whether the defendant may invoke the violation in his own prosecution

**Holding:** A defendant prosecuted on custody obtained in violation of the Treaty’s allocation may invoke the violation defensively in that prosecution when the contracting State whose rights were infringed formally objects. The holding creates no general private action for damages under treaties and does not authorize courts to supervise the conduct of diplomacy.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part III of Stone-Zsela's opinion of the Court, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter; five direct joins. White, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas dissent from the 5–4 affirmance.

**Material application:** Mexico formally objected: its protest was prompt and unequivocal and demanded return, so Alvarez-Machain may invoke the bar defensively. The Government's argument that only the offended sovereign may enforce the Treaty is answered by that condition rather than by excluding the defendant.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The decision does not authorize abduction, require extradition for every international acquisition of custody, adjudicate guilt, or decide a constitutional, due-process, outrageous-conduct or customary-international-law bar to trial. Captures by private persons not attributable to the Government, voluntary surrender, lawful deportation and consensual transfers are judged under their own law, and Ker–Frisbie remains the default outside the stated predicates. No general private damages action under treaties is created, courts do not supervise diplomacy, and whether a treaty confers enforceable individual rights in other settings is not decided. The independently rejected outrageous-conduct theory is not reconsidered, and no separate Verdugo-Urquidez docket is adjudicated.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The Ninth Circuit's judgment sustaining the treaty bar to trial, the discharge and the repatriation order is affirmed, 5–4; the prosecution founded on the custody obtained through the seizure ends. The district order had discharged respondent and ordered repatriation without separately dismissing the indictment; the affirmance creates no district dismissal order, dismissal with prejudice, acquittal or permanent immunity, and extradition or another transfer Mexico authorizes remains available.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- United States v. Rauscher, 119 U.S. 407 (1886): applied; a defendant may invoke a treaty limit in his own prosecution.
- Cook v. United States, 288 U.S. 102 (1933): applied; a private litigant may rely on a treaty limit on the Government’s seizure in the proceeding the Government brings.
- Ker v. Illinois, 119 U.S. 436 (1886): distinguished; it presented no government-directed circumvention of an applicable treaty for a defendant to invoke.

##### What remedy follows from the violation

**Holding:** The remedy for the violation is to end the prosecution founded on the unlawfully acquired custody and to restore the position the Treaty protects: Alvarez-Machain is discharged from that custody and returned to Mexico. The remedy is not an acquittal, a dismissal with prejudice, a finding of guilt or innocence, or damages, and it confers no permanent immunity. The United States may seek his surrender through extradition under the Treaty or another transfer Mexico authorizes, and this holding does not bar a prosecution on custody so obtained.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part IV of Stone-Zsela's opinion of the Court, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter; five direct joins. White, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas dissent from the 5–4 affirmance.

**Material application:** Alvarez-Machain is discharged from the custody obtained through the seizure and returned to Mexico, which may surrender him, submit the case to its own prosecutors under Article 9 or decline. The allegations of participation in the charged crimes remain unadjudicated.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The decision does not authorize abduction, require extradition for every international acquisition of custody, adjudicate guilt, or decide a constitutional, due-process, outrageous-conduct or customary-international-law bar to trial. Captures by private persons not attributable to the Government, voluntary surrender, lawful deportation and consensual transfers are judged under their own law, and Ker–Frisbie remains the default outside the stated predicates. No general private damages action under treaties is created, and courts do not supervise diplomacy. The independently rejected outrageous-conduct theory is not reconsidered, and no separate Verdugo-Urquidez docket is adjudicated.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The Ninth Circuit's judgment sustaining the treaty bar to trial, the discharge and the repatriation order is affirmed, 5–4; the prosecution founded on the custody obtained through the seizure ends. The district order had discharged respondent and ordered repatriation without separately dismissing the indictment; the affirmance creates no district dismissal order, dismissal with prejudice, acquittal or permanent immunity, and extradition or another transfer Mexico authorizes remains available.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Cook v. United States, 288 U.S. 102 (1933): applied; the Government may not retain the benefit of a seizure that exceeded its treaty authority.
- Frisbie v. Collins, 342 U.S. 519 (1952): preserved; outside the treaty circumvention addressed here, an unlawful capture does not by itself require release.
- United States v. Rauscher, 119 U.S. 407 (1886): applied by analogy; a treaty limit on prosecution operates on the Government’s authority to proceed and does not adjudicate the charged conduct.




## Standards and Tests selection: Received individual actions for law-of-nations torts

### Received individual actions for law-of-nations torts

**Governing question:** Domestic civil authority for an alien’s official-torture claim, distinct from §1350 jurisdiction.

**Current rule:** Section 1350 grants jurisdiction over an alien’s civil tort claim for a law-of-nations or United States treaty violation; jurisdiction alone does not create a damages action. Its received common-law setting supports a confined domestic action against an individual who deliberately participates in or personally supervises official torture: severe physical or mental suffering intentionally inflicted under official authority for interrogation, punishment or intimidation. The specific obligatory international prohibition and the received domestic enforcement action perform distinct functions. Compensation requires proved injury caused by qualifying personal conduct. Within that established intentional-torture action, punitive damages may be available for malicious, willful or equivalently reckless aggravated wrongdoing; that remedial culpability rule does not relax the underlying intentional-torture predicate or make punitive relief mandatory. Section 1350 jurisdiction, the international norm, a domestic cause of action, personal responsibility, causation and lawful relief are distinct. Its confined received civil action reaches an individual who personally perpetrates, plans or directs genocide, without an official-action requirement: intent to destroy in whole or part a national, ethnical, racial or religious group as such must accompany killing members, serious bodily or mental harm, deliberately imposed conditions calculated to cause the group's physical destruction in whole or part, measures intended to prevent births, or forcible transfer of children to another group. The action also reaches personally perpetrated, planned or directed deliberate killing, torture and grave sexual violence as core war crimes, requiring armed conflict, a protected victim, a prohibited act and the act's connection to the conflict. Diplomatic nonrecognition alone does not foreclose proof of governmental attributes or concerted state action where the applicable norm requires official authority.

**Authority by component:**

- Jurisdiction distinguished from an action: Abebe-Jira v. Negewo — Abebe-Jira v. Negewo — I. Jurisdiction does not itself establish a damages action; unanimous, January 14, 1997.
- Received action and compensation: Abebe-Jira v. Negewo — II. A confined received action for personally committed official torture and Abebe-Jira v. Negewo — III. Compensation for proved injury within that action; Stevens, Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

## Standards and Tests selection: Antarctic claims under the FTCA foreign-country exception

### Antarctic claims under the FTCA foreign-country exception

**Governing question:** What instructions govern antarctic claims under the FTCA foreign-country exception?

**Current rule:** Section 2680(k), read with the FTCA's liability and place-of-law provisions, excludes claims entirely based on Antarctic acts or omissions despite absence of another recognized sovereign. Sections 1346(b) and 2674 do not substitute the claimant's home-state law merely because residence supplies proper venue. No universal territorial bar to constitutional, maritime or other statutory channels follows; those waivers retain their terms.

### Smith v. United States (Antarctica), No. 91-1538

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing or portion | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Exact scope |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court: territorial exception and place-of-law structure | White | Stone, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Eight affirm | The complete controlling holding below; valid Oregon venue acknowledged |
| Opinion of the Court: additional extraterritorial-presumption discussion | White | Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Seven affirm | Confirmatory interpretive reason for this FTCA Antarctic claim; no absolute all-offshore bar |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Stone | No other Justice | Affirms and joins statutory portion only | Rejects the proposed domiciliary-law substitution under the enacted structure; does not join additional presumption discussion |
| Dissent | Stevens | No other Justice | Would reverse/remand | Exception confined to foreign sovereign territory, domestic conflicts route available, maritime history against absolute territorial premise |



#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stone concurs in the statutory portion and the judgment. Oregon residence supplies venue, and Oregon tort law is a possible proposed rule rather than a factual impossibility. The defect is that the FTCA selects the whole law where the negligent acts occurred and supplies no direction to substitute the claimant's domiciliary law for this Antarctic claim. Stone accepts the territorial reading through Spelar and that enacted structure. He does not join extraterritoriality as an additional interpretive reason, emphasizing that the maritime history prevents using offshore location alone as an absolute bar.

Stevens dissents. He reads foreign country to mean territory under another sovereign and regards Spelar's avoidance of foreign-law liability as the reason for that exception. Antarctica does not present that problem. Yellow Cab's broad-waiver approach and the private-person language of § 2674 support asking whether a private defendant would be liable under ordinary conflicts principles, which can point to Oregon law for its resident's claim. He invokes United Continental Tuna's account of maritime claims to reject an exclusively domestic understanding of the original FTCA waiver. A place without its own sovereign can still be subject to legal duties. Because Smith has Oregon venue, hypothetical nonresident plaintiffs' venue difficulties cannot justify foreclosing her suit. He would reverse and permit the claim to proceed under an appropriate legal rule, without adjudging negligence or damages.

#### Separate Writings

Justice Scalia, joined by Justice Thomas, concurs in Parts I and V and dissents from the damages judgment. They accept the statutory forum and reject a categorical political-question bar, but find no sufficient congressional authority for the asserted domestic damages action. The prohibition of torture does not itself authorize a federal cause of action. A forum for independently actionable law-of-nations torts does not necessarily authorize judicial recognition of every later norm; the common-law setting and lower-court practice are the strongest affirmative arguments, but do not establish the needed source for these Justices.

Suter requires an actual source of private liability; O'Melveny rejects the manufacture of a federal rule from general federal interests; Meyer keeps jurisdiction and substantive action distinct. Scalia and Thomas would reverse the damages judgment and remand on the absence-of-action ground, rejecting both compensation and punishment through that ground. They announce no categorical objection to punitive damages in an otherwise authorized intentional-tort action, do not approve torture, and do not reach an unpleaded TVPA claim, limitations or appointed-counsel question.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Scalia, joined by Justice Thomas, concurs in the jurisdictional, justiciability, general-immunity, and preserved-route rulings but dissents from recognition of the two additional received civil actions. They accept that genocide and core war crimes can impose duties on private persons. Their objection concerns domestic authority to enforce those duties through the new damages actions. They neither seek restoration of blanket jurisdictional dismissal nor deny international obligations, and they announce no categorical rule against relief in a properly authorized action.
