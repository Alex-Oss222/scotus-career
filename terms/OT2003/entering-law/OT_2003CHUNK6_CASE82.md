## Standards and Tests selection: Diversity cured before trial after erroneous removal

### Diversity cured before trial after erroneous removal

**Governing question:** Effect of a preserved removal error when complete diversity actually exists before trial and through judgment.

**Current rule:** Absent complete diversity at removal, a timely remand motion requires remand under the applicable statutes. A later lawful dismissal of the nondiverse party does not validate the earlier refusal, waive the objection or require extraordinary pretrial review. If actual §1332 diversity arises before trial and continues through judgment, that earlier defect alone does not require vacating the completed federal judgment. Actual jurisdiction at trial and judgment is indispensable; consent, efficiency and finality cannot supply it.

**Authority by component:**

- Earlier statutory duty: Caterpillar Inc. v. Lewis — Caterpillar Inc. v. Lewis — The erroneous refusal to remand remains error; bounded judgment-preservation rule: Caterpillar Inc. v. Lewis — Actual jurisdiction before trial preserves the completed judgment against this earlier defect. O’Connor, all nine Justices, December 10, 1996.

**Present operation effective:** Caterpillar, December 10, 1996.

**Exceptions and limits:** No universal cure for removal defects, collusive diversity manufacture or collateral review of an entered covered remand is authorized. Independently available fees, sanctions and ordinary merits appeals retain their own requirements.

**Holdings navigation:** Caterpillar Inc. v. Lewis (1996-12-10) — The erroneous refusal to remand remains error; Caterpillar Inc. v. Lewis (1996-12-10) — Actual jurisdiction before trial preserves the completed judgment against this earlier defect.

#### Caterpillar Inc. v. Lewis

**Docket or dockets:** No. 95-1263.

**Decided:** December 10, 1996.

##### The erroneous refusal to remand remains error

**Holding:** Complete diversity was absent when Caterpillar removed, and Lewis's timely motion required remand under the applicable removal and remand statutes. The later dismissal of the nondiverse party does not make the earlier ruling correct, waive Lewis's preserved objection, or require him to have sought extraordinary pretrial relief.

**Proposition-level authority:** O'Connor's Part I, unanimous.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No collateral challenge to an entered covered remand is authorized. No late alternative removal argument or independent fee or sanction entitlement is decided.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Things Remembered and Quackenbush retain the mandatory-remand and covered-remand-review rules at their proper stages. This case concerns an erroneous refusal to remand followed by trial, not review of an actually entered remand protected by section 1447(d).

##### Actual jurisdiction before trial preserves the completed judgment against this earlier defect

**Holding:** When complete diversity actually arises through the nondiverse party's lawful dismissal before trial and persists through judgment, an earlier erroneous refusal of a timely remand motion does not by itself require vacating the completed federal judgment. The rule depends on actual section 1332 jurisdiction during trial and judgment; consent, finality or efficiency cannot cure a judgment entered without that jurisdiction.

**Proposition-level authority:** O'Connor's Part II, unanimous.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No universal cure for every removal defect follows. The decision authorizes no collusive manufacture of diversity, finds no manipulation, and does not extinguish a separately authorized fee or sanction request.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Finn's defect persisting through judgment is distinguished from actual diversity before trial; its bar on jurisdiction by consent survives. Grubbs's completed-judgment distinction is expressly extended beyond its unobjected-removal facts to this preserved objection and actual pretrial cure. Newman-Green retains lawful dismissal, dispensability and prejudice limits without authorizing manufactured trial jurisdiction. Willy preserves independently authorized collateral consequences but supplies no merits jurisdiction or cure for an uncured trial.

##### Restoration against jurisdictional vacatur leaves the ordinary appeal for decision

**Holding:** The Sixth Circuit's jurisdiction-based vacatur and state-court-remand direction are reversed; the district-court judgment is restored against that jurisdictional ground, subject to the unresolved ordinary appeal. The case returns to the Sixth Circuit to decide the five preserved issues it did not reach: prior-accident discovery restrictions, the discovery-sanction request, excluded exhibits, excluded witnesses and the omitted failure-to-warn instruction.

**Proposition-level authority:** O'Connor's Part III, joined by all seven other Associates; eight Justices. Stone-Zsela agrees only with this remand disposition.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court does not finally affirm the defense verdict against the five remaining challenges or decide any of their merits.

### Kontrick v. Ryan — 2004-01-14

The specified bankruptcy filing and enlargement rules remain mandatory procedural prescriptions, but their protection is lost when a debtor first invokes untimeliness after adjudication of the discharge objection's merits. Statutory subject-matter jurisdiction survives that filing defect, and a timely invoked objection continues to receive the rules' prescribed treatment.

Public procedure: The denial of discharge stands, and the bankruptcy court may continue administering the estate with that judgment in force.

28 U.S.C. section 1332, United States Code, 2003 edition. Official enacted-text reading copy.

§1332. Diversity of citizenship; amount in controversy; costs

(a) The district courts shall have original jurisdiction of all civil actions where the matter in controversy exceeds the sum or value of $75,000, exclusive of interest and costs, and is between—

(1) citizens of different States;

(2) citizens of a State and citizens or subjects of a foreign state;

(3) citizens of different States and in which citizens or subjects of a foreign state are additional parties; and

(4) a foreign state, defined in section 1603(a) of this title, as plaintiff and citizens of a State or of different States.

For the purposes of this section, section 1335, and section 1441, an alien admitted to the United States for permanent residence shall be deemed a citizen of the State in which such alien is domiciled.

(b) Except when express provision therefor is otherwise made in a statute of the United States, where the plaintiff who files the case originally in the Federal courts is finally adjudged to be entitled to recover less than the sum or value of $75,000, computed without regard to any setoff or counterclaim to which the defendant may be adjudged to be entitled, and exclusive of interest and costs, the district court may deny costs to the plaintiff and, in addition, may impose costs on the plaintiff.

(c) For the purposes of this section and section 1441 of this title—

(1) a corporation shall be deemed to be a citizen of any State by which it has been incorporated and of the State where it has its principal place of business, except that in any direct action against the insurer of a policy or contract of liability insurance, whether incorporated or unincorporated, to which action the insured is not joined as a party-defendant, such insurer shall be deemed a citizen of the State of which the insured is a citizen, as well as of any State by which the insurer has been incorporated and of the State where it has its principal place of business; and

(2) the legal representative of the estate of a decedent shall be deemed to be a citizen only of the same State as the decedent, and the legal representative of an infant or incompetent shall be deemed to be a citizen only of the same State as the infant or incompetent.

(d) The word “States”, as used in this section, includes the Territories, the District of Columbia, and the Commonwealth of Puerto Rico.

(June 25, 1948, ch. 646, 62 Stat. 930; July 26, 1956, ch. 740, 70 Stat. 658; Pub. L. 85–554, §2, July 25, 1958, 72 Stat. 415; Pub. L. 88–439, §1, Aug. 14, 1964, 78 Stat. 445; Pub. L. 94–583, §3, Oct. 21, 1976, 90 Stat. 2891; Pub. L. 100–702, title II, §§201(a), 202(a), 203(a), Nov. 19, 1988, 102 Stat. 4646; Pub. L. 104–317, title II, §205(a), Oct. 19, 1996, 110 Stat. 3850.)
