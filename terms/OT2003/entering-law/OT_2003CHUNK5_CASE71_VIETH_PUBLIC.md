# Completed public law effective April 28, 2004

Source: Vieth v. Jubelirer, No. 02-1580. Exact bounded Public Projection copied from its completed Record. Read as law effective before the April 29 Martinez event.

## Event

**Case:** Vieth v. Jubelirer, No. 02-1580.
**Event:** Merits decision, April 28, 2004; argued December 10, 2003.
**Route and judgment below:** Direct appeal from the three-judge District Court for the Middle District of Pennsylvania, 241 F. Supp. 2d 478, dismissing the partisan challenge to Act 34; the population issue was separately resolved.
**Questions:** Whether partisan districting is justiciable, what personal constitutional injury and proof are required, and whether projected seat-vote disproportionality independently violates Article I.
**Posture:** Act 34 replaced Act 1 and is the operative congressional plan. Act 1 had been held malapportioned and was used in 2002 under a stay. The renewed Act 34 challenge presents a dismissal record.

Length designation: Extended controlling explanations for Holding I, equal electoral treatment supplies a justiciable constitutional rule, and Holding II, political capacity, material impairment, prohibited purpose and causation: up to 1,000 words each. Other controlling explanations: up to 350 words each.

## Participation

Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The Court vacates the partisan equal-protection dismissal and remands for amendment and proceedings under the announced standard. It affirms rejection of the independent Article I seat-ratio theory.

## Holdings

### I. Equal electoral treatment supplies a justiciable constitutional rule

**Holding and operative rule:** The Equal Protection Clause prohibits the State from selecting district boundaries to impose a material electoral disadvantage on identified voters because of their political affiliation or activity. The protected interest is equal treatment of an existing or demonstrably attainable political capacity. It is not an entitlement to a particular constituency, probability of victory, number of seats or proportional statewide delegation. Liability requires each element in Part II and remains subject to the State's same-decision defense in Part III.

**Authority:** Parts I–III and V of Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's opinion are joined by Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. All five adopt the complete substantive rule, proof requirements, burdens, defense and remand. Part IV is unanimous.

**Controlling explanation:** Baker v. Carr requires a claim-specific inquiry into judicially manageable legal standards. Davis v. Bandemer preserves the justiciability of partisan districting claims. The rule announced here identifies discriminatory treatment causing a practical impairment, rather than asking a court to select a fair allocation of political power or determine how much intentional partisan discrimination is tolerable.

Shaw v. Reno, Johnson v. Miller and Cano v. Davis require an injury independent of district design. Page v. Bartels and Robertson v. Bartels distinguish actual political activity from an expectation of favorable constituents or incumbent support. The Court extends those principles to intentional partisan impairment. The earlier decisions did not themselves establish this complete constitutional claim. A party label, irregular shape or partisan design alone remains insufficient, but an actual impairment need not await an electoral defeat.

Political awareness remains permissible. Officials may account for population equality, geography, statutory obligations, genuine communities, continuity of representation and independently justified representative accommodation. Gaffney v. Cummings preserves a genuine policy of political fairness; it does not confer immunity on intentional disadvantage imposed on voters because they support opponents. A fairness explanation must be independently legitimate and genuinely applied. Bipartisan agreement is no categorical defense to targeted impairment of challengers, minor parties or other identified voters.

The equal-protection inquiry is informed by the First Amendment's protection of political belief, expression and association. Those guarantees explain why political affiliation cannot supply an independent justification for the material electoral disadvantage defined here. Police Department of Chicago v. Mosley decided an equal-protection claim in light of closely connected First Amendment interests; districting nonetheless requires its own showing of injury and is not equivalent to excluding speech from a forum. The absence of an entitlement to a particular constituency does not authorize government to impose that disadvantage because voters support its opponents. Political awareness and independently legitimate representative choices remain permissible. The claimant must still establish the identified capacity, material impairment, prohibited purpose and causal connection required by this opinion, subject to the State's same-decision defense. The Court decides the equal-protection claim presented and leaves any independent First Amendment claim for determination when properly presented.

The distinction concerns the purpose and operation of the governmental choice, not whether officials describe it as advantage, accommodation or suppression. An effort to entrench power may be powerful evidence, but statewide entrenchment and total exclusion are not additional elements. A discriminatory electoral injury does not become lawful because the affected voters retain some chance to win.

### II. Political capacity, material impairment, prohibited purpose and causation

**Holding and operative rule:** A claimant must establish each of the following four elements. Evidence relevant to one does not substitute for another.

1. **Identified political capacity.** Identify the affected voters and an existing or demonstrably attainable practical capacity to organize, participate, nominate or elect. An electoral claim requires evidence of the voters' capacity to translate their support into representation under lawful conditions, including through a supported emerging coalition. Prior victory is unnecessary. Party identity, a preferred incumbent, a desired additional seat or a favorable hypothetical result alone is insufficient.
2. **Material impairment.** Establish a concrete and practically significant reduction of that capacity through an identified districting operation. Materiality excludes nominal, speculative and practically insignificant effects; it does not require destruction of every realistic chance of success or authorize a permissible amount of deliberate discrimination. A lost election, complete political exclusion, a particular vote share and statewide entrenchment are unnecessary. An unfavorable result or the availability of an easier election alone does not establish the impairment.
3. **Prohibited purpose.** Establish that imposing the identified electoral disadvantage on the affected voters because of their political affiliation or activity was a substantial motivating purpose of the challenged decision. The purpose need be neither exclusive nor predominant. Knowledge of political consequences or an incidental partisan benefit is insufficient. Deliberately weakening the identified voters because they support political opponents is not an independently lawful purpose merely because officials call it partisan advantage or incumbent protection.
4. **Boundary-specific causation.** Establish that the challenged assignments materially contributed to the identified impairment, using an independently justified lawful comparison to distinguish the boundaries' effect from residential geography, changes in voter support, candidate circumstances and other relevant conditions. The boundaries need not be the sole cause. This initial causal showing concerns what the boundaries did; it does not require the claimant to disprove every independently lawful reason for the government's decision before the State's same-decision burden arises.

**Authority:** The five-Justice majority joins every element and qualification.

**Controlling explanation:** Reynolds v. Sims and White v. Regester support practical political participation without creating proportional party representation. Cano, Page and Robertson require an identified activity or electoral capacity and an operation that actually impairs it. The Court does not equate unfavorable electoral prospects with a constitutional violation. The question is whether the State discriminatorily imposed a demonstrated practical impairment, measured against independently justified lawful conditions.

Materiality is a demonstrated, nontrivial reduction in the identified voters' effective ability to organize, nominate or elect preferred representatives, assessed under ordinary electoral conditions. The impairment need not eliminate a realistic possibility of victory. A change in predicted vote share, without reliable evidence of its practical electoral consequences, does not independently establish materiality. Proof and severity are distinct: strong evidence of a trivial disadvantage does not establish material impairment, and a potentially substantial disadvantage cannot support liability while its existence or causation remains speculative.

A reduction in projected support from 51 percent to 47 percent is neither automatically actionable nor automatically harmless. A reliably demonstrated material impairment deliberately imposed because of political affiliation can satisfy the rule even if the district remains competitive. The same numerical change attributable to independently lawful requirements or ordinary changes in voter support does not establish that violation. A larger numerical change likewise cannot substitute for proof of the protected capacity, lawful comparison, prohibited purpose and causation. These figures illustrate the rule; they are not findings about Act 34 or constitutional thresholds.

Packing can impair electoral effectiveness even when the affected voters continue to elect a preferred candidate. Such a claim must demonstrate the affected voters' actual impairment through the concentration and a feasible arrangement of the affected districts; a request for an additional favorable seat is insufficient. A claim about organizing, participation or nomination instead requires proof of the activity actually disrupted. Electoral disappointment cannot be relabeled organizational injury without that proof.

Arlington Heights supplies contextual evaluation of purpose. Mapmaking instructions, precinct selections, treatment of comparable communities, rejected alternatives, contemporaneous explanations and the sequence of decisions may establish purposeful disadvantage. No confession, predominant-motive showing or mandatory category of evidence is required. Conversely, an overall partisan benefit does not establish that the challenged assignments were selected to impose the identified impairment on the identified voters.

### III. Comparison, administration, ordinary proof and the State's defense

**Holding and operative rule:** The claimant bears persuasion by a preponderance of the evidence on all four elements. Once that showing is made, the State bears persuasion by the same standard that materially the same lawful districting decision would have occurred without the prohibited purpose. The State must establish its actual independent explanation, not a merely conceivable justification or the existence of some other lawful map. A purpose to secure power by materially weakening the identified voters because of their affiliation cannot itself establish the defense. If the defense fails, balancing additional partisan benefits against the proved injury cannot excuse the violation.

A preponderance means more likely true than not. That burden concerns proof of the legal elements; it does not require a greater-than-50-percent probability that the affected voters will win an election. No fixed vote share, electoral-success rate, partisan-symmetry score, seat-vote disparity or statistical significance level supplies a substantive constitutional threshold.

**Authority:** The same five Justices adopt the complete comparison requirement, proof sequence, administrative instructions and defense.

**Comparison and administration:**

1. **Define the injury and operation.** Identify the affected voters, the political capacity at issue and the assignments alleged to impair it. Personal injury, traceability and likely redress must also satisfy standing. A statewide partisan grievance cannot substitute for those showings.
2. **Specify the lawful conditions of comparison.** Identify mandatory population, geographic and statutory requirements and the State's genuinely pursued, independently permissible representative priorities. Explain the priorities and any material tradeoffs independently of the desired partisan result. The court must examine whether asserted criteria or their application are discriminatory pretexts. Neither the State nor the claimant may define the comparison simply by selecting a preferred party outcome.
3. **Demonstrate feasibility when the claim depends on different boundaries.** Establish that the proposed configuration can operate lawfully, including consequential changes in neighboring districts and compliance with independent voting protections. A local comparison may suffice; a broader plan is required when necessary to establish feasibility. The comparison cannot cure the claimed injury by reproducing an unlawful impairment elsewhere. No particular number of maps or complete statewide alternative is invariably required.
4. **Explain the electoral consequence.** Use reliable evidence suited to the asserted capacity, including actual organizing or nomination activity and, where relevant, voting behavior, turnout, crossover support, candidate effects and the applicable election rules. Disclose material assumptions and uncertainty and address whether reasonable variations undermine the asserted impairment. No named model, statistical ensemble, expert or completed election is universally mandatory. Multiple lawful comparisons may strengthen proof; the availability of one favorable map does not by itself prove discrimination or entitlement.
5. **Make distinct findings.** Resolve each element, the legal and practical adequacy of the comparison and any same-decision defense. Evidence may overlap, but the findings must identify what establishes capacity, practical impairment, purpose and causal connection. A conclusion that a map is unfair, unusual or insufficiently competitive cannot replace those determinations.

The comparator establishes factual capacity, impairment and causation within the defined constitutional claim. It does not become the constitutionally required map merely because it supports those findings. Equally, the claimant need not identify a uniquely ideal map or disprove every lawful configuration. For a claim based on actual organizing or nomination activity, reliable evidence may establish the relevant lawful comparison without a complete substitute plan; when relief or causation depends on different assignments, their feasibility still must be shown.

**Controlling explanation:** Holder v. Hall supplies comparison discipline by analogy, not the statutory elements of its distinct cause. Miller and Easley v. Cromartie permit varied evidence while preserving the proof required by the actual claim. Brooks v. Barnes and Montiel v. Davis distinguish a substantially motivating prohibited purpose from the government's proof that the same lawful decision would have occurred. Mt. Healthy supplies that mixed-motive sequence, and Crawford-El preserves ordinary proof rather than a newly invented heightened standard.

The claimant initially proves the boundaries' contribution to the practical impairment and the prohibited purpose's substantial role in their selection. The State's subsequent defense concerns whether independent lawful reasons would nonetheless have produced materially the same decision. Treating those as distinct inquiries avoids requiring the claimant to carry the State's burden before it shifts. Actual population, geographic or statutory requirements, or a genuinely applied and independently justified representative policy, may carry the defense. A promise of electoral advantage to the governing party does not.

Act 1 was adjudged malapportioned. Its use under a stay does not establish a lawful baseline for Act 34. This record supplies no finding that the two plans produced the same relevant effects. The District Court must evaluate the challenged Act 34 assignments against a supported lawful comparison, rather than presume continuity of injury or liability from the earlier plan.

### IV. Article I does not require proportional party seats

**Holding and operative rule:** A projected congressional-seat supermajority for a party without a statewide majority of votes does not, by itself, violate Article I, sections 2 or 4. Congressional district elections create no entitlement to a proportionate statewide party delegation; such figures may serve as evidence under a separately established claim.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join Part IV and affirm dismissal of this independent theory.

**Controlling explanation:** Article I provides for election of Representatives; it does not turn separate district elections into a statewide proportional contest. Cook v. Gralike prohibits the official ballot penalty adjudicated there, rather than guaranteeing a party's share of seats. Reynolds and Gaffney likewise do not supply that guarantee. The voters' proposed vote-seat trigger therefore fails as an independent rule, even though the same evidence may illuminate purpose or actual operation under Parts I–III.

### V. Apply the rule to Act 34 and fit any remedy to the proved violation

**Holding and operative rule:** The partisan equal-protection dismissal is vacated. The District Court shall permit conforming amendment, determine personal standing and apply Parts I–III to the live Act 34 claim. No violation of Act 34 is adjudicated on this dismissal record. The Article I dismissal and separate population disposition retain their stated force.

**Authority:** The five-Justice majority joins Part V. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would affirm the partisan dismissal.

**Controlling explanation:** Ordinary notice pleading requires identification of the affected political capacity and challenged operation, with allegations supporting purposeful disadvantage and causation. It does not require merits proof, an expert model or a replacement map at the pleading stage. The court must evaluate the amended allegations and actual evidence, rather than assume that the facts concerning Act 1 establish the effect of Act 34. A standalone First Amendment claim remains subject to ordinary preservation and amendment rules; the Court's holding rests on equal protection.

If a violation is proved and the State fails to establish its defense, the court shall identify the impairment and responsible boundaries and afford the State the first opportunity to cure them. Growe v. Emison preserves that initial state responsibility. The State may choose among lawful remedies that fully correct the injury; it need not adopt the claimant's evidentiary comparator. Any judicial remedy must achieve a complete cure, comply with independent districting requirements and extend no further than necessary, including consequential neighboring changes where required. Neither a proportionate delegation nor a guaranteed electoral victory is the remedial objective.

The court shall proceed on a prompt, workable schedule consistent with the election calendar. Act 34 remains operative pending properly supported relief. The rule governs this dispute and other open cases in which the claim is properly presented.

## Precedent Treatment

Baker v. Carr supplies the claim-specific justiciability inquiry. Davis v. Bandemer's justiciability holding is retained; this decision supplies the controlling substantive standard and rejects complete political exclusion as a necessary condition of relief. Gaffney v. Cummings retains political awareness and genuine representative accommodation. Its permission does not extend to the material discriminatory impairment defined here, and ordinary partisan advantage is not an independent justification for such impairment.

Reynolds v. Sims and White v. Regester support equal political participation without a proportional-seat guarantee. Shaw v. Reno, Johnson v. Miller, Cano v. Davis, Page v. Bartels and Robertson v. Bartels retain the requirement of actual injury independent of political design, preferred constituents or expected incumbent support. The Court extends those principles to the partisan equal-treatment claim announced here; it does not attribute this complete rule to the earlier holdings. Police Department of Chicago v. Mosley supplies the method of deciding an equal-protection claim informed by closely connected First Amendment interests; it does not equate districting with exclusion from a speech forum.

Holder v. Hall supplies independently grounded comparison by analogy. Its statutory elements, and Gingles's racial vote-dilution predicates, are not incorporated into the partisan constitutional claim. Miller and Easley v. Cromartie preserve varied proof. Brooks v. Barnes, Montiel v. Davis and Mt. Healthy supply the purpose and same-decision sequence; Arlington Heights governs contextual proof of purpose; Crawford-El preserves ordinary proof. Cook v. Gralike retains its official-ballot-penalty scope. Growe governs the State's initial remedial responsibility. Separate constitutional racial-opportunity and Voting Rights Act claims retain their own elements and remedies.

## Law After Decision

The decisive principle is the prohibition of intentional discriminatory treatment causing material practical injury. First Amendment principles of political belief, expression and association inform why affiliation cannot justify the defined disadvantage; the Court adopts no independent First Amendment holding. Functional analysis determines political capacity, impairment and causation; burden shifting resolves the consequence of mixed purposes. Structural concerns about entrenchment explain the importance of the protection but do not require proof of statewide political lockout. The opinion announces a rule for this and other properly presented open cases because the substantive standard is necessary to govern adjudication. It adopts no scrutiny tier, free-standing competitiveness mandate, numerical electoral floor or balancing of a proved violation against partisan gains.

A personally injured voter may challenge an identified, existing or demonstrably attainable political capacity materially impaired through district assignments selected in substantial part to disadvantage the voter because of political affiliation or activity. The five-Justice rule requires the four elements, an independently justified lawful comparison and proof by a preponderance. The State then bears the same-decision defense by the same standard. Materiality requires a demonstrated, nontrivial reduction in effective ability to organize, nominate or elect under ordinary electoral conditions; a predicted vote-share change without reliable evidence of practical consequence does not independently establish it.

Political awareness, incidental partisan consequences and independently legitimate representative choices remain permissible. Deliberate material impairment is not excused as ordinary partisan advantage. Total exclusion, a lost election, statewide entrenchment and crossing a specified percentage are unnecessary. Political design, a preferred alternative map and seat-vote disproportionality alone remain insufficient. A successful comparator proves elements of the claim but does not dictate the remedy. The separate Article I seat-ratio theory fails unanimously.

## Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, concurring.** The Chief Justice joins the Court's equal-protection reasoning, including its reliance on First Amendment principles, and would further hold that the same proved conduct independently violates the First Amendment's guarantees of political expression and association. O'Hare Truck Service and Umbehr show that the absence of an entitlement to a government benefit does not authorize a penalty for protected political activity. He would extend that reasoning to the material electoral impairment defined by the Court, subject to the same capacity, injury, purpose, comparison, causation and same-decision requirements. Political design or an unfavorable result alone would remain insufficient. A standalone First Amendment claim must be properly presented. No other Justice joins this additional constitutional ground.

**O'Connor, concurring in part and dissenting in part; Scalia and Thomas join.** These Justices join Part IV but would affirm the partisan dismissal as presenting a political question and overrule Bandemer's contrary conclusion for this category. They regard the majority's equal-treatment formulation as insufficient to identify a judicially enforceable entitlement to a distribution of political support. A feasible alternative does not establish that its electoral opportunity is constitutionally owed. They also conclude that the majority substantially restricts intentional pursuit of partisan advantage without adequately reconciling that restriction with the political character of districting recognized by Gaffney. Holder reinforces their objection to a court-selected allocation, and Growe preserves legislative responsibility. Independently defined exclusion, retaliation, population and racial claims retain their own standards; they reject the affirmative partisan cause announced here.

**Kennedy, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** Kennedy joins Part IV and would affirm this partisan dismissal without categorically foreclosing all future claims. The distinction between political awareness and a discriminatory electoral penalty, in his view, does not sufficiently identify an administrable constitutional burden on this record. Holder requires a principled comparison; Cano requires injury independent of design; Cook and political-expression principles leave room for a sufficiently defined participation or association claim. He does not join the four-element rule or the categorical dissent's proposal to overrule Bandemer's openness to a future standard.

## Procedure After Action

Vacated and remanded, 5–4, on the partisan equal-protection claim; affirmed, 9–0, on the independent Article I seat-ratio claim. The three-judge District Court shall permit conforming amendment, determine personal standing and apply the complete rule to Act 34 through ordinary proceedings. No finding of liability, entitlement to a particular map or entitlement to a seat is made. Upon a proved violation and failure of the State's defense, the State receives the first opportunity to provide a full lawful cure. Any further judicial relief must fit the proved injury and necessary consequential changes. Act 34 remains operative pending supported relief, and the separate population disposition remains intact.
