### Demore v. Kim — 2003-10-14

Effective October 14, 2003, section 1226(e) preserves independently proper habeas review of the legality of the mandatory framework, with effective relief and the ordinary limits on discretionary review and initial agency factfinding. Section 1226(c) may operate before a final removal order, subject to actual consideration of disputed statutory membership. Lawful permanent residents with a category question that is not insubstantial and not interposed solely for delay have a statutory entitlement to individual flight-risk and danger consideration, with each independently sufficient mandatory ground addressed.

These jurisdiction and statutory-threshold propositions are direct majority holdings. Kim's remand applies the detainee clear-and-convincing risk showing and preserves his current release through the threshold determination and any consequent bond decision under a five-vote judgment. The burden rationale has four joins and supplies no general majority proof rule. The independent constitutional hearing and continued-detention formulations remain without a majority at the necessary level of generality; a distinct duration claim also requires findings about the course of detention. Classification and individual-risk findings on remand are applications of the stated instructions, not unresolved statutory standards.

Published separate positions:

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and concurring in the judgment.** He joins Parts I, II-A and II-B and accepts the entire vacatur, remand and interim-release remedy so Kim can receive the individual assessment available under the statutory pathway. He withholds assent from the allocation of persuasion to Kim. In his view, when genuinely contested inclusion or constitutionally insufficient continuing authority requires individual review, the Government should prove by clear and convincing evidence that reasonably available conditions, separately or together, cannot adequately address flight risk or danger; he would also accept governmental preponderance with the same object of proof and effective relief. Mathews directs attention to liberty and error; Salerno and Addington offer regime-specific support for the preferred quantum; Cooper illustrates the consequences of a burden that wrongly allocates uncertainty. Krimstock supports meaningful review and consideration of alternatives, while his separate preponderance position there remains his own. Kim seeks liberty in a civil immigration proceeding, not release pending appeal of his criminal convictions, so section 3143(b) does not persuade him to place the risk of uncertainty on Kim. The neutral decisionmaker should receive contrary evidence, assess residence, family and community ties, convictions, release conduct, appearance evidence and feasible conditions, and give reviewable reasons; failure of the governmental showing should permit conditional release.

He also explains that lawful initial detention does not justify continuation after its nature, duration or circumstances lose a reasonable relationship to removal proceedings pursued with reasonable diligence. A resident would identify a concrete continuing-custody concern; the Government would supply the chronology, basis, delay explanations and anticipated course and prove disputed diligence facts by preponderance. Legitimate substantial defenses are not automatically misconduct; solely dilatory conduct may count against the resident, and unjustified governmental or institutional delay weighs against categorical continuation. If that authority fails, individualized justification and effective relief are required; material change permits renewed review. Gilbert, Fairley, Foucha and Jackson support the continuing-authority inquiry; Flores preserves lawful temporary custody, and Zadvydas contributes the removal-purpose relationship without its separate calendar or burden. The present statutory holding leaves this constitutional limit open. His proposals are noncontrolling, supply no finding that Kim's past detention crossed the limit, and neither condition the entered judgment nor alter its remand instructions.

**Justice Souter, concurring, joined by Stevens and Ginsburg.** They join the entire lead opinion because its substantial-question pathway affords genuine individual consideration and protects Kim's existing release during the inquiry. They retain the broader view that a lawful permanent resident must receive a reasonably prompt, impartial opportunity to contest the necessity of custody on flight-risk and danger grounds. A past criminal category cannot conclusively establish present need throughout the proceeding. Carlson retained individual discretion, Flores retained real release and review avenues, and Zadvydas ties detention to its purpose; National Center and the continuing-deprivation decisions support effective inquiry into the actual justification. A rebuttable initial category presumption may be permissible, but meaningful consideration and effective release authority are essential. Their statutory join accepts Part III's allocation within that pathway, without making it a universal burden or approving categorical constitutional validity outside the threshold. That broader constitutional position is noncontrolling.

**Justice O'Connor, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.** They accept pre-order operation of section 1226(c), but would reverse the resident-wide constitutional hearing requirement and remand for implementation in the actual custody and removal posture. Congress may make a properly established criminal category sufficient during reasonably dispatched removal proceedings. Flores supports temporary categorical arrangements; the statutory classification, rather than risk alone, supplies the substantive predicate. Gilbert, Krimstock and Fairley require testing the actual legal justification, not adding a new risk exception to a valid category. Joseph and the proper review channels must address genuine membership disputes. A substantial challenge does not itself suspend Congress's custody rule, and the express witness-protection exception does not authorize the Court's implied bond pathway. The disputed charges require their own lawful treatment; the requested continuance and elapsed six months do not establish the exceptional constitutional defect asserted here. O'Connor and Kennedy join the Court's jurisdiction holding; Scalia and Thomas reach these merits on the Court's jurisdiction determination. This proposed reversal is noncontrolling.

**Justice Kennedy, additionally concurring in part and dissenting in part.** His disagreement with the statutory pathway preserves individual protection against unreasonable, unjustified or arbitrary continuing detention and denial of prescribed review. The Government must have actual support for mandatory category membership; unreasonable delay or failure of that justification can require individual flight-risk and danger consideration. His Flores and Zadvydas positions and Krimstock's inquiry into continuing authority support that distinction. The present record, including Kim's requested continuance and incomplete findings, does not establish that constitutional defect from elapsed time alone. This qualification of his reversal position is noncontrolling.

**Justice Scalia, additionally concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by Thomas.** They read section 1226(e)'s second sentence as a bar on setting aside an executive detention or release action, including through this challenge to the mandatory framework. A legal or constitutional label cannot evade the action-based language. Their application of retained channels under other statutes does not settle this text. They decline Part I and proceed to the statutory merits on the Court's contrary jurisdiction determination, without announcing a comprehensive Suspension Clause rule. Their jurisdictional objection is noncontrolling.

Public procedure: The mandate returns the case for the lower court to obtain proper agency consideration of the current removal and custody posture and every asserted mandatory category, including the original theft theory and the added burglary and two-crimes grounds. The decisionmaker applies Part II-B's not-insubstantial and not-solely-for-delay conditions to each independently sufficient ground. If those conditions are met, the consequent individual risk assessment applies Kim's clear-and-convincing showing of unlikely flight or danger and effective authority to grant lawful conditional release. If mandatory membership fails, ordinary section 1226(a) procedures govern; if the substantial-question condition fails while mandatory coverage remains, section 1226(c) applies subject to an independently established constitutional defect. Kim's existing $5,000 bond release remains effective pending the threshold determination and any consequent lawful bond decision. The lower court implements and enforces these instructions through the retained legal-authority channel; the agency performs the initial determinations assigned to it.

### Yarborough v. Alvarado; No. 02-1684; 541 U.S. 652 — 2004-06-01

The presented Miranda-custody ground is foreclosed by section 2254(d)(1) on this record. The objective arrest-equivalence inquiry remains enforceable through established general holdings, and statutory reasonableness remains a legal inquiry into the full supported state adjudication. The decision imposes no universal exclusion of visible age from custody analysis. A generally applicable direct-review age rule remains outside this adjudication.

Published separate positions:

Breyer, joined by Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg, agrees with the review framework but finds both statutory unreasonableness and actual custody. A young person transported by parents at police request, separated from them, and questioned alone in a small station room for two hours while confronted with contradictory evidence would reasonably understand that officials controlled whether he could end the encounter. Breaks offered only near the end can reinforce that understanding; eventual release does not resolve the restraint during interrogation. Obvious youth describes the objectively apparent position of the interviewee, while undisclosed police experience invites an unrelated psychological inquiry. Stansbury and Thompson already supply the objective rule, and Williams and Andrade permit that general rule to compel a result without identical facts. The dissent relies on the supported encounter, not the disputed account that the parents requested admission and were refused, and not a later constitutional holding. It would preserve the Ninth Circuit's statutory and custody conclusions, then require whatever admission and trial-prejudice determinations remain before a final writ. Custody alone would not supply immediate release or an unconditional new trial.

Public procedure: The case returns for reinstatement and implementation of denial of the presented Miranda-custody claim. The conviction and sentence remain in force on this ground.

### Hiibel v. Sixth Judicial District Court of Nevada; No. 03-5554; 542 U.S. 177. — 2004-06-21

A State may enforce a precise, relevant name duty within an independently lawful and properly limited investigative stop. Courts must verify the stop's justification, the demand's investigative relationship and any properly raised real danger of incrimination before imposing liability. Names have no categorical exemption from the privilege.

Published separate positions:

**Stevens, dissenting.** A spoken name conveys information, and punishment for refusal compels that information. Hoffman protects an evidentiary link rather than only a completed confession. The State seeks Hiibel's identity to investigate his possible responsibility for a reported crime; Stevens regards that concrete purpose as the contextual basis for apprehending incrimination. Requiring an additional explanation of how the name connects to proof risks demanding the very disclosure the privilege protects. Hubbell reinforces protection for knowledge used as an investigative aid, and Muniz's routine-booking context differs from questioning targeted at a suspect's offense. He would reverse under the Fifth Amendment and supplies no separate Fourth Amendment ground.

**Breyer, dissenting, joined by Souter and Ginsburg.** Terry's limited seizure authority permits police to investigate on suspicion while leaving answers voluntary. A useful identity question remains distinct from a criminal obligation to answer. Berkemer's refusal principle expresses that boundary even though its immediate issue was Miranda custody. Nevada's name-only construction avoids Kolender's uncertainty, and the accepted stop avoids Brown's suspicion defect, but neither supplies the additional power to punish silence. A stable voluntary-answer rule protects the limited character of suspicion-based detention without preventing officers from asking questions. The dissent would set aside this conviction under the Fourth Amendment; its disposition does not depend on a Fifth Amendment application ruling.

Public procedure: The Nevada judgment remains in force. The ordinary mandate returns the case for implementation of the affirmance.

## Event

Demore v. Kim, No. 01-1491. October Term 2003, chunk 1. Merits decision on reargument, October 14, 2003. Argued January 15, 2003; restored for reargument April 29, 2003; reargued October 6, 2003. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, 276 F.3d 523. The Ninth Circuit affirmed a conditional habeas judgment requiring reasonably prompt individual flight-risk and danger consideration for Kim, a lawful permanent resident detained under section 1226(c). The questions concern section 1226(e)'s effect on habeas jurisdiction, the pre-final-order reach of section 1226(c), and the statutory and constitutional grounds for individual custody review during removal proceedings. Kim's criminal-category membership is disputed.

## Participation

Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument, reargument and decision.

## Public Action

The judgment of the Ninth Circuit is vacated, and the case is remanded for application of the substantial-question statutory pathway.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Vacated and remanded, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

The lower court must obtain the proper agency consideration of each asserted mandatory-detention ground and apply the statutory threshold in Part II-B. If that threshold is met, Kim receives an individualized flight-risk and danger assessment under Part III, at which he bears clear-and-convincing persuasion that he is unlikely to flee or endanger others. His existing release on $5,000 bond remains effective pending the threshold determination and any consequent lawful bond decision. The five-Justice judgment requires this complete remand, although Part III's allocation has only four opinion joins. Stone-Zsela accepts the judgment and remedy while withholding that join; his concurrence supplies no competing remand instruction.

## Holdings

#### I. Independent habeas reaches the legality of the mandatory framework

**Controlling proposition:** Section 1226(e) does not withdraw independently proper jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. section 2241 to determine the statutory and constitutional legality of Congress's mandatory detention framework and afford effective relief when its application lacks lawful authority. The retained channel requires the ordinary custody, respondent, forum and exhaustion predicates and does not authorize reweighing lawful discretionary bond decisions or initial judicial retrial of removability facts.

**Authority:** Part I, joined by the seven Justices identified in Opinion Topology.

**Controlling explanation:** Goncalves and Kolster distinguish substantial legal objections to executive authority from review of discretionary equities. Calcano-Martinez requires the retained writ to decide the legal objection and provide effective relief; St. Cyr applies that channel to a legal eligibility rule. Zadvydas applies it to present and prospective detention authority. Section 1226(e) must independently be read in that light: its first sentence protects discretionary judgments, and its second addresses executive detention, release, bond and parole actions. Together they do not clearly foreclose adjudication of the legislative framework's legal authority.

The action-based objection is substantial: a hearing order can affect detention even when styled a challenge to the statute. But deciding what the statute lawfully authorizes differs from substituting judicial custody discretion for the executive's. Both sentences retain work under that distinction; the Court requires no particular repeal formula. Kim's existing release leaves the dispute live because the requested reversal could expose him to renewed mandatory custody. Ventura preserves the agency's initial factfinding role. Whether Congress could constitutionally eliminate every effective channel remains outside the necessary statutory resolution.

#### II-A. Mandatory coverage can precede a final removal order

**Controlling proposition:** Section 1226(c) can govern custody during pending removal proceedings before a final removal order. Its application requires the actual statutory category to be addressed through the proper classification process; acceptance of lawful initial custody does not itself concede each asserted ground of continuing mandatory coverage.

**Authority:** Part II-A, joined by all nine on the statutory merits.

**Controlling explanation:** The command to take custody upon release from criminal confinement would not fit a rule postponing all section 1226(c) authority until a final removal order. Section 1231 separately governs the post-order stage. Kim's final-order-only interpretation therefore fails. National Center preserves actual determinations of status beneath a general immigration custody rule; the charging allegation and the legal category remain distinct. Kim disputes the original theft ground and the added grounds. Proper classification review must address those issues, with initial agency determinations left to the agency under Ventura. Agreement on this pre-order construction does not decide the separate threshold-to-bond question in Part II-B.

#### II-B. A substantial category question opens individualized custody consideration

**Controlling proposition:** A lawful permanent resident challenging mandatory membership under section 1226(c) is entitled to an individualized flight-risk and danger assessment when the contention raises a question of law or fact that is not insubstantial and is not interposed solely for purposes of delay. The inquiry must address every independently sufficient asserted mandatory ground; a challenge to one ground cannot displace another established ground capable of sustaining mandatory custody.

**Authority:** Part II-B, directly joined by Breyer, Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg. This five-Justice statutory entitlement is distinct from Part III's four-Justice proof allocation.

**Controlling explanation:** Zadvydas supplies a purpose-sensitive construction of detention authority in light of serious liberty concerns. National Center requires attention to the predicate of an immigration rule's application. Here the question is whether mandatory classification can conclusively determine custody while a substantial dispute over membership remains. The construction permits genuine risk consideration during that dispute rather than treating the charge as its own answer.

The Government's strongest objection is that Congress specified a witness-protection exception and otherwise commanded custody. The Court construes the threshold at which unresolved membership may support mandatory custody; it does not create a general equitable exception for every person conclusively within the class. Joseph's requirement that the Government be substantially unlikely to prevail cannot be the exclusive route to risk consideration when the lower threshold announced here is satisfied. The distinct witness-protection provision retains its terms.

A request for time to obtain evidence, the time needed to litigate, or a mixed motive does not alone make the challenge solely dilatory. A request for discretionary relief after covered removability is accepted is different from disputing mandatory membership. Corona-Sanchez requires examination of the original theft theory, while the added burglary and two-crimes theories require their own inquiry. Ventura places those initial determinations on remand. The statutory resolution leaves the broader constitutional hearing entitlement without a decision; it neither establishes categorical constitutional validity outside this threshold nor imports Zadvydas's post-order six-month mechanism.

#### III–IV. The risk allocation and the enforceable remand have different authority

**Judgment and proposition-level authority:** The five-Justice judgment vacates and remands under the complete statutory pathway, preserves Kim's present release through its determination, and directs the risk assessment described below if the threshold is met. Four Justices—Breyer, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg—join Parts III and IV as opinion reasoning. Stone-Zsela concurs in the judgment, accepting its remedy without joining the detainee allocation.

**Risk instruction applied on remand:** At the individualized assessment, Kim must show by clear and convincing evidence that he is unlikely to flee or endanger others. The decisionmaker must genuinely consider the individual evidence and possess effective authority to release on lawful bond conditions when that showing is made. The statutory-membership threshold and the later risk showing are separate inquiries. If Kim fails the substantial-question condition with respect to an independently sufficient mandatory ground, the categorical statute governs subject to any independently established constitutional defect; actual exclusion from the mandatory category returns custody to the ordinary section 1226(a) framework.

**Plurality explanation and limit:** Part III uses section 3143(b)'s release-pending-appeal structure by analogy to implement this confined statutory pathway. It does not directly apply that criminal statute or prescribe a universal detainee burden. The plurality answers the Government-burden objection by distinguishing the opportunity created during unresolved but substantial membership litigation from a constitutional determination that categorical detention authority has ended. Stone-Zsela accepts the practical remedy but disagrees with that allocation of uncertainty. Those opposite allocations are not logical subsets; no Marks rationale supplies a fifth opinion join. The risk instruction binds this remand through the judgment; its general precedential force remains that of a four-Justice view. The statutory ground resolves the judgment. The broader constitutional hearing and continued-detention formulations lack a majority at the necessary level of generality and therefore remain open; the application of a distinct constitutional duration defect also requires findings about the specific course and justification of Kim's confinement.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Goncalves and Kolster:** Applied to preserve independent review of substantial legal-authority objections while excluding mere discretionary reweighing; their channel-specific limits remain operative.
- **Calcano-Martinez and St. Cyr:** Applied to require a capable legal-review channel and effective relief; the retained writ's ordinary predicates continue to govern.
- **Zadvydas:** Its legal-authority jurisdiction holding is applied to section 1226(c), and its purpose-sensitive construction informs the substantial-question threshold. Its distinct post-order duration and proof mechanism remains confined to its own statutory setting.
- **National Center for Immigrants' Rights:** Applied to actual category determinations beneath a general custody rule; a charge does not itself settle a genuinely contested predicate.
- **Ventura:** Applied to preserve initial agency determination of unresolved classification and factual matters while the courts supply and enforce the legal standard.
- **Matter of Joseph:** Limited insofar as its substantially-unlikely-to-succeed threshold would be the sole route to bond consideration despite the substantial-question condition now required by Part II-B. Its category-review function remains available.
- **Corona-Sanchez:** Considered as relevant lower-court authority requiring examination of the original theft classification; the added grounds receive their own analysis under the remand.

## Event

Yarborough v. Gentry, No. 02-1597; 540 U.S. 1. Merits decision, October 20, 2003. The Ninth Circuit reversed the denial of federal habeas relief on a challenge to defense counsel's closing argument. The question is whether the state court unreasonably applied the Sixth Amendment performance standard in rejecting that claim. The matter was decided on the merits submission.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at submission and decision.

## Public Action

The judgment of the Ninth Circuit is reversed, and the case is remanded.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Judgment:** Reversed and remanded, **9–0**, to reject the reviewed closing-argument habeas claim.

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. **Opposing:** None.

The District Court's denial of relief on this claim stands. The conviction remains operative against this ground. The court of appeals must implement rejection of the claim and resolve any independently pending preserved ground under its own requirements.

## Opinion Topology

The Court issues a per curiam opinion joined in full by all nine participating Justices.

## Holdings

**Controlling authority:** The complete per curiam opinion has nine direct joins: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. That coalition controls both propositions below.

**1. Review of counsel's closing argument.** A federal habeas court must assess the actual summation in the context of the supported defense and may grant relief on a state-adjudicated performance claim only when the state decision satisfies section 2254(d)(1). Omitting useful arguments or using offensive language does not by itself establish deficient performance; the court must examine what the argument actually did, and a strategy label cannot substitute for that examination.

**Controlling explanation:** Strickland requires professionally reasonable representation, with room for informed choices about emphasis. Herring protects closing advocacy without prescribing a script. Williams supplies the distinct contrary-rule and objectively-unreasonable-application inquiries; independent disagreement with a state assessment is insufficient. Bell applies those requirements to particular omissions within actual defense advocacy. Visciotti requires examination of the supported reasoning and record as a whole, and Andrade preserves the substantive-law cutoff at the state merits adjudication without demanding a factual twin. These rules require attention to a summation's substance, rather than either automatic deference to a tactical description or a catalogue of preferable phrases.

**2. This state performance determination was reasonable.** The state court could reasonably conclude that counsel's closing presented a coherent accident defense, attacked the eyewitness's account and separated past misconduct from proof of intentional stabbing. Its rejection of deficient performance therefore defeats the reviewed claim under section 2254(d)(1).

**Controlling explanation:** Counsel identified intent as the dispute, challenged the witness's changing description of the lighting, explained confusion about prior convictions and argued against guilt based on character. Gentry identifies substantial omitted points, including inconsistent recollections, wound evidence and corroboration concerning another man's presence. Counsel's statement connecting disbelief of Gentry with conviction is also unsound as a statement of the prosecution's burden: disbelief alone does not prove every element. But the state court could read that compressed formulation within an argument that continued to advance accident and challenge the prosecution's proof. The question is whether its performance assessment fell outside the lawful range, not whether a stronger summation was possible. The actual defense work supports the state conclusion under Strickland and Bell. Neither the trial judge's favorable assessment nor the length of deliberations controls that inquiry.

## Precedent Treatment

- Strickland v. Washington is applied to the actual, contextual performance inquiry; reasonable professional choice remains subject to examination of the supported record.
- Herring v. New York is applied as protection for meaningful closing advocacy, with no prescribed summation script.
- Williams v. Taylor is applied to section 2254(d)(1)'s independent inquiries and objective legal limit; its contrary-rule and unreasonable-application holdings retain their force.
- Bell v. Cone is applied to the sufficient performance ground for a confined tactical claim within substantive advocacy.
- Woodford v. Visciotti is applied to whole-record review rather than isolated wording or formal recitation of a standard.
- Lockyer v. Andrade is applied to the objective statutory gateway and the state-adjudication cutoff for clearly established substantive law.

## Law After Decision

The established statutory and Sixth Amendment rules remain in force. They permit rejection of this closing-argument claim because the state performance assessment was substantively reasonable on the complete summation. Courts must examine actual defense advocacy and its context when applying the rule to other closing arguments.

## Event

Yarborough v. Alvarado; No. 02-1684; 541 U.S. 652. Argued March 1, 2004; decided June 1, 2004. The Court reviews the Ninth Circuit's decision granting habeas relief on the premise that the state court unreasonably found a seventeen-year-old noncustodial during a stationhouse interview. The relevant state merits adjudication occurred in 1999. The warden seeks reinstatement of denial on that claim; Alvarado seeks relief from admission of the unwarned statements. His parents brought him to the station at the detective's request and waited in the lobby while one detective interviewed him for about two hours. He declined two offered breaks near the end and returned home.

## Participation

Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer participate.

## Public Action

Reversed, 5–4; denial of habeas relief on the presented Miranda-custody ground reinstated.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

**Opposing:** Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The denial of habeas relief on the presented Miranda-custody ground is reinstated. The mandate requires implementation of that denial.

## Opinion Topology

O'Connor delivered the opinion of the Court. All nine joined Part I's review framework; the five-Justice majority joined Part II's application and disposition.

## Holdings

### I. Objective custody and the independent habeas restriction

**Controlling proposition:** Miranda custody turns on whether the full objective circumstances would make a reasonable person in the interviewee's position understand restraint equivalent to formal arrest. In habeas review of a state merits adjudication, section 2254(d)(1) additionally requires a decision contrary to this Court's then-established governing holdings or an objectively unreasonable application of them; independent disagreement, a correct formula alone, or the number of judges taking either view does not decide that inquiry.

**Authority:** Part I, joined by all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Miranda supplies the warning consequence of custodial interrogation. Berkemer and Stansbury require objective, communicated circumstances rather than undisclosed beliefs or personal psychology; suspicion conveyed in questioning matters through its effect on that setting, not as automatic custody. Thompson classifies the ultimate custody question as mixed law and fact under the former statute. It does not displace the separate AEDPA restriction governing this case. Williams v. Taylor and Lockyer v. Andrade require objective statutory review and preserve the distinction between the constitutional law established at the state merits adjudication and the current interpretation of the federal review method. A general rule can clearly establish law without a factual twin. Woodford v. Visciotti requires examination of the complete supported adjudication, rather than selecting isolated language or treating a correct quotation as sufficient. Mitchell v. Esparza and Middleton v. McNeil preserve the distinction between the statutory review inquiry, constitutional error and ultimate entitlement to relief. The substantive custody law here is measured at the 1999 state adjudication; a later constitutional refinement cannot supply an earlier command.

### II. The state noncustody determination remains within the permitted range

**Controlling proposition:** On this complete supported record, the state court's noncustody conclusion was not an objectively unreasonable application of the established arrest-equivalent-restraint rule. Alvarado therefore fails the section 2254(d)(1) condition for relief on this Miranda-custody ground, even though youth, station isolation, duration and accusatory questioning support a substantial contrary assessment.

**Authority:** Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; five direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** Mathiason and Beheler establish that station questioning and suspicion alone do not create custody. Alvarado arrived voluntarily with his parents, was neither formally arrested nor physically restrained or threatened, received offers of breaks, and returned home afterward. These circumstances permit the state conclusion. The Court also considers the two-hour interview in a small room, separation from parents, accusations and absence of an express assurance that he could leave. Ultimate departure or a friendly tone alone does not resolve custody, but the whole supported encounter remains within the range permitted by the established rule. The contrary view treats the combination as a clear message of official control. That is substantial, yet the arrival, actual interview conditions and departure support a reasonable noncustody assessment without making handcuffs or violence necessary to custody. Alvarado was approximately seventeen years and eight months old at the interview. Obvious youth can differ from private police experience, but the established law did not require the additional age treatment demanded here, and the other circumstances independently sustain the statutory conclusion. The absence of a youth-specific precedent alone is insufficient to decide reasonableness. This habeas holding neither categorically excludes apparent age from objective circumstances nor adopts a new generally applicable direct-review age rule; that broader question is outside the necessary inquiry.

- Berkemer v. McCarty and Stansbury v. California: applied to communicated objective circumstances and arrest-equivalent restraint.

## Law After Decision

The presented Miranda-custody ground is foreclosed by section 2254(d)(1) on this record. The objective arrest-equivalence inquiry remains enforceable through established general holdings, and statutory reasonableness remains a legal inquiry into the full supported state adjudication. The decision imposes no universal exclusion of visible age from custody analysis. A generally applicable direct-review age rule remains outside this adjudication.

## Separate Writings

Breyer, joined by Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg, agrees with the review framework but finds both statutory unreasonableness and actual custody. A young person transported by parents at police request, separated from them, and questioned alone in a small station room for two hours while confronted with contradictory evidence would reasonably understand that officials controlled whether he could end the encounter. Breaks offered only near the end can reinforce that understanding; eventual release does not resolve the restraint during interrogation. Obvious youth describes the objectively apparent position of the interviewee, while undisclosed police experience invites an unrelated psychological inquiry. Stansbury and Thompson already supply the objective rule, and Williams and Andrade permit that general rule to compel a result without identical facts. The dissent relies on the supported encounter, not the disputed account that the parents requested admission and were refused, and not a later constitutional holding. It would preserve the Ninth Circuit's statutory and custody conclusions, then require whatever admission and trial-prejudice determinations remain before a final writ. Custody alone would not supply immediate release or an unconditional new trial.

## Event

Hiibel v. Sixth Judicial District Court of Nevada, No. 03-5554; 542 U.S. 177. Argued March 22, 2004; decided June 21, 2004. On writ of certiorari to the Supreme Court of Nevada, 118 Nev. 868, 59 P.3d 1201. The questions are whether Nevada may require disclosure of a name during an independently lawful investigative stop and whether punishing refusal violates the Fourth Amendment or the Fifth Amendment privilege. An officer investigating a reported assault involving a man and woman near a truck repeatedly requested Hiibel's identity. Hiibel refused and was convicted. Nevada's authoritative construction requires communication of a name, not a document. Hiibel seeks reversal; Nevada seeks affirmance.

## Participation

Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The judgment sustaining Hiibel's conviction is affirmed.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

**Opposing:** Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The conviction remains enforceable. Stevens would reverse under the Fifth Amendment; Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer would reverse under the Fourth Amendment.

## Opinion Topology

O'CONNOR, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and SCALIA, KENNEDY, and THOMAS, JJ., joined. STEVENS, J., filed a dissenting opinion. BREYER, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which SOUTER and GINSBURG, JJ., joined.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | O'Connor | Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Affirms | Both constitutional holdings and full mandate |
| Fifth Amendment dissent | Stevens | No other Justice | Would reverse | Testimonial identification and contextual evidentiary-link danger; no separate Fourth Amendment ground |

## Holdings

**Common authority:** The Opinion of the Court is adopted in full by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. Those five direct joins control both holdings.

### I. A relevant name duty can operate within a lawful investigative stop

**Controlling proposition:** During an independently lawful investigative stop, a State may require a person to disclose a name when the demand is reasonably related to the circumstances justifying the stop and does not enlarge its lawful scope or duration. A prosecution must establish the lawful stop, the properly bounded demand and the statutory violation under the applicable criminal burden; refusal cannot supply retroactive suspicion or authority for unrelated questioning.

**Controlling explanation:** Terry v. Ohio permits a brief investigation supported by reasonable suspicion and limits its scope to its justification. Nevada's precise name requirement serves the particular investigation within those limits. It differs from Brown v. Texas, where independent suspicion was absent, and Kolender v. Lawson, where an undefined demand for reliable identification left the obligation to police discretion. Statutory clarity alone is not enough; the demand must actually relate to the lawful investigation. That requirement is satisfied on this accepted record.

The contrary argument correctly distinguishes permission to ask from power to punish silence. Berkemer v. McCarty's discussion of voluntary answers addressed whether an investigative stop amounted to Miranda custody; it did not decide an enacted, definite name duty. Terry's reasonableness inquiry permits this limited obligation without authorizing compelled explanations about the suspected conduct. Atwater v. City of Lago Vista separately requires an actual enforcement function for greater fine-only station transport and confinement. The name duty supplies no automatic power to enlarge the detention.

### II. A real evidentiary-link danger preserves the privilege

**Controlling proposition:** A compelled name demand remains subject to the Fifth Amendment when disclosure presents a real and appreciable danger of furnishing criminal evidence, including an evidentiary link. The claimant must supply a sufficient contextual basis to identify that danger without being forced to disclose the privileged substance itself; a claim of privacy alone does not establish the privilege, and this record supplies no such incriminating link.

**Controlling explanation:** Hoffman v. United States protects answers that could furnish a link in criminal proof; the claimant need not first reveal the protected information. Ohio v. Reiner confirms that professed innocence does not forfeit a supported privilege. United States v. Hubbell protects compelled testimonial knowledge and its derivative use at its subpoena-and-immunity scope. These rules require attention both to what information is compelled and to the real danger presented in context.

Identification can communicate information, and a name can be incriminating in a supported case. The Court's conclusion rests on the absence of a real danger here, not a categorical identity exception. The investigative usefulness of a name does not by itself establish that disclosing this name would furnish criminal evidence. Although Stevens regards the targeted criminal investigation as sufficient contextual exposure, this record identifies no concrete connection of that kind. The privilege is not waived by silence; its substantive predicate has not been established. Dickerson retains its separate constitutional safeguards for custodial interrogation.

## Precedent Treatment

- Terry v. Ohio: applied to independent suspicion and the continuing scope and duration limits; its framework now permits the defined relevant name duty.
- Brown v. Texas: distinguished because this review accepts an independently justified stop; its suspicion requirement remains binding.
- Kolender v. Lawson: distinguished because Nevada requires a name rather than undefined reliable identification.
- Berkemer v. McCarty: confined to its Miranda-custody question; its general discussion of voluntary answers does not settle the enacted name duty.
- Atwater v. City of Lago Vista: preserved at its separate requirement of an actual function for added fine-only station transport and confinement.
- Hoffman v. United States and Ohio v. Reiner: applied to real contextual exposure, including an evidentiary link and despite asserted innocence.
- United States v. Hubbell: preserved at its compelled-testimony and derivative-use scope without eliminating the actual incrimination requirement.
- Dickerson v. United States: preserved at its distinct custodial-interrogation scope.

## Law After Decision

A State may enforce a precise, relevant name duty within an independently lawful and properly limited investigative stop. Courts must verify the stop's justification, the demand's investigative relationship and any properly raised real danger of incrimination before imposing liability. Names have no categorical exemption from the privilege. 

## Separate Writings

**Stevens, dissenting.** A spoken name conveys information, and punishment for refusal compels that information. Hoffman protects an evidentiary link rather than only a completed confession. The State seeks Hiibel's identity to investigate his possible responsibility for a reported crime; Stevens regards that concrete purpose as the contextual basis for apprehending incrimination. Requiring an additional explanation of how the name connects to proof risks demanding the very disclosure the privilege protects. Hubbell reinforces protection for knowledge used as an investigative aid, and Muniz's routine-booking context differs from questioning targeted at a suspect's offense. He would reverse under the Fifth Amendment and supplies no separate Fourth Amendment ground.

**Breyer, dissenting, joined by Souter and Ginsburg.** Terry's limited seizure authority permits police to investigate on suspicion while leaving answers voluntary. A useful identity question remains distinct from a criminal obligation to answer. Berkemer's refusal principle expresses that boundary even though its immediate issue was Miranda custody. Nevada's name-only construction avoids Kolender's uncertainty, and the accepted stop avoids Brown's suspicion defect, but neither supplies the additional power to punish silence. A stable voluntary-answer rule protects the limited character of suspicion-based detention without preventing officers from asking questions. The dissent would set aside this conviction under the Fourth Amendment; its disposition does not depend on a Fifth Amendment application ruling.
