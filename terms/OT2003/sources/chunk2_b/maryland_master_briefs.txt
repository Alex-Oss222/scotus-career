INTRODUCTION 

Maryland seeks the entry of summary judgment in its favor because there is simply 
no merit in Virginia’s claim that Maryland’s waterway permitting laws “infringe upon 
Virginia’s sovereignty” and “impede and interfere with Virginia’s rights of access to the 
Potomac River.” Complaint § 48. Maryland’s present-day regulation of the river is 
comprehensive in all respects and the result of such longstanding ownership of the Potomac 
that Virginia has acknowledged it since colonial times. Virginia’s claims are contradicted 
not simply by the history pre-dating Virginia’s suit against Maryland, but also by the 
Commonwealth’s continued recognition, even after suit was filed, that its authority over the 
river ends, and Maryland’s begins, at the low-water mark boundary on the Virginia side of 
the river. 


Maryland’s authority over the Potomac River traces back almost four-hundred years 
to the time the Potomac was included in the territory granted by King Charles I to Lord 
Baltimore. That authority was explicitly recognized in Virginia’s first Constitution, it was 
reaffirmed a century later by the Black-Jenkins Award, and it applies to the activity that 
Virginia now claims is beyond Maryland’s jurisdiction, as that activity takes place in 
Maryland. Neither the Compact nor the Black-Jenkins Award contains any language that 
strips Maryland of this fundamental aspect of its ownership of and sovereignty over the 
River. On the contrary, both States have acted in innumerable ways since their adoption of 
the Award to reflect their clear understanding that the boundary described there represents 


the line of sovereignty for all purposes and corfirms Maryland’s right to regulate waterway 


construction activity in and water withdrawals from the Potomac River up to the low-water 
mark on Virginia’s side. For these reasons, as discussed more fully below, Maryland’s 


motion for summary judgment should be granted.

STATEMENT OF FACTS 

Pre-Compact History | 

Virginia traces its territorial roots to 1609, when King James I of England issued a 
charter to the London Company encompassing all of the lands 200 miles north and south of 
Point Comfort and extending inland, without limitation, “from the sea, west and northwest.” 
Johnson v. McIntosh, 21 U.S. 543, 544 (1823). This 1609 grant included within its reach 
much of the land now comprising Maryland and Pennsylvania, as well as the uninhabited 
western lands to the Pacific Ocean. It is on this 1609 grant that Virginia in earlier times 


based its historical claims to the Potomac, Maryland, and the frontier. 
The 1609 grant was short-lived. As a result of a dispute between the London 


Company and the Crown, the grant was annulled in 1624 by judgment of the Court of King’s 
Bench and the powers of government and title to the lands within the limits of the grant were 
re-vested in the Crown. Johnson v. McIntosh, 21 U.S. at 545. As of 1624, Virginia had 
become a royal colony, whose boundaries were subject to change at the pleasure of the 


Crown. /d.; Howard v. Ingersoll, 54 U.S. 381, 400 (1851). 
In 1632, King Charles I issued to Lord Baltimore the charter that marks the origin of 


the Province and State of Maryland.' The charter encompassed land previously included 
within the annulled 1609 grant and specifically included the entire Potomac River from bank 
to bank. Morris v. United States, 174 U.S. 196, 225 (1899). The charter was also significant 
for the breadth of the powers vested in the newly created colony. See Declaration of Jack N. 


Rakove, Exhibit A, at 8 (Maryland’s powers granted in Charter, and as a proprietorial colony,

Although the 1609, 1649, and 1688 grants did not alter the scope of Maryland’s 
Charter, Virginia in 1776 formally renounced any rights or claims it may have harbored with 


respect to Maryland territory: 


[T]he territories within the charters erecting the colonies Maryland, 
Pennsylvania, North and South Carolina, are hereby ceded, released, and 
forever confirmed to the people of those colonies respectively, with all the 
rights of property, jurisdiction, and government, and all other rights 
whatsoever which may at any time heretofore have been claimed by Virginia, 
except the free navigation and use of the rivers Potowmack and Pokomoke, 
with the property of the Virginia shores or strands bordering on either of the 
said rivers, and all improvements which have been made or shall be made 
thereon. 


The effect of Virginia’s concession was not lost on James Madison, who, writing just eight 
years after the enactment of the Virginia Constitution, recognized that this provision 
constituted “an entire relinquishment of the Jurisdiction of these [the Potomac and 


Pocomoke] rivers.”

October of that ycar denying that Virginia had any territorial rights over the Potomac or any 


other portion of Mary!and territory to begin with:

The Compact of 1785 


Thus, when the two States came to the negotiations that would result in the Compact 
of 1785, Maryland held a decided advantage with respect to the Potomac. Virginia had 
already conceded to Maryland “all the rights of property, jurisdiction, and government” in 
the Potomac, reserving only “the free navigation and use” of the river and property nights to 
the shore and shoreline improvements. As recognized by its congressional delegation less 
than twenty years later, Virginia claimed no rights of jurisdiction or governance whatsoever.

Maryland’s Post-Compact Regulation of the Potomac through the Black-Jenkins 
Award Era 


Maryland’s regulation of the Potomac in the late 18th and 19th centuries is reflected 
in the actions of its legislature, courts, and its Commissioner of Land Records. The modern 
administrative State, and its attendant files and records, were still some years off, and so the 
regulatory record is reflected in periodic events that shed light on how Maryland exercised 


its sovereign role in the Potomac. 

The first such event arises in the context of the creation of the District of Columbia.

Post-Black-Jenkins Award History 
Although Virginia by its 1776 Constitution relinquished any claim to the Potomac 


River beyond its “shores or strands,” the precise location of the boundary line on the south 
side of the River had not been decided, with Maryland claiming under its Charter to the high 
water mark. The two States also disagreed about the location of the line, called for in the 
Maryland Charter, that extended across the Chesapeake Bay and Eastern Shore to the 
Atlantic. To avoid litigation over the issues, Maryland and Virginia appointed 
commissioners in 1872 and 1873 to negotiate a settlement of the outstanding disputes. When 
that effort failed, the two States turned to binding arbitration. The arbitrators issued their 
award on January 16, 1877, and determined the boundary to be the low water mark on the 


south shore of the Potomac. (App. 45a.)'°

Since their adoption of the Black-Jenkins Award, Maryland and Virginia have 
embarked and continued on a course of conduct for more than a century manifesting the 
belief and understanding of both States that the low-water mark boundary line represented 
a uniform line of sovereignty with respect to all activity occurring on Maryland’s side of the 
line. In 1888, Maryland began the active regulation of sand and gravel mining from 
Maryland waters, including the Potomac. Under the common law, riparian owners had no 
right to mine sand and gravel from the navigable waters in front of their land. Bostick v. 
Smoot Sand & Gravel Corporation, 260 F.2d 534, 537 (4th Cir. 1958). With the advent of 
the mechanical dredge in the 1 9th century, it became possible to dredge sand and gravel from 
the river bed itself without the permission or knowledge of the riparian owners on the

I. MARYLAND HAS REGULATORY AUTHORITY OVER ACCESS TO

Maryland is entitled to the entry of summary judgment in its favor for the principal 
reason that the relief Virginia seeks is based on an incorrect interpretation of the Compact

not address, much less in any way restrict, Maryland’s broad authority over property within

A. This Case Is Controlled By The Presumption That 
Maryland Has The Right, As The Owner Of The Potomac, 
To Regulate Access To The River. 


“The principle has long been settled in this court, that each State owns the beds of all 
tide-waters within its jurisdiction, unless they have been granted away.” McCready v. 
Virginia, 94 U.S. 391, 394 (1876). This principle is reinforced by a “strong presumption of 
state ownership” because “navigable waters uniquely implicate sovereign interests.” /daho 
v. Coeur d ‘Alene Tribe of Idaho, 521 U.S. 261, 283 (1997). The “ownership of this land was 
considered an essential attribute of sovereignty,” Utah Division of State Lands v. United 
States, 482 U.S. 193, 195 (1987); see also Idaho v. United States, 121 S.Ct. 2135, 2142 
(2001) (citing cases), because “title to such land was important to the sovereign’s ability to 
control navigation, fishing, and other commercial activity on rivers and lakes. .. .” Utah 
Division of State Lands v. United States, 482 U.S. at 195. See also Illinois Central Railroad 
Co. v. Illinois, 146 U.S. 387, 452 (1894). Such “control over the property underlying 
navigable waters is so strongly identified with the sovereign power of government,” 
Montana v. United States, 450 U.S. 544, 552 (1981), that “a presumption against their 
separation from sovereignty must be indulged. . . .” Massachusetts v. New York, 271 U.S. 
65, 89 (1926). See also Idaho v. Coeur d'Alene Tribe of Idaho, 521 U.S. at 283-84. This 
presumption applies “in construing either grants by the sovereign of the lands to be held in 
private ownership or transfer of sovereignty itself,” United States v. Oregon, 295 U.S. 1, 14 
(1935), and is applicable here, as Maryland's ownership of the river is undisputed and its 
right to regulate the construction of wharves and other improvements beyond the low-water

control over the river. Virginia is unable to ‘overcome this presumption and cannot 
demonstrate that Maryland divested itself of its sovereign mght to regulate access to the 
Potomac when it ratified either the Compact or the Black-Jenkins Award. 

Maryland’s territorial claim over the Potomac derives from the charter granted on 
June 20, 1632 from King Charles I to Cecilius Calvert, Lord Baltimore. As the Court long 
ago recognized, that grant “in unmistakable terms included the Potomac River, . . . and 
declared that thereafter the province of Maryland and its freeholders and inhabitants should 
not be held or reputed a member or part of the land of Virginia, ‘from which we do separate 
both the said province and the inhabitants thereof.’” Morris v. United States, 174 U.S. at 223 
(quoting Maryland charter). While King James II in 1688 purported to give by patent to 
Lord Culpeper territory that also included the Potomac River, “the territory and title thus 
granted to Lord Baltimore, his heirs and assigns, were never devested by any valid 
proceedings prior to the Revolution, nor was such grant affected by the subsequent grant to 
Lord Culpeper.” /d. at 225.

B. |The Compact Does Not Contain Any Language That Divests 
Maryland Of Its Right To Regulate Potomac River Access. 


As the cases demonstrate, Maryland is presumed not to have relinquished any 
governmental authority over the exercise of Virginia citizens’ “privilege of making and 
carrying out wharfs and other improvements” in the Potomac River. That presumption can 
be overcome only by clear, especial, or plain language to the contrary in the Compact, as 
Maryland’s regulation of such a privilege is an essential part of its ownership of the Potomac 
and its “absolute right” — as the trustee for its citizens — “to all their navigable waters, and 
the soils under them, for their own common use, subject only to the rights surrendered by the 
constitution to the general government.” Martin v. Waddell, 41 U.S. 367, 410 (1842). See 
also Yates v. Milwaukee, 77 U.S. at 504. While the Compact addressed Virginia’s interest 
in having access to the Potomac, it did nothing to change the Potomac River’s location within 
the State of Maryland as set forth in the original 1632 charter from King Charles I. Under 
the presumption and rule of construction set forth in other comparable cases, the Compact 
does not contain any language that strips Maryland of its governmental authority over the 
Potomac originating in the 1632 charter that Virginia expressly recognized in its 1776 
Constitution. 

In Massachusetts v. New York, 271 U.S. 65 (1926), for example, the Court applied a 
rule of strict construction in holding that Massachusetts did not acquire any title to the bed 
of Lake Ontario by the Treaty of Hartford, in which New York granted to Massachusetts “the 
Right of pre-emption of the Soil from the native Indians and all other the Estate, Right, Title 
and Property (the Right and Title of Government Sovereignty and Jurisdiction excepted)

states.” Jd. at 80, 85. The Court stated that “(t]he dominion over navigable waters and 
property in the soil under them, are so identified with the exercise of the sovereign powers 
of government that a presumption against their separation from sovereignty must be 
indulged, in construing all grants by the sovereign, of lands to be held in private ownership.” 
Id. at 89. 


In applying that presumption and concluding that Massachusetts did not acquire 
private title to the bed of Lake Ontario, the Court observed that “[t}he right of Massachusetts 
and her grantees to use the waters of the lake was amply secured and protected by a clause 
of the treaty” providing that the citizens of Massachusetts shall “have and enjoy the same and 
equal Rights respecting the navigation and fishery on and in Lake Ontario . . . as shall from 
time to time be had and enjoyed by the Citizens of the State of New York.” /d. at 88. Stating 
that “a grant of the soil under water in private ownership would have set material limits on 
the free exercise of the sovereign control of New York over navigable waters of the state and 
on the free use of the principal waterway of the newly settled territory,” id., the Court held 
that “all grants by or to a sovereign government as distinguished from private grants, must 
be construed so as to diminish the public rights of the sovereign only so far as is made 


necessary by an unavoidable construction.” /d. at 89.

Even if ownership of the Potomac were in dispute at that time, despite the plain 
statements to the contrary made in Virginia’s Constitution and the Maryland legislative 
session, the Compact does not stip Maryland of any regulatory authority over the exercise 


of access rights. Rather, the Compact did not resolve any boundary issues but only 


37 


MD L-43 


recognized a privilege to build wharves, wherever the boundary was, without stating 
anything about which State could regulate the exercise of such a privilege. Thus, even if the 
States disagreed on the location of the boundary at the time the Compact was formed and 
elected to leave that question for another day, the Compact neither divests Maryland of, nor 
vests Virginia with, any regulatory authority. As demonstrated by the language found in 
Virginia’s Constitution, Maryland’s 1776 legislative response, the Treaty of Hartford, and 
other contemporaneous historical documents, see Rakove Aff. (Exhibit A) at 13-20, the 
States were well aware of the differences between the words “property, jurisdiction, 
government, and sovereignty.” The mere recognition of a “privilege of making and carrying 
out wharfs and other improvements” simply does not, by itself, operate to strip Maryland of, 
or imbue Virginia with, any jurisdictional, governmental, or sovereign rights over the 
exercise of such a privilege. 


Nor does such a privilege confer, as Virginia alleges in its Complaint, any unilateral

and unrestricted right upon Virginia or “her riparian communities” “to withdraw water from 
the Potomac River. .. .” Complaint § 46. First, Maryland’s ownership of the Potomac River 
bed and waters to the low water mark confirms that Maryland has the authority to regulate 
the reasonable use, including the withdrawal, of water from a river within its own 
boundaries. Indeed, as Virginia’s highest court has stated, “the navigable waters and the soil 
under them, within the territorial limits of a state, are the property of the state, to be 
controlled by the state, in its own discretion, for the benefit of the people of the state... .” 
Taylor v. Commonwealth, 102 Va. 759, 47 S.E. at 879. That control includes the authority 
to regulate the appropriation of waters within that State’s boundaries. See United States v. 


Rio Grande Dam & Irrigation Co., 174 U.S. 690, 702-03 (1899) (“as to every stream within 


38 


MD L-44 


its dominion a state may . . . permit the appropriation of the flowing waters for such purposes 
as it deems wise”); see also Sporhase v. Nebraska ex rel. Douglas, 458 U.S. 941, 956-57 
(1982) (recognizing State’s police power to regulate water withdrawals within its boundanes, 
while striking down Nebraska's reciprocity requirement as discriminating against interstate 
commerce in appropriated groundwaters). 

Second, neither Virginia nor its “riparian communities” have any riparian right to 
withdraw water from the Potomac. Rather, such a right belongs only to the individual 
property owner. Indeed, in Virginia, as in many other States, “([wJhile a mparian owner is 
entitled to a reasonable use of the water, he has no right to divert it for use beyond his 
riparian land... .” Town of Purcellville v. Potts, 179 Va. 514, 521, 19 S.E.2d 700, 703 
(1942). Recognizing that “[t]}he use of the waters of a stream to supply the inhabitants of a 
municipality with water for domestic purposes is not a riparian right,” id. (quoting Pernell 
v. City of Henderson, 220 N.C. 79, 16 S.E.2d 449, 451 (1941) (other citations omitted), “[t}he 
weight of authority * * * holds a municipal corporation civilly liable for diverting the waters 
of a private watercourse for the purposes of a public water supply, either with or without 
legislative authority.” /d. See also Town of Gordonsville v. Zinn, 129 Va. 542, 106 S.E. 508 
(1921). 

This rule is based on the rationale that “giving an individual the nght to consume 
water for his domestic needs is founded upon the needs of the single individual and the 
possible effect which his use will have on the rights of others, and cannot be expanded so as 
to render a collection of persons numbering thousands, and perhaps hundreds of thousands, 
organized into a political unit, a riparian owner, and give this unit the nght of the natural 
unit.” Pernell v. City of Henderson, 220 N.C. 79, 16 S.E.2d at 451 (quoting Farnham, Water 


39 


MD L-45 


and Water Rights, Vol. 1, at 611). Accordingly, “[t)here is little, if any, dissent from the 
generally accepted view that riparian status does not entitle a municipality to abstract stream 
water for distribution to its inhabitants for domestic purposes.” Oklahoma Water Resources 
Board v. Central Oklahoma Conservancy District, 464 P.2d 748, 755 (Okl. 1969). Cf Atlee 
v. Packett Co., 88 U.S. at 394 (rejecting contention that “at any place where a riparian owner 
can make .. . a structure useful to his personal pursuits or business, he can, without license 
or special authority, and by virtue of his ownership, and of his own convenience, project a 
pier or roadway into the deep water of a navigable stream, provided he does it with care, and 
leaves a large and sufficient passway of the channel unobstructed”). 

While there is no riparian right to withdraw water on behalf of non-riparian owners, 
Maryland has long sought to accommodate Virginia’s need for water. Maryland follows the 
“reasonable use” doctrine, which allows for any use of water — even non-riparian uses — so 
long as that use is reasonable and does not harm other users or the aquatic environment. See 
generally COMAR 26.17.06.02B. The evidence discussed below demonstrates that, within 
the Maryland system, Virginians enjoy the same rights as Marylanders and are treated 
similarly. The Compact does not, however, give Virginians any greater access rights than 


those possessed by Marylanders.

In Traverso v. Commonwealth, 6 Va.App. 172, 366 S.E.2d 719 (1988), for example, 
the Virginia Court of Appeals reversed a criminal defendant’s murder conviction and

decedent’s body was found in Maryland between six and seven and one-half feet from the

Court of Special Appeals, which rejected his argument that “there was insufficient evidence 
to support the [trial] court’s determination that the murder occurred in Maryland, so as to 
establish its territorial jurisdiction over the prosecution for this crime.” Traverso v. 
Maryland, 83 Md.App. 389, 396, 574 A.2d 923, 926 (1990).

d. Licensing activities. 
Both States have also recognized that the jurisdictional dividing line set forth in the 


Black-Jenkins Award marks the point at which Virginia’s general police power authority 
terminates and Maryland’s starts. Responding to an inquiry made in 1906 whether Virginia 
could license a boat upon which alcohol was believed to be sold while on the Potomac, an

MD L-62

and thus on Maryland's side of the bovadary line. “I am, therefore, of [the] opinion that the 
Potomac river beyond low water mark is not Virginia territory, hence this State has no legal 
right to impose a license for the conduct of any business carried on outside of her territory. 
From this it necessarily follows that this State can neither require a license to sell, or prohibit 
the sale of, liquor on a vessel of any kind habitually anchored below low water mark.” July

no provision as to the sale of alcohol,” id., the opinion concludes “that the State of Maryland 
has jurisdiction of this case, and the liquor laws of that State must control.” /d.

e. Waterway construction activities. 


Notwithstanding the claim it makes in its Complaint that the Commonwealth and its 
citizens have the unrestricted right to build piers and wharves from the Virginia side of the

activities in precisely the same way it has addressed every other activity that takes place on

admitted Maryland’s jurisdiction.

the Potomac occurs in the tidal portion, below Washington, D.C. Owners of riparian

groins, jetties, or breakwaters to protect their shoreline property against erosion. As was also

than thirty years, could not be made the subject of dispute by reference to courses and 
distances called for in the patents under which the parties claimed, or on some newly 
discovered construction of their title deeds. We see no reason why the same principle should 
not apply in the present case... .” United States v. Stone, 69 U.S. 525, 537 (1865) (quoted 
in Rosebud Sioux Tribe v. Kneip, 430 U.S. at 605 n.28). Indeed, the arbitrators who 
established the boundary between Maryland and Virginia stated that “[t]he length of time 
which creates a right by prescription in a private party raises a presumption in favor of a 
State, that is to say, twenty years.” (App. 28a). Regardless of the period of time required, 
Maryland’s longstanding assertion of authority over the Potomac in all respects, coupled with 
Virginia’s acquiescence in and recognition of that authority, demonstrate Maryland’s night 
to regulate the activity at issue in this case. 

For these reasons, judgment should be entered in favor of Maryland and against 
Virginia because Maryland has acted properly in regulating the Potomac as it has for the past 


two centuries.

For the reasons stated, Maryland’s motion for summary judgment should be granted.

INTRODUCTION 


In claiming that it has unrestricted access to the Potomac River, Virginia has failed 
to dispute any of the evidence that Maryland has produced demonstrating Maryland’s 
entitlement to the entry of summary judgment in its favor. While Virginia argues that the 
Compact gives it the unrestricted right to build wharves and other improvements in the 
Potomac, nowhere in its 170-page brief does the Commonwealth make any reference to the 
undisputed fact that, at the time it entered into the Compact with Maryland, Virginia had 
already explicitly and unambiguously relinquished in its original Constitution any and all 
rights of “property, jurisdiction, and government” in or over the Potomac, save for a very 
limited claim to the shores or strands bordering the river. Such a sweeping surrender of 
sovereignty necessarily informs and defines the “plain language” that Virginia contends 
gives it an absolute right of access to the Potomac, and yet the Commonwealth does not even 
mention it. Virginia’s attempt to bury this omission by arguing that the boundary was in 
dispute is based exclusively on statements that were made in entirely different contexts more 
than a hundred years after the Compact was written, and is no substitute for the need, in light 
of both States’ undisputed understanding of Maryland’s claim to the river at the time the 
Compact was formed, for clear and unequivocal language that either stripped Maryland of, 
or vested Virginia with, governmental authority over access to the Potomac. Virginia’s claim 


fails because that language simply does not exist in the Compact. 


Like its failure to even so much as acknowledge its own Constitution’s recognition 
of Maryland’s authority over the Potomac, Virginia also offers no evidence that refutes the 
undisputed understanding of both States, as consistently demonstrated since the time of the 
Black-Jenkins Award and manifested by the hundreds of exhibits that Maryland has 
produced, that the boundary line described there was and continues to be an absolute line of 


MD L-97 


sovereignty for all purposes. It is undisputed that, since the beginning of the 1900s, 
Virginia’s Attorneys General, Governors, and other government officials have expressed 
unequivocally that Virginia has no jurisdiction of any kind beyond low-water mark, either 
on a wharf or other improvement built from the Virginia side, or otherwise. -It is also 
undisputed, indeed, Virginia has stipulated, that it does not exercise its authority beyond that 
point and that it has not objected to Maryland’s multiple exercises of its authority over any 
and all property and activity beyond low-water mark, whether that authority is in the form 
of taxes, criminal jurisdiction, occupational safety and health laws, liquor regulations, 
gambling statutes, or any of the other laws and ordinances that Maryland has enforced on the 


Potomac. 


Virginia’s actions speak louder than its words, and even its words, as set forth in its 
brief, neither question nor dispute three unshakable truths: Maryland has had title to the 
Potomac River since 1632 and has been its undisputed owner since 1877; Maryland has 
proven its ownership by exercising every possible police power with respect to any and all 
property and activities in the river on the Maryland side of the boundary line; and Virginia 
has never taken a single action that has in any way interfered with Maryland’s exercise of 
sovereignty over the Potomac. These truths are borne out by a highly developed and 
uncontroverted factual record and point overwhelmingly to the conclusion that the river 


belongs to Maryland and is subject to Maryland’s regulatory authority. 


As the Special Master noted previously, “[s]ummary judgment is appropriate when 
the pleadings and other materials show that there is no genuine issue of material fact and the 
moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” Mem. of Dec. No. 4 at 5. The 


exhibits that Maryland has filed in support of its summary judgment motion “demonstrate 


MD L-98 


the absence of a genuine issue of material fact,” Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 323 
(1986), and Virginia has neither disputed the authenticity of that evidence nor offered “any 
significant probative evidence tending to support the complaint.” Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, 
Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 256 (1986) (quotations and citation omitted). Judgment should 
accordingly be entered in favor of Maryland and against Virginia because Maryland’s proof 
shows that Maryland’s ownership of the Potomac includes the sovereign right to regulate any 
activity, including waterway construction and water withdrawals, occurring in the river 


beyond the low-water mark.

A.  Maryland’s Ownership Of The Potomac Includes The Right

give Maryland the authority to regulate Virginia’s Potomac River access rights,” VA Br. at

case. The Commonwealth does not dispute that Maryland is the owner of the Potomac to the 
low-water mark on the Virginia side of the river, nor does it disagree with the well-settled 
rule that such ownership includes the right to regulate activity that takes place in the river. 
Virginia thus misstates the question in terms of whether the Compact or the Black-Jenkins 
Award gives Maryland the authority to regulate, as that authority is a basic aspect of any 
State’s ownership and sovereign control of a river within its borders. See, e.g., Utah Division
