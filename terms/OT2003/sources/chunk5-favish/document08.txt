QUESTION PRESENTED 


Exemption 7(C) of the Freedom of Information Act 
protects law enforcement information from public disclosure 
that “could reasonably be expected to constitute an 
unwarranted invasion of personal privacy.” 5 U.S.C. 
§ 552(b)(7)(C). 


The question presented is whether, under Exemption 
7(C), the Office of Independent Counsel properly withheld 
photographs of the body of Vincent W. Foster, Jr., former 
Deputy White House Counsel, taken by the Park Police at 
the scene of his suicide.

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


Respondent Favish's ghoulish attempt to obtain and 
publicize photographs of Vince Foster lying dead in Fort 
Marcy Park is not supported by any legitimate public interest 
and, if successful, would trample on fundamental privacy 
interests of Mr. Foster's survivors. 


l. FOIA Exemption 7(C) authorizes agencies to 
withhold law enforcement information where public 
disclosure “could reasonably be expected to constitute an 
unwarranted invasion of personal privacy.” 5 U.S.C § 
552(b)(7)(C). Public disclosure may be “warranted” only if 
it would throw light on the operations of government. In this 
case, plaintiff asserts that public disclosure may show that 
the numerous investigations of Vincent Foster’s death — all 
of which concluded that he committed suicide — ignored 
contrary evidence and were mishandled. But the D.C. 
Circuit, in a case involving the same photographs at issue 
here, correctly concluded that plaintiff's evidence of official 


9 


wrongdoing was not compelling or persuasive and thus 
should not be allowed to override the invasion of the Foster 
family’s privacy that publication of photographs of Mr. 
Foster’s body would entail. 


The D.C. Circuit was right. Given the numerous 
investigations of the Foster death, the unanimous conclusion 
that he committed suicide, and the voluminous material 
already made public, it may “reasonably be expected” that 
publication of these photographs will add nothing further to 
public understanding of the workings of government and will 
accomplish nothing but further intrusion on the Foster 
family’s privacy. The D.C. Circuit correctly required 
compelling, or at least persuasive, evidence that public 
release of these photographs would further the public interest 
by showing official wrongdoing before concluding that such 
release was warranted. The Ninth Circuit’s minimal 
standard for overriding privacy concerns would undermine 
the significant privacy protections Congress intended 
Exemption 7(C) to provide. The kinds of minor 
inconsistencies plaintiff alleges are typically present in any 
large, high-profile investigation. If they are enough to 
overcome privacy protection, persons like plaintiff will have 
free rein to rummage through law enforcement records and 
publish any material they obtain, no matter how damaging 
that may be to privacy nights. 


The Ninth Circuit also erred by failing to recognize 
that the numerous investigations of Foster’s death, all of 
which found that he shot himself, bolstered the conclusion 
that publication of the photographs was not warranted 
because they could not reasonably be expected to support a 
finding that there was official falsification. And the Ninth 
Circuit’s error was compounded by its failure to find any 
plausible nexus between the photographs and the public 
interest asserted as a justification for their public release. 


10 


2. Unanimous case law under FOIA establishes 
that there is a survivors’ privacy interest and that public 
disclosure of autopsy and other photographs of a corpse 
violates the privacy of the surviving family. The decision 
rejecting a request for FOIA disclosure of the autopsy 
photographs of President Kennedy is just one example of the 
firm stance courts have taken against such appalling 
requests. Katz v. Nat'l Archives & Records Admin, 862 


F.Supp. 476 (D.D.C. 1994), aff'd on other grounds, 68 F.3d 
1438 (D.C. Cir. 1995). 


This Court should follow this authority. Publication 
of photographs of a family member’s body is a highly 
offensive intrusion into the family’s privacy. It is the 
modern equivalent of putting the body itself on public 
display — an act that since ancient times has been regarded as 
a highly offensive intrusion on family rights. The harm 
exists whether or not the photographs display gruesome 
details. Both law and long-standing tradition establish that a 
deceased's family has the right to deal with the body 
according to the family’s customs and beliefs, and to take 
final leave of their loved one in a private manner, if they so 
choose. Public release of these photographs would intrude 
on that right and cause the Foster family extreme grief,

ARGUMENT 


PLAINTIFF DID NOT MAKE A SHOWING 
OF PUB] ‘C INTEREST SUFFICIENT TO 
OVERCOME THE FOSTER FAMILY’S 
SIGNIFICANT PRIVACY INTEREST IN 
PHOTOGRAPHS OF MR. FOSTER’S BODY. 


FOIA Exemption 7(C) protects from public 
disclosure law enforcement records or information the 
production of which “could reasonably be expected to 
constitute an unwarranted invasion of personal privacy.” 5 
U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(C). To determine whether an invasion of 
privacy could reasonably be expected to be “unwarranted,” 

“a court must balance the public interest in disclosure against 
the interest Congress intended the Exemption to protect.” 
United States Dept. of Justice v. Reporters Committee for 
Freedom of the Press, 489 U.S. 749, 776 (1989). 


The court of appeals erred in this case on both sides 
of the balance, by i) concluding that Favish had 
demonstrated a public interest in disclosure with respect to 
the photographs the court ordered released, and by ii) 
concluding that the invasion of privacy occasioned by 
release of these photographs did not outweigh the purported 
public interest served. The result is a decision that allows a 
shocking intrusion into the Foster family's privacy that is not 
justified by any public interest in understanding the workings 
of government. The court of appeals conclusion is all the 
more disturbing because it confirmed the district court's 
palpably misguided opinion without undertaking to wmite an 
opinion that explained its problematic conclusions. We thus 
are left with an unprincipled decision that may well render 
government agencies investigating incidents of violent death 
unable to prevent gross intrusions on the privacy of surviving 
family members.

Under FOIA, public disclosure that could reasonably 
be expected to invade privacy rights is “warranted” only if it 
would “contribute[] significantly to public understanding of 
the operation or activities of the government.” Reporters 
Committee, 489 U.S. at 775 (emphasis in original). In the 
courts below, plaintiff contended that public disclosure of the 
photographs of Mr. Foster’s body would meet this standard, 
because the photographs might show that the various 
investigations of Mr. Foster’s death — all of which concluded 
that he committed suicide — were mishandled and 
untrustworthy. 


The D.C. Circuit properly concluded that public 
disclosure of the same photographs would not meet that 
standard, because the various inconsistencies alleged in the 
evidence concerning Mr. Foster’s death -— the same 
inconsistencies Favish alleges here — were not “persuasive 
evidence . . . much less compelling evidence” of falsification 
in the Starr Report or in the other investigations also 
concluding that Mr. Foster committed suicide. Accuracy in 
Media, 194 F.3d 120, 124 (D.C. Cir. 1999). 


The Ninth Circuit’s opposite conclusion stemmed 
from three errors: 1) its use of an overly lax standard for 
determining whether the plaintiff had shown a public interest 
in disclosure; 2) its refusal to take into account the 
unanimous conclusion of the five previous investigations, 
including that by Independent Counsel Starr, all of which 
found that Mr. Foster committed suicide, and the extensive 
public disclosures that accompanied those investigations;

and 3) its failure to find a nexus between the photographs 
released and the asserted public interest. 


l. The D.C. Circuit was correct, in a situation 
where government malfeasance is asserted, in requiring 
“compelling” evidence that the photographs would show 
official wrongdoing before concluding that disclosure of the 
photographs would be “warranted.” Accuracy in Media, 194 
F.3d at 124; see also SafeCard Serv., Inc. v. SEC, 926 F.2d 
1197, 1205-6 (D.C. Cir. 1991). This requirement is 
consistent with the language and the purpose of the statute. 
Before its amendment in 1986, Exemption 7(C) accorded 
protection from public disclosure only if it “would 
constitute” an unwarranted invasion of personal privacy.’ 
Exemption 7(C) was amended in 1986 to provide protection 
if public disclosure “could reasonably be expected” to 
constitute an unwarranted invasion of personal privacy.* 
Absent compelling evidence that the photographs may be 
expected to contradict the conclusion of the Starr Report and 
the four previous investigations that Mr. Foster committed 
suicide, it “could reasonably be expected” that the 
photographs will be consistent with that determination and 
thus their public disclosure would be an “unwarranted” 
invasion of the Foster family’s privacy. Only compelling, or 
at the very least persuasive, evidence would be sufficient to 
dispel the “reasonable expectation” that the invasion of 
privacy resulting from public disclosure of these photographs 
would be “unwarranted.”

family — not disinterested observers — that is at stake. Any 
public display of photographs of Foster’s body, blood-

family’s privacy.
