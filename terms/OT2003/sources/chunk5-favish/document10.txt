In applying FOIA Exemption 7(C) to determine 
whether disclosure of the photographs at issue “could 
reasonably be expected to constitute an unwarranted invasion 
of personal privacy”, this Court must engage in a balancing 
process. Whatever public interest there is in knowing what 
the government is up to must be balanced against the privacy 
interests involved. The requester has the burden of 
“identify[ing] with reasonable specificity the public interest 
that would be served by release.”’ Only disclosure that 
would “contribute significantly to public understanding of 
the operations or activities of government” is in the public 
interest.” 

The Foster family has powerful privacy interests in 
defeating Respondent Favish’s macabre, disquieting request 
for the ten photographs of Vince Foster’s body taken shortly 
after his suicide. The family seeks to avoid seeing images of 
Mr. Foster’s body on television or in supermarket tabloids. 
It wants to be free of the oppressive knowledge that the 
photographs are on the Internet and available worldwide to 
millions of people in virtual perpetuity. It desires to forestall 
the noxious intrusions by the media and others that 
inevitably will follow disclosure of the photographs. 


There is, on the other hand, no legitimate public 
interest in disclosure. Public cunosity about a tragic death 
does not equate to the public interest. Where uncovering of

IL. The Foster Family Has A Personal Privacy 
Interest In Non-Disclosure Of The Photographs 


1. Favish and some amici argue that, under both 
FOIA and the common law, the right of privacy is personal 
and does not survive death. Accordingly, they argue, 
invasion of a deceased person’s privacy cannot be asserted 
by that person’s surviving family. Favish Brief at 3-18; Silha 
Center Brief at 5-9, Ass'n of Am. Physicians & Surgeons 
Brief at 6-8. 


That argument erroneously assumes that the Foster 
family is seeking to assert Mr. Foster’s right of privacy. To 
the contrary, the Foster family asserts its own right as a 
family against public display of photographs of the dead 
body of a loved one. The right exists to protect the surviving 
family against the deeply personal hurt and indignity that 
would be occasioned, inter alia, by the public display of 
their loved one’s corpse in the media or on the Internet. 
There can be no denying the personal nature of the “shock” 
Mr. Foster’s widow experienced on seeing on television the 
leaked picture of his dead hand holding the gun, or the 
personal nature of “nightmares and _heart-pounding 
insomnia” that disclosure caused his sister. JA 91, 93. The 
mortification and horror the family itself would suffer if 
additional photographs are “placed on the Internet for world 
consumption” would be deeply personal. JA 95. Where 
autopsy photographs had been published on Internet sites 
devoted to images of violent death, one court found that “the 
knowledge that these photographs are available to the public 
for unnecessary perusal caused overwhelming personal 


4 


distress and trauma” to the families involved. Earnhardt v. 
Volusia County, No. 2001-30373-CICI, 2001 WL 992068, 
*3 (Fla. Cir. Ct. July 10, 2001). The public dissemination of 
such photographs invades the “personal privacy” of the 
surviving family under any reasonable definition. 


All the courts that have considered the matter under 
the FOIA, including the court below, recognize that the 
statute protects the privacy nghts of surviving family 
members. Favish v. Office of Indep. Counsel, 217 F.3d 
1168, 1173 (9" Cir. 2000), Pet. App. 13a (“the personal 
privacy in the statutory exemption extends to the memory of 
the deceased held by those tied closely to the deceased by 
blood or love”); Accuracy in Media, Inc. v. Nat'l Park Serv., 
194 F.3d at 123 (‘[w]hile law enforcement sometimes 
necessitates the display of such ghoulish materials, there 
seems nothing unnatural in saying that the interest asserted 
against it by spouse, parent, and children of the deceased is 
one of privacy.”); Badhwar v. U.S. Dep't of the Air Force, 
829 F.2d 182, 185-6 (D.C. Cir. 1987) (autopsy reports that 
“shock the sensibilities of surviving kin” are subject to 
privacy exemption); Hale v. U.S. Dep't of Justice, 973 F.2d 
at 902 (“personal privacy interests of the victim’s family” 
would be invaded by public release of photographs of 
murder victim’s body); New York Times Co. v. NASA, 782 
F.Supp. 628, 632 (D.D.C. 1991)(surviving families of 
Challenger astronauts have a “substantial” privacy interest 
against public disclosure of tapes of their last moments); 
Katz v. Nat'l Archives & Records Admin., 862 F.Supp. 476, 
483 (D.D.C. 1994)(surviving family has privacy interest in 
autopsy photographs of President Kennedy), aff'd on other 
grounds, 68 F.3d 1438 (D.C. Cir. 1995). 


2. There is, moreover, significant support in the 
common law for recognition of a family’s privacy right in 
images of the body of a deceased family member. Recently, 
the Supreme Court of Washington, sitting en banc, sustained

County, 961 P.2d 333 (Wash. 1998). Reid relied on a

photograph of it, a like wrong was done; the

However, as this Court stated in Reporters Committee, “the

not the same as the question whether a tort action might lie

Images of the dead body of a family member are normally

circumstances for law enforcement; certainly such images

is no common-law tradition in this country of public access 
to autopsy or death-scene photographs. Petitioner’s Brief at

There are at least two reasons of no relevance in the 
FOIA context why a court might be reluctant to recognize a 
common-law nght of privacy against publication of 
gruesome details of a family member’s death. The 
consequence of recognizing a common-law nght is to allow 
a survivor to sue for damages. In that context, some courts 
believe that “the danger of spurious claims is too great.” 
Cordell v. Detective Publications, Inc., 419 F.2d 989, 991 
(6™ Cir. 1969). See also Smith v. City of Artesia, 772 P.2d 
373, 375 (N.M. 1989), citing “possible misuse” of a damages 
action and the “traditional reluctance to permit damages that 
are solely emotional.” 


, In Marzen v. U.S. Dep't of Health & Human Serv., 825 F.2d 
1148, 152 (7" Cir. 1987), the court stated that “the privacy interest 
protected under the FOIA extends beyond the common law.” 


8 


In the FOIA context, however, persons asserting a 
privacy right are not improperly attempting to gain a 
damages recovery. Unlike plaintiffs in a damages action, the 
Foster family seeks no profit from its distress; it simply is 
striving to prevent a further intrusive prolongation of the 
nightmarish ordeal it has suffered for more than a decade. 


In addition, some common-law cases involved 
publication of the details of a loved one’s death that already 
were a matter of public record. In one case, the defendant 
had identified plaintiff's daughter as the victim of a rape and 
murder, on the basis of publicly-available records of a 
judicial proceeding. Cox Broadcasting Corp. v. Cohn, 420 
U.S. 469 (1975). In another case, defendant had published a 
grisly account of the murder of plaintiff's daughter, where 
the facts already were a part of the public record. Cordell v. 
Detective Publications, Inc., 307 F.Supp. 1212, 1218 (E.D. 
Tenn. 1968), aff'd, 419 F.2d 989 (6" Cir. 1969). Allowing a 
damages action for violation of privacy against an entity that 
publishes facts already on the public record, this Court has 
found, would be a violation of the First Amendment. Cox 
Broadcasting Corp. v. Cohn, supra.® 


By contrast, the issue in the present case is not 
whether the media may publish material already on the 
public record. The issue is whether death scene photographs 
should be released to the public. Favish has no First 
Amendment right to obtain these photographs; any rights he 
may have are derived exclusively from the FOIA. 
Application of FOIA Exemption 7(C) to protect the Foster 
family’s privacy raises no constitutional issue. Houchins v. 
KQED, 438 U.S. 1 (1978).

4. Some amici argue that the Foster family’s 
privacy interest is “diminished” because Foster was a 
significant public official who committed suicide while 
embroiled in a high-profile political controversy. Reporters

However, while government officials themselves “may have 
a somewhat diminished privacy interest,” they “do not 
surrender all rights to personal privacy when they accept a

high government official is not required to endure the same 
privacy intrusions that may be inflicted on the official. Cf 
Time, Inc. v. Firestone, 424-U.S. 448, 453 (1976) (wife of 
Firestone heir not a “public figure”). After the death of the 
Governor of Washington, Dixie Lee Ray, her family’s nght 
of privacy protected them from the indignity of having her 
autopsy photographs passed around at cocktail parties. Reid 
v. Pierce County, 961 P.2d at 335.’ Despite Foster’s status 
as a public official, the Foster family had the right to choose, 
as it did, a closed-casket funeral. J.A. 91. The 
indiscriminate circulation on the Internet of photographs of 
his body would make a mockery of that choice.

5. Another amicus brief, while conceding that 
the two cases involving the photographs at issue “address 
only the interests of family members in avoiding graphic or 
emotionally disturbing images of a deceased loved one,” 
expresses concern that a survivor’s privacy right “easily 
could evolve into a generalized ‘family privacy’ interest,” 
precluding any damaging revelations about the deceased. 
Silha Center Brief at 20 (citing Accuracy in Media v. Nat'l 
Park Serv., 194 F.3d 120 (D.C. Cir. 1999); Favish v. Office 
of Indep. Counsel, 217 F.3d 1168 (9™ Cir. 2000)). However, 
as observed in our opening brief, the moments immediately 
surrounding death are viewed as unique and private by both 
law and tradition. The case law recognizes that the surviving 
family has a legal right to dispose of the body of a deceased 
family member, and long-standing cultural tradition regards 
the public display of a corpse without family consent as 
deeply offensive. Brief for Respondents Anthony and 
Moody at 25-27 and nn.15, 17. There is no comparable legal 
protection or cultural stricture against criticism of, or 
damaging revelations about, the deceased, even though such 
conduct also may cause the surviving family distress. 
However a family’s right of privacy may be limited, it 
clearly attaches where anguishing moments surrounding 
death are displayed or recorded. 


For this reason, cases extend the family’s privacy 
right to sounds and images of loved ones nearing death. New 
York Times Co. v. NASA, 782 F.Supp. 628 (D.D.C. 1991) 
(rejecting FOIA request for tape of last moments of the 
Challenger space shuttle crew); New York Times Co. v. City 
of New York Fire Dep't, 754 N.Y.S.2d 517, 523 (N.Y. Sup. 
Ct. 2003) (rejecting request under State public information 
law for transcript and tapes of “calls for help in extremis” 
from World Trade Center victims). Such a limited 
application of family’s privacy rights presents no danger of 
the blanket extension of such rights that amicus 
hypothesizes.

Indeed, the alternative to recognition of such a 
privacy right is unthinkable. There are Internet sites devoted 
to the public display of gruesome images of dead bodies, 
including autopsy photographs. Petitioner’s Brief at 30 n.17; 
Earnhardt Brief at 5-6. To visit those sites is to be repulsed. 
If there is no privacy right of survivors against such 
offensive displays, autopsy photographs in the hands of the 
federal government soon routinely will become the grist of 
the grisly displays on such sites. It is inconceivable that 
Congress, in enacting Exemption 7(C)’s protections of 
“personal” privacy, intended to deny privacy protection to 
surviving families seeking to avoid the gross indignity and 
anguish such displays would cause. 


II. The FOIA Balancing Required By Exemption 
7(C) Is Not Tilted Toward Disclosure 


Favish and the some amici argue that, because 
disclosure is the overriding purpose of FOIA, the privacy 
exemptions should be construed narrowly to exclude any 
privacy right of a surviving family. Favish Brief at 4, 8, 47; 
Silha Center Brief at 1; Reporters Committee Brief at 7-8, 
9-10. However, while other FOIA exemptions may be 
construed narrowly to favor the Act’s overall policy of 
disclosure, Exemption 7(C) is “notable” in requiring a 
balance that “is not similarly ‘tilted emphatically in favor of 
disclosure.’” Senate of Puerto Rico v. U.S. Dep't of Justice, 
823 F.2d 574, 587 (D.C. Cir. 1987) (R. Ginsburg, J.) 
(quoting Bast v. U.S. Dep't of Justice, 665 F.2d 1251, 1254 
(D.C. Cir. 1981)). Indeed, Congress has shown a concern in 
ensuring the effectiveness of Exemption 7(C)’s protection of 
privacy by amending it to broaden the scope of its protection. 
Reporters Committee, 489 U.S. 749, 756 (1989). Moreover, 
the legislative history of the Exemption 6 privacy provision 
shows that Congress regarded protection of privacy as 
“equally important” as the broad policy of public disclosure. 
S. Rep. No. 813, 89" Cong. 1* Sess. 3 (1965), quoted in U.S.

Dep't of the Air Force v. Rose, 425 U.S. at 372 n.9. In these 
circumstances, Exemption 7(C) must be construed to provide 
the effective protection Congress intended.” 


To support the argument that Exemption 7(C) should 
be construed narrowly, two amicus briefs cite U.S. Dep't of 
the Air Force v. Rose, 425 U.S. 352, 361 (1976), U.S. Dep't

Agency v. John Doe Corp., 493 U.S. 146 (1989). Reporters

these cases did not involve Exemption 7(C). Moreover, in 
John Doe the Court stated generally that, in construing
