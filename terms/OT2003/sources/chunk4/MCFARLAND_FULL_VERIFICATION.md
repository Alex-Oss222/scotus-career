## Record and component precision

The developed factual account is a **published recitation in Garwood's dissent**, not a trial transcript or a majority factual finding. It reproduces testimony and the relevant charge, which can support a bounded account with that source status. Nothing in the remaining writings supplies contradictory record testimony.

|---|---|---|

| 7 | Quickway Shopping, December 11, 1998 | Approximately $50 according to the employee present; the absent former owner's uncertain estimate was approximately $100, nn. 4 and 10 |

The ten-count indictment charged five robberies and five corresponding 18 U.S.C. §924(c)(1) counts. Counts 3 and 4, concerning Haynie's on November 24, resulted in acquittal. Four Hobbs Act and four corresponding firearm convictions remained. Although the Hobbs counts alleged an attempt as well, the jury received only completed-offense submissions. There was no conspiracy allegation or count, and the jury was told to consider each count and its evidence separately (pp. 4–5, nn. 2–3). No aggregation rationale should replace the separate proof of each submitted offense or invent an attempt/conspiracy verdict.

All four stores stocked merchandise shown to originate outside Texas. Gateway bought from three Texas wholesalers; one testified that 95 percent of its distribution, including to Gateway, originated outside Texas. Quickway bought from a Texas wholesaler whose purchases included identified out-of-state products. The identity and location of suppliers to Buy-Low and Jeff Stop were not proved. The recitation does not establish direct interstate purchasing by every retailer (pp. 6–9, nn. 5–6).

The owners connected store receipts with replenishing inventory and described loss of operating money as impairing that ability. Footnote 7 reproduces those answers. The record nevertheless contains **no testimony that a store actually reduced or delayed a merchandise purchase because of the charged robbery**. Quickway's former owner said purchases could be maintained by using bank funds or another source; Gateway's sales and purchases rose during the seasonal period. Those facts do not erase the cash depletion, but they preclude a statement that an actual reduction of interstate purchases was proved (pp. 9–11).

Gateway's owner separately testified that the robbery stretched the business and caused distributor payments to be deferred by two or three days. The dissent identifies this as the only evidence of even a slight delay in payment caused by the charged robberies. Thus neither “actual inventory purchases were reduced” nor “no operational impact was proved” accurately states the complete recitation. Operating-capital depletion, ability to replenish, actual inventory purchasing, and supplier-payment timing are distinct facts.

Footnote 8 reports that Buy-Low closed later, after another robbery, and identifies both sale of the premises by its landlord and the owner's frustration with robberies. It supplies no finding that this charged robbery caused closure. Footnote 9's wholesaler testimony about extra deliveries after robberies was general; there was no evidence of that occurrence at these four stores. Quickway also sold Minnesota-company money orders, but the monthly figure and store-specific allocation were unclear (pp. 8–10; n. 28). Do not turn that uncertain collateral activity into an independently established dispositive connection.

The percentage-allocation sentence on printed page 9 repeats Gateway while omitting Buy-Low in its enumeration. It should not be silently repaired into precise per-store percentages. The reproduced qualitative testimony and express Quickway profit-margin example are sufficient to describe the operating-capital issue without relying on that apparent textual error.

## Preservation, charge and remedy boundaries

McFarland timely moved for acquittal and renewed his motion, asserting among other things that the required interstate nexus was unproved for every Hobbs count (p. 11). This preserves a nexus sufficiency objection, not merely a constitutional facial attack. The source's opening description identifies both constitutional and statutory insufficiency and a charge challenge (p. 3).

The quoted charge allowed a minimal effect and permitted an inference from a business's regular out-of-state purchases that robbery might impair a future purchase. It then required a finding beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant's conduct affected interstate commerce. This is a **permissive inference with an express criminal burden**, not a conclusive instruction that every commercial label establishes the element. The preserved instruction objections actually recited on page 11 target “any,” the minimal-effect sentence, and refusal to insert “substantially.” The complete opinion supplies **no separately identified objection to the permissive-inference sentence**, no holding that it directed a verdict on the commerce element, and no broader instruction defect adopted by the affirming court. Absence of another objection from this published account is not a finding that no other objection exists in the trial record.

Garwood's footnote 53 stops short of resolving the charge question after concluding that the evidence failed his constitutional standard. His operative proposed disposition reverses the Hobbs convictions; he then states that every §924(c)(1) conviction is concededly dependent on its corresponding Hobbs conviction and would also fall (p. 67). The printed conclusion once says §942(c)(1), an obvious transposition; the preceding sentence and the complete account consistently identify §924(c)(1). This source presents no independent firearm-element, firearm-instruction or sentencing defect. The actual en banc judgment affirms conviction and sentence; a later disposition must specify its effect on the four corresponding firearm counts if that derivative ground is within review.

Robinson's entering rule separates the legally sufficient commercial connection from its magnitude and retains proof of the statutory element. Applying that rule requires distinguishing actual removal of operating capital and the permissible proof of its commercial function from an added requirement to identify a cancelled replenishment order. The factual distinctions above inform that application; this verification does not decide whether they satisfy Robinson, alter the frozen commitments, or adopt the lower dissent's rejected legal premises.
