## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

* **Case Name:** Demore v. Kim

* **Citation or Docket:** No. 01-1491; Kim v. Ziglar, 276 F.3d 523 (9th Cir. 2002)

* **October Term:** 2003

* **Entry Stage and Event:** MERITS; decision on reargument of the identified judgment. The case was argued January 15, 2003, restored to the calendar for reargument in October Term 2003 by order of April 29, 2003, and reargued October 6, 2003.

* **Simulated Event Date:** 2003-10-14

* **Question Presented:** Whether respondent's mandatory detention under 8 U.S.C. §1226(c) violates the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment, where respondent was convicted of an aggravated felony after his admission to the United States. Whether 8 U.S.C. §1226(e) withdraws habeas jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. §2241 over the challenge is a necessary threshold question. The premise that the convictions are aggravated felonies is disputed (Material Record).

* **Question Granted:** Certiorari was granted June 28, 2002, on the question presented. The April 29, 2003 restoration order directed no supplemental question, briefing or filing. Reargument addresses the granted question, the §1226(e) threshold and Kim's preserved alternative statutory ground.

* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Ninth Circuit (January 9, 2002) affirmed the Northern District of California's conditional habeas judgment (Kim v. Schiltgen). The district court had held §1226(c) facially invalid and ordered an individualized bond determination. The Ninth Circuit held the provision unconstitutional as applied to Kim as a lawful permanent resident and required a bail hearing with reasonable promptness on flight risk and danger. It rejected Kim's argument that §1226(c) does not reach him. Under the April 29, 2003 order, that judgment and Kim's release on $5,000 bond remain in effect.

* **Relief Requested:** The federal immigration officials, represented by the Solicitor General, seek reversal. Kim seeks affirmance on the constitutional ground or, alternatively, on the ground that §1226(c) does not apply to him before a final removal order.

* **Material Record:** Kim, a citizen of South Korea, entered the United States in 1984 at age six and became a lawful permanent resident in 1986; by his account his mother is a citizen and his father and brother are permanent residents. California convicted him of first-degree burglary in 1996 and of petty theft with priors in 1997. He received a three-year term on the second conviction and a concurrent two-year term on the first. On February 2, 1999, the day after his release from state custody, the INS arrested him and held him without bond under §1226(c). Its charge, filed March 10, 1999, alleged that the 1997 conviction was an aggravated felony under §1227(a)(2)(A)(iii). His withholding-of-removal hearing was set for July 1999 and, at his request, continued to September to let him obtain documents for that application. He filed for habeas in May 1999. After the August 1999 order, and more than six months in custody, the INS released him on $5,000 bond. Kim's brief, citing the joint appendix, states that the INS then found him neither a threat nor a significant flight risk. The record does not establish that an immigration-judge hearing preceded release. The record identifies no request for a Joseph hearing before the habeas proceeding; that absence does not establish waiver of classification objections. Kim disclaims any challenge to the initial authority to take him into custody. In June 2002 the Ninth Circuit held that the California petty-theft-with-priors offense does not categorically qualify as an aggravated felony, and Kim stated that he would contest that charge. In August 2002 the INS added the 1996 conviction and a charge of two crimes involving moral turpitude under §1227(a)(2)(A)(ii), which is also a §1226(c) predicate. Kim states that he will contend that neither conviction supports removal and that he remains eligible for relief. The Government describes him as conceding that his convictions place him within §1226(c). Accepting initial custody is not necessarily conceding every removal or category question, and the two descriptions must be kept distinct. The Government's aggregate detention figures are not findings about Kim. The record supplies no removal order and no later change in custody.

* **Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** The parties' merits briefs do not press §1226(e); an amicus argues that it bars review, and the Court must resolve jurisdiction regardless. Kim's release following the conditional judgment leaves a live controversy, because reversal would expose him to renewed mandatory custody. The Government calls the habeas petition a facial challenge; the Ninth Circuit decided only the application to Kim as a permanent resident. Kim's statutory argument was pressed and rejected below. No challenge to a discretionary bond amount is presented. Since March 1, 2003, the Department of Homeland Security has exercised the former INS's detention functions, and the immigration-court custody regulations now appear at 8 C.F.R. §1003.19. The transfer changes neither the statutory text nor the posture.

* **Relevant Dates:** Convictions, 1996 and 1997; INS custody, February 2, 1999; charging document, March 10, 1999; habeas petition, May 1999; conditional judgment and release on bond, August 1999; Ninth Circuit judgment, January 9, 2002; Ninth Circuit petty-theft classification decision, June 6, 2002; certiorari granted, June 28, 2002; amended charges, August 2002; argument, January 15, 2003; restoration order, April 29, 2003; reargument, October 6, 2003.

### Issue 1: Habeas jurisdiction and §1226(e)

* **Controlling Law:** Reno v. Goncalves (unanimous on jurisdiction) and Kolster v. INS hold that the 1996 amendments did not clearly withdraw §2241 jurisdiction over substantial statutory or constitutional objections to executive authority, without reweighing discretionary equities or retrying deportability facts. Calcano-Martinez v. INS holds that the permanent review provisions do not clearly displace that jurisdiction over a substantial objection that custody or removal rests on an unlawful eligibility rule; the retained review must be capable of deciding the objection and affording effective relief. INS v. St. Cyr applies the channel to an independently filed legal-eligibility action. Zadvydas v. Davis (Part I, unanimous) holds that §2241 permits independently proper challenges to the legal authority for immigration detention, including asserted authority to resume custody, without reopening removal orders. The court must be able to determine that authority and afford effective relief, but may not retry removability or reweigh lawful discretion. The custody, respondent, forum and exhaustion predicates remain.

* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** A review withdrawal is construed by the acts and objects it names. Restricting one channel does not itself repeal an independent writ, although a clear withdrawal needs no particular verbal formula or section reference. Section 2241 reaches substantial legal or constitutional objections to executive authority to detain or deport, with effective relief if warranted. Reweighing discretionary equities and general factual retrial are distinct.

* **Other Governing Law:** 8 U.S.C. §1226(e): the Attorney General's discretionary judgment regarding application of §1226 is not subject to review, and no court may set aside any action or decision by the Attorney General under §1226 regarding the detention or release of any alien or the grant, revocation or denial of bond or parole. 28 U.S.C. §2241; the Suspension Clause.

* **Material Other Authority:** Reno v. American-Arab Anti-Discrimination Committee confines §1252(g) to its three named actions. It preserves existing jurisdiction for a substantial constitutional claim that lacks a capable channel, applying Webster v. Doe's clear-intent requirement. INS v. Ventura (November 4, 2002) returns unperformed, statutorily assigned factual judgments to the agency. It preserves independent judicial review of law and gives no new deference to agency legal conclusions. Parra v. Perryman, 172 F.3d 954 (7th Cir. 1999), distinguished a challenge to the statute itself from a challenge to its implementation.

* **Existing Rule's Reach:** Entering law preserves §2241 against provisions that do not clearly withdraw it and separates legal-authority review from discretionary judgments. No decision construes §1226(e). No individual discretionary bond determination is challenged.

* **Open Point:** Whether §1226(e)'s two sentences withdraw §2241 jurisdiction over a challenge to a statutory framework that forbids individualized custody review, as distinct from the Attorney General's discretionary judgments and individual detention or bond decisions. If they do, whether the Court must reach the Suspension Clause question that entering law leaves open.

### Issue 2: Statutory reach of §1226(c) and contested category membership

* **Controlling Law:** No decision of this Court construes §1226(c). Zadvydas v. Davis construes detention authority by its removal purpose and constitutional limits rather than invalidating the statute. INS v. National Center for Immigrants' Rights sustains a general release rule because the rule preserves individual status determinations. Officials must address colorable citizenship or status claims, and the rule presumes no disputed deportability.

* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** General immigration custody and release rules operate together with the individualized determinations on which their validity depends. Custody authority is read in light of its statutory purpose.

* **Other Governing Law:** Section 1226(c)(1)(B) requires custody of an alien who "is deportable" by reason of offenses covered in §1227(a)(2)(A)(ii), (A)(iii), (B), (C) or (D). Section 1226(c)(2) permits release only when necessary for witness protection under 18 U.S.C. §3521 and the alien shows that he will not endanger others and is likely to appear. Section 1226(a) otherwise permits release on bond or conditional parole. Section 1231(a)(6) permits supervised release of the same criminal categories after a final order and the removal period. Under 8 C.F.R. §3.19(h)(2) (now §1003.19(h)(2)) and Matter of Joseph, 22 I. & N. Dec. 799 (BIA 1999), a detainee may seek an immigration judge's determination that he is not properly included in §1226(c). He prevails only if the Government is substantially unlikely to establish the charge. The pre-1996 mandatory-detention statute applied to aliens "convicted" of an aggravated felony.

* **Material Other Authority:** The Ninth Circuit below found "is deportable" ambiguous but rejected Kim's final-order reading as inconsistent with other statutory language. United States v. Corona-Sanchez, 291 F.3d 1201 (9th Cir. 2002) (en banc), held that California petty theft with priors does not categorically qualify as an aggravated felony.

* **Existing Rule's Reach:** No decision addresses whether "is deportable" requires a final order, whether Joseph's standard permissibly implements the statute, or what review attends a substantial dispute over category membership. Classification of Kim's convictions belongs to the removal proceedings.

* **Open Point:** Whether §1226(c) reaches a resident not yet ordered removed. If it does, whether a resident who raises a substantial, nondilatory challenge to category membership "is deportable" for custody purposes before that challenge is resolved, and what determination and standard decide inclusion.

### Issue 3: Due process limits on detention pending removal proceedings

* **Controlling Law:** Zadvydas v. Davis holds that, for previously admitted persons, §1231(a)(6) authorizes detention after the removal period only for a period reasonably necessary to accomplish removal. A final order, past crime or danger cannot supply permanent preventive authority detached from removal. Six months is presumptively reasonable under that provision; after it, a supported showing shifts a rebuttal burden to the Government. Distinct criminal, civil-commitment and security regimes are excluded, and no universal dangerousness burden is adopted. Reno v. Flores sustains temporary, nonpunitive juvenile custody with release avenues and request-based immigration-judge review against a facial challenge. It does not approve ineffective notice, denial of requested review, improper delay or unlawful custody in a particular case, and custody remains subject to a reasonable-dispatch limit enforceable by habeas. Foucha v. Louisiana bars continued confinement once its authorizing predicate ends; a legitimate preventive purpose and periodic review do not cure the absence of carefully limited substantive authority. Ordinary civil commitment requires clear and convincing proof, but that burden is not made universal. Connecticut Department of Public Safety v. Doe (March 5, 2003) requires a hearing only on a disputed fact that is material under the governing law. Adequate process remains required for a genuinely disputed conviction or other operative inclusion criterion. Gilbert v. Homar (continuing-process holding, unanimous) holds that justification for an immediate deprivation does not independently justify its continued duration; a prompt, meaningful opportunity is assessed by the continuing loss, the reasons for delay and the reliability of the remaining grounds, without a fixed deadline. Krimstock v. Kelly (June 23, 2003) holds that prolonged retention of seized vehicles without timely neutral review violates due process (Part I, unanimous). The hearing must reach the likely merits and the necessity of physical custody rather than adequate security; the City must support its continued deprivation, and the decisionmaker must be able to order interim release (Part III-A, unanimous). The City makes a preliminary showing of probable validity and present need for possession, while the claimant bears the separate innocent-owner showing (Part III-B, eight Justices). Fairley v. Luman (June 23, 2003) requires reasonable, timely identity verification during prolonged detention when repeated specific protests, readily accessible reliable checks and modest burdens show a serious avoidable risk that the wrong person remains confined. Mathews governs the continuing deprivation, and initial authority is distinct from continuation.

* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** Identify the custody authority, its purpose, duration and conditions, and the actual review safeguards. Civil labels do not remove the liberty interest, and a rule from one detention regime is not a universal formula. Post-order immigration custody must remain tied to removal; juvenile custody review is assessed on its actual operation; continued confinement requires a continuing lawful predicate.

* **Other Governing Law:** Fifth Amendment Due Process Clause; 8 U.S.C. §§1226(a), (c), 1231(a)(2), (6); 8 C.F.R. §3.19(h)(2); Matter of Joseph.

* **Material Other Authority:** Carlson v. Landon, 342 U.S. 524 (1952), sustained discretionary detention without bail pending deportation, bounded by the statute; it does not authorize indefinite custody. United States v. Salerno, 481 U.S. 739 (1987), sustained pretrial detention resting on prompt adversarial hearings and Government proof by clear and convincing evidence. Entering law treats Salerno as a focused pretrial regime; its all-applications facial formulation does not govern abortion-access challenges, and its operation elsewhere is unaddressed. Mathews v. Eldridge weighs the private interest, the risk of error and value of added safeguards, and the government's interest. Jackson v. Indiana requires that the nature and duration of commitment bear a reasonable relation to its purpose. Addington v. Texas fixes clear and convincing proof for ordinary civil commitment. Kansas v. Crane requires a functional showing that distinguishes a committable person from a dangerous ordinary recidivist. Kansas v. Hendricks treats a civil label as rebuttable only on the clearest proof of punitive purpose or effect, and on that basis held Hendricks's retrospective commitment to be punishment as applied, in violation of the Ex Post Facto Clause; its due-process discussion is noncontrolling. Sell v. United States (June 16, 2003) requires individualized findings, weighing detention already served, before prosecution-only compelled medication, and sets no universal proof quantum. Cooper v. Oklahoma forbids requiring a criminal defendant to prove incompetence by clear and convincing evidence, because that allocation permits trial when the greater weight of the evidence shows incapacity; defendant-preponderance persuasion remains permissible. 18 U.S.C. §3143(b) requires a convicted defendant seeking release pending appeal to show by clear and convincing evidence that he is unlikely to flee or endanger others, and to raise a substantial question not for delay. Patel v. Zemski, 275 F.3d 299 (3d Cir. 2001), Welch v. Ashcroft, 293 F.3d 213 (4th Cir. 2002), and Hoang v. Comfort, 282 F.3d 1247 (10th Cir. 2002), held §1226(c) invalid as applied to permanent residents. Parra v. Perryman sustained it for an alien who raised no challenge to removal.

* **Existing Rule's Reach:** The Government may initially take custody on a valid statutory predicate, and Kim does not challenge the initial custody authority. Zadvydas's purpose limit and six-month presumption construe §1231(a)(6) and do not directly govern custody before a final order. Flores sustained request-based review in a facial juvenile setting, and Foucha and Crane address commitment regimes. Applying a settled rule to Kim's six months of custody would be ordinary application; whether category membership can replace individual justification would not.

* **Open Point:** Whether, for a permanent resident not yet ordered removed, membership in a §1226(c) category constitutionally substitutes for an individualized determination of flight risk or danger throughout removal proceedings. Whether prolonged or delayed custody, or a substantial category dispute, changes the answer. If review is required, its procedure (including Joseph's adequacy) and the allocation and quantum of proof.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

**Reasoning below:** The district court held §1226(c) facially invalid. The Ninth Circuit decided only the application to Kim as a permanent resident. It declined plenary-power deference because detention is a means of implementing immigration policy. It held that the interests in securing removal and public safety do not justify eliminating every individualized determination of flight risk and danger. It contrasted the individualized custody in Salerno, Hendricks and Carlson, and noted that §1231(a)(6) permits release of the same categories after a final order. Its Hendricks citation concerns the individualized findings Kansas required; this Court's Hendricks decision held that commitment punitive as applied. It rejected Kim's statutory reading.

**Petitioners (through the Solicitor General):** Detention during removal proceedings is incident to the political branches' power to expel and receives deferential review; Carlson and Flores permit categorical judgments. Congress acted on evidence that criminal aliens released during proceedings often failed to appear and reoffended. Detention lasts only for proceedings that have a definite end and are ordinarily brief. The Government cites agency figures of a 47-day average and 30-day median for most cases, and about four further months when a case is appealed. Zadvydas turned on potentially indefinite post-order custody. The Joseph procedure lets a detainee contest inclusion. Kim's petition presented a facial challenge, which fails under Salerno because the provision validly applies in many circumstances, and Kim concedes that his convictions bring him within §1226(c). Proposed rule: §1226(c) is constitutional in the ordinary case. Limiting principle: exceptional circumstances that present special due process concerns may be addressed case by case, and a fixed six-month limit lacks statutory foundation. The Government's merits brief does not contend that §1226(e) bars review. At the January 15, 2003 argument (transcript), the Solicitor General stated that the Government had initially taken that position in this litigation, that three courts of appeals rejected it, and that the Government now agrees §1226(e) does not preclude a habeas challenge to the constitutionality of the statute itself. Asked whether §1226(e) would be constitutional if it did withdraw review, he answered that it would be within Congress's power in some circumstances, while noting that the point was unbriefed.

**Respondent (Kim):** Kim accepts detention to secure appearance or protect the public where it is actually needed, and he does not challenge the initial custody. Proposed rule: due process requires, at minimum, an individualized determination that detaining a permanent resident serves the purposes of detention. Carlson, Flores, Salerno, Foucha and Zadvydas each involved individual decisionmaking or discretion. Section 1226(c) reaches minor nonviolent offenses and residents with bona fide removal defenses who often prevail. Appealed cases mean many months of custody. Joseph tests only inclusion, not flight, danger or eligibility for relief. Individualized hearings are routine and inexpensive and could be added to the existing procedure. Alternatively, the Court should avoid the constitutional question: §1226(c) applies only to an alien who "is deportable," unlike the earlier "convicted" standard, and no alien is deportable until a final order issues. Kim disputes the Government's description of a concession. At the January 15, 2003 argument (transcript), counsel described the challenge as as-applied to Kim. She stated that Congress may presume that persons charged with these offenses are a danger and a flight risk and require them to come forward to show that they are not, and that under individualized regimes the detainee bears that burden; no quantum was addressed. Asked about a rule modeled on bail pending appeal, requiring a substantial question not raised for delay together with a showing of no flight or danger, counsel said she would support it because it would resolve this case, equated a substantial question with a nonfrivolous one, and maintained that due process also requires an opportunity to show no danger or flight for other detainees.

**Amicus:** The Washington Legal Foundation and others argue that §1226(e) withdraws jurisdiction. The reargument order directed no supplemental question or briefing, and no supplemental submission or later concession is identified.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

**Threshold paths:** If §1226(e) withdraws §2241 jurisdiction over this challenge, the judgment would be vacated with instructions to dismiss, and the open Suspension Clause question would arise unless another capable channel exists. If jurisdiction exists, it may be confined to legal challenges to the statutory framework, leaving discretionary bond judgments unreviewable.

**Statutory paths:** (1) A final-order reading affirms on Kim's alternative ground and returns him to §1226(a) consideration. (2) A coverage reading holds that a resident who raises a substantial, nondilatory challenge to category membership is entitled to a meaningful inclusion determination, with §1226(a) applying when inclusion is not established; this path would test Joseph's substantially-unlikely standard. (3) A statutory-threshold reading makes bail consideration available when a challenge to deportability is not insubstantial and not interposed for delay, with release conditioned on a heightened showing by the detainee modeled on 18 U.S.C. §3143(b). Paths (2) and (3) would ordinarily require vacatur and remand for application to Kim's challenges to both charges.

**Constitutional paths:** (4) Categorical validity: detention of persons within §1226(c) for the duration of removal proceedings satisfies due process, with exceptional prolonged or arbitrary detention left to case-specific review; the judgment would be reversed. (5) An individualized hearing for every permanent resident held under §1226(c): the judgment would be affirmed. (6) Continued-detention review: initial categorical custody is valid, but continued detention requires individualized review after a defined point in the proceedings, such as the close of the initial stage, or when its length, or governmental delay, ceases to bear a reasonable relation to the removal purpose, measured functionally; the disposition depends on how the trigger applies to Kim's six months, including the continuance he requested. (7) Hearing procedure and proof: any required review could place the burden on the Government to prove by clear and convincing evidence (by analogy to Salerno and Addington) or to make a preliminary showing (by analogy to Krimstock), use a procedure calibrated under Mathews, or place a burden on the detainee (by analogy to §3143(b)); the Court could also leave the question to the lower courts.

**Judgment and relief consequences:** Affirmance leaves the conditional judgment and Kim's release on bond in place. Reversal or a jurisdictional dismissal removes the judicial basis for the hearing requirement; Kim's custody would then turn on §1226(c) and the status of his removal proceedings. A vacatur and remand requires the judgment to state the governing standard and whether existing release continues pending remand. No class is before the Court; relief runs to Kim's custody, although the rule announced governs other §1226(c) detainees.

**Plausible Holding Scope:** A jurisdiction holding can be confined to challenges to the statutory framework. Categorical validity would govern every §1226(c) detainee, including residents with substantial removal defenses, and must account for this record's months of custody and disputed category question. A permanent-resident hearing rule would reach every resident held under §1226(c), however strong the charge; noncitizens without permanent residence are not presented. A coverage rule reaches only substantial inclusion disputes. A duration rule reaches prolonged or delayed custody without a judicially invented day count. A decision resting on a final classification of Kim's convictions or on a numerical ceiling would require facts the record lacks.

**Uncertainty:** The status of Kim's removal proceedings after the August 2002 amended charges; whether the INS release reflected an individualized assessment; the accuracy and representativeness of the aggregate detention figures; whether forgoing a Joseph hearing bears on the coverage claims, the record establishing no waiver; and the parties' competing descriptions of any concession.

## B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

* **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals as modified. Section 1226(e) does not bar this §2241 petition. The conditional writ stands insofar as it forbids holding Kim under §1226(c) without an individualized custody hearing. It rests on the narrower holding in Issue 3 rather than on the Court of Appeals' broader rationale, and it is modified to require that any hearing apply the standard stated there.

* **Stone's Remedy and Remand Position:** Remand to the Court of Appeals with instructions to return the case to the district court. The district court amends the conditional writ to state the Issue 3 standard and retains jurisdiction to enforce it. Before Kim may be returned to custody, an immigration judge must hold a hearing; the only exception is custody taken at once on a new criminal charge or a violation of a release condition, after which the hearing must be held promptly. (1) If the Government does not establish the predicate convictions on which it relies and show at least some merit to its charge that they place Kim within a §1226(c) category, ordinary §1226(a) bond procedures govern. (2) Otherwise, Kim remains at liberty if he shows by clear and convincing evidence that, if released on conditions the immigration judge may impose, he is not likely to flee or to endanger the safety of any other person or the community. The district court reviews the outcome only for legal error in the standard applied; §1226(e) bars reweighing the immigration judge's findings. The judgment decides neither removability nor the classification of Kim's convictions.

* **Remedial Reach:** Kim alone. No class was certified, and no policy-wide relief is before the Court. Issues 2 and 3 govern other §1226(c) detainees as precedent; the judgment orders no one else's release.

* **Practical Relief:** Kim remains released on his existing $5,000 bond and conditions, and no new hearing or rearrest is required while the Government leaves that release in place. The Government may return him to custody only after he loses the hearing, with one exception: if he is arrested on a new criminal charge or violates a release condition, the Government may take him into custody at once, and the immigration judge must hold the hearing promptly after custody. Otherwise, if removal proceedings remain pending and the Government seeks custody, it must request the hearing; the immigration judge must hold it promptly, and Kim stays released until the written decision. At the hearing, if the Government does not establish the predicate convictions and show at least some merit to its charge, the immigration judge decides custody at that hearing under ordinary §1226(a) bond procedures, and Kim stays released unless the judge orders otherwise. If it does, the judge considers Kim's residence here since childhood and his permanent-resident status, his record while released, the nature and age of the 1996 and 1997 convictions, the strength of the Government's case on the classification questions he raises, the reasons for any delay, and conditions that would secure appearance and safety. If Kim shows by clear and convincing evidence that, if released on conditions the immigration judge may impose, he is not likely to flee or to endanger the safety of any other person or the community, he stays released, or is released again, on existing or modified conditions; if he does not, the Government may hold him under §1226(c) on the written decision. Materially prolonged further delay or another material change permits either side to seek a new hearing. Custody taken without the hearing, other than at once on a new criminal charge or a release violation, violates the writ, and the district court must order release. If a final removal order has been entered, §1231 and Zadvydas govern custody instead. If the proceedings have ended in Kim's favor, §1226 supplies no custody authority.

## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Review of categorical detention authority

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Kim challenges the categorical denial of any individualized custody determination, not a discretionary bond judgment. His release under the conditional writ does not end the controversy, because the Government asserts authority to return him to no-bail custody.

* **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether §1226(e) withdraws §2241 jurisdiction over that challenge.

* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply existing law: the provision-specific review-channel construction of Reno v. Goncalves, Calcano-Martinez v. INS and Zadvydas v. Davis.

* **Reach Calibration:** Narrow. Stone's Goncalves opinion preserved the writ for legal-authority objections while denying review of discretionary weighing; this holding does the same for §1226(e). One step broader, review of individual custody findings would erase what §1226(e) actually withdraws. One step narrower, reading §1226(e) to bar this claim would leave a constitutional challenge to categorical confinement with no forum and no clear statement withdrawing one.

* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Section 1226(e) bars review of the Attorney General's discretionary custody judgments, not a §2241 challenge to the statutory or constitutional authority for categorical detention. The habeas court decides whether §1226(c) lawfully authorizes holding the petitioner without an individualized determination and, if it does not, grants effective relief. It does not reweigh flight, danger or bond findings, which are reviewable only for legal error in the standard applied.

* **Reach Within the Question:** Substantial statutory and constitutional challenges to the authority for §1226 custody, including asserted authority to resume custody. Challenges to a bond amount or to the weighing of custody factors remain barred.

* **Next-Case Guidance:** A claim that an immigration judge applied the wrong legal standard at a hearing required by Issue 2 or Issue 3 is a legal-authority claim within §2241. A claim that the judge weighed the evidence wrongly is not.

* **Transition:** Not applicable.

* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Goncalves held, for all nine Justices, that §2241 survived the 1996 amendments for substantial statutory and constitutional objections to executive authority, without licensing reweighing of discretion or retrial of deportability facts. Calcano-Martinez held that the retained writ “must be capable of deciding the legal-authority objection and affording effective relief when warranted.” Zadvydas, Part I, applied that channel to the legal authority for immigration detention, “including the government's asserted authority to resume custody.” Section 1226(e) shields the Attorney General's “discretionary judgment regarding the application of this section” and any “action or decision ... regarding the detention or release of any alien.” The categorical bar Kim challenges is Congress's command, not anyone's discretionary judgment. Nothing in §1226(e) clearly withdraws review of whether that command may lawfully be applied to him. Ventura keeps assigned factfinding with the agency, which is why individual custody findings stay with the immigration judge.

* **Precedent Treatment:** Reno v. Goncalves, Kolster v. INS, Calcano-Martinez v. INS and Zadvydas v. Davis, Part I: applied to §1226(e). INS v. Ventura: applied to the allocation of custody factfinding.

* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** None.

### Issue 2: A substantial challenge to deportability

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** The parties contest whether §1226(c), read to avoid constitutional doubt, permits individualized release when the detainee disputes that he is deportable on a listed ground. The statutory question precedes the constitutional one. Kim has identified challenges he intends to press to the classification of both convictions; whether they clear the threshold below has never been determined.

* **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether §1226(c) forbids any individualized bail determination for a detainee who raises a substantial challenge to his deportability on a listed ground.

* **Stone's Legal Position:** New construction of §1226(c) under the avoidance method of Zadvydas; no precedent changes. For bail eligibility, it replaces the agency rule that a resident escapes mandatory detention only when the Government is substantially unlikely to prevail on the charge. Its release allocation departs from Stone's earlier approved position in this case for the reasons stated in Issue 3.

* **Reach Calibration:** Intermediate. Basis: Stone's Goncalves and Zadvydas method and his earlier approved coverage trigger in this case. One step broader, treating any contest as displacing the no-bail command would let a frivolous or dilatory objection defeat Congress's choice. One step narrower, leaving the substantially-unlikely standard as the only exit keeps a detainee with substantial arguments on both sides confined for the life of the proceedings.

* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** A person held under §1226(c) who raises a question of law or fact about his deportability on a listed ground that is not insubstantial and is not interposed solely for delay is entitled, on request, to a prompt bail hearing before an immigration judge, at which he obtains release by showing by clear and convincing evidence that, if released on conditions the immigration judge may impose, he is not likely to flee or to endanger the safety of any other person or the community. The detainee makes the threshold showing. The immigration judge decides it and the release showing at one hearing, and release then proceeds under §1226(a). Meeting the threshold decides neither the classification nor removability. Section 1226(c)(2)'s witness-protection exception remains a separate, unaltered route.

* **Reach Within the Question:** Persons held under §1226(c) on a charge that they are deportable on a listed ground, whether or not they are permanent residents. Inadmissibility and terrorism grounds are not presented.

* **Next-Case Guidance:** “Not insubstantial” means fairly debatable on the law or the conviction record, a lower bar than likely success. A challenge pressed only to postpone removal fails. The immigration judge decides the threshold without deciding the classification, which remains for the removal proceeding.

* **Transition:** Not applicable.

* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Section 1226(c)(1)(B) reaches an alien who “is deportable by reason of having committed” a listed offense, and paragraph (2) permits release only for witness protection. Kim's alternative reading, that no one “is deportable” until a final removal order, fails: paragraph (1) commands custody “when the alien is released” from criminal custody, before any removal order could issue, and §1231 separately governs custody after a final order. Under Matter of Joseph, 22 I. & N. Dec. 799 (BIA 1999), a resident leaves the category only if the Government is substantially unlikely to establish the charge, so a person with substantial arguments stays confined throughout. Zadvydas, in Parts II and IV, which Stone joined, read a detention statute in light of serious liberty concerns to authorize only what its purpose requires, although its text set no limit. Whether a person “is deportable” remains genuinely unsettled while he presses a substantial challenge. Reading the phrase to attach on the charge alone would make a charging allegation an irrebuttable finding. National Center for Immigrants' Rights sustained a general release condition because legally required individual determinations remained, and it did not presume disputed deportability. The release standard is the one Congress prescribes in 18 U.S.C. §3143(b)(1) for a convicted defendant seeking release pending appeal: clear and convincing proof that, if released under §3142(b) or (c), he is not likely to flee or pose a danger, and an appeal not brought for delay that raises a substantial question. Borrowing Congress's own allocation, rather than inventing another, departs least from the no-bail command while removing the doubt.

* **Precedent Treatment:** Zadvydas v. Davis, Parts II and IV: avoidance method applied to §1226(c); its six-month presumption is not transplanted. INS v. National Center for Immigrants' Rights: applied.

* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Under Ventura, the agency classifies Kim's 1996 and 1997 convictions in the removal proceeding; that question is not presented.

### Issue 3: Continued detention of a resident who contests deportability

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** This is the question presented. Issue 2 cannot by itself secure Kim an individualized determination, because the threshold has never been decided for him. The record establishes about six months of custody under §1226(c), which forbade any individualized release determination.

* **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether the Due Process Clause permits holding a lawful permanent resident who contests his deportability under §1226(c), beyond the initial stage of removal proceedings, without an individualized hearing; and if not, what the hearing decides and who bears what showing.

* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply the continuing-deprivation rule of Gilbert v. Homar and Krimstock v. Kelly to custody of the person. This is a new constitutional holding, narrower than the Court of Appeals' rule. For residents who contest deportability, its initial-stage trigger replaces the duration-based continued-detention trigger of Stone's earlier approved position in this case, which required custody to have ceased to bear a reasonable relation to diligently conducted proceedings; that requirement survives only in the interim instruction in Next-Case Guidance. It rests on the Fifth Amendment because, on Stone's reading, §1226(c)(2)'s exclusive release exception leaves no statutory room for a general continued-custody hearing; the fallback states the construction he adopts if the judgment requires it. For this case, the detainee's clear-and-convincing release showing replaces the governmental clear-and-convincing burden of Stone's earlier approved position, whose fallback would have left proof to Mathews but not placed it on the detainee. It also departs from his Krimstock concurrence, which would have required the City to prove the facts behind necessity by a preponderance because the City sought continued possession. The Government seeks continued custody here too, so the reasons lie elsewhere: the predicate convictions are adjudicated and are charged, with at least some merit, as falling in a category Congress itself defined for convicted persons; the showing concerns release on conditions, not unconditional release; it arises only after the Government's initial showing; and it is the allocation Congress prescribes in 18 U.S.C. §3143(b) for convicted persons seeking release pending appeal. Cooper v. Oklahoma's limit on the quantum a defendant may bear concerns proof of trial incompetence and does not govern this setting.

* **Reach Calibration:** Intermediate. Basis: Stone's Flores concurrence (lawful initial custody does not answer a challenge to a mandatory barrier causing continued detention; no invented hearing schedule), his Zadvydas concurrence (individualized necessity without a calendar), and his joins in Parts II-A and II-C of Foucha, the continuing-process holding of Gilbert and Parts I through III-A of Krimstock. One step broader, a hearing at the outset for every detainee would displace Congress's judgment for the initial stage, which Flores and the unchallenged initial custody support. One step narrower, a trigger requiring a finding of unreasonable delay or a substantial coverage challenge would let a resident who has already spent months confined receive no individualized look at all. Category membership would then be the only answer to whether this person must be held.

* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** A lawful permanent resident who contests his deportability may be held under §1226(c) without an individualized hearing only through the initial stage of his removal proceedings; to hold him longer, the Government must afford him, on request, a prompt hearing before an immigration judge. The initial stage comprises taking custody, service of the charge, the initial custody determination and the first hearing before an immigration judge; custody during it rests validly on the category alone. At the hearing, the Government must first establish the predicate conviction on which it relies and show at least some merit to its charge that the conviction places him within a §1226(c) category; if it does not, ordinary §1226(a) bond procedures apply. If it does, the detainee obtains release by showing by clear and convincing evidence that, if released on conditions the immigration judge may impose, he is not likely to flee or to endanger the safety of any other person or the community. The judge weighs his ties and residence, his record, the nature and age of the convictions, the strength of the Government's removal case, the reasons for any delay and the available conditions. Delay the detainee causes solely to prolong the proceedings weighs heavily against release. A material change permits a renewed hearing. There is no automatic release date or numerical calendar, and the Government may detain on an adverse decision. Applied here: Kim entered as a child, is a lawful permanent resident and has not conceded deportability. He was held about six months under §1226(c), and his removal proceedings have been pending since the March 1999 charge, with his withholding-of-removal hearing set for July 1999 and amended charges filed in August 2002, so any return to custody would come well beyond the initial stage. He is entitled to the hearing and remains released pending it, subject only to immediate custody on a new criminal charge or a release violation, followed promptly by the hearing.

* **Reach Within the Question:** Lawful permanent residents held under §1226(c) who have not conceded deportability, while removal proceedings are pending. Initial-stage custody, residents who concede deportability, other noncitizens, custody after a final order and the validity of the charges are outside the rule.

* **Next-Case Guidance:** The initial stage ends at the first hearing before an immigration judge, not on a fixed day, and the hearing must then follow promptly on request. A resident contests deportability when he has not conceded it in the removal proceeding. Until this Court decides the custody rule for a lawful permanent resident who has conceded deportability and whose custody is prolonged by his pursuit of relief from removal, courts apply, through the habeas review stated in Issue 1, the requirement of Jackson v. Indiana, applied in Foucha and Zadvydas, that the nature and duration of custody bear a reasonable relation to its purpose.

* **Transition:** Not applicable.

* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Kim did not contest initial custody. Reno v. Flores held that request-based review does not facially require “an automatic hearing for every detained juvenile,” while approving no “improper delay or unlawful custody in a particular case”; that is why the rule leaves the initial stage alone and operates on request. Gilbert v. Homar held for all nine Justices that “justification for immediate suspension does not independently justify its continued duration”; the employee must receive a prompt, informed opportunity, assessed by the continuing loss, the reasons for delay and the reliability of the remaining grounds, without a fixed deadline. Krimstock v. Kelly held unanimously that prolonged retention of a seized car without timely neutral review violates due process (Part I), and that the hearing must reach seizure probable cause, the likely merits and the necessity of physical custody rather than bond or other security, that the City must support its continued deprivation, and that the decisionmaker must be able to order interim release (Part III-A). If continued loss of pay and continued retention of a car each require a prompt, separately justified opportunity once the initial basis has served its purpose, continued confinement of the person does too. Foucha and Jackson place that liberty at the core: the nature and duration of custody must bear a reasonable relation to its purpose, and a valid initial basis does not answer every later question about continued confinement. Category membership establishes only the charged conviction and says nothing about whether this person will flee or endanger anyone. Carlson v. Landon sustained discretionary detention without bail, bounded by the statute; it approved an exercise of discretion, not a bar on any individualized judgment. Under Mathews v. Eldridge, the hearing is the safeguard of greatest value because it addresses the one error categorical custody cannot. Krimstock's Part III-B, which the eight Associate Justices joined and Stone did not, adds that the City must make a preliminary showing of probable validity: a supported likely forfeiture basis and a sufficient present need for physical possession rather than adequate security. The hearing keeps a governmental first step, though a lighter one on the merits than Krimstock's likely forfeiture basis: no release burden falls on the detainee until the Government establishes the predicate conviction and shows at least some merit to its charge. The showing of present need is supported differently. The City held cars on arrests and unadjudicated forfeiture claims, sometimes against owners who were never arrested; here the predicate convictions are adjudicated, and Congress defined the convicted category on evidence that its members, released during proceedings, often failed to appear and reoffended. That judgment, applied to an adjudicated conviction charged with at least some merit, supplies the preliminary showing of need, as a conviction does under 18 U.S.C. §3143(b)(1), which requires a convicted defendant seeking release pending appeal to show by clear and convincing evidence that, if released under §3142(b) or (c), he is not likely to flee or pose a danger. The detainee also holds the best evidence of his ties, conduct and plans. Cooper v. Oklahoma, which Stone joined, forbade requiring a defendant to prove trial incompetence by clear and convincing evidence, because that allocation permits trial when the greater weight of the evidence favors his inability to understand the proceedings or rationally assist counsel; it left a defendant-preponderance burden in place. That limit concerns proof of incapacity before a criminal trial; here capacity is unimpaired, custody is civil and temporary, a material change permits a renewed hearing, and the showing concerns release on conditions. Medina v. California permitted California to require a defendant to prove incompetence by a preponderance at an adequate hearing, despite the risk that incapacity may impair the proof; Stone dissented, urging a State burden once doubt requires a hearing, and that risk is absent here. Foucha's Part II-C, which Stone joined, condemned indefinite confinement of an acquittee because he had not proved nondangerousness after the authorizing predicate had ended, and Stone reserved universal proof rules there; here the predicate stands, and a pending removal proceeding supplies both purpose and endpoint. Salerno's governmental clear-and-convincing burden attended a focused pretrial regime for persons not yet convicted. The trigger is met on this record without further findings: Kim spent roughly six months in custody during which §1226(c) forbade any individualized release determination, and because his proceedings have been pending since March 1999, any return to custody would come long after the initial stage.

* **Precedent Treatment:** Gilbert v. Homar: continuing-process holding extended from employment to custody of the person. Krimstock v. Kelly: Parts I and III-A extended from property retention to bodily custody; Part III-B's preliminary governmental showing adapted to an adjudicated predicate conviction. Reno v. Flores: applied; there is no automatic hearing at the outset, and its particular-case reservation governs. Foucha v. Louisiana and Jackson v. Indiana: applied to continued custody; Foucha's Part II-C distinguished on the release burden. Zadvydas v. Davis: six-month presumption not transplanted. Carlson v. Landon, Cooper v. Oklahoma, Medina v. California and United States v. Salerno: distinguished. Mathews v. Eldridge: applied.

* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Residents who have conceded deportability, and noncitizens who are not permanent residents, are not presented: the record shows no comprehensive concession, and Kim is a permanent resident. Under Ventura, the agency classifies Kim's convictions; that question is not presented.

* **Approved Conditional Position or Fallback:** Each paragraph turns on whether a judgment would command at least five votes, counting every Justice who would vote for it on any ground and whether or not Stone's vote is needed; none turns on whether other Justices adopt Stone's reasoning. The protected hearing is a neutral decisionmaker's individualized hearing on whether his custody is needed to prevent flight or danger, held before any return to custody, with his existing release continuing until then; custody taken at once on a new criminal charge or a violation of a release condition, followed promptly by that hearing, is consistent with it. (1) Narrower ground. If the Part B judgment would not command at least five votes while Stone's opinion rests on Issue 3, but a judgment applying the same hearing rule, with its trigger, standard and relief, would command at least five if Stone's opinion rested instead on §1226(c), Stone adopts that construction of §1226(c) under the avoidance method of Zadvydas. Section 1226(c)(2) governs who may be released while categorical custody is authorized, not how long that custody lasts; holding a lawful permanent resident who contests deportability beyond the initial stage with no individualized look is not reasonably necessary to the statute's purpose of securing appearance and public safety during proceedings; and Gilbert and Krimstock show that the constitutional doubt is serious. No day count follows. The judgment then rests on Issues 1 and 2 and that construction, and Stone does not reach the Due Process Clause. (2) Other ground, an alternative judgment vote rather than a narrower one. If neither the Part B judgment nor a paragraph (1) judgment would command at least five votes, or if at least five other Justices join an opinion resting on another ground, Stone joins a judgment, if any, that secures Kim the protected hearing and commands at least five votes, with the opinion parts consistent with Issues 1 through 3 or with the paragraph (1) construction, whatever its allocation of proof. Limit: Stone joins no disposition that permits Kim's return to custody under §1226 without the protected hearing; custody under §1231 after a final removal order is outside this limit.

Vacate and remand if the lower courts applied the wrong class and did not resolve the supported alternative mechanisms.
