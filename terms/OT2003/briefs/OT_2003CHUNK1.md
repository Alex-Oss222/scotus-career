* **Question Presented:** Whether respondent's mandatory detention under 8 U.S.C. §1226(c) violates the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment, where respondent was convicted of an aggravated felony after his admission to the United States. It rejected Kim's argument that §1226(c) does not reach him. Under the April 29, 2003 order, that judgment and Kim's release on $5,000 bond remain in effect.

California convicted him of first-degree burglary in 1996 and of petty theft with priors in 1997. He received a three-year term on the second conviction and a concurrent two-year term on the first. On February 2, 1999, the day after his release from state custody, the INS arrested him and held him without bond under §1226(c). Its charge, filed March 10, 1999, alleged that the 1997 conviction was an aggravated felony under §1227(a)(2)(A)(iii). His withholding-of-removal hearing was set for July 1999 and, at his request, continued to September to let him obtain documents for that application. He filed for habeas in May 1999. After the August 1999 order, and more than six months in custody, the INS released him on $5,000 bond. Kim's brief, citing the joint appendix, states that the INS then found him neither a threat nor a significant flight risk. The record does not establish that an immigration-judge hearing preceded release. The record identifies no request for a Joseph hearing before the habeas proceeding; that absence does not establish waiver of classification objections. Kim disclaims any challenge to the initial authority to take him into custody. In June 2002 the Ninth Circuit held that the California petty-theft-with-priors offense does not categorically qualify as an aggravated felony, and Kim stated that he would contest that charge. In August 2002 the INS added the 1996 conviction and a charge of two crimes involving moral turpitude under §1227(a)(2)(A)(ii), which is also a §1226(c) predicate. Kim states that he will contend that neither conviction supports removal and that he remains eligible for relief. The Government describes him as conceding that his convictions place him within §1226(c). Accepting initial custody is not necessarily conceding every removal or category question, and the two descriptions must be kept distinct. The Government's aggregate detention figures are not findings about Kim. The record supplies no removal order and no later change in custody.

Kim's release following the conditional judgment leaves a live controversy, because reversal would expose him to renewed mandatory custody. No challenge to a discretionary bond amount is presented. Since March 1, 2003, the Department of Homeland Security has exercised the former INS's detention functions, and the immigration-court custody regulations now appear at 8 C.F.R. §1003.19. The transfer changes neither the statutory text nor the posture.

### Issue 1: Habeas jurisdiction and §1226(e)

* **Controlling Law:** Reno v. Goncalves (unanimous on jurisdiction) and Kolster v. INS hold that the 1996 amendments did not clearly withdraw §2241 jurisdiction over substantial statutory or constitutional objections to executive authority, without reweighing discretionary equities or retrying deportability facts. Calcano-Martinez v. INS holds that the permanent review provisions do not clearly displace that jurisdiction over a substantial objection that custody or removal rests on an unlawful eligibility rule; the retained review must be capable of deciding the objection and affording effective relief. INS v. St. Cyr applies the channel to an independently filed legal-eligibility action. Zadvydas v. Davis (Part I, unanimous) holds that §2241 permits independently proper challenges to the legal authority for immigration detention, including asserted authority to resume custody, without reopening removal orders. The court must be able to determine that authority and afford effective relief, but may not retry removability or reweigh lawful discretion. The custody, respondent, forum and exhaustion predicates remain.

* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** A review withdrawal is construed by the acts and objects it names. Restricting one channel does not itself repeal an independent writ, although a clear withdrawal needs no particular verbal formula or section reference. Section 2241 reaches substantial legal or constitutional objections to executive authority to detain or deport, with effective relief if warranted. Reweighing discretionary equities and general factual retrial are distinct.

* **Other Governing Law:** 8 U.S.C. §1226(e): the Attorney General's discretionary judgment regarding application of §1226 is not subject to review, and no court may set aside any action or decision by the Attorney General under §1226 regarding the detention or release of any alien or the grant, revocation or denial of bond or parole. 28 U.S.C. §2241; the Suspension Clause.

* **Material Other Authority:** Reno v. American-Arab Anti-Discrimination Committee confines §1252(g) to its three named actions. It preserves existing jurisdiction for a substantial constitutional claim that lacks a capable channel, applying Webster v. Doe's clear-intent requirement. INS v. Ventura (November 4, 2002) returns unperformed, statutorily assigned factual judgments to the agency. It preserves independent judicial review of law and gives no new deference to agency legal conclusions. Parra v. Perryman, 172 F.3d 954 (7th Cir. 1999), distinguished a challenge to the statute itself from a challenge to its implementation.

* **Existing Rule's Reach:** Entering law preserves §2241 against provisions that do not clearly withdraw it and separates legal-authority review from discretionary judgments. No decision construes §1226(e). No individual discretionary bond determination is challenged.

* **Open Point:** Whether §1226(e)'s two sentences withdraw §2241 jurisdiction over a challenge to a statutory framework that forbids individualized custody review, as distinct from the Attorney General's discretionary judgments and individual detention or bond decisions. If they do, whether the Court must reach the Suspension Clause question that entering law leaves open.

### Issue 2: Statutory reach of §1226(c) and contested category membership

* **Controlling Law:** No decision of this Court construes §1226(c). Zadvydas v. Davis construes detention authority by its removal purpose and constitutional limits rather than invalidating the statute. INS v. National Center for Immigrants' Rights sustains a general release rule because the rule preserves individual status determinations. Officials must address colorable citizenship or status claims, and the rule presumes no disputed deportability.

* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** General immigration custody and release rules operate together with the individualized determinations on which their validity depends. Custody authority is read in light of its statutory purpose.

* **Other Governing Law:** Section 1226(c)(1)(B) requires custody of an alien who "is deportable" by reason of offenses covered in §1227(a)(2)(A)(ii), (A)(iii), (B), (C) or (D). Section 1226(c)(2) permits release only when necessary for witness protection under 18 U.S.C. §3521 and the alien shows that he will not endanger others and is likely to appear. Section 1226(a) otherwise permits release on bond or conditional parole. Section 1231(a)(6) permits supervised release of the same criminal categories after a final order and the removal period. Under 8 C.F.R. §3.19(h)(2) (now §1003.19(h)(2)) and Matter of Joseph, 22 I. & N. Dec. 799 (BIA 1999), a detainee may seek an immigration judge's determination that he is not properly included in §1226(c). He prevails only if the Government is substantially unlikely to establish the charge. The pre-1996 mandatory-detention statute applied to aliens "convicted" of an aggravated felony.

* **Material Other Authority:** The Ninth Circuit below found "is deportable" ambiguous but rejected Kim's final-order reading as inconsistent with other statutory language. United States v. Corona-Sanchez, 291 F.3d 1201 (9th Cir. 2002) (en banc), held that California petty theft with priors does not categorically qualify as an aggravated felony.

* **Existing Rule's Reach:** No decision addresses whether "is deportable" requires a final order, whether Joseph's standard permissibly implements the statute, or what review attends a substantial dispute over category membership. Classification of Kim's convictions belongs to the removal proceedings.

* **Open Point:** Whether §1226(c) reaches a resident not yet ordered removed. If it does, whether a resident who raises a substantial, nondilatory challenge to category membership "is deportable" for custody purposes before that challenge is resolved, and what determination and standard decide inclusion.

### Issue 3: Due process limits on detention pending removal proceedings

* **Controlling Law:** Zadvydas v. Davis holds that, for previously admitted persons, §1231(a)(6) authorizes detention after the removal period only for a period reasonably necessary to accomplish removal. A final order, past crime or danger cannot supply permanent preventive authority detached from removal. Six months is presumptively reasonable under that provision; after it, a supported showing shifts a rebuttal burden to the Government. Distinct criminal, civil-commitment and security regimes are excluded, and no universal dangerousness burden is adopted. Reno v. Flores sustains temporary, nonpunitive juvenile custody with release avenues and request-based immigration-judge review against a facial challenge. It does not approve ineffective notice, denial of requested review, improper delay or unlawful custody in a particular case, and custody remains subject to a reasonable-dispatch limit enforceable by habeas. Foucha v. Louisiana bars continued confinement once its authorizing predicate ends; a legitimate preventive purpose and periodic review do not cure the absence of carefully limited substantive authority. Ordinary civil commitment requires clear and convincing proof, but that burden is not made universal. Connecticut Department of Public Safety v. Doe (March 5, 2003) requires a hearing only on a disputed fact that is material under the governing law. Adequate process remains required for a genuinely disputed conviction or other operative inclusion criterion. Gilbert v. Homar (continuing-process holding, unanimous) holds that justification for an immediate deprivation does not independently justify its continued duration; a prompt, meaningful opportunity is assessed by the continuing loss, the reasons for delay and the reliability of the remaining grounds, without a fixed deadline. Krimstock v. Kelly (June 23, 2003) holds that prolonged retention of seized vehicles without timely neutral review violates due process (Part I, unanimous). The hearing must reach the likely merits and the necessity of physical custody rather than adequate security; the City must support its continued deprivation, and the decisionmaker must be able to order interim release (Part III-A, unanimous). The City makes a preliminary showing of probable validity and present need for possession, while the claimant bears the separate innocent-owner showing (Part III-B, eight Justices). Fairley v. Luman (June 23, 2003) requires reasonable, timely identity verification during prolonged detention when repeated specific protests, readily accessible reliable checks and modest burdens show a serious avoidable risk that the wrong person remains confined. Mathews governs the continuing deprivation, and initial authority is distinct from continuation.

* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** Identify the custody authority, its purpose, duration and conditions, and the actual review safeguards. Civil labels do not remove the liberty interest, and a rule from one detention regime is not a universal formula. Post-order immigration custody must remain tied to removal; juvenile custody review is assessed on its actual operation; continued confinement requires a continuing lawful predicate.

* **Other Governing Law:** Fifth Amendment Due Process Clause; 8 U.S.C. §§1226(a), (c), 1231(a)(2), (6); 8 C.F.R. §3.19(h)(2); Matter of Joseph.

* **Material Other Authority:** Carlson v. Landon, 342 U.S. 524 (1952), sustained discretionary detention without bail pending deportation, bounded by the statute; it does not authorize indefinite custody. United States v. Salerno, 481 U.S. 739 (1987), sustained pretrial detention resting on prompt adversarial hearings and Government proof by clear and convincing evidence. Entering law treats Salerno as a focused pretrial regime; its all-applications facial formulation does not govern abortion-access challenges, and its operation elsewhere is unaddressed. Mathews v. Eldridge weighs the private interest, the risk of error and value of added safeguards, and the government's interest. Jackson v. Indiana requires that the nature and duration of commitment bear a reasonable relation to its purpose. Addington v. Texas fixes clear and convincing proof for ordinary civil commitment. Kansas v. Crane requires a functional showing that distinguishes a committable person from a dangerous ordinary recidivist. Kansas v. Hendricks treats a civil label as rebuttable only on the clearest proof of punitive purpose or effect, and on that basis held Hendricks's retrospective commitment to be punishment as applied, in violation of the Ex Post Facto Clause; its due-process discussion is noncontrolling. Sell v. United States (June 16, 2003) requires individualized findings, weighing detention already served, before prosecution-only compelled medication, and sets no universal proof quantum. Cooper v. Oklahoma forbids requiring a criminal defendant to prove incompetence by clear and convincing evidence, because that allocation permits trial when the greater weight of the evidence shows incapacity; defendant-preponderance persuasion remains permissible. 18 U.S.C. §3143(b) requires a convicted defendant seeking release pending appeal to show by clear and convincing evidence that he is unlikely to flee or endanger others, and to raise a substantial question not for delay. Patel v. Zemski, 275 F.3d 299 (3d Cir. 2001), Welch v. Ashcroft, 293 F.3d 213 (4th Cir. 2002), and Hoang v. Comfort, 282 F.3d 1247 (10th Cir. 2002), held §1226(c) invalid as applied to permanent residents. Parra v. Perryman sustained it for an alien who raised no challenge to removal.

* **Existing Rule's Reach:** The Government may initially take custody on a valid statutory predicate, and Kim does not challenge the initial custody authority. Zadvydas's purpose limit and six-month presumption construe §1231(a)(6) and do not directly govern custody before a final order. Flores sustained request-based review in a facial juvenile setting, and Foucha and Crane address commitment regimes. Applying a settled rule to Kim's six months of custody would be ordinary application; whether category membership can replace individual justification would not.

* **Open Point:** Whether, for a permanent resident not yet ordered removed, membership in a §1226(c) category constitutionally substitutes for an individualized determination of flight risk or danger throughout removal proceedings. Whether prolonged or delayed custody, or a substantial category dispute, changes the answer. If review is required, its procedure (including Joseph's adequacy) and the allocation and quantum of proof.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

**Reasoning below:** The district court held §1226(c) facially invalid. The Ninth Circuit decided only the application to Kim as a permanent resident. It declined plenary-power deference because detention is a means of implementing immigration policy. It held that the interests in securing removal and public safety do not justify eliminating every individualized determination of flight risk and danger. It contrasted the individualized custody in Salerno, Hendricks and Carlson, and noted that §1231(a)(6) permits release of the same categories after a final order. Its Hendricks citation concerns the individualized findings Kansas required; this Court's Hendricks decision held that commitment punitive as applied. It rejected Kim's statutory reading.

**Petitioners (through the Solicitor General):** Detention during removal proceedings is incident to the political branches' power to expel and receives deferential review; Carlson and Flores permit categorical judgments. Congress acted on evidence that criminal aliens released during proceedings often failed to appear and reoffended. Detention lasts only for proceedings that have a definite end and are ordinarily brief. The Government cites agency figures of a 47-day average and 30-day median for most cases, and about four further months when a case is appealed. Zadvydas turned on potentially indefinite post-order custody. The Joseph procedure lets a detainee contest inclusion. Kim's petition presented a facial challenge, which fails under Salerno because the provision validly applies in many circumstances, and Kim concedes that his convictions bring him within §1226(c). Proposed rule: §1226(c) is constitutional in the ordinary case. Limiting principle: exceptional circumstances that present special due process concerns may be addressed case by case, and a fixed six-month limit lacks statutory foundation. The Government's merits brief does not contend that §1226(e) bars review. At the January 15, 2003 argument (transcript), the Solicitor General stated that the Government had initially taken that position in this litigation, that three courts of appeals rejected it, and that the Government now agrees §1226(e) does not preclude a habeas challenge to the constitutionality of the statute itself. Asked whether §1226(e) would be constitutional if it did withdraw review, he answered that it would be within Congress's power in some circumstances, while noting that the point was unbriefed.

**Respondent (Kim):** Kim accepts detention to secure appearance or protect the public where it is actually needed, and he does not challenge the initial custody. Proposed rule: due process requires, at minimum, an individualized determination that detaining a permanent resident serves the purposes of detention. Carlson, Flores, Salerno, Foucha and Zadvydas each involved individual decisionmaking or discretion. Section 1226(c) reaches minor nonviolent offenses and residents with bona fide removal defenses who often prevail. Appealed cases mean many months of custody. Joseph tests only inclusion, not flight, danger or eligibility for relief. Individualized hearings are routine and inexpensive and could be added to the existing procedure. Alternatively, the Court should avoid the constitutional question: §1226(c) applies only to an alien who "is deportable," unlike the earlier "convicted" standard, and no alien is deportable until a final order issues. Kim disputes the Government's description of a concession. At the January 15, 2003 argument (transcript), counsel described the challenge as as-applied to Kim. She stated that Congress may presume that persons charged with these offenses are a danger and a flight risk and require them to come forward to show that they are not, and that under individualized regimes the detainee bears that burden; no quantum was addressed. Asked about a rule modeled on bail pending appeal, requiring a substantial question not raised for delay together with a showing of no flight or danger, counsel said she would support it because it would resolve this case, equated a substantial question with a nonfrivolous one, and maintained that due process also requires an opportunity to show no danger or flight for other detainees.

**Amicus:** The Washington Legal Foundation and others argue that §1226(e) withdraws jurisdiction. The reargument order directed no supplemental question or briefing, and no supplemental submission or later concession is identified.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

**Threshold paths:** If §1226(e) withdraws §2241 jurisdiction over this challenge, the judgment would be vacated with instructions to dismiss, and the open Suspension Clause question would arise unless another capable channel exists. If jurisdiction exists, it may be confined to legal challenges to the statutory framework, leaving discretionary bond judgments unreviewable.

**Statutory paths:** (1) A final-order reading affirms on Kim's alternative ground and returns him to §1226(a) consideration. (2) A coverage reading holds that a resident who raises a substantial, nondilatory challenge to category membership is entitled to a meaningful inclusion determination, with §1226(a) applying when inclusion is not established; this path would test Joseph's substantially-unlikely standard. (3) A statutory-threshold reading makes bail consideration available when a challenge to deportability is not insubstantial and not interposed for delay, with release conditioned on a heightened showing by the detainee modeled on 18 U.S.C. §3143(b). Paths (2) and (3) would ordinarily require vacatur and remand for application to Kim's challenges to both charges.

**Constitutional paths:** (4) Categorical validity: detention of persons within §1226(c) for the duration of removal proceedings satisfies due process, with exceptional prolonged or arbitrary detention left to case-specific review; the judgment would be reversed. (5) An individualized hearing for every permanent resident held under §1226(c): the judgment would be affirmed. (6) Continued-detention review: initial categorical custody is valid, but continued detention requires individualized review after a defined point in the proceedings, such as the close of the initial stage, or when its length, or governmental delay, ceases to bear a reasonable relation to the removal purpose, measured functionally; the disposition depends on how the trigger applies to Kim's six months, including the continuance he requested. (7) Hearing procedure and proof: any required review could place the burden on the Government to prove by clear and convincing evidence (by analogy to Salerno and Addington) or to make a preliminary showing (by analogy to Krimstock), use a procedure calibrated under Mathews, or place a burden on the detainee (by analogy to §3143(b)); the Court could also leave the question to the lower courts.

**Judgment and relief consequences:** Affirmance leaves the conditional judgment and Kim's release on bond in place. Reversal or a jurisdictional dismissal removes the judicial basis for the hearing requirement; Kim's custody would then turn on §1226(c) and the status of his removal proceedings. A vacatur and remand requires the judgment to state the governing standard and whether existing release continues pending remand. No class is before the Court; relief runs to Kim's custody, although the rule announced governs other §1226(c) detainees.

**Plausible Holding Scope:** A jurisdiction holding can be confined to challenges to the statutory framework. Categorical validity would govern every §1226(c) detainee, including residents with substantial removal defenses, and must account for this record's months of custody and disputed category question. A permanent-resident hearing rule would reach every resident held under §1226(c), however strong the charge; noncitizens without permanent residence are not presented. A coverage rule reaches only substantial inclusion disputes. A duration rule reaches prolonged or delayed custody without a judicially invented day count. A decision resting on a final classification of Kim's convictions or on a numerical ceiling would require facts the record lacks.

**Uncertainty:** The status of Kim's removal proceedings after the August 2002 amended charges; whether the INS release reflected an individualized assessment; the accuracy and representativeness of the aggregate detention figures; whether forgoing a Joseph hearing bears on the coverage claims, the record establishing no waiver; and the parties' competing descriptions of any concession.

## B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

### Docket No. 01-1491: Demore v. Kim

* **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** **AFFIRM AS MODIFIED AND REMAND.** Affirm the Ninth Circuit's judgment insofar as it recognizes habeas jurisdiction and protects Kim against renewed categorical detention without the individualized process constitutionally required here. Modify the rationale: §1226(c) is not facially invalid, and an individualized hearing is not automatically required for every detainee immediately upon arrest. Its detention command is subject to the contested-inclusion and continued-detention limits set out below. Remand for implementation of those limits. Do not decide that Kim is removable, that either conviction qualifies under §1226(c), or that detention exceeding a fixed number of days is unlawful.

* **Stone's Remedy and Remand Position:** The district court retains authority to enforce its conditional writ. Kim's existing release on $5,000 bond remains effective. If the Government seeks to return him to immigration custody under §1226(c), it must first obtain a prompt determination applying the following procedure:

  1. Identify every conviction and statutory category relied upon for mandatory detention. A new allegation alone does not establish inclusion.
  2. Determine whether Kim presents a substantial, good-faith challenge capable of defeating each asserted mandatory-detention ground. On the present legal record, his challenge clears the threshold for review. That conclusion does not resolve the ultimate classifications.
  3. Determine whether §1226(c) is established as the lawful basis for custody. If it is not, custody proceeds under the ordinary §1226(a) framework.
  4. While inclusion remains genuinely disputed, conduct an individualized interim custody assessment under the Fifth Amendment, addressing danger, flight, available conditions, and the necessity of confinement.
  5. Independently determine whether the duration and circumstances of any renewed or continued detention remain reasonably related to removal proceedings prosecuted with reasonable diligence. If not, categorical detention cannot continue without an individualized custody justification.

  At an individualized hearing constitutionally required by either gate, the Government must establish by clear and convincing evidence that no reasonably available conditions will adequately address flight risk or danger. Stone's proposed fallback would require the Government to establish the same proposition by a preponderance of the evidence.

  The immigration judge must permit submission of relevant evidence, consider workable release conditions, and issue findings sufficient for legal review. The district court may correct legal error and enforce the writ, but may not substitute its own assessment for a lawful discretionary bond determination.

* **Remedial Reach:** The operative writ and release protection cover Kim, the named habeas petitioner. No class or nationwide challenge seeking relief for all persons held under §1226(c) is before the Court. The constitutional and statutory rules announced in this judgment govern analogous cases as precedent, but this judgment does not itself release other detainees or issue a system-wide injunction. The proved defect is the threatened application of categorical custody to Kim without the required process; the writ reaches that application completely.

* **Practical Relief:**
  1. Kim remains free under his existing $5,000 bond and conditions. Neither the decision nor the remand requires a new arrest merely to conduct a hearing.
  2. If removal proceedings remain pending and the Government seeks renewed custody solely under §1226(c), it must give notice and request a custody determination. The hearing must occur promptly and, absent an independently lawful ground for immediate custody, before Kim is taken back into immigration detention.
  3. The immigration judge must identify the statutory ground, decide the contested-inclusion threshold, examine any duration claim supported by the record, and decide the individualized custody question when either gate requires it.
  4. If the Government fails its applicable custody burden, Kim remains released on lawful conditions. If it satisfies that burden and detention is otherwise authorized, the immigration judge may order custody. Any modification of existing judicial bond relief must conform to the amended conditional writ.
  5. A new criminal charge or proved violation of a release condition may supply an independent lawful basis for immediate custody. Such custody does not permanently excuse the required immigration review. When §1226(c) remains the asserted basis for continued custody, the appropriate hearing must follow promptly.
  6. The immigration judge must make a written or otherwise reviewable record of the statutory predicates, reasons for delay, evidence of flight or danger, considered alternatives, and resulting custody decision. The district court retains jurisdiction to enforce compliance and order release or an appropriately prompt hearing when detention lacks lawful authority.
  7. A material change in the removal proceedings, evidence, detention conditions, or anticipated duration permits renewed review.

  The record does not establish that an immigration judge previously conducted the hearing required by this proposed rule. Kim's actual release on bond is not treated as proof that one occurred.

## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Habeas jurisdiction under §1226(e)

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Kim challenges the legal authority for mandatory detention without individualized review, not the discretionary weight assigned to particular bond evidence. His existing release does not moot the dispute because reversal of the conditional writ could permit renewed detention. The Court must resolve the asserted jurisdictional barrier before reaching the merits.

* **Question Stone Resolves:** Does §1226(e) withdraw jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. §2241 to decide whether §1226(c), as interpreted and applied, authorizes Kim's detention consistently with federal law and the Fifth Amendment?

* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply entering Simulation Court law. No new habeas cause of action is created.

* **Reach Calibration:** **Intermediate.** Stone's prior immigration-jurisdiction positions distinguish legal-authority review from discretionary custody review. One step broader would permit courts to reweigh ordinary bond decisions contrary to §1226(e). One step narrower would treat a challenge to Congress's detention rule as an unreviewable executive discretionary judgment.

* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** **Section 1226(e) does not withdraw §2241 jurisdiction to determine whether immigration detention has statutory and constitutional authority.** Federal courts may resolve substantial legal objections to mandatory custody, including objections to the governing inclusion and continued-detention standards, and may grant effective habeas relief. They may not simply reweigh otherwise lawful discretionary findings concerning flight, danger, or bond.

* **Reach Within the Question:** Legal challenges to detention under §1226, including the legality of renewed custody after conditional habeas relief. It does not authorize factual retrial of removability or ordinary appellate review of discretionary custody weighing.

* **Next-Case Guidance:** A claim that the immigration judge applied the wrong mandatory-detention category, denied a legally required hearing, shifted the constitutionally prescribed burden, or detained after lawful authority expired presents a reviewable legal-authority question. A claim that the judge gave insufficient weight to a witness or community tie, after applying the correct standard, remains governed by §1226(e).

* **Transition:** Not applicable.

* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** In the Simulation Court, *Reno v. Goncalves* and *Kolster v. INS* preserved habeas review of substantial legal objections after the 1996 immigration legislation while distinguishing discretionary judgments. *Calcano-Martinez v. INS* required the surviving review channel to be capable of providing effective relief. *INS v. St. Cyr* applied that principle to an independent legal-eligibility challenge. *Zadvydas v. Davis*, Part I, applied §2241 to the asserted legal authority for immigration detention, including prospective custody authority. Those holdings govern the jurisdictional inquiry here. Kim asks whether the Government may hold him without the process the statute and Constitution require. Section 1226(e)'s protection of discretionary judgments does not answer that question. *INS v. Ventura* preserves agency responsibility for unperformed factfinding, which is why the habeas court determines the legal standard and enforces it without becoming the initial immigration custody factfinder.

* **Precedent Treatment:**
  * *Reno v. Goncalves* and *Kolster v. INS*: reaffirmed and applied to §1226(e).
  * *Calcano-Martinez v. INS* and *INS v. St. Cyr*: reaffirmed as preserving effective review of legal-authority objections.
  * *Zadvydas v. Davis*, Part I: extended to the asserted §1226(c) detention authority.
  * *INS v. Ventura*: applied to the division between judicial legal review and administrative factfinding.

* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Whether Congress could constitutionally withdraw every effective judicial channel for this class of custody claims is outside the decision because §1226(e) does not accomplish that withdrawal. The Suspension Clause question therefore need not be reached.

* **Approved Conditional Position or Fallback:** None required. Stone maintains jurisdiction over legal-authority challenges.  permits separate joinder of this jurisdictional proposition without implying agreement on the merits.

### Issue 2: Contested inclusion in §1226(c)

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** The Government initially charged Kim based on his 1997 theft conviction. A subsequent Ninth Circuit decision questioned the aggravated-felony classification of that California offense. The Government later added the 1996 burglary conviction and a two-crimes-involving-moral-turpitude ground. Kim disputes whether those convictions establish any qualifying mandatory-detention category. His acceptance of the initial arrest does not establish concession of each statutory ground. That dispute is material because §1226(c) applies by statutory category, not by accusation alone.

* **Question Stone Resolves:** When a lawful permanent resident raises a substantial, nondilatory dispute over whether he belongs to a mandatory-detention category, what determination and interim custody process must the Government provide?

* **Stone's Legal Position:** Propose a new construction and constitutional implementation of §1226(c), consistent with Stone's approved OT2002 position. Entering Simulation Court law has not already adopted this particular threshold-and-interim-custody rule. The agency's *Matter of Joseph* standard currently governs its category-review procedure; Stone would hold that it is insufficient as the exclusive protection for a resident with a substantial and genuine inclusion dispute.

* **Reach Calibration:** **Intermediate.** The rule concerns a genuine challenge to legal inclusion, not every disagreement with the Government's immigration case. One step broader would make any allegation of error an automatic escape from Congress's detention categories. One step narrower would permit an unresolved charging allegation to settle the liberty question merely because the detainee cannot show that the Government is substantially unlikely to succeed.

* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** **The Government may not make its assertion of §1226(c) coverage conclusive against a lawful permanent resident who raises a substantial, good-faith challenge to every mandatory-detention ground asserted against him.** The rule operates as follows:

  1. **Detainee's initial showing:** The resident must identify a material legal or factual objection capable, if sustained, of defeating the asserted §1226(c) grounds. The objection must be substantial rather than frivolous and must not be interposed solely to delay proceedings. This is a burden of production, not a burden to prove ultimate nonremovability.
  2. **Government's category showing:** The Government must identify each statutory ground and produce reliable records supporting the predicate convictions. At the provisional custody stage, it bears the burden of establishing disputed historical predicates by a preponderance of the evidence. Legal classification receives independent judicial consideration when presented in habeas; neither the agency's charge nor an unsupported assertion of categorical inclusion binds the court.
  3. **If inclusion fails:** When no qualifying category is lawfully established, the detainee is governed by §1226(a), subject to its ordinary custody and bond procedures.
  4. **If inclusion remains genuinely disputed:** Pending the legally required determination, the Fifth Amendment affords access to individualized interim custody review. The Government may not use the unresolved classification dispute as an irrebuttable substitute for flight-risk or danger assessment. Release is governed by the hearing standard in Issue 4.
  5. **If qualifying inclusion is established:** The ordinary mandatory-detention rule may operate while removal proceedings are diligently pursued, subject independently to Issue 3's continued-detention limit.

  A challenge to one charge does not defeat mandatory detention if another independent qualifying ground is actually established.

  **Application:** Kim's challenge satisfies the threshold for review. *United States v. Corona-Sanchez* supplies a substantial legal objection to treating the 1997 petty-theft-with-priors conviction as an aggravated felony. The added burglary and moral-turpitude allegations require separate legal analysis; they cannot be ignored, but their assertion does not finally establish coverage. The administrative tribunal must examine all asserted grounds without assuming Kim's ultimate success.

* **Reach Within the Question:** Lawful permanent residents held under §1226(c) who genuinely contest whether the asserted conviction-based statutory grounds include them. It governs inclusion decisions and interim custody while those decisions remain materially disputed. It does not adjudicate substantive eligibility for withholding of removal, establish the merits standard for a final removal order, or rewrite §1226(c)(2)'s witness-protection provision as an ordinary bond exception.

* **Next-Case Guidance:** An objection may be substantial even when the Government has a plausible competing interpretation. A conclusory denial or challenge made solely to prolong detention does not satisfy the threshold. Where several independent statutory predicates are asserted, the decisionmaker must address each ground capable of independently sustaining mandatory custody. A resident does not lose the threshold merely by acknowledging that officials lawfully arrested him at the outset.

  Until this Court decides whether the same interim constitutional protection extends to nonpermanent residents with materially different admission circumstances, courts apply the statutory requirement that §1226(c) custody rest on a legally supported qualifying category and the existing *Mathews v. Eldridge* framework for any preserved procedural due-process claim.

* **Transition:** Not applicable. The newly announced standard governs this pending habeas case and other properly pending challenges under ordinary adjudicatory principles; Stone proposes no special reopening of completed immigration or habeas judgments.

* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Section 1226(c)(1)(B) requires detention of an alien who **is deportable** for specified offenses. It does not declare every allegation by immigration officials conclusive. The statute also commands taking custody when the alien is released from criminal confinement, so Kim's proposed reading that no person can come within §1226(c) before a final removal order does not fit the statutory structure. Section 1231 separately addresses post-order custody.

  In *INS v. National Center for Immigrants' Rights*, the Simulation Court sustained a general immigration release rule while preserving the individualized status determinations on which lawful application depended. That decision supports requiring an actual category determination rather than treating a charge as its own proof.

  In *Zadvydas v. Davis*, Parts II and IV, Stone joined a purpose-sensitive construction of detention authority to avoid grave constitutional concerns. This case presents a different statute and an earlier stage of immigration proceedings. Stone uses the interpretive method, not *Zadvydas*'s separate six-month rule.

  The agency's *Matter of Joseph* procedure permits an individual to challenge coverage but generally demands a showing that the Government is substantially unlikely to prevail. That does not adequately answer the interim liberty problem when both sides possess substantial legal arguments. It tests the anticipated outcome of the removal dispute without necessarily examining whether physical confinement is presently necessary.

  *Connecticut Department of Public Safety v. Doe*, as decided in this simulation, holds that due process does not require a hearing on danger when danger is legally irrelevant to a statutory inclusion decision. Stone therefore does not create a general danger hearing simply by labeling an allegation disputed. He first identifies the constitutional limit on treating genuinely contested inclusion as an irrevocable custody predicate. Once that limit operates, individualized custody facts become legally material.

  Kim's 1997 offense requires consideration of *Corona-Sanchez*. The 1996 conviction and alternative moral-turpitude category must also be examined. The Court cannot make those classifications on an incomplete custody record. It can decide that Kim has raised a sufficiently substantial challenge to require process without deciding the underlying removal case.

* **Precedent Treatment:**
  * *INS v. National Center for Immigrants' Rights*: applied to actual determinations underlying immigration custody rules.
  * *Zadvydas v. Davis*, Parts II and IV: avoidance method extended to contested pre-removal category inclusion; its six-month presumption is not adopted.
  * *Connecticut Department of Public Safety v. Doe*: distinguished because the proposed constitutional limit makes necessity evidence material to interim custody once statutory inclusion remains genuinely contested.
  * *Matter of Joseph*: limited as an exclusive method of protecting a resident against categorical confinement during a substantial inclusion dispute. The agency may retain its category procedure but cannot make its substantially-unlikely standard the only route to meaningful interim custody review.
  * *United States v. Corona-Sanchez*: applied as relevant lower-court classification authority, not transformed into a Simulation Court holding on every charge against Kim.

* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The ultimate classification of Kim's 1996 and 1997 convictions and his eligibility for removal relief require agency determinations and a record not finally resolved here. Whether the same interim constitutional safeguard extends in identical form to all nonpermanent residents is outside the presented as-applied claim.

* **Approved Conditional Position or Fallback:** **None newly approved.** Proposed fallback, subject to Stone's approval: retain the substantial, nondilatory threshold and an enforceable inclusion determination while allowing the Government to satisfy a Kennedy-compatible preliminary showing of some merit to the charge. **That showing does not eliminate Kim's individualized interim hearing while material coverage remains genuinely contested.** Stone does not accept a rule that treats some merit as conclusive inclusion or requires Kim to prove nondangerousness by clear and convincing evidence.

### Issue 3: Continued detention and the constitutional duration limit

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Kim spent approximately six months detained without bond after the INS arrested him in February 1999. The record shows a continuance he requested to obtain withholding-of-removal documents, but it does not establish that all delay was attributable to him or that the Government acted improperly. The case presents the legal question whether a valid initial mandatory-detention ground remains sufficient throughout proceedings regardless of their actual length and operation. His conditional release leaves that question live because the Government seeks authority to detain him again.

* **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether the Fifth Amendment permits continued §1226(c) detention solely by statutory category after its actual duration and circumstances cease to bear a reasonable relationship to removal proceedings pursued with reasonable diligence.

* **Stone's Legal Position:** Propose a new application of entering due-process principles to §1226(c). The Simulation Court has not already established this complete immigration-specific duration test. Preserve the approved OT2002 functional approach, without an automatic numerical deadline or a compulsory hearing immediately upon every lawful arrest.

* **Reach Calibration:** **Intermediate.** The test applies to continuing custody of permanent residents within §1226(c), including residents whose category membership has been properly established. One step broader would transplant the post-order six-month presumption from *Zadvydas* or require immediate individualized hearings in every case without examining the initial statutory justification. One step narrower would permit detention to continue solely because it began lawfully, even after the removal-related justification no longer explains its duration.

* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** **Lawful initial detention under §1226(c) does not authorize continued confinement after its nature, length, or circumstances cease to bear a reasonable relationship to removal proceedings prosecuted with reasonable diligence.**

  The rule contains one continuing-authority inquiry:

  1. The detainee identifies a concrete reason to question continued confinement, including significant elapsed time, avoidable delay, an uncertain course of proceedings, or circumstances making the statutory detention purpose increasingly remote.
  2. The Government must supply the relevant chronology, the current basis for custody, the reasons for material delays, and the reasonably anticipated course of proceedings. It bears the burden of proving disputed facts necessary to establish its asserted diligence by a preponderance of the evidence.
  3. The decisionmaker assesses the actual proceedings and custody as a whole. Legitimate litigation of a substantial defense is not automatically misconduct or an excuse for indefinite confinement. Delay pursued solely to postpone removal may be attributed to the detainee. Government-caused or unjustified institutional delay weighs against continued categorical detention.
  4. If categorical detention has lost the reasonable relationship required by due process, detention cannot continue solely on the statutory category. Continued confinement requires the individualized showing under Issue 4, including consideration of effective release conditions.
  5. A material change in duration, prospects, or custody circumstances permits renewed review.

  There is no automatic release at six months, no presumption copied from §1231(a)(6), and no rule that the Government loses solely because a case is difficult. The constitutional question is whether the continued confinement remains justified by the proceedings actually being conducted.

  **Application:** Kim's approximately six months of initial confinement, unresolved classification questions, and the procedural course of his case require a real inquiry. Stone does not find governmental bad faith or unconstitutional delay solely from that history. Because Kim already qualifies for contested-inclusion review, he does not need to win the independent duration claim to receive individualized custody consideration.

* **Reach Within the Question:** Continuing pre-final-order detention of lawful permanent residents under §1226(c). It applies even when a qualifying criminal-removal category has been established. It does not govern detention under a different criminal statute, ordinary civil commitment, or the separate post-order regime in §1231(a).

* **Next-Case Guidance:** Courts must examine actual timing and reasons for delay rather than the Government's aggregate statistics or a mechanical day count. A lengthy but diligently prosecuted proceeding may remain reasonably related to removal; a shorter confinement marked by a serious failure of the predicate or unjustified continuation may not. Repeated custody challenges must identify an initially unconsidered or materially changed circumstance, not merely reargue a completed lawful determination.

  Until this Court decides a distinct claim concerning custody after a final removal order, courts apply *Zadvydas v. Davis* and the governing §1231(a) rules, rather than this §1226(c) formulation.

* **Transition:** Not applicable. Stone proposes no special retrospective application or reopening rule.

* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Several entering-law decisions address different settings. Stone would articulate their shared continuing-authority principle as one functional inquiry for §1226(c), **without merging their separate statutory commands, hearing schedules, or proof burdens**.

  *Reno v. Flores* sustained a temporary immigration custody arrangement with available release and request-based review against a facial challenge. It did not approve unlawful custody, ineffective process, or unreasonable continuation in a particular case. It supports preserving initially lawful detention without granting the Government perpetual authority.

  *Gilbert v. Homar* held, in the continuing-process portion joined by all nine Justices, that justification for an immediate deprivation does not independently establish the lawfulness of continued deprivation. Duration, reliability of the underlying grounds, and the reasons for delay matter. Stone applies that principle to the more serious deprivation of physical liberty.

  *Krimstock v. Kelly*, decided by this Simulation Court on June 23, 2003, required meaningful neutral review of prolonged government retention of seized vehicles and effective consideration of alternatives to continued possession. Its unanimous provisions required the City to support the continuing deprivation. Stone extends the insistence on meaningful review of actual ongoing custody, while recognizing that physical detention has different statutory grounds and risks.

  *Fairley v. Luman*, also decided June 23, 2003, held that an initially supported arrest does not excuse avoidable prolonged mistaken-identity detention when specific protests and readily accessible checks require a timely response. It reinforces the distinction between lawful initial custody and its later operation.

  *Foucha v. Louisiana*, in the portions Stone joined, bars confinement when the authority justifying it has ceased to exist. *Jackson v. Indiana* requires the nature and duration of confinement to maintain a reasonable relationship to its purpose. Those principles supply the substantive boundary.

  *Zadvydas v. Davis*, Parts II and IV, applied a removal-related duration limit to post-order detention under §1231(a)(6). Stone carries forward the connection between custody and removal without importing the separate six-month presumption, which he did not join in the simulation.

  *Carlson v. Landon* sustained detention under a discretionary immigration scheme. It did not determine that Congress may forbid any individualized inquiry regardless of how long detention continues. *Mathews v. Eldridge* governs the procedural adequacy of the required review.

  Applied here, category membership may justify initial confinement; it does not conclusively answer whether the Government may resume holding Kim after the proceedings have developed as they have.

* **Precedent Treatment:**
  * *Reno v. Flores*: reaffirmed as allowing lawful temporary immigration custody, distinguished on actual continuing-custody process.
  * *Gilbert v. Homar*: continuing-deprivation reasoning extended to immigration custody.
  * *Krimstock v. Kelly*: continuing-custody and effective-remedy principles extended from property to personal liberty.
  * *Fairley v. Luman*: applied by analogy to continued custody after a lawful starting point.
  * *Foucha v. Louisiana* and *Jackson v. Indiana*: reaffirmed as substantive limits tying confinement to lawful purpose.
  * *Zadvydas v. Davis*: removal-purpose reasoning extended to §1226(c); the §1231(a)(6) six-month presumption remains confined to its own setting.
  * *Carlson v. Landon*: distinguished; not overruled.

* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Whether Kim's historical detention independently crossed the constitutional duration boundary requires findings about the actual course of his proceedings and cannot be determined solely from the six-month figure. The lawfulness of any future custody must be decided on its then-current record.

* **Approved Conditional Position or Fallback:** **None newly approved.** Proposed fallback, subject to approval: Stone will join a compatible formulation requiring review for unreasonable or unjustified continued detention, even if colleagues decline his complete functional explanation, provided the resulting judgment supplies an enforceable individualized remedy when categorical authority fails. Stone will not accept a formulation under which the mere pendency of removal proceedings conclusively establishes reasonable duration.

### Issue 4: Individualized custody procedure, burden of proof, and effective release

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** The inclusion and duration rules would be ineffective if the required hearing could consider only whether the Government filed a qualifying charge, or if the detainee had to disprove necessity under an unjustified evidentiary presumption. Kim's existing conditional relief expressly concerns release based on flight risk and danger. The hearing's subject, allocation of proof, and power to order conditional release are therefore part of the question and remedy before the Court.

* **Question Stone Resolves:** Once §1226(c)'s categorical basis is constitutionally insufficient or genuinely unsettled, what process and governmental showing permit continued detention, and what relief must follow if the showing fails?

* **Stone's Legal Position:** Propose a new immigration-specific procedural rule. Stone's previously approved OT2002 position placed the clear-and-convincing burden on the Government. His noncontrolling *Krimstock* concurrence favored a governmental preponderance burden for disputed facts underlying continued deprivation in that setting. Neither position is already a Simulation Court holding establishing the burden for §1226(c).

* **Reach Calibration:** **Intermediate.** The rule governs individualized hearings required by the two specified defects, not every administrative release decision. One step broader would constitutionalize the same governmental burden for every immigration custody category and stage without examining the governing authority. One step narrower would recognize a hearing without resolving who must prove necessity, risking continuation of the same deprivation through an evidentiary presumption.

* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** **When the Fifth Amendment requires an individualized custody hearing because §1226(c)'s categorical justification is genuinely contested or no longer sufficient, the Government must justify continued physical confinement and the decisionmaker must consider whether effective release conditions will address the established risks.**

  **Stone's preferred rule:** The Government bears the burden of proving, by clear and convincing evidence, that no combination of reasonably available conditions can adequately address flight risk or danger. The detainee must identify a substantial coverage dispute or a concrete continued-detention concern to invoke the applicable gate, but need not prove that release is safe.

  **Proposed  fallback:** If a five-Justice majority does not support the heightened quantum, Stone may join a compatible judgment placing the burden of persuasion on the Government by a **preponderance of the evidence**. The object of proof remains the same: whether actual detention, rather than reasonably enforceable conditions, is necessary.

  Under either formulation:

  1. The Government identifies the claimed risks and supporting evidence.
  2. The detainee receives a meaningful opportunity to present contrary evidence.
  3. A neutral decisionmaker considers residence, family and community ties, the nature and age of relevant convictions, conduct during release, reliability of the removal case insofar as it affects appearance incentives, and feasible conditions.
  4. Conditions are considered individually and in combination.
  5. A reasoned determination explains why conditions suffice or why custody is required.
  6. When the Government fails its burden, continued detention is unauthorized under the applicable constitutional rule and conditional release must be available.
  7. Renewed review follows material changes, not a fixed automatic schedule.

  **Application:** Kim's long residence beginning in childhood, permanent-resident status, convictions, conduct during his existing bond release, any reliable evidence of missed appearances, and available conditions are all relevant. The record does not permit this Court to make the ultimate danger or flight finding.

* **Reach Within the Question:** Required individualized custody hearings for lawful permanent residents affected by Issue 2 or Issue 3. The rule does not replace every ordinary §1226(a) discretionary bond procedure, change the burden in a final removal proceeding, or alter the separate statutory witness-protection requirements of §1226(c)(2).

* **Next-Case Guidance:** A hearing limited to repeating the qualifying charge is insufficient after a constitutional gate opens. A decisionmaker must possess actual authority to order conditional release when the Government fails its burden. An earlier adverse decision may be reconsidered on a material change in risks, conditions, or proceedings.

  Until this Court determines whether identical burdens apply to different detention statutes, courts apply the rules of those statutes and the existing *Mathews* framework, not an automatic extension of this §1226(c) hearing rule.

* **Transition:** Not applicable. The hearing rule governs this pending challenge and appropriately pending analogous detention proceedings without creating a separate reopening command for final judgments.

* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** *Mathews v. Eldridge* requires attention to the liberty interest, the danger of error, the value of additional safeguards, and the Government's legitimate administrative interests. Physical confinement is a severe deprivation. Once the category itself cannot constitutionally settle the necessity question, an individualized hearing is the procedure that addresses the precise remaining uncertainty.

  *United States v. Salerno* sustained a carefully limited preventive-detention regime featuring individualized adversarial proceedings and governmental clear-and-convincing proof of dangerousness. It does not establish a universal burden for all civil detention, but supplies a strong analogy for Stone's preferred standard when the Government seeks confinement of a person who cannot be held categorically.

  *Addington v. Texas* required clear and convincing proof for ordinary civil commitment. Again, that does not automatically govern immigration custody. It reinforces why Stone regards the risk of mistaken deprivation of liberty as requiring persuasive governmental evidence.

  In *Foucha v. Louisiana*, Stone joined the holdings invalidating continued confinement after the authorizing basis had ended, but did not join a general theory imposing one proof quantum across detention regimes. Stone's burden here is therefore a proposed rule grounded in this record, not a falsely attributed universal *Foucha* holding.

  In *Krimstock v. Kelly*, the Simulation Court required the City to support continued deprivation and required the decisionmaker to consider interim release. Stone separately proposed governmental proof by a preponderance for disputed predicates of continued custody. That concurrence supplies an internally consistent fallback where colleagues resist clear and convincing proof, although it did not itself establish a binding general rule.

  *Cooper v. Oklahoma* rejected an excessive clear-and-convincing burden on a criminal defendant contesting competence to stand trial. Its holding is not a direct rule for immigration bond, but it illustrates why a burden may impermissibly skew the consequences of uncertainty. The critical choice here is who bears the risk of uncertain need for detention after the categorical justification has ceased to suffice.

  *Connecticut Department of Public Safety v. Doe* does not defeat this hearing requirement. Before either gate opens, individual danger may be irrelevant to the statutory category. After the category is genuinely unsettled or constitutionally insufficient to support continued confinement, actual necessity becomes a material legal question.

  Congress's release-pending-appeal provision, 18 U.S.C. §3143(b), puts a clear-and-convincing nondangerousness and nonflight burden on a convicted criminal defendant. Breyer has identified it as an analogy. Stone declines to transplant that allocation: Kim is not seeking release pending appeal of the criminal convictions, and the Government seeks to confine him under a separate civil immigration scheme after the categorical justification has become legally inadequate. A final criminal conviction may support a charge and supply relevant risk evidence, but it does not decide whether conditions can serve the purposes of this detention.

* **Precedent Treatment:**
  * *Mathews v. Eldridge*: applied to the constitutionally required custody process.
  * *United States v. Salerno*: used as a persuasive, regime-specific analogy for governmental clear-and-convincing proof; not treated as universally controlling.
  * *Addington v. Texas*: distinguished as civil-commitment law while reinforcing protection against erroneous confinement.
  * *Foucha v. Louisiana*: reaffirmed on the continuation of custody after lawful authority fails; no universal burden attributed to Stone's prior joins.
  * *Krimstock v. Kelly*: its binding procedural holdings applied by analogy; Stone's separate governmental-preponderance view identified expressly as noncontrolling.
  * *Cooper v. Oklahoma*: distinguished in subject matter, applied by analogy concerning the consequences of an excessive burden.
  * *Connecticut Department of Public Safety v. Doe*: distinguished according to whether the fact to be heard is legally material.
  * *18 U.S.C. §3143(b)*: distinguished as a statutory criminal postconviction release allocation, not adopted as the constitutional rule for this custody.

* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The precise constitutionally required proof allocation for a different immigration detention category is outside the question presented. The record lacks a completed risk assessment sufficient for the Court to decide whether Kim personally qualifies for detention on the individualized evidence.

* **Approved Conditional Position or Fallback:** **None newly approved.** Stone proposes  for approval: governmental preponderance as a permissible fallback from governmental clear and convincing, with identical individualized hearing authority, conditions analysis, and enforceable remedy. Stone does **not** authorize a fallback imposing Breyer's clear-and-convincing burden on Kim.

# SECTION III: HISTORICAL COMPARATOR SUPPLEMENT

## A. MERITS COMPARATOR

* **Historical Outcome and Vote:** Reversed, April 29, 2003 (argued January 15, 2003). The Court sustained habeas jurisdiction 6-3 and upheld mandatory detention under §1226(c) for the period of removal proceedings 5-4.
* **Historical Opinion Structure:** Rehnquist, C.J., delivered the opinion of the Court. Kennedy joined it in full; Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer joined Part I (jurisdiction); O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas joined all but Part I. Kennedy filed a concurring opinion. O'Connor, joined by Scalia and Thomas, concurred in part and in the judgment: she read §1226(e) to withdraw habeas review of §1226(c) detention decisions and doubted a Suspension Clause objection, but joined the merits once jurisdiction was sustained. Souter, joined by Stevens and Ginsburg, concurred in part and dissented in part. Breyer concurred in part and dissented in part.
* **Historical Holding:** (1) Section 1226(e) contains no explicit bar on habeas review. It does not preclude a constitutional challenge to the statutory framework that permits detention without bail, as distinct from a challenge to a discretionary judgment. (2) Congress, concerned by evidence that deportable criminal aliens who were not detained continued to commit crimes and failed to appear, may require detention of persons such as Kim for the brief period necessary for removal proceedings. The Court relied on Carlson v. Landon and Reno v. Flores. It distinguished Zadvydas because detention before a removal order serves its removal purpose and has a definite, usually brief, end point. It treated Kim as having conceded deportability, and noted that he sought no Joseph hearing and had himself requested a continuance. Kennedy wrote that a permanent resident could be entitled to an individualized determination of flight risk and dangerousness if continued detention became unreasonable or unjustified, for example through unreasonable governmental delay; he noted that the adequacy of the Joseph procedure was not decided. Souter would require an individualized finding of flight risk or danger for permanent residents; he rejected the concession characterization and would affirm. Breyer would read §1226(c) to permit bail where the challenge to deportability is not insubstantial and not interposed solely for delay, borrowing 18 U.S.C. §3143(b)'s requirement that the alien show by clear and convincing evidence that he is unlikely to flee or pose a danger. He would accept brief detention without bail if deportability were conceded, and would remand to the Ninth Circuit to apply his standard.
* **Historical Authority:** Demore v. Kim, 538 U.S. 510 (2003). The Court's account of detention length rested on agency figures: a 47-day average and 30-day median for most cases, and about four further months on appeal.
