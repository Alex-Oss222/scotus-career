## Common rule and actual disagreement

All eight accept that custody asks whether the objective circumstances would cause a reasonable person in the interviewee's position to understand restraint equivalent to formal arrest. Miranda supplies the warning consequence; Berkemer and Stansbury exclude uncommunicated subjective beliefs, while communicated words and conduct matter to the extent they affect the objective setting. Thompson classifies the ultimate issue as mixed law and fact under the former statute; it does not remove AEDPA's separately applicable restriction. Mathiason and Beheler show that station location and suspicion alone do not establish custody.

The reversal group considers voluntary arrival with parents, absence of formal arrest, physical restraint or threats, offered breaks and eventual departure sufficient to leave the state conclusion within the established rule's reasonable range. It acknowledges the opposing force of the two-hour duration, station isolation, absence of an express freedom-to-leave assurance and accusatory questioning. Neither ultimate departure nor politeness alone is dispositive. Section 2254(d)(1) requires objectively unreasonable application, not an independently preferred result.

The opposing group finds the encounter objectively arrest-equivalent: a young person brought by parents at police request, separated from them and questioned alone in a small station room for two hours while confronted with contrary evidence would understand official control over whether he could terminate the encounter. Permission for breaks near the end can reinforce that control; ultimate release does not answer the setting during questioning. The combination, in their view, exceeds reasonable application rather than merely presenting a better interpretation. They do not require violence, handcuffs or an express arrest declaration and do not treat apparent age alone as custody.

Apparent youth and private experience are distinct. The reversal group concludes that established custody holdings did not require the additional age treatment the Ninth Circuit demanded on this record; it need not adopt a universal direct-review rule excluding age. O'Connor additionally emphasizes proximity to adulthood and the difficulty of assigning a fixed age-based reaction. The four custody proponents consider obvious youth an objective circumstance within the already established reasonable-person-in-position inquiry. All reject deriving custody from uncommunicated psychological assumptions about prior police experience. A separate voluntariness or waiver test cannot silently replace the custody trigger.
