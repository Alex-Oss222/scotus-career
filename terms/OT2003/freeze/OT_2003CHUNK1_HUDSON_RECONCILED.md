## Components retained after reconciliation

### 1. Actual delegation before preemption

Sections 1103(a)(1), (2), (3), (9)(A) and (c), read together, authorize retained federal control of public release of information acquired in carrying out the covered federal detention function. The administration-and-enforcement assignment and necessary-regulations provision operate with the specific control of Service files and records and the authorization for paid nonfederal housing, care and security of persons detained under federal law. The Commissioner's authority operates through the Attorney General's delegation under subsection (c), rather than originating in the Commissioner's own assessment of national importance.

The subsection (a)(1) assignment preserves powers allocated by law to the President, Secretary of State, State Department officers and diplomatic or consular officers. Its Attorney-General legal-ruling proviso does not displace independent judicial examination of statutory boundaries under Haggar and Mead. Subsection (a)(9)(A)'s funded-custody authority does not make every county document a Service record, and subsection (a)(2) standing alone is not treated as an express transfer of all local files. The combined detention, records and implementing scheme supplies authority for the covered custody information. A voluntary agreement supplies neither an absent congressional delegation nor a power over unrelated sovereign records.

Haggar requires courts to enforce unambiguous statutory limits before accepting a reasonable delegated choice. Mead distinguishes actual force-of-law authority from administrative responsibility, expertise and institutional endorsement. The general, binding detention-information rule exercises the specific statutory assignment; it differs from Mead's revisable transaction-specific classification letter. Rulemaking form alone is insufficient. Scalia's different Mead approach permits agreement here because he also locates delegation in the statutory scheme, not in signatures or labels. No revival of his broader Mead position is necessary to this conclusion.

Section 236.6 reaches public and private custodians housing, maintaining, serving or otherwise holding detainees on the Service's behalf, and other persons acquiring the information through an official or contractual relationship with such custodians. It forbids their public release of the covered information outside applicable federal laws, regulations and executive orders. Its declaration that documents containing that information are not public records applies in the covered setting. It channels disclosure; it does not itself establish that every request under federal disclosure law must be denied. Detainees remain free under the stated rule explanation to identify themselves, communicate and seek counsel. This case decides no underlying detention's legality, independent detainee-rights claim or unappealed common-law-access claim.

### 4. Own-operation regulation and genuine participation

Condon directly permits regulation of a State's own covered information handling, including the employee work required for compliance, without assigning legislation or a regulatory program against unrelated private persons. The counties voluntarily undertook paid federal custody arrangements. Section 236.6 controls public handling of information acquired through that accepted function; it applies to private custodians and related holders as well. No States-only regulation question is decided.

Lomont's actual-refusal rule preserves the distinction between genuine voluntary assistance and a compelled assignment. Its unconditional freedom to decline assistance to private applicants does not grant an existing federal custodian a right to ignore valid rules for the resulting information. The affirmative record premise is voluntary entry into a paid custody operation. No immediate termination right, contractual notice term, entitlement to discharge detainees or authority to disclose retained information after termination is inferred from the agency's assertion that contractors can take steps to end their relationship.

EDC adds the unanimous requirement to examine the actual regulated object and legal choices, not an ownership label. A lawful and available direct-discharge route answered the optional third-party-management issue there. Hudson regulates the custodian's own information handling and imposes no analogous third-party regulatory assignment; it needs no invented alternative permitting scheme. EDC's actual-choice requirement does not establish missing contract-exit terms and is not treated as evidence of such terms. Both decisions preserve the separate statutory-source inquiry. Printz's rule against compelled executive administration remains distinct; it turns on compulsion rather than reimbursement and creates no compensation rule.
