The Deatons bought approximately twelve acres for a proposed five-lot subdivision. The middle was wet in winter and after heavy rain; the attempted sewage permit failed. Despite the conservation-service warning about wetlands permitting, they hired a contractor in 1990 to excavate an approximately 1,100-foot drainage ditch and sidecast the soil. The prior appeal settled that this sidecasting was a discharge. That conclusion is not reopened in this second appeal.

The property's wetlands adjoin the roadside ditch. Surface water drains to that ditch, then through a culvert under the road to the John Adkins Prong of Perdue Creek, to Beaverdam Creek, and eventually to the navigable Wicomico River and Chesapeake Bay. Dams and ponds occur along the route. The receiving navigable river is about eight miles away; the route to the Bay is approximately thirty-two miles. The parties dispute the amount and consistency of flow, not the described drainage route. This is not a finding that every point carries water continuously, that every ditch drains to a navigable river, or that this particular fill was traced to the Bay.

The owners concede adjacency of the wetlands to the roadside ditch; their relevant objection denies that the ditch is a covered tributary. They do not independently argue that wetlands must touch the navigable river itself even if this ditch qualifies. Vegetation and hydric soils satisfy the applicable criteria without dispute; the delineation challenge concerns hydrology. The restoration alternative remains independently presented.

Legal authority and constitutional eligibility receive independent review. Agency interpretation does not establish disputed hydrological facts. Factual findings retain their appropriate review protection, and selection of this restoration remedy is reviewed for abuse of discretion. No missing finding is manufactured to sustain the judgment.

## Selected propositions and competing paths

### D1. Statutory coverage and the two distinct interpretive inquiries

Sections 1311, 1344 and 1362 operate together: the discharge prohibition and permit program apply to navigable waters, defined in section 1362(7) as waters of the United States. Section 1344(a) entrusts dredged-or-fill permits to the Secretary; subsection (d) identifies the Secretary of the Army acting through the Chief of Engineers. The program's structured permit, guideline, general-permit and state-administration provisions supply actual administrative authority. This is a congressional delegation implemented through binding Corps regulations, not authority inferred solely from statutory ambiguity, expertise, the regulation's label or longstanding practice. Under Haggar and Mead, the Court first enforces the statute and the limits of that delegation, then reviews a reasonable agency choice within genuine unresolved scope. Barnhart's completed holding reinforces that sequence; its separate section 405(a) delegation does not supply the Army's authority.

Congress did not resolve the exact reach of every remote, nonnavigable branch of a navigable water's tributary system. The enacted definition is broader than navigability in fact; Riverside Bayview's surviving adjacent-wetland rule confirms that point without deciding every connection. The Corps reasonably includes this physically connected tributary system and its conceded adjacent wetlands to protect the receiving waters against upstream discharges. This is an application of sections 328.3(a)(1), (5) and (7), including the latter provision's exclusion of wetlands themselves as the predicate waters. It is not approval of every other-water criterion in subsection (a)(3), every isolated pond, or every possible use of the term tributary.

There is a separate regulatory question: whether tributaries in subsection (a)(5) include feeder branches that reach the navigable water through intermediate streams. The text permits that ordinary whole-system meaning; the alternative direct-feeder meaning is linguistically plausible but not compelled. The Corps' responsible, considered construction consistently uses the term for the feeder system. The 1975 preamble's primary, secondary and tertiary tributaries support that usage; the former headwaters restriction limited coverage then rather than establishing that more remote streams were never tributaries. Under the current Auer rule, this fair construction controls because it is neither plainly erroneous nor inconsistent with the regulation. It does not receive force simply because it appears in a brief or preamble, and it cannot displace contrary text or prove that a drainage route exists.

**Strong contrary path:** SWANCC's statutory restraint, the word navigable, state responsibility and the potential breadth of all tributaries could favor a direct-feeder limit or rejection of this regulation as beyond delegation. The answer is the actual statutory program plus a supported water connection. Current SWANCC excludes bird-use-only authority over the isolated ponds there and expressly preserves materially different supported water connections and Riverside Bayview. It supplies neither a universal present-navigability rule nor a direct-adjacency requirement. Nor does it authorize jurisdiction whenever environmental benefits cross state lines. Here the physical drainage relationship, supported receiving-water effects and lawful regulatory chain supply what bird use alone did not.

The lower opinion's proposition that ambiguity alone necessarily delegates interpretive authority is not adopted. Mead requires the preceding inquiry into statutory conferral and its actual exercise. Likewise, incompatible historical descriptions of SWANCC or Commerce doctrine in the lower opinion are not carried into this commitment. If statutory coverage failed, relief from the application would precede the constitutional question; that rival disposition is rejected on this record for the reasons above.

### D2. Congress may protect the navigable receiving channel against this upstream discharge class

The enacted conduct at issue is discharge of dredged or fill material into the covered connected waters, not the owners' development business in general. The interstate mechanism is physical protection of a navigable waterway from pollutant effects carried through its actual tributary system, including the effects of discharge into adjacent wetlands. Congress's channel power permits protection against injurious uses; it is not confined to keeping a boat passage free of a physical obstruction. Rio Grande Dam, 174 U.S. 690, 708–709, supplies protection of the navigable water against upstream interference; Appalachian Electric Power, 311 U.S. 377, Oklahoma ex rel. Phillips, 313 U.S. 508, 525–526, and Grand River Dam Authority, 363 U.S. 229, 232, support protection through relevant nonnavigable reaches. Caminetti, 242 U.S. 470, 491, supports protection of channels against harmful uses. None creates a general federal power over local land.

Lopez preserves direct channel protection while requiring identification of the regulated class and a real interstate mechanism before aggregation can do work. The agreed route to the Wicomico establishes the particular water connection. The lower opinion reports the Corps' supporting evidence that discharges into nonnavigable tributaries and adjacent wetlands substantially affect water quality in navigable receiving waters; the owners did not contend that the reported class effect was overstated. That is affirmative supported evidence, not a missing-file inference against them. Together the actual drainage and the supported pollution mechanism justify preventative permit regulation of the connected discharge class. Neither an independently substantial effect from this single fill nor proof that an identified particle arrived in the river is required. The dispute over amount and consistency of flow does not itself disprove the agreed drainage route or the supported mechanism. No unstated flow-volume threshold or permanent-water requirement is invented.

**Strong contrary path:** distant flow through intervening streams, ponds and dams may attenuate the relationship so far that channel language becomes a label for ordinary local land regulation. This objection would matter if the Government relied on eventual economic consequences, a geographical assertion alone, or unsupported speculation that every ditch connects. Here the record identifies the route and supplies the class's receiving-water effect. Regulating discharge into that connected system fits protection of the identified navigable channel. The holding therefore does not rest on an unlimited claim that the CWA reaches every constitutional power Congress might possess.

Home Builders' independent substantial-interstate-externality route would require its own concrete cross-border effect, substantial class importance, territorial-control limit and fit. The Bay's name, multiple states' environmental interest or desirability of national uniformity alone would not meet that test. Because direct channel protection fully resolves this application, these commitments neither add an independent externality holding nor silently erase Scalia and Thomas's opposition to that route. Nor is the mere breadth of the CWA a demonstrated necessary-inclusion mechanism under Holston and the Hale line.

Ho's completed November 20 holding supplies a distinct comparison. It sustained regulation of an actual asbestos-abatement service operation in an interstate service market; prevention and notice did not require proof of an actual asbestos release once that market connection was established. A contractor was hired here, but no new market findings, bid comparison or commercial element may be invented from Ho. The hydrological and channel ground is sufficient on this record. Ho confirms that prevention may follow a demonstrated eligible connection; it does not establish the water connection in this case or make every development project a service-market case. The Associates' earlier differences about economic habitat conversion remain intact without requiring an unnecessary alternative holding here.

### D3. The twelve-inch root-zone indicator supports this delineation within the complete definition

Section 328.3(b) requires inundation or saturation by surface or ground water at a frequency and duration sufficient to support, and under normal circumstances actually supporting, a prevalence of vegetation typically adapted to saturated soils. Water within twelve inches on one occasion is not a replacement for those conditions. The 1987 Manual applies the regulatory definition through vegetation, hydric soil and hydrology. It ranks recorded water data above field indicators; within field methods it recognizes visual observation of saturation, including saturation within a major portion of the root zone, usually within twelve inches of the surface. That saturation can have the overriding effect on vegetation relevant to wetlands hydrology.

The Corps used the Manual's express root-zone indicator and visual methodology. The owners monitored twenty-two wells for several months and argued that the sites were not saturated to the surface for sufficient time. They relied on the Manual's description of saturation to the surface at some time during the growing season. Their preserved contention is that the Corps misapplied the Manual; they did not argue that the Manual itself is a plainly erroneous or inconsistent interpretation of the regulation. Reading its general surface description together with its specific root-zone indicator defeats the claimed contradiction. Their reported monitoring assertion addresses a surface threshold and does not itself refute application of the express root-zone indicator.

**Strong contrary path:** a lower-ranked field observation cannot automatically overcome more reliable measurements, and the regulation retains frequency and duration. That would require a return for findings if the Court had to resolve genuinely contrary root-zone measurements or supply an absent regulatory condition. But the described challenge does not establish that factual conflict: it uses the asserted surface-only legal requirement to attack an express indicator. Auer supports the responsible, scientifically grounded regulatory interpretation; it does not elevate an observation above all recorded data or transform the Manual into authority to disregard the regulation. Affirmance rejects this preserved interpretation objection, retains the undisputed soil and vegetation findings, and leaves the full regulatory combination intact. No fourteen-day rule, growing-season percentage, universal twelve-inch classification or new factual presumption is adopted.

### D4. The restoration order survives independent review of the owners' alternative

The district court required refilling the excavated ditch and restoration of previolation elevation. The owners proposed hauling sidecast soil to a nonwetland part of their land, arguing that digging alone is not the regulated discharge and that this removal would have avoided the violation if used initially. The court found that their proposal would retain the drainage benefit of the unlawful project and doubted that removal could occur without further ecological harm. Those are actual reasons for the selected restoration, not consequences presumed from jurisdiction.

A restoration order must bear an equitable relationship to the actual violation, be practically achievable and serve restoration of the affected environment. Cumberland Farms, 826 F.2d 1151, 1164, supplies persuasive factors concerning environmental benefit, feasibility and equitable relation; this commitment does not turn those factors into a new mandatory checklist or require a formulaic finding under each heading. The supported findings adequately connect refilling to restoration of the previolation condition and explain why removal alone would be deficient or harmful. Section 1251(a)'s restoration objective supports that remedial choice; it is not an independent source of unlimited jurisdiction over the property.

**Strong contrary path:** because digging itself was not the established discharge violation, refilling may appear to punish an otherwise lawful excavation rather than correct the deposited material. The answer is the relationship of the chosen remedy to this completed unlawful project and the supported harm, not a newly announced prohibition on digging. An option that might have avoided a violation before the work does not necessarily restore the affected wetlands afterward. Removing the piles while preserving the drainage benefit and risking further damage need not be accepted as an adequate remedy. On this record the district court did not abuse its discretion. The affirmance authorizes neither general redesign of the parcel nor unrelated enhancement or punishment.

## Disposition, current-law screen and source limits

Affirm the reviewed jurisdiction and delineation determination and the restoration order. The district court implements refilling and restoration of previolation elevation within the existing order; this is not a permit grant, a new penalty, or an enlargement of the owners' duties beyond that order. The earlier sidecasting decision remains settled. No retained objection is disposed of merely by reference to the Government's environmental goal.
