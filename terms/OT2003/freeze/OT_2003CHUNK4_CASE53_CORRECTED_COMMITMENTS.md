## Seven-Associate ground: presentation remains claim-specific

A prisoner must fairly alert the appropriate state court to the federal nature of each claim in the petition or other papers properly presenting it. The court need not inspect a lower-court opinion outside those ordinary submissions to discover an unstated federal basis. No fixed citation formula is required: a constitutional provision, a case identifying the federal right, or a description that actually identifies the federal claim may suffice. A federal reference associated with one claim does not automatically identify every other claim as federal.

Picard requires a meaningful opportunity to decide the federal claim, and Boerckel requires that claim to be presented through the State's ordinary available round. Castille preserves the need for proper presentation, rather than locating a theory somewhere in the larger record. Reese's petition names trial and appellate assistance, but its express Fifth, Sixth and Fourteenth Amendment references address coercion to waive a jury. Its appellate-assistance allegation does not identify a federal source. The postconviction court's Jones v. Barnes citation appears in a different lower opinion; the intervening appellate disposition was a summary affirmance. The rule requiring attachment of that appellate disposition establishes neither attachment nor incorporation of the postconviction opinion.

The circuit's presumption that the highest court must have examined the latter substitutes possible discovery for actual fair presentation. This case supplies no finding of proper incorporation or actual notice that independently cures the omission. The State's opportunity to choose discretionary cases from the submissions made to it does not include a general obligation to reconstruct an unstated claim from earlier documents.

## Guinn considered on its actual merits

Guinn addressed a petition expressly alleging federal and state appellate-assistance violations arising from an omitted shackling issue. It introduced its discussion as concerning adequate assistance under Oregon Article I, section 11, or the Sixth Amendment. It then required proof that competent appellate counsel would have raised the omitted claim and that a different result was more probable than not. That is meaningful evidence of a shared discussion of both constitutional sources in that setting.

It does not establish Reese's proposed automatic inference. Guinn's own pleading expressly identified both constitutions, and the opinion did not hold that a bare state appellate-assistance allegation necessarily presents both rights. Its omitted-issue standard, its notice-of-appeal discussion and its particular shackling proof sequence do not establish universal identity of all state and federal assistance standards. Its more-probable-than-not wording must be read as written, rather than silently converted into a different federal prejudice formulation. The substantive question whether two protections overlap also differs from the procedural question whether this litigant asked the state court to decide a federal claim.

The seven therefore reject Guinn as a cure for these papers' missing federal identification. A recognized state-law formulation may help identify a federal theory when the actual submission gives that notice; the Court does not impose a categorical rule that state-law citations or contextual descriptions can never do so. Here Reese neither cited Guinn nor otherwise identified the appellate theory as federal, while expressly identifying federal sources for other allegations. The complete Guinn text does not change that conclusion. This is a rejection of the offered fair-notice inference on the established record, not a universal ruling that Oregon and federal assistance law always differ or that equivalent standards could never matter.

## Stevens: the Oregon context supplies meaningful notice

Stevens considers Guinn and accepts its narrower significance for fair presentation. Oregon's highest court itself discussed inadequate appellate assistance as a protection under both constitutions and supplied a unified formulation for counsel's failure to raise an appellate claim. A petition expressly complaining of inadequate appellate counsel, within a short submission asserting federal constitutional defects in representation and conviction, would naturally alert that court to the recognized constitutional claim. Guinn makes this an identified Oregon legal context, rather than an assumption that every allegation about a lawyer is federal.

Stevens does not require proof that every Oregon and federal assistance element is identical in all circumstances. The question is notice, not ultimate entitlement to relief or equivalence of every prejudice quantum. Nor does he depend on a rule requiring the highest court to search all lower opinions: the submitted appellate-assistance allegation is understood against that court's own published legal framework. The earlier postconviction court's Jones v. Barnes treatment confirms the existing claim's identity, but does not itself become an attachment or an independently sufficient presentation to the highest court.

The strongest objection is that the petition's express federal citations concern trial rights and jury waiver. Stevens regards the petition's collective references to ineffective trial and appellate counsel, its federal constitutional framing and Guinn's treatment of the appellate right as sufficient together. Isolating each federal citation from that setting demands more formality than Picard's fair-opportunity standard requires. He therefore retains his vote to affirm federal consideration without adopting the Ninth Circuit's broader presumed-search rationale or deciding the assistance claim's ultimate merits.
