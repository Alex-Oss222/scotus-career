## ZO1 — Titles III and IV

Source: `sources/chunk2_comparator/02-1674.ZO1.html.txt`, https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/02-1674.ZO1.html. Historical author: Rehnquist. The closing joinder note specifies O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter in full; Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer except section 305; Thomas for sections 304, 305, 307, 316, 319 and 403(b). Thomas's treatment of the other provisions must be determined from his own writing rather than inferred from this table.

Section 305: the writing treats McConnell's next possible lowest-unit-rate injury, before his 2008 primary, as too remote. It affirms dismissal for lack of standing. It describes the statutory loss of the lowest rate and the identification/approval alternative; it does not hold that broadcasters must charge more.

Section 307: Adams's diffuse equal-influence injury is distinguished from denied ballot access or an unequal vote. The candidate plaintiffs' personal decision not to solicit larger lawful contributions defeats traceability for the competitive theory. Paul's distinct Press Clause and institutional-media theory receives a redressability disposition: the special direct-review route reaches BCRA's increases and indexing, while the underlying FECA limits belong in the section 437h route. Invalidating only the increases would leave those limits and the media exemption intact. The writing affirms dismissal of both groups' section 307 challenges. It does not merge Paul's asserted injury with Adams's theory.

Sections 304, 316 and 319: the writing rejects the Adams theories on the same cognizable-injury and traceability grounds and adds that no plaintiff was identified as a candidate facing the required self-funded opponent. It affirms dismissal. Notes 1 and 2 preserve the distinction between the opposition-personal-funds calculation, the threshold for increased contributions, and the separate circumstances removing coordinated-party limits. The latter are ten times the threshold in a Senate race or more than $350,000 in a House race; these are not one universal trigger.

Section 311: the actual challenge concerns extending attribution/disclaimer requirements to electioneering communications. The writing sustains that extension for its campaign-finance information relationship, expressly assuming the validity of the underlying provision and the other unchallenged amendments. It does not adjudicate every attribution detail.

Section 318: the writing affirms invalidation of the entire contribution/donation prohibition for persons aged seventeen or younger. It applies contribution scrutiny, identifies the scant supported conduit-evasion showing and existing anti-conduit prohibition, and independently finds the complete age ban too broad. It mentions alternative state approaches without approving their constitutionality. Note 3 distinguishes examples in the district record from examples offered in the Supreme Court. The historical judgment is not evidence that Thomas joined this scrutiny rationale.

Section 403(b): the intervenor-standing objection need not be reached because FEC is a proper party advancing the identical position. The writing preserves, rather than decides, whether the intervenors independently require or possess standing. All nine historical Justices join this part under the closing note.

## ZO2 — Title V

Source: `sources/chunk2_comparator/02-1674.ZO2.html.txt`, https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/02-1674.ZO2.html. Historical author: Breyer, joined in the whole writing by Stevens, O'Connor, Souter and Ginsburg. The opinion reverses the district court's facial invalidation of section 504. Its closing note names those four joins; there are no numbered substantive footnotes. The appendix reproduces both the amended statute and the earlier candidate-file regulation.

The analysis separates three categories: candidate requests; anyone's requests about identified candidates or federal elections; and national political/legislative issue requests. The first rests on the longstanding candidate-file rule, the agency's modest annual-burden estimate, equal-time and lowest-rate enforcement, campaign disclosure/source verification, and public/regulatory access. The second extends overlapping enforcement and licensing purposes to requests by others, with a small incremental administrative burden. The third relies on the licensing/public-issues functions, comparable longstanding language, and the FCC's authority to specify administration and reduce burdens.

The opinion rejects facial vagueness and overbreadth objections but preserves challenges to implementing regulations and particular applications. Its campaign-strategy discussion assumes constitutional protection only for argument's sake. It distinguishes required request/identity information from substantive campaign content, identifies statutory timing language as allowing appropriate rules, and finds no demonstrated basis to invalidate all applications. It does not find that strategic disclosure can never occur or that a constitutionally protective timing rule already exists.

Part V expressly addresses purchasers' speech interests as well as broadcasters' administrative interests. It also rejects the proposition that old unchallenged regulations alone prove constitutionality: their supporting records, experience, enforcement purposes and burdens must be assessed. The historical opinion concludes this facial challenge fails under any potentially applicable First Amendment standard, including heightened scrutiny; it does not announce one exclusive scrutiny category for every future application.

The appendix requires records of accepted and rejected qualifying purchase requests, with the applicable rate, airing date/time, class, candidate/election/issue identification, candidate/committee/treasurer data or other purchaser/contact/leadership data. The statutory retention minimum is two years. The older regulation separately includes candidate free time and specifies immediate filing absent unusual circumstances. Do not transfer every feature of that older regulation into the statutory text as if Congress repeated it.

## ZD1 — Stevens on section 305

Source: `sources/chunk2_comparator/02-1674.ZD1.html.txt` and the recovered PDF above. Historical author: Stevens, joined throughout by Ginsburg and Breyer. Both the star joinder note and the dagger Lujan citation have been verified.

The dissent rejects a categorical temporal standing barrier: the claimed effect on a planned campaign can be concrete even when distant. It acknowledges a closer traceability question because broadcasters may retain ordinary candidate rates, but nevertheless would reach and sustain section 305. Its merits grounds are information about sponsors, substantial overlap with section 311, a modest marginal burden and withdrawal of a benefit rather than a new general suppression. It rejects the viewpoint characterization because the condition treats references to opposing candidates alike, including responses. Its concession that traceability is arguable is not a vote to dismiss, and no separate concurrence changing that disposition appears.
