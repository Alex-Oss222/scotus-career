## Authority and complete disposition

Six Associates support reversal: Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Scalia and Souter support affirmance. Four Associates directly support an affirmative constitutional-restoration rationale: Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer. Kennedy's and Thomas's judgment votes cannot be counted as full joins in that rationale.

The four restoration supporters hold that Congress may lift this restriction on an existing tribe's criminal jurisdiction over nonmember Indians, that section 1301(2) does so, and that the Tribe's prosecution consequently exercises tribal power for the separate-sovereigns rule. Wheeler supplies the ultimate-source inquiry, Duro supplies the prior restriction, and the amendment changes the governing federal policy. The strongest dissenting point is that Duro described outside criminal authority as delegation and dependent status as the cause. The four answer that Duro resolved the sources then existing and did not adjudicate an affirmative restoration statute as constitutionally forbidden. That answer must accompany the holding; a label cannot replace it.

Kennedy instead treats the statute's choice as excluding the federal-delegation theory needed for this defense and leaves the legitimacy of the asserted tribal authority to a proceeding actually contesting it. His constitutional concern is structural and cannot be reduced to lack of counsel or cured merely by mentioning due process. Thomas regards statutory recognition and inherited federal-common-law premises as enough here, while declining the Court opinion's affirmative account of congressional constitutional power. Both positions perform substantive separate-writing work if the lead opinion adopts the four-Associate restoration ground. Thomas's full comparator also discusses executive-control difficulties with a federal delegation; those historical discussions do not silently import any post-divergence holding into this timeline or establish new facts about federal participation.

The judgment rejecting double jeopardy permits the federal prosecution to proceed under ordinary criminal procedure. It does not review the tribal conviction on a separate due-process or equal-protection claim. That limit follows from the question and posture supplied in the frozen packet; no additional waiver, consent, procedural default, or merits resolution of tribal trial rights is inferred. The holding's scope concerns the nonmember Indian and existing tribe before the Court; it supplies no ruling authorizing tribal criminal jurisdiction over non-Indians.
