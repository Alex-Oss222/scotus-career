# Martinez v. Bush — neutral primary-source addendum

Event: April 29, 2004. This addendum supplies lower-court record facts and source limits only. It makes no commitment, imports no later Supreme Court treatment, and supplies no hypothetical Vieth holding.

Source: Martinez v. Bush, 234 F. Supp. 2d 1275 (S.D. Fla. ## Three distinct partisan branches

The main text of Part III concentrates on congressional districting. **Footnote 58 expressly establishes that the second amended complaint also presented partisan challenges to the State House and State Senate plans.** The three branches must remain distinct:

## Congressional evidence and the lower court's separate legal conclusions

Part III.D.1 finds partisan intent without difficulty and notes the defendants' trial stipulation. Part III.D.2.a applies three prerequisites imported from Gingles: compactness, cohesion and opposing-bloc voting. It rejects reliance on party registration as proof of consistent voting and criticizes the statewide voting index because the same voters need not have produced each election's total and because the index excludes the congressional races at issue. Those empirical limitations may remain relevant; their conversion into universal constitutional prerequisites is the lower court's legal rule.

The court reports actual local evidence from Representative Deutsch concerning approximately 69,909 residents, nearly all Black Democrats, placed in CD 11 and removed from CD 10, increasing the Republican margin in CD 10 while concentrating Democrats in CD 11. The court viewed this as evidence concerning one small compact area, insufficient to remedy the broader alleged statewide wrong. It did not find that this local evidence established a constitutional violation. The distinction matters because inability to prove a statewide case is not itself a finding resolving a legally sufficient personal district claim under a different rule.

Footnote 94 reports an alternative congressional plan proposed by Senator Campbell, S33C0009. It yielded 12 districts with Democratic vote-index majorities and 13 with Republican vote-index majorities. The court discounted it for lack of the cohesion and opposing-bloc proof its rule demanded. The opinion does not establish that the plan is a lawful, feasible neutral comparator under a different constitutional test, nor that it has no evidentiary value apart from proportional output. The facts cannot be restated as an absence of any alternative plan.

Part III.D.2.b also rejects the congressional claim under a purported independent totality inquiry. It identifies no evidence of inaccessible candidate selection, relevant past exclusion, or unresponsive Republican representatives. It then relies on the large population of unregistered potential voters, voters' capacity to change affiliations, and Florida's recent shift in partisan control. These observations and the cited population and election data are separable from the legal proposition that potential voter recruitment necessarily defeats injury. The court's designation of its analysis as an alternative does not establish that it independently suffices under the actual governing Vieth rule. Any later operator must test the particular retained facts against that rule and may not simply preserve or discard the entire branch by its heading.

Footnote 99 separately reports and rejects three congressional theories: a more demanding constitutional standard for a State's exercise of delegated congressional-election power; automatic Article I, §2 injury from allegedly predetermined party victories; and partisan predominance as sufficient injury by itself. The court regards the first as unsuccessful even under a less demanding effects standard; it regards predetermined outcomes as unproved; and it rejects the third by distinguishing race from party. Current simulated law, including its distinct Shaw/Miller rule, controls the legal analysis. No standalone First Amendment claim is identified in this opinion.

## Racial claims, districts and relief actually sought

The racial §2 litigation concerns electoral opportunity, not a demand for a specified overall count of minority districts. In Part II.B the court characterizes the parties as accepting, for this litigation, the sufficiency of the number of ostensibly performing districts; their disagreement concerns whether the identified districts would actually perform and whether alleged packing deprived voters of opportunity. This is a court-reported litigation scope, not a universal maximization or proportionality rule.

The court found that all three would probably perform throughout the decade, supported by history, registration, demographic evidence and election analyses. CD 23's performance received detailed competing analysis. |

Part II's findings distinguish the preferred candidate of Black voters from the candidate's own race. A Black officeholder is not automatically the voters' preferred candidate, and incumbency does not conclusively establish equal opportunity. Footnote 37 expressly says expected performance for a particular incumbent does not immunize the district; the court separately finds open-seat performance.

Racial polarization is expressly found in the pertinent south and northeast Florida areas. The court treats the Gingles predicates as undisputed in the relevant capacity context; it does not find the absence of polarization merely because the created districts would perform. The final inquiry addresses actual opportunity under the challenged arrangements. Current Valdespino law permits rigorously proved dependable crossover capacity; the source opinion's discussion of whether numerical majority was then an open legal question cannot replace that controlling rule.

## Material performance qualifications

The 2002 trial findings address Florida's then-existing conditional open-primary system: a party primary opens to all electors when the same-party candidates' winner will have no general-election opposition; a properly qualified write-in candidate counts as opposition under the then-applied administrative construction. These mechanics are recited in findings 156 and 160 and notes 28–32, not inferred from the shorthand open primary.

For CD 23, plaintiffs' most favorable open-primary projection was 48.0 percent; the court explains why its turnout and cohesion assumptions understate expected performance. It also predicts that a write-in candidate would probably qualify if open-primary conditions threatened performance, supported by prior election frequency and the post-amendment increase in qualified write-in candidates. That is an express predictive trial finding, not a historical fact that a particular candidate had filed or a statutory guarantee of a closed primary.

For HD 108, the court accepts 47.2 percent as a reasonable projection in the unlikely open-primary setting, with candidate and issue variations capable of changing the outcome. Its overall favorable finding relies in part on closed-primary likelihood and expected coalescence around a preferred candidate. Finding 215 additionally compares combined HD 108/118 performance. Current De Grandy law does not allow opportunity elsewhere to erase a proved local barrier, so that aggregate observation cannot independently answer a demonstrated HD 108 injury. The local findings must do their own work.

The lower opinion also describes the then-operative temporary elimination of majority-vote runoffs, its stated January 1, 2004 sunset, and the possibility of a later legislative change. This source establishes that description as of the 2002 decision; it does not verify enactments or operation in April 2004. A holding that depends on asserting whether the sunset actually occurred would require the applicable enacted source. Review of the supported 2002 trial findings need not silently assert a later statutory event. No actual subsequent election or newly changed electorate is presumed.

## Process and Title VI

Count I challenges hearing and adoption procedures for the congressional and legislative plans through equal protection, §1983 and Title VI. The lower court applies ordinary preponderance and motivating-factor purpose, expressly refusing a sole-purpose requirement. It finds no racial purpose in software choice, allocation methodology, hearing places and times, notice or treatment of public input. The record includes serious procedural criticisms: short notices, inconvenient hours, limited hearing geography, restricted substantive debate and exclusion of Democrats from actual deliberation. It also includes numerous hearings with increased attendance, accessible low-cost software, available recordings and submissions, and unchanged ordinary means of contacting legislators. The court accepts some criticisms without finding racial purpose. These are findings and competing evidence, not a conclusion that procedural exclusion can never be actionable.

The opinion identifies the pleaded Title VI claim as intentional discrimination under §601. It identifies no separate §602 effects regulation or preserved direct prospective regulatory claim. Current Powell, Sandoval and South Camden therefore require keeping the established but unpresented bounded regulatory route distinct from this claim. The court's broad description of Title VI as no broader than equal protection cannot be adopted as a statement negating that separate current-law route.

Footnote 7 additionally reports that Count I originally included a §2 process theory dismissed earlier on the pleadings. The present published merits discussion does not supply that dismissal's detailed reasoning or identify a separately presented appellate challenge to it. The addendum records the prior procedural treatment; it does not create an omitted claim, revive it, or announce a new rule disposing of it.

## Other procedure and amendment limits

The opinion recounts separate preclearance proceedings, the Department's objection to HD 101 and adoption of an interim State House plan for the 2002 elections. These are reported procedural facts. Administrative preclearance does not establish validity against the independent claims under review. The reviewed opinion's treatment of the separate federal preclearance litigation is not a newly adopted preclusion holding.

The state court's facial legislative-plan review expressly left fact-intensive federal claims for a court of competent jurisdiction. It supplies no merits bar to the present claims. The opinion reports the second amended complaint and subsequent proposed findings. Its references to motions to alter or amend concern opportunities to contest judicial notice; they are not permission to add new partisan or First Amendment claims. No ordinary amendment application or ruling material to a new association claim is established here. Any future remand can apply ordinary procedure without declaring an unverified amendment granted or a new claim preserved.

## Separate lower writings

Hinkle joins the judgment and opinion, agreeing that meaningful electoral opportunity remains judicially protected and that ordinary partisan advantage is not automatically prohibited. He emphasizes changing voters, candidates, issues and affiliations as reasons not to regard predicted outcomes as fixed. He acknowledges in his second note that the Gingles subsidiary requirements can apply differently to political groups because their identifying characteristic already concerns political behavior. His third note expressly says the record does not establish the exact seat distribution of a plan drawn without partisan considerations. These are his separate reasoning and evidentiary cautions, not additional majority findings of compliance with a future rule.

Jordan also joins the opinion and judgment. He asks for clearer higher-court guidance on the discriminatory-effects standard. His view does not alter a branch disposition or establish that any claimant has proved a constitutional injury. Neither concurrence supplies an unreported amendment, new evidence or an additional claim.
