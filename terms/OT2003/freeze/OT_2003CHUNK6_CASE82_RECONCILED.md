## Comparison

The historical four Associates supporting reversal were O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer supported affirmance through the partnership-membership cure. The frozen positions match those judgments. Their premises remain materially intact: the same partnership was the original plaintiff, its nondiverse members actually left before trial, and the objection followed the verdict but preceded judgment. Effective Caterpillar expressly requires lawful removal of the actual nondiverse party and actual jurisdiction before trial and through judgment; its efficiency reasoning alone is no jurisdictional source. Kontrick independently bars converting subject-matter jurisdiction into a forfeitable litigation rule.

The affirmance group must therefore describe its rule as an extension of the existing cure doctrine. History confirms its willingness to make that extension; it does not make the extension existing law. The opposing group retains Carden's distinction between attributing members' citizenship to a single entity and treating those members as parties.

## Qualifications and remedies

The reversal group requires dismissal without prejudice to a properly grounded action. It does not decide a future limitations defense or order a second trial. The affirmance group would preserve the litigation outcome against this jurisdictional ground; it does not decide independent contract or appellate questions. Its extension is confined to the genuine membership change before trial and actual complete diversity persisting through judgment. It supplies no general permission to change an individual's citizenship, manufacture jurisdiction, excuse a defect persisting through trial, or treat a late objection as waiver.

The strongest objection to the extension is that attribution does not make members actual plaintiffs; the four proponents accept that distinction but consider the removal of the citizenship interests sufficiently equivalent for this cure. The four opponents conclude that this analogy changes the established commencement rule and creates uncertain exceptions. These are competing grounds, not a common eight-Justice cure rationale.

The primary majority's note 9 records that the two individual partners were separately added on the defendant's counterclaim under Rule 13(h). That fact does not make their withdrawal from the partnership a court-ordered dismissal from the original action. The frozen packet's formulation is used only to deny a party-dismissal cure of the original plaintiff's defect; it must not be expanded into a historical finding that the individuals never appeared in the litigation in any capacity. No new cure premise or Rule 21 remand is based on that note.
