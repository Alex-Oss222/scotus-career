## Reconciliation of the displaced legal premises

The six affirming Associates' discharge-specific conclusion accords with the comparator. The bankruptcy court determines the debtor's discharge, including whether the statutory education-debt exception is satisfied. It does not impose an affirmative State payment obligation. The required complaint and summons do not transform that limited determination into personal recovery. This is a reasoned distinction based on the object and legal effect of the proceeding, not a categorical exemption for anything called bankruptcy or property jurisdiction.

The historical Article I prohibition is displaced by controlling Florida Prepaid, Garrett and Hibbs. Section 106(a) unmistakably covers sections 523 and 524, and ordinary adjudication of their application implements the Bankruptcy Clause's valid debt-adjustment function. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer therefore retain their independently sufficient Article I ground. O'Connor and Kennedy retain structural disagreement with that rule but can decide this case on the discharge-specific ground without purporting to overrule it. Their judgment agrees with history.

Scalia and Thomas retain their judgment disagreement and compulsory-process objection, but cannot describe historical Article I incapacity or the historical Federal Maritime Commission result as current law. Their prior Federal Maritime Commission dissent applied the existing authorization rule while preserving their structural objection; their later Hibbs dissent expressly renewed the request to reconsider Florida Prepaid, Kimel and Garrett. That later published position, together with Garrett's earlier express reconsideration dissent, supports the frozen proposal to reconsider the rule to the necessary extent here. It does not itself change controlling law. Their actual-private-process objection remains, and the discharge rationale is the narrower alternative they reject on its own terms.

## Rule, scope and remedy carried forward

Six Associates—Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer—support affirmance and the discharge-specific rationale. Four of those six—Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer—also support the independent Article I rationale. Scalia and Thomas support reversal of the denial as to this adversary action. The Chief Justice's position is neither inferred nor counted.

The affirming disposition permits adjudication of dischargeability; it awards no discharge. Section 523(a)(8) applies to an educational benefit overpayment or loan made, insured or guaranteed by a governmental unit, a loan made under a program funded in whole or part by a governmental unit or nonprofit institution, and an obligation to repay funds received as an educational benefit, scholarship or stipend. The debt remains excepted from discharge unless keeping it outside the discharge imposes undue hardship on the debtor and the debtor's dependents. State nonparticipation is not a finding of that hardship, and no new default rule is adopted.

TSAC's nonfiling does not establish section 106(b)'s claim-linked waiver. Neither ordinary lending activity nor an immunity objection is consent. Section 106(c)'s estate-claim offset is distinct. Section 106(a) separately prohibits punitive damages, makes costs and fees subject to section 2412(d)(2)(A)'s provisions and limitations, requires enforcement consistent with appropriate nonbankruptcy law, and creates no otherwise absent substantive cause of action. None of those provisions awards money here.

The discharge ground adjudicates only the scope of Hood's debt obligation. Personal recovery, a voidable-transfer action seeking property held by the State, later enforcement sanctions and a separate injunction requiring affirmative State compliance demand their own legal basis; none is decided. Nordic Village's original-section-106(c) rule for affirmative federal monetary recovery remains intact. Local Loan supplies the discharge-status principle, and Irving Trust supplies bankruptcy-administration context without establishing waiver or deciding this precise nonparticipating-State adversary action. Current Federal Maritime Commission retains its actual administrative-forum holding and later-enforcement limits.

The dissenting remedy dismisses this adversary action against TSAC, not Hood's underlying bankruptcy case. It does not decide a hypothetical alternative motion procedure. All parties' actual undue-hardship merits remain undecided under either position.
