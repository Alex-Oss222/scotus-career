## Scope and complete statutory qualifications

Review concerns two constitutional questions: Commerce/Necessary and Proper authority and prohibited commandeering. The distinct state-period, wrongful-death, survival and discovery-application issues are not new questions before this Court. The lower court's disputed-discovery remand remains part of the judgment; neither the landfill allegations nor constitutional validity establishes actual toxic causation, timeliness or entitlement to damages.

Section 9658 covers an action under state law for personal injury or property damage caused or contributed to by exposure to a hazardous substance, pollutant or contaminant released into the environment from a facility. It substitutes the federally required commencement date only when the otherwise applicable state statutory or common-law commencement date is earlier. State limitations law otherwise governs. It does not apply to an action under §9607 and creates no independent tort cause of action.

The ordinary federal date is when the plaintiff knew or reasonably should have known that the covered injury or damage was caused or contributed to by the substance, pollutant or contaminant concerned. For a minor or incompetent plaintiff, §9658(b)(4)(B) supplies the later of that discovery date or the applicable special date: for a minor, reaching the state-law age of majority or appointment of a legal representative; for an incompetent individual, becoming competent or having had a legal representative appointed. Those are separate statutory alternatives, not a new requirement of both majority/competence and appointment. No finding that any particular plaintiff qualifies for those alternatives is made. ## Competing constitutional paths

Path A sustains the provision as a reasonably adapted component of the commercial hazardous-waste regime. Congress validly regulates commercial production, handling, disposal and response to hazardous waste. The covered class is the specified exposure-based state damages action, and the operative relationship is preserving meaningful consequences of that waste activity when latent injury would otherwise be barred before reasonable discovery. Preserving those liability consequences supports the cost allocation and incentives of the underlying waste regime. It is not enough that §309 appears in CERCLA; the limited covered subject and the direct latency problem supply the functional relationship. Home Builders supplies identification of the regime, enacted class and actual fit, with independent judicial determination of those boundaries and respect for reasonable legislative implementation. Guillen confirms that a confined litigation rule can implement an identified interstate program. Jinks confirms that a properly sourced federal limitations rule may govern state claims, but its Article III/federal-adjudication source is not the source here.

Path B invalidates the displacement as regulation of state tort procedure disconnected from any federal cause of action or adjudication. Its strongest point is that §9658 can govern local injuries and affects claims Congress did not create; merely describing litigation as useful to pollution control would furnish a limitless rationale. A answers with the actual exposure, release, facility and earlier-commencement conditions and the latency mechanism: the federal rule preserves consequences of the very hazardous-waste activity the interstate regime regulates. It creates no general power over unrelated deadlines. A does not rely on unsupported cross-border pollution findings, speculative national health costs, a finding that each tort itself is commerce, or a least-restrictive-means rule.

On commandeering, Path C rejects the challenge because a valid federal rule of decision binds state judges under Article VI. It operates in existing adjudication of private claims; it orders no legislature to enact a cause of action and no executive office to establish or administer a program. Path D would characterize mandatory federal commencement as forbidden management of state judicial machinery. Jinks defeats that categorical characterization; New York and Printz distinguish state-court adjudication from compulsory legislative or executive administration. Guillen expressly reserved its separate Tenth Amendment question, so it supplies no holding resolving C. The actual judicial duty is sustained here by Article VI, the valid federal power established under A and Jinks's distinct adjudicative rule. Nebraska's direct public-operator compliance holding is consistent but is not the source of the state-judge obligation.

## Component reconciliation and operative limits

All eight Associate Justices support the fitted Commerce/Necessary and Proper ground, reject the commandeering challenge on the state-judge distinction, and affirm the lower court in the reviewed constitutional respect. Their shared ground does not import a private federal damages cause, a universal procedural power, a new interstate element or findings of waste migration. Scalia and Thomas distinguish their Home Builders dissent by the direct preservation of consequences for the commercial hazardous-waste activity and Guillen's intervening confined-means holding, not by abandoning the requirement of actual fit. No separate writing is required merely to restate those limits; any writing must preserve the actual shared ground.

The lower court's remand continues because material discovery facts remain disputed. Neither validity nor the federal definition settles those facts, changes the underlying state limitations period beyond the statutory displacement, or decides the ungranted statutory applications. No additional source gap blocks these constitutional commitments.

§9658. Actions under State law for damages from exposure to hazardous substances
(a) State statutes of limitations for hazardous substance cases
(1) Exception to State statutes
In the case of any action brought under State law for personal injury, or property damages, which are caused or contributed to by exposure to any hazardous substance, or pollutant or contaminant, released into the environment from a facility, if the applicable limitations period for such action (as specified in the State statute of limitations or under common law) provides a commencement date which is earlier than the federally required commencement date, such period shall commence at the federally required commencement date in lieu of the date specified in such State statute.
(2) State law generally applicable
Except as provided in paragraph (1), the statute of limitations established under State law shall apply in all actions brought under State law for personal injury, or property damages, which are caused or contributed to by exposure to any hazardous substance, or pollutant or contaminant, released into the environment from a facility.
(3) Actions under section 9607
Nothing in this section shall apply with respect to any cause of action brought under section 9607 of this title.
(b) Definitions
As used in this section—
(1) Subchapter I terms
The terms used in this section shall have the same meaning as when used in subchapter I of this chapter.
(2) Applicable limitations period
The term “applicable limitations period” means the period specified in a statute of limitations during which a civil action referred to in subsection (a)(1) of this section may be brought.
(3) Commencement date
The term “commencement date” means the date specified in a statute of limitations as the beginning of the applicable limitations period.
(4) Federally required commencement date
(A) In general
Except as provided in subparagraph (B), the term “federally required commencement date” means the date the plaintiff knew (or reasonably should have known) that the personal injury or property damages referred to in subsection (a)(1) of this section were caused or contributed to by the hazardous substance or pollutant or contaminant concerned.
(B) Special rules
In the case of a minor or incompetent plaintiff, the term “federally required commencement date” means the later of the date referred to in subparagraph (A) or the following:
(i) In the case of a minor, the date on which the minor reaches the age of majority, as determined by State law, or has a legal representative appointed.
(ii) In the case of an incompetent individual, the date on which such individual becomes competent or has had a legal representative appointed.
(Pub. L. 96–510, title III, §309, as added Pub. L. 99–499, title II, §203(a), Oct. 17, 1986, 100 Stat. 1695.)
Effective Date
Section 203(b) of Pub. L. 99–499 provided that: “The amendment made by subsection (a) of this section [enacting this section] shall take effect with respect to actions brought after December 11, 1980.”

This section is referred to in section 9659 of this title.
