# Jones v. R. R. Donnelley & Sons Co.: independent commitments

Event: May 3, 2004. Research cutoff: May 3, 2004. These are independent commitments of the eight participating Associate Justices, based on the frozen case 72 neutral packet and entering-law slice and the effective neutral public projection. No current controlled Chief Justice position, comparator, reconciliation or raw Decision Record was consulted. No authorship or final Court coalition is selected.

## Question, disposition and sufficient rule

Each Associate would reverse the Seventh Circuit's categorical rejection of the federal period and remand. Except where law otherwise provides, section 1658's four-year period governs a claim made possible by a congressional enactment after December 1, 1990, including an amendment of an older statute. The date of the code section's original enactment does not exclude a right newly created by a later Act. The postformation hostile-environment, termination and transfer claims described in the packet fall within the amendment-enabled category to the extent they depend on section 1981(b)'s expanded contractual coverage. The lower courts must apply that period to the identified claims and determine accrual, timeliness and the remaining merits under the governing law.

Rivers establishes that section 101 created new substantive coverage prospectively; it does not itself decide section 1658. Patterson's earlier-text boundary identifies whether the asserted claim depended on the expansion. Goodman supplies borrowing where no applicable federal period displaces it. A claim already available under the earlier text does not become a new-enactment claim merely because the complaint cites amended section 1981. Different legally independent claims in one action can therefore require different limitation inquiries.

## Enhanced comparison

The live alternatives are the claim-enabling-enactment reading and the original-statute reading adopted below. The latter's strongest argument is continuity: the suit still enforces section 1981, and replacing established borrowing whenever Congress alters a statute risks uncertain boundaries. The former's strongest objection is that an amendment does not automatically create a new cause of action; the claim-specific dependency requirement answers that objection. An amendment is itself enacted by Congress, and treating its new liability as an Act for temporal purposes but not as an Act under section 1658 would make limitation coverage turn on codification technique. The claim-enabling reading gives effect both to the new enactment and to the continued availability of old claims. No Justice's commitment rests on treating every amendment as sufficient or on a general exception to statutory temporal limits.

## Remedy, limits and readiness

All eight commitments support the same reversal and remand, the four-year rule at the stated level of generality and its claim-enabling application. A conclusory direction to reconsider the statute without answering whether amendments qualify would not complete the question. The described claims receive the new-rule classification; remaining accrual, timeliness and liability work belongs on remand because the packet supplies no necessary findings for that work.

No record gap prevents these bounded commitments. The packet does not establish a separate argument date; no date is invented. May 3 matters use the common pre-date baseline. The statutory texts were read in the mechanically supplied slice; no long source passage is reauthored here. These commitments are ready for separate historical reconciliation.
