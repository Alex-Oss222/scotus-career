## Reconciled commitments

All eight Associates — Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer — reverse the four-image disclosure mandate and remand for judgment permitting withholding of the protected death-image material under Exemption 7(C), subject to the mandatory segregation qualification below. They support the same three propositions at the stated scope.

First, the exemption reaches the surviving relatives' own substantial privacy interest against intrusive public dissemination of a close relative's death images. Their declarations establish personal distress and unwanted publicity; this is not inheritance of the deceased's rights. The undisputed law-enforcement compilation predicate is satisfied. The statute permits withholding when disclosure could reasonably be expected to constitute an unwarranted invasion of personal privacy. It creates neither a family veto nor an independent prohibition on disclosure. Exemption 6's different language does not replace 7(C)'s standard.

Second, where demonstrated third-party privacy is opposed by a claim that officials negligently investigated or misrepresented a death, the requester must identify a significant governmental-transparency interest and an objective foundation connecting the particular images to that asserted deficiency. Evidence warranting a reasonable belief that meaningful impropriety or an investigative deficiency might have occurred suffices; conclusive proof, clear evidence of established illegality, and proof that the death was a homicide are not required. Bare accusation or a general desire to inspect everything again is insufficient. The agency retains the ultimate withholding burden. Other transparency purposes receive their own analysis rather than a universal misconduct prerequisite.

Third, the actual competing submissions do not establish sufficient incremental value in these four images to overcome the concrete family invasion. Favish supplies documents and specific hypotheses, not merely an unsupported slogan. His gun-hand argument concerns thumb entrapment, alleged backspatter, location beneath the leg and the better quality of an original than a leaked copy. His shoulder-image argument concerns blood transfer, possible later flow and asserted investigative omission. The disclosed report discusses the thumb indentation and trigger position, laboratory residue, high-velocity spatter, transfer to the face and possible head movement in checking vital signs. Those explanations are evidence about what the investigation did, not an irrebuttable answer that every inference is correct. The report itself exposes limitations, including possible contamination of trace evidence by common packaging of clothes. A court can assess the limited added transparency of these images without adjudicating cause of death or resolving every forensic controversy.

The alleged changes to the Haut report, gun-identification statements, autopsy attendance, x-ray records and vehicle observations present criticism of the reporting and investigation. The question here remains how the four images would illuminate those asserted failures. Death-scene photographs do not themselves resolve who attended the later autopsy, which version of a written report was altered, or which vehicle witnesses saw. The gun and blood hypotheses have the closest image-specific connection and must be addressed expressly; the possibility of another independent visual assessment does not on this record outweigh the supported privacy injury. The other two images have a still more general asserted contribution. This ruling does not declare each alleged inconsistency disproved or all investigative conduct proper.

## Mandatory segregability and scope of the mandate

Any reasonably segregable nonexempt material must be disclosed after exempt material is removed. The agency bears the ultimate statutory burden; the requester need not prove that a separate portion would be useful, would resolve his theory, or would independently justify its disclosure. Photographic form creates no exception. The existing frozen reference to a useful separate portion cannot be used as a new legal condition or as a categorical finding that each image contains no reasonably segregable nonexempt material.

The remand permits withholding the protected depiction. Before complete withholding can be implemented, the ordinary statutory duty requires the actual determination that no reasonably segregable nonexempt material remains, or release of such material if it does. This handoff supplies no unsupported image-by-image segregation finding and authorizes no automatic withholding of a nonexempt background or other severable portion. Conversely, a proposed crop that continues to depict protected remains is not automatically nonexempt merely because the face is omitted; the actual privacy and transparency analysis still applies to the retained content. Favish's particular example of a neck-only crop concerns an additional, more graphic photograph outside this four-image review and does not enlarge the present judgment.

Unauthorized publication of a degraded gun-hand photograph does not by itself waive the relatives' remaining interest in official dissemination of the original. The holding concerns these four images, not every photograph associated with the investigation, a discovery entitlement, or the proposed deposition. The materials support no additional judicial production order or factual finding about hidden evidence.

## Historical comparator and changed premises

The historical Court unanimously recognized family privacy, required more than bare suspicion for this misconduct-based public-interest claim, and ordered summary judgment for the custodian concerning the four photographs. That result and its reasonable-belief threshold remain strong evidence for every Associate. The history itself distinguished the Government's proposed clear-evidence rule from the lesser FOIA threshold and did not require proof of completed wrongdoing. Those limits must not be lost when describing its rule.

The actual entering law changes the route by which the present threshold and application can be justified. Historical Favish invoked historical Ray as a presumption-of-legitimacy decision. The actual simulated Ray adopts concrete privacy and incremental-transparency balancing, expressly leaves derivative inquiry possible, and rejects a general prior-wrongdoing requirement. Actual FLRA distinguishes permission to withhold from the separate Privacy Act prohibition. Actual Center for National Security Studies requires agency persuasion, exemption-specific evaluation and real segregation. Thus a presumption of official correctness cannot displace examination of the furnished allegations, nor may historical language suggesting mandatory nondisclosure become the operative statutory rule.

The supplied evidence does not change into nonexistent evidence because historical Favish described its legal insufficiency tersely. The historical reasonable-belief threshold is retained as a bounded public-interest production requirement; the current explanation evaluates the actual claims and the relation of these images to them, while the agency retains persuasion. This is a substantive limitation on the historical presumption-based explanation and blanket formulation, not a change inferred from who wrote the opinion. No judgment departure on the protected images is necessary.
