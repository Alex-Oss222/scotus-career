## Reconciled result and proposition coalitions

All eight Associates support vacating the categorical judgment and remanding. Seven support the following governing construction: Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. A legally enforceable requirement that purchasers acquire new motor vehicles or engines meeting an emissions criterion can adopt or enforce an emission standard within section 209(a); addressing buyers rather than manufacturers or sellers does not remove the command from the clause, and commercial availability alone does not exclude an otherwise covered regulatory standard. The statutory new-vehicle and subject-to-this-part requirements, the actual regulatory character of the act and legally available distinct applications still must be established.

Souter supports the judgment on a narrower ground set out below. His vote does not join the seven's treatment of a compulsory selection confined to actually available certified products. No logical-subset inference or judgment arithmetic converts his ground into a full join. The seven direct supporters supply their own proposition coalition, without counting him.

## Souter: changed-premise test and narrow conclusion

The historical position remains strong evidence. The enacted Fleet Rules are not historically different in this timeline, and a more detailed reading of the same record is not by itself a reason to move Souter. His actual dissent supplies a specific conditional principle: ordinary selection among available certified products does not impose the production mandate at which he understands section 209(a) to be directed; a purchase restriction operating as a requirement to produce a vehicle with prescribed characteristics can be preempted. He did not subscribe to the lower court's broadest possible buyer/seller distinction.

The material correction concerns the premise on which he applied that principle to the whole judgment. The frozen fresh record no longer describes all prospective applications as subject to an unconditional commercial-availability escape. Rule 1186.1(d)(1)(B) permits a transaction-specific technical-infeasibility route only before July 1, 2004, with continuing control-device and fuel requirements. Its government service-contract/no-alternative-bids route in (d)(2) is distinct and cannot simply replace the purchase exception. Rule 1191's availability paragraph is limited to medium-duty vehicles and also requires inability to use its offset mechanism. Other Rules retain their own certification, vehicle-category and availability/use conditions. Their exact construction has not been adjudicated merely by respondents' general description.

April 28 precedes the sweeper cutoff. That fact is not bypassed: the Court finds no expired exception, no post-cutoff transaction, no actual unavailable product, and no future violation. The temporal term matters because the challenged enactment is prospective and the actual judgment broadly validates all six Rules without limiting itself to purchases before July 2004. The docket establishes declaratory and injunctive claims and that broad judgment; the absent complaint prayer prevents quoting its exact wording but does not turn the judgment into a past-transaction-only disposition. No supported amendment or authoritative construction extends the purchase route in the supplied record.

The strongest case for retaining historical affirmance is that the exception is still operative on the decision date, no inability to buy a compliant vehicle has been proved, and a later problematic application could be challenged when it occurs. Those points defeat a present finding that the Rule necessarily forces an unavailable design and defeat an injunction based on predicted future facts. They do not establish the broader legal premise that every prospective application validated by this judgment remains confined to available products. Souter's own distinction makes that premise consequential. His current Medtronic joins require the actual statutory comparison, and his Miccosukee join permits a confined remand where a categorical legal premise leaves a material application issue unresolved. Neither case supplies a universal remand entitlement or changes his reading of section 209.

The supported response is therefore a limited vacatur allowing the lower courts to identify the challenged applications and construe the operative qualifications under the competing statutory rules. Souter would preserve any adjudicated, severable application demonstrably confined to selection among actually available certified products. He does not adopt petitioners' disputed argument that possible-use language necessarily permits demands for nonexistent products. He would permit preemption under his own construction only when the actual command operates as the kind of production requirement he accepts section 209(a) to prohibit. This is a separate ground for the same remand, not assent to the seven's broader rule and not an automatic consequence of finding more detailed sources.

**Historical departure:** Souter changes from historical affirmance to a narrow concurrence in vacatur because the corrected operative record does not support his historical premise of an availability escape throughout the prospective Rules validated below: the sweeper purchase route expressly ends before July 1, 2004, while the separate contracting route and other conditional exceptions do not establish a universal replacement. His own production-focused interpretation makes that limitation material to the unrestricted judgment, although it supplies no finding of future unavailability and no join in the seven's broader standard-enforcement rule.

## Statutory context and remand boundaries

Section 246 remains structural evidence that Congress addresses fleet-purchase programs through detailed statutory conditions. These respondents did not invoke adoption under section 246 as an authorization for these Rules, and the fresh packet establishes that the Rules did not satisfy all of that program's conditions. No compliance inquiry or search for section 246 approval is directed. Their structural argument and preservation objection to a distinct section-246 preemption theory are not a concession that every statutory inference petitioners draw is correct.

The intervenors also disavowed sections 209(b) and 177 as saving grounds for these Rules. A generic reminder that an actually applicable waiver must be respected is not an instruction to invent a waiver defense on remand. CARB certification of particular vehicles, approval of California standards and approval of these Fleet Rules are distinct propositions. Section 209(d)'s use argument was not litigated below; ordinary rules govern any procedurally available application issue without a new preserved merits ruling by assumption.

The remand must distinguish compulsory regulation from any genuine proprietary action, the statutory definition of a new vehicle from ordinary references to new purchases or fleet additions, and severable provisions and applications. Rule 1191 expressly addresses some used-vehicle purchases; no blanket used-vehicle exclusion is inferred. The Court does not issue an all-rules injunction from the threshold construction. Public/private coverage is not uniform: the refreshed source shows private applications in Rules 1186.1, 1192, 1193 and 1194, while 1191 and 1196 contain relevant private-contractor exemptions.

The exact six-rule conditions incorporated in the fresh preflight remain incorporated here. In particular, no unavailability claim dispenses automatically with certification, technical showings, credits, controls or fuel duties; Rule 1196's June 2004 sunset names its fueling-station ground rather than its separate availability/use ground; its C/D/E connective and Rule 1186.1's service-contract cross-reference remain genuinely unestablished constructions. No public formulation may replace those terms with a uniform unconditional exemption when that proposition does substantive work.
