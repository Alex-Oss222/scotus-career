## Two complete holdings supported by five Associates

**Statutory authority.** Sections 113(a)(5) and 167 authorize EPA to prevent construction that fails the Clean Air Act's PSD requirements. Section 165(a)(4)'s BACT condition is a substantive requirement defined by section 169(3): the State makes the case-specific achievable-reduction determination after considering energy, environmental and economic impacts and other costs. EPA may intervene when that determination lacks a reasoned basis under those statutory guides. Its authority does not permit replacement of a supported state choice with EPA's preferred technology or weighing merely because EPA would choose differently.

This reading gives effect to both assignments. The State remains the primary permitting decisionmaker within a reasoned statutory range; the separately enacted federal construction-enforcement provisions remain capable of enforcing substantive compliance. Issuing a permit containing the BACT label does not conclusively establish that compliance. The objection that this makes EPA a general appellate permitting board is answered by the reasoned-range limit and EPA's obligation to identify and demonstrate the actual deficiency. Ordinary state review does not displace the independent enforcement channels Congress supplied.

**Exercise here.** EPA lawfully acted on the identified inconsistency and missing evidentiary link in Alaska's actual explanation. Alaska originally found SCR technically and economically feasible, abandoned the offset rationale supporting its different choice, and then relied on costs and competitiveness while acknowledging that it lacked the information necessary to assess those economic consequences. The rural-utility analogy and the mine's regional importance do not themselves establish the claimed effect on this mine. The deficiency is that unexplained reasoning, not a universal prohibition on economic considerations or an automatic requirement for SCR whenever a financial statement is absent.

The State chose to use top-down analysis; neither the Act nor the present holding universally mandates that method. The reviewing court applies section 706(2)(A)'s arbitrary-and-capricious standard to EPA's actual explanation, considering its orders with the explanatory correspondence where the path is reasonably discernible. It does not supply missing administrative reasons or itself select BACT. EPA must establish why the State's determination lies outside the reasoned range; its enforcement choice cannot make the State prove the substantive reasonableness of EPA's preferred technology. No new numerical proof quantum is adopted here.

## Three Associates' contrary statutory position

Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas read the specific permitting-authority designation as assigning the actual BACT weighing to the State. EPA may enforce a required permit, required consideration and independently specified federal conditions; the general enforcement provisions do not themselves create a federal merits-review channel over the completed substantive state balance. A possibly weak state explanation is not proof that Congress made EPA its reviewing tribunal. Ordinary administrative and judicial review remains the route for contesting that discretionary determination on this reading.

Their position rests on statutory allocation, not a constitutional immunity from the Clean Air Act. They do not need to hold Low NOx economically superior or conclude that EPA lacks every federal enforcement power. Their allocation ground requires reversal; the alternative question whether an authorized supervisory EPA reasonably evaluated the explanation is unnecessary under that ground. They preserve a substantive dissent on authority and corresponding remedy, without implying an additional judgment on BACT merits.
