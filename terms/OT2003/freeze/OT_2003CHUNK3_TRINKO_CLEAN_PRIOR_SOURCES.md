### Opinion of the Court, Part II

II

9

This case requires us to decide, at the outset, a question we have not previously addressed: whether a private plaintiff seeking an injunction under § 16 of the Clayton Act must show a threat of antitrust injury. To decide the question, we must look first to the source of the antitrust injury requirement, which lies in a related provision of the Clayton Act, § 4, 15 U.S.C. § 15.

10

Like § 16, § 4 provides a vehicle for private enforcement of the antitrust laws. Under § 4, "any person who shall be injured in his business or property by reason of anything forbidden in the antitrust laws may sue therefor in any district court of the United States . . ., and shall recover threefold the damages by him sustained, and the cost of suit, including a reasonable attorney's fee." 15 U.S.C. § 15. In Brunswick Corp. v. Pueblo Bowl-O-Mat, Inc., supra, we held that plaintiffs seeking treble damages under § 4 must show more than simply an "injury causally linked" to a particular merger; instead, "plaintiffs must prove antitrust injury, which is to say injury of the type the antitrust laws were intended to prevent and that flows from that which makes the defendants' acts unlawful." Id., 429 U.S., at 489, 97 S.Ct., at 697 (emphasis in original). The plaintiffs in Brunswick did not prove such injury. The plaintiffs were 3 of the 10 bowling centers owned by a relatively small bowling chain. The defendant, one of the two largest bowling chains in the country, acquired several bowling centers located in the plaintiffs' market that would have gone out of business but for the acquisition. The plaintiffs sought treble damages under § 4, alleging as injury "the loss of income that would have accrued had the acquired centers gone bankrupt" and had competition in their markets consequently been reduced. Id., at 487, 97 S.Ct., at 696. We held that this injury, although causally related to a merger alleged to violate § 7, was not an antitrust injury, since "[i]t is inimical to [the antitrust] laws to award damages" for losses stemming from continued competition. Id., at 488, 97 S.Ct., at 697. This reasoning in Brunswick was consistent with the principle that "the antitrust laws . . . were enacted for 'the protection of competition, not competitors.' " Ibid., quoting Brown Shoe Co. v. United States, 370 U.S. 294, 320, 82 S.Ct. 1502, 1521, 8 L.Ed.2d 510 (1962) (emphasis in original).

11

Subsequent decisions confirmed the importance of showing antitrust injury under § 4. In Blue Shield of Virginia v. McCready, 457 U.S. 465, 102 S.Ct. 2540, 73 L.Ed.2d 149 (1982), we found that a health-plan subscriber suffered antitrust injury as a result of the plan's "purposefully anticompetitive scheme " to reduce competition for psychotherapeutic services by reimbursing subscribers for services provided by psychiatrists but not for services provided by psychologists. Id., at 483, 102 S.Ct., at 2550. We noted that antitrust injury, "as analyzed in Brunswick, is one factor to be considered in determining the redressability of a particular form of injury under § 4," id., at 483, n. 19, 102 S.Ct., at 2550, n. 19, and found it "plain that McCready's injury was of a type that Congress sought to redress in providing a private remedy for violations of the antitrust laws." Id., at 483, 102 S.Ct., at 2550. Similarly, in Associated General Contractors of California, Inc. v. Carpenters, 459 U.S. 519, 103 S.Ct. 897, 74 L.Ed.2d 723 (1983), we applied "the Brunswick test," and found that the petitioner had failed to allege antitrust injury. Id., at 539-540, 103 S.Ct., at 909.5

12

Section 16 of the Clayton Act provides in part that "[a]ny person, firm, corporation, or association shall be entitled to sue for and have injunctive relief . . . against threatened loss or damage by a violation of the antitrust laws. . . ." 15 U.S.C. § 26. It is plain that § 16 and § 4 do differ in various ways. For example, § 4 requires a plaintiff to show actual injury, but § 16 requires a showing only of "threatened" loss or damage; similarly, § 4 requires a showing of injury to "business or property," cf. Hawaii v. Standard Oil Co., 405 U.S. 251, 92 S.Ct. 885, 31 L.Ed.2d 184 (1972), while § 16 contains no such limitation.6 Although these differences do affect the nature of the injury cognizable under each section, the lower courts, including the courts below, have found that under both § 16 and § 4 the plaintiff must still allege an injury of the type the antitrust laws were designed to prevent.7 We agree.

13

The wording concerning the relationship of the injury to the violation of the antitrust laws in each section is comparable. Section 4 requires proof of injury "by reason of anything forbidden in the antitrust laws"; § 16 requires proof of "threatened loss or damage by a violation of the antitrust laws." It would be anomalous, we think, to read the Clayton Act to authorize a private plaintiff to secure an injunction against a threatened injury for which he would not be entitled to compensation if the injury actually occurred.

14

There is no indication that Congress intended such a result. Indeed, the legislative history of § 16 is consistent with the view that § 16 affords private plaintiffs injunctive relief only for those injuries cognizable under § 4. According to the House Report:

15

"Under section 7 of the act of July 2, 1890 [revised and incorporated into Clayton Act as § 4], a person injured in his business and property by corporations or combinations acting in violation of the Sherman antitrust law, may recover loss and damage for such wrongful act. There is, however, no provision in the existing law authorizing a person, firm, corporation, or association to enjoin threatened loss or damage to his business or property by the commission of such unlawful acts, and the purpose of this section is to remedy such defect in the law." H.R.Rep. No. 627, 63d Cong., 2d Sess., pt. 1, p. 21 (1914) (emphasis added).8

16

Sections 4 and 16 are thus best understood as providing complementary remedies for a single set of injuries. Accordingly, we conclude that in order to seek injunctive relief under § 16, a private plaintiff must allege threatened loss or damage "of the type the antitrust laws were designed to prevent and that flows from that which makes defendants' acts unlawful." Brunswick, 429 U.S., at 489, 97 S.Ct., at 697. We therefore turn to the question whether the proposed merger in this case threatened respondent with antitrust injury.

### Opinion of the Court, footnotes 5 and 6

5

 A showing of antitrust injury is necessary, but not always sufficient, to establish standing under § 4, because a party may have suffered antitrust injury but may not be a proper plaintiff under § 4 for other reasons. See generally Page, The Scope of Liability for Antitrust Violations, 37 Stan.L.Rev. 1445, 1483-1485 (1985) (distinguishing concepts of antitrust injury and antitrust standing). Thus, in Associated General Contractors we considered other factors in addition to antitrust injury to determine whether the petitioner was a proper plaintiff under § 4. 459 U.S., at 540, 103 S.Ct., at 909. As we explain, n. 6, infra, however, many of these other factors are not relevant to the standing inquiry under § 16.

6

 Standing analysis under § 16 will not always be identical to standing analysis under § 4. For example, the difference in the remedy each section provides means that certain considerations relevant to a determination of standing under § 4 are not relevant under § 16. The treble-damages remedy, if afforded to "every person tangentially affected by an antitrust violation," Blue Shield of Virginia v. McCready, 457 U.S. 465, 476-477, 102 S.Ct. 2540, 2546-2547, 73 L.Ed.2d 149 (1982), or for "all injuries that might conceivably be traced to an antitrust violation," Hawaii v. Standard Oil Co., 405 U.S., at 263, n. 14, 92 S.Ct., at 891, n. 14, would "open the door to duplicative recoveries," id., at 264, 92 S.Ct., at 892, and to multiple lawsuits. In order to protect against multiple lawsuits and duplicative recoveries, courts should examine other factors in addition to antitrust injury, such as the potential for duplicative recovery, the complexity of apportioning damages, and the existence of other parties that have been more directly harmed, to determine whether a party is a proper plaintiff under § 4. See Associated General Contractors, 459 U.S., at 544-545, 103 S.Ct., at 911-912; Illinois Brick Co. v. Illinois, 431 U.S. 720, 97 S.Ct. 2061, 52 L.Ed.2d 707 (1977). Conversely, under § 16, the only remedy available is equitable in nature, and, as we recognized in Hawaii v. Standard Oil Co., "the fact is that one injunction is as effective as 100, and, concomitantly, that 100 injunctions are no more effective than one." 405 U.S., at 261, 92 S.Ct., at 890. Thus, because standing under § 16 raises no threat of multiple lawsuits or duplicative recoveries, some of the factors other than antitrust injury that are appropriate to a determination of standing under § 4 are not relevant under § 16.

### Stevens dissent, complete main text

32

Justice STEVENS, with whom Justice WHITE joins, dissenting.

33

This case presents the question whether the antitrust laws provide a remedy for a private party that challenges a horizontal merger between two of its largest competitors. The issue may be approached along two fundamentally different paths. First, the Court might focus its attention entirely on the postmerger conduct of the merging firms and deny relief unless the plaintiff can prove a violation of the Sherman Act. Second, the Court might concentrate on the merger itself and grant relief if there is a significant probability that the merger will adversely affect competition in the market in which the plaintiff must compete. Today the Court takes a step down the former path;1 I believe that Congress has directed us to follow the latter path.

34

In this case, one of the major firms in the beef-packing market has proved to the satisfaction of the District Court, 591 F.Supp. 683, 709-710 (Colo.1983), and the Court of Appeals, 761 F.2d 570, 578-582 (CA10 1985), that the merger between Excel and Spencer Beef is illegal. This Court holds, however, that the merger should not be set aside because the adverse impact of the merger on respondent's profit margins does not constitute the kind of "antitrust injury" that the Court described in Brunswick Corp. v. Pueblo Bowl-O-Mat, Inc., 429 U.S. 477, 97 S.Ct. 690, 50 L.Ed.2d 701 (1977). As I shall demonstrate, Brunswick merely rejected a "novel damages theory," id., at 490, 97 S.Ct., at 698; the Court's implicit determination that Brunswick forecloses the appropriate line of inquiry in this quite different case is therefore misguided. In my view, a competitor in Monfort's position has standing to seek an injunction against the merger. Because Monfort must compete in the relevant market, proof establishing that the merger will have a sufficient probability of an adverse effect on competition to violate § 7 is also sufficient to authorize equitable relief.

35

* Section 7 of the Clayton Act was enacted in 1914, 38 Stat. 731, and expanded in 1950, 64 Stat. 1125, because Congress concluded that the Sherman Act's prohibition against mergers was not adequate.2 The Clayton Act, unlike the Sherman Act, proscribes certain combinations of competitors that do not produce any actual injury, either to competitors or to competition. An acquisition is prohibited by § 7 if "the effect of such acquisition may be substantially to lessen competition, or to tend to create a monopoly." 15 U.S.C. § 18. The legislative history teaches us that this delphic language was designed "to cope with monopolistic tendencies in their incipiency and well before they have attained such effects as would justify a Sherman Act proceeding." S.Rep. No. 1775, 81st Cong., 2d Sess., 4-5 (1950), U.S.Code Cong.Service 1950, p. 4293, 4296.3 In Brunswick, supra, this Court recognized that § 7 is "a prophylactic measure, intended 'primarily to arrest apprehended consequences of intercorporate relationships before those relationships could work their evil. . . .' " 429 U.S., at 485, 97 S.Ct., at 695 (quoting United States v. E.I. du Pont de Nemours & Co., 353 U.S. 586, 597, 77 S.Ct. 872, 879, 1 L.Ed.2d 1057 (1957)).

36

The 1950 amendment to § 7 was particularly concerned with the problem created by a merger which, when viewed by itself, would appear completely harmless, but when considered in its historical setting might be dangerous to competition. As Justice Stewart explained:

37

"The principal danger against which the 1950 amendment was addressed was the erosion of competition through the cumulative centripetal effect of acquisitions by large corporations, none of which by itself might be sufficient to constitute a violation of the Sherman Act. Congress' immediate fear was that of large corporations buying out small companies. A major aspect of that fear was the perceived trend toward absentee ownership of local business. Another, more generalized, congressional purpose revealed by the legislative history was to protect small businessmen and to stem the rising tide of concentration in the economy. These goals, Congress thought, could be achieved by 'arresting mergers at a time when the trend to a lessening of competition in a line of commerce was still in its incipiency.' Brown Shoe Co. v. United States, [370 U.S.,] at 317, 82 S.Ct., at 1519." United States v. Von's Grocery Co., 384 U.S. 270, 283-284, 86 S.Ct. 1478, 1485-1486, 16 L.Ed.2d 555 (1966) (dissenting).

38

Thus, a merger may violate § 7 of the Clayton Act merely because it poses a serious threat to competition and even though the evidence falls short of proving the kind of actual restraint that violates the Sherman Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1. The language of § 16 of the Clayton Act also reflects Congress' emphasis on probable harm rather than actual harm. Section 16 authorizes private parties to obtain injunctive relief "against threatened loss or damage" by a violation of § 7.4 The broad scope of the language in both § 7 and § 16 identifies the appropriate standing requirements for injunctive relief. As the Court has squarely held, it is the threat of harm, not actual injury, that justifies equitable relief:

39

"The evident premise for striking [the injunction at issue] was that Zenith's failure to prove the fact of injury barred injunctive relief as well as treble damages. This was unsound, for § 16 of the Clayton Act, 15 U.S.C. § 26, which was enacted by the Congress to make available equitable remedies previously denied private parties, invokes traditional principles of equity and authorizes injunctive relief upon the demonstration of 'threatened' injury. That remedy is characteristically available even though the plaintiff has not yet suffered actual injury; . . . he need only demonstrate a significant threat of injury from an impending violation of the antitrust laws or from a contemporary violation likely to continue or recur." Zenith Radio Corp. v. Hazeltine Research, Inc., 395 U.S. 100, 130, 89 S.Ct. 1562, 1580, 23 L.Ed.2d 129 (1969) (citations omitted).

40

Judged by these standards, respondent's showing that it faced the threat of loss from an impending antitrust violation clearly conferred standing to obtain injunctive relief. Respondent alleged, and in the opinion of the courts below proved, the injuries it would suffer from a violation of § 7:

41

"Competition in the markets for the procurement of fed cattle and the sale of boxed beef will be substantially lessened and a monopoly may tend to be created in violation of Section 7 of the Clayton Act;

42

"Concentration in those lines of commerce will be increased and the tendency towards concentration will be accelerated." 1 App. 21.

43

More generally, given the statutory purposes to protect small businesses and to stem the rising tide of concentration in particular markets, a competitor trying to stay in business in a changing market must have standing to ask a court to set aside a merger that has changed the character of the market in an illegal way. Certainly the businesses—small or large—that must face competition in a market altered by an illegal merger are directly affected by that transaction. Their inability to prove exactly how or why they may be harmed does not place them outside the circle of interested parties whom the statute was enacted to protect.

II

44

Virtually ignoring the language and history of § 7 of the Clayton Act and the broad scope of the Act's provision for injunctive relief, the Court bases its decision entirely on a case construing the "private damages action provisions" of the Act. Brunswick, 429 U.S., at 478, 97 S.Ct., at 692. In Brunswick, we began our analysis by acknowledging the difficulty of meshing § 7, "a statutory prohibition against acts that have a potential to cause certain harms," with § 4, a "damages action intended to remedy those harms." Id., at 486, 97 S.Ct., at 696. We concluded that a plaintiff must prove more than a violation of § 7 to recover damages, "since such proof establishes only that injury may result." Ibid. Beyond the special nature of an action for treble damages, § 16 differs from § 4 because by its terms it requires only that the antitrust violation threaten the plaintiff with loss or damage, not that the violation cause the plaintiff actual "injur[y] in his business or property." 15 U.S.C. § 15.

45

In the Brunswick case, the Court set aside a damages award that was based on the estimated additional profits that the plaintiff would have earned if competing bowling alleys had gone out of business instead of being acquired by the defendant. We concluded "that the loss of windfall profits that would have accrued had the acquired centers failed" was not the kind of actual injury for which damages could be recovered under § 4. 429 U.S., at 488, 97 S.Ct., at 697. That injury "did not occur 'by reason of' that which made the acquisitions unlawful." Ibid.

46

In contrast, in this case it is the threatened harm—to both competition and to the competitors in the relevant market—that makes the acquisition unlawful under § 7. The Court's construction of the language of § 4 in Brunswick is plainly not controlling in this case.5 The concept of "antitrust injury," which is at the heart of the treble-damages action, is simply not an element of a cause of action for injunctive relief that depends on finding a reasonable threat that an incipient disease will poison an entire market.

47

A competitor plaintiff who has proved a violation of § 7, as the Brunswick Court recognized, has established that injury may result. This showing satisfies the language of § 16 provided that the plaintiff can show that injury may result to him. When the proof discloses a reasonable probability that competition will be harmed as a result of a merger, I would also conclude that there is a reasonable probability that a competitor of the merging firms will suffer some corresponding harm in due course. In my opinion, that reasonable probability gives the competitor an interest in the proceeding adequate to confer standing to challenge the merger. To hold otherwise is to frustrate § 7 and to read § 16 far too restrictively.

48

It would be a strange antitrust statute indeed which defined a violation enforceable by no private party. Effective enforcement of the antitrust laws has always depended largely on the work of private attorney generals, for whom Congress made special provision in the Clayton Act itself.6 As recently as 1976, Congress specifically indicated its intent to encourage private enforcement of § 16 by authorizing recovery of a reasonable attorney's fee by a plaintiff in an action for injunctive relief. The Hart-Scott-Rodino Antitrust Improvements Act of 1976, 90 Stat. 1396 (amending 15 U.S.C. § 26).

49

The Court misunderstands the message that Congress conveyed in 1914 and emphasized in 1950. If, as the District Court and the Court of Appeals held, the merger is illegal, it should be set aside. I respectfully dissent.

### In re Independent Service Organizations Antitrust Litigation: CSU, L.L.C. v. Xerox Corporation

Merits decision, June 4, 2001

#### Chronology and Posture

Federal Circuit No. 99-1323; District of Kansas MDL-1021; judgment below, 203 F.3d 1322. The Federal Circuit affirmed summary judgment on February 17, 2000; rehearing was disposed of April 13, 2000. The intellectual-property counterclaims explain the Federal Circuit route. The Court reviews whether statutory patent or copyright exclusivity conclusively defeats the preserved monopolization claims concerning replacement parts, service manuals and diagnostic software. CSU seeks further adjudication; Xerox seeks affirmance on its statutory exclusion rights.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate.

#### Judgment

The Court vacates the affected antitrust judgment and remands for assessment under the governing monopolization requirements, giving the distinct patent and copyright interests substantive weight.

The judgment removes the conclusive legal barriers sustaining the affected summary judgments. It awards no damages, parts supply, software license or other compulsory access and determines no infringement issue. Summary judgment remains available on an adequately supported ground.

#### Opinion Topology

Justice Stevens delivered the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. The opinion controls both vacaturs, both holdings, the ordinary proof requirements, the substantive exclusion interests and the bounded remand.

Justice Scalia dissented, joined by Justices O'Connor and Thomas. They would affirm both components on the patent-refusal and copyright-justification grounds, while preserving liability for an independently unlawful antitrust wrong.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**Common authority:** Stevens's six-Justice opinion, with the full joins identified above, directly controls both holdings and the remand.

##### Patent exclusion and monopoly maintenance

**Holding and operative rule:** A valid patent and unilateral refusal to supply protected parts neither establish monopolization nor conclusively defeat a supported claim that control of those inputs maintains monopoly power in a distinct service market. Without an additional prerequisite of procurement fraud, sham litigation or illegal tying, the plaintiff must prove relevant-market power, unlawful acquisition or maintenance, competitive injury and causation; lawful statutory exclusion and returns on invention retain substantive justificatory force.

**Authority:** The common six-Justice Court opinion directly controls this patent holding.

**Controlling explanation:** Eastman Kodak requires actual market and exclusion analysis and permits a single-brand aftermarket only when real substitutes and constraints support it. Its separate section 2 holding is not limited to tying, but it did not decide this patent defense. Grinnell distinguishes unlawful maintenance from success through superior products, business acumen or accident. Aspen Skiing makes actual exclusion and business explanation material without imposing a general duty to help rivals.

Patent exclusion is a legal entitlement, not an ordinary preference that a rival's desire for access can overcome. Nor does a distinct antitrust market by itself prove conduct exceeds the patent grant. But neither the grant nor the patent-misuse protection against loss of relief merely for refusing to license supplies immunity from an independently proved monopolization wrong. The statutes operate together by requiring competitive misconduct beyond the ordinary consequences of enjoying exclusivity while retaining the entitlement's genuine weight.

Xerox's end-user sales, selective verification, barriers to alternate supply and alleged service consequences warrant assessment under that rule, not a finding of liability. The lower court's exhaustive gate foreclosed that inquiry without fraud, sham suit or tying. Removing it leaves CSU the burden of competent evidence; refusal, hostility to competitors, harm to CSU and a service-market label are insufficient. Quality, inventory, workable support, superiority, lawful innovation returns and supported efficiencies remain material. Documents, testimony and actual conduct may establish or defeat the elements; no universal econometric-study requirement applies. Professional Real Estate Investors protects petitioning on its own terms, not every commercial refusal.

##### Copyright justification and the required remand

**Holding and operative rule:** Copyright's exclusive rights provide a substantial justification for refusing protected manuals or diagnostic-software access, but do not conclusively defeat an objectively supported monopoly-maintenance claim merely because unlawful acquisition or extension beyond the copyright grant is absent. The same ordinary monopolization burdens apply; subjective pretext, rival injury or refusal alone cannot rebut the statutory justification, and every eventual remedy must fit a proved violation and causal injury while preserving lawful protected returns insofar as compatible with curing that violation.

**Authority:** The common six-Justice Court opinion directly controls this copyright holding and both remand components.

**Controlling explanation:** The lower court's copyright analysis differs from its patent rule: it gave exclusion a presumptively valid justification and found no definitive rebuttal through unlawful acquisition or extension beyond the grant. The Court preserves the strength of that statutory justification but rejects a conclusive limit that excludes an otherwise supported antitrust wrong from consideration. A copyright label cannot replace the competitive inquiry, just as an allegation of exclusion cannot nullify copyright.

California Dental requires attention to the actual competitive mechanism and substantial explanations rather than a fixed label or compulsory form of market study. NYNEX and Brunswick keep harm to competition and antitrust injury distinct from the loss suffered by one supplier. Those requirements remain CSU's burdens. The Court does not transfer Kodak's evidentiary result to this different record or treat ordinary copyright exclusion as unlawful intent.

On remand, the courts must identify the actual protected rights, challenged conduct, competitive mechanism and supported evidence under ordinary summary-judgment standards. A failure of proof or a claim concerning only lawful exclusion permits renewed summary judgment; this decision requires no new discovery, hearing or trial automatically. The settlement's temporary access provisions neither establish antitrust liability nor show that forced access is an appropriate remedy for this opt-out plaintiff.

**Limits and work remaining:** Actual market definition, durable monopoly power, unlawful exclusion, causation, antitrust injury and lawful relief require adjudication under the announced rule. They are remaining liability and remedial work, not reservations of the rule against categorical protection. Patent validity, infringement and copyright ownership are outside the presented question. No final misuse ruling follows; any live consequence of removing the lower court's patent-scope predicate must be decided below on its own preserved grounds. No damages or compulsory-access terms are entered.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Eastman Kodak Co. v. Image Technical Services, Inc.: applies the distinct power, conduct and justification inquiries and its fact-supported aftermarket rule; the patent and copyright defense left open there is now resolved. Its tying holding creates no tying claim here.

- United States v. Grinnell Corp.: preserves monopoly power and unlawful acquisition or maintenance as distinct requirements, with superiority, business acumen and accident outside the offense.

- Aspen Skiing Co. v. Aspen Highlands Skiing Corp.: applies scrutiny of actual exclusion and business justification without a general duty to supply competitors.

- California Dental Association v. Federal Trade Commission: applies restraint-specific consideration of effects and substantial explanations; no automatic full-market study or motive-only test follows.

- NYNEX Corp. v. Discon, Inc.: preserves the distinction between injury to one supplier and injury to the competitive process; its section 1 categories are not converted into a new section 2 exemption.

- Brunswick Corp. v. Pueblo Bowl-O-Mat, Inc.: preserves the independent antitrust-injury requirement.

- Professional Real Estate Investors, Inc. v. Columbia Pictures Industries, Inc.: preserves its objectively-baseless-litigation prerequisite for the sham petitioning theory it addressed; that rule is not transplanted as immunity for commercial refusal.

- TrafFix Devices, Inc. v. Marketing Displays, Inc.: its expired-patent functionality and trade-dress holding remains intact and supplies no disposition of valid patent or copyright exclusion here.

#### Law After Decision

A properly supported monopoly-maintenance claim involving protected parts or service information receives rights-sensitive section 2 analysis without an exhaustive fraud, sham-litigation, tying, unlawful-acquisition or beyond-grant gate. Genuine patent and copyright exclusion remain substantial legal interests. No automatic aftermarket, unlawful refusal, duty to supply or access entitlement follows. Both types of claim retain ordinary evidentiary and injury requirements; infringement and remedial design remain distinct.

#### Separate Writings

**Scalia, dissenting, joined by O'Connor and Thomas.** They would affirm both components because the presented refusal theory identifies no adequate wrong beyond protected exclusion. Patent law protects the owner's decision to withhold protected products; service-market effects do not by themselves establish conduct outside that grant. CSU presents no procurement fraud, sham enforcement or illegal tie. For copyright, the strong statutory exclusion justification remains unrebutted by a legally sufficient showing of unlawful acquisition, extension or another independent wrong. Those are distinct grounds, not identically worded immunities.

Kodak's inquiry into aftermarket power remains binding, but power does not itself establish prohibited conduct or decide the stronger statutory-right defense it left open. Its dissenting concerns about proprietary-product choices reinforce the need to identify an offense beyond refusal; they do not displace Kodak's actual holding. NYNEX keeps rival loss distinct from competitive injury, and Professional Real Estate Investors separates legally protected conduct from hostile motive without making every refusal an act of petitioning. These Justices preserve liability for an independently unlawful antitrust wrong; they reject the remand because the supported refusal theory supplies none here. Their position would end the affected antitrust claims, without deciding infringement, damages or compulsory access.
