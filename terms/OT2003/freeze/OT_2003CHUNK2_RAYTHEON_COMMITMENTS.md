## Governing propositions and supported alternatives

**P1 — lawful production-stage explanation.** An employer's actual uniform policy against rehiring persons separated for misconduct can be a legitimate nondiscriminatory reason in this disparate-treatment action. The ADA's protection for qualifying recovered persons does not require treating past misconduct as the protected status itself. Section 12114's treatment of present illegal use, rehabilitation and workplace rules must coexist with section 12112's prohibition of disability-based decisions. The production burden does not require the employer to prove its explanation true.

**P2 — motive and evidentiary sufficiency.** Whether this explanation actually caused the decision remains separate. McDonnell Douglas and Burdine retain the employee's ultimate burden; Hicks rejects automatic liability from falsity while permitting the prima-facie-plus-falsity inference. Reeves requires whole-record consideration without credibility choices and rejects a mandatory extra category of evidence. Hazen Paper permits distinction between misconduct and protected status, but an analytically distinct criterion can be a proxy for the forbidden consideration. The policy is therefore neither automatically unlawful nor an evidentiary safe harbor.

**P3 — corrective disposition.** Vacate the Ninth Circuit judgment on the preserved disparate-treatment claim and remand for the actual knowledge, qualification, pretext and sufficiency inquiries under P1–P2. The appellate decision rejected the offered explanation at the production stage and therefore did not perform the required subsequent analysis under the correct rule. The lower court must examine the evidence in the light required by summary-judgment review and decide whether a reasonable factfinder could find intentional disability discrimination. If that inference is reasonably supported, the claim proceeds; if the entire record permits no such finding, summary judgment is appropriate. The untimely impact theory remains outside the remand.

The stronger competing path is an affirmance of the denial of summary judgment on a corrected intentional-discrimination rationale: the personnel file, recovery letter and Medina statement may permit an inference of protected-status knowledge and discriminatory motive. Its weakness is that the Ninth Circuit's ultimate sufficiency reasoning depended materially on declaring the policy unlawful even without knowledge. Correction therefore requires an actual assessment after crediting the legally permissible explanation, rather than treating its unlawful-impact premise as harmless.

The employer's stronger competing request is outright judgment: an honestly uniform misconduct rule and genuine lack of knowledge would defeat the pleaded motive theory. Its weakness is the disputed evidence. Bockmiller's testimony that she reviewed the file and would have seen submitted material does not conclusively prove knowledge, but neither can her denial be automatically credited. Medina was not the identified decisionmaker, yet his company explanation to the EEOC cannot be erased merely on that label; its connection to the actual decision must be evaluated. The 1999 failure does not conclusively establish lack of qualification in 1994. The preserved claim cannot be finally rejected by assuming those disputed propositions.

## Scope, remedy and verification

All six commitments support the same three propositions at the stated level of generality. Their differing emphasis on the evidence supplies no additional holding or necessary separate writing. No Justice commits to a final finding of intentional discrimination or to the ultimate sufficiency result after the correct whole-record inquiry. Those are factual/liability tasks on remand, not an unanswered legal standard.

The remand must distinguish the actual employee's 1994 qualification from the later examination, the decisionmaker's knowledge from Medina's later explanation, and the legal availability of the policy from its genuine application. It must preserve the assumed disability premise and the preserved claim's scope. It may not demand a new evidentiary category, impose a new employer persuasion burden, or revive disparate impact.
