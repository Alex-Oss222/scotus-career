## Standards and Tests selection: Verification cure for timely administrative discrimination charges

### Verification cure for timely administrative discrimination charges

**Governing question:** When a timely Title VII charge may be verified after the filing period.

**Current rule:** An otherwise sufficient timely charge may receive required verification later under 29 C.F.R. §1601.12(b), relating back to its original filing. Verification remains compulsory and must protect the employer before it must answer the substance. Relation back cures verification only: it does not extend the charge deadline or make an otherwise insufficient paper a charge.

**Authority by component:**

- Canonical Decision Record `edelman-v-lynchburg-college_merits_2002-03-19.md`, Public Projection / Holdings / A timely charge may receive later verification, 2002-03-19; Ginsburg with Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer, seven on the independent statutory construction; O'Connor and Scalia support the same cure on a separate rationale.

**Present operation effective:** Edelman v. Lynchburg College, 2002-03-19.

**Exceptions and limits:** Timely initiation, charge sufficiency and actual verification remain distinct; the cure does not determine when the challenged employment practice accrued.

## Standards and Tests selection: An appellate brief functioning as a notice of appeal

### An appellate brief functioning as a notice of appeal

**Governing question:** Whether an informally styled document supplies appellate notice and how an omitted signature on otherwise sufficient timely notice is cured.

**Current rule:** A document filed within the applicable appeal period may serve as the notice if its objective content identifies the appealing party, judgment or order appealed, and appellate court, as Rule 3(c) requires. Informality of title or form cannot alone require dismissal; every required item remains necessary. Under Rule 4's mistaken-filing provision, a notice mistakenly filed in the court of appeals is treated as filed in the district court upon the appellate court's receipt.

A timely notice satisfying the required identification must be signed, but Civil Rule 11(a) permits prompt correction after the omission is called to the filer’s attention, including after the appeal period. The filer may sign the existing paper or provide a signed duplicate; correction completes the timely notice rather than filing a new appeal. A court may not dismiss for the omission without allowing that cure. The typed name alone on a paper form is insufficient; authorized electronic execution is a different unresolved question. These same-rule protections are not confined to pro se or prisoner litigants.

**Authority by component:**

- Objective content, timely functional compliance, and mistaken appellate filing: Smith v. Barry — Law of the decision, Holding and operative rule, January 14, 1992; O'Connor's opinion joined by eight Justices. Scalia's judgment concurrence is not counted as joining the full rationale.

- Signature requirement, prompt cure and preserved time/content conditions: Canonical Decision Record `Becker_v_Montgomery_merits_2001-05-29.md`, Public Projection / Holdings / The required signature may be supplied through the rule’s express cure, 2001-05-29; Ginsburg’s unanimous opinion.

**Present operation effective:** Becker, May 29, 2001, for signature correction; Smith v. Barry’s January 14, 1992 timely objective-notice and mistaken-filing rules retain their scope.

**Holdings navigation:** Smith v. Barry, 502 U.S. 244 (1992) — Appellate jurisdiction and notices of appeal. [Becker v. Montgomery, 532 U.S. 757 — Appellate notices and prompt signature correction](HOLDINGS.md#becker-v-montgomery-532-us-757).

**Exceptions and limits:** Rule 3(c)(2)’s spouse-and-minor-children provision concerns persons who are parties and yields to a clearly contrary notice; it supplies neither an added appellant nor a right of representation without its own basis. The signature cure creates no general power to excuse missing timely notice or required identification.

## Standards and Tests selection: Social Security remand judgments and EAJA fees

### Social Security remand judgments and EAJA fees

**Governing question:** What instructions govern social Security remand judgments and EAJA fees?

**Current rule:** A sentence-four §405(g) judgment reversing a benefits denial with remand terminates the civil action and confers prevailing-party status; judgment must be entered rather than retaining the merits action pending an administrative award. Sentence-six remands are available on the Secretary's pre-answer motion for good cause, or for new material evidence with good cause for its earlier omission; these proceedings retain jurisdiction for the further administrative proceedings and return to court. Under the then-effective Rules, EAJA's thirty-day application period follows proper judgment entry and expiration of the applicable sixty-day appeal period; absence of the Rule 58 separate judgment prevents expiration. Hudson's integrated-administration fee rationale does not govern a properly entered sentence-four remand without retained jurisdiction, or retention challenged as improper; it remains available for qualifying sentence-six proceedings subject to EAJA's other conditions. No later deemed-entry rule applies. A claimant who requested an immediate benefits award but obtained only a final sentence-four remand remains adversely affected by the denied relief and may appeal the judgment under §1291.

## Standards and Tests selection: Administrative EAJA coverage of deportation hearings

### Administrative EAJA coverage of deportation hearings

**Governing question:** Whether an INA deportation hearing qualifies as an adjudication under §554 for administrative fees under EAJA §504.

**Current rule:** A deportation hearing governed by the INA's exclusive procedure instead of 5 U.S.C. §554 is outside EAJA §504's administrative fee authorization, even if it resembles a formal APA adjudication. Actual statutory governance controls; functional similarity, agency regulations, and remedial purpose do not replace the enacted cross-reference. Failure of coverage makes it unnecessary to decide substantial justification.

**Authority by component:**

- Actual §554 governance and exclusion of the independent INA proceeding: Ardestani v. INS — Law of the decision, Holding and operative rule, December 10, 1991; O'Connor's opinion joined by Stone, White, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter.

**Present operation effective:** *Ardestani*, December 10, 1991.

**Exceptions and limits:** This construction does not restrict a qualifying judicial EAJA action or administrative work integral to it; that distinct predicate must be established independently.

**Holdings navigation:** Ardestani v. INS, 502 U.S. 129 (1991) — Administrative fee awards.

## Canonical Holdings selection from state/holdings/federal-courts.md

#### Becker v. Montgomery, 532 U.S. 757

**Docket or dockets:** No. 00-6374.
**Decided:** May 29, 2001.

##### Whether a missing signature on an otherwise sufficient timely notice can be supplied after the appeal period

**Holding:** Civil Rule 11(a), applicable to this district-court filing through Appellate Rule 1(a)(2), permits an omitted signature on an otherwise sufficient timely notice of appeal to be supplied promptly after notification, including after the appeal period. The court must afford that prescribed correction opportunity before dismissing for the omission; correction completes the existing timely notice rather than commencing a late appeal.

**Proposition-level authority:** Ginsburg's unanimous opinion of the Court.

**Material application:** Becker's notice timely identified the appellant, judgment and appellate court, and he promptly sought correction when first notified. He may sign the filed notice or supply a signed duplicate; his typed name alone did not sign this paper notice.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Timing and required identifying content remain mandatory, and the cure applies equally to represented litigants. Rule 3(c)(2)'s spouse-and-minor-children rule concerns persons already parties, yields to a clearly contrary notice and supplies no distinct correction deadline or adjudicated representation right. Authorized electronic signatures, underlying exhaustion and pleading issues remain outside review.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Reverse the jurisdictional dismissal, allow the prescribed prompt correction and restore the timely appeal for ordinary review. No general equitable extension or prisoner exception is created.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Smith v. Barry's timely-objective-notice distinction extends to this signature setting through Rule 11's express cure, without relaxing original timing or content requirements.

## Canonical Holdings selection from state/holdings/labor-and-employment.md

#### Edelman v. Lynchburg College, 535 U.S. 106

**Docket or dockets:** No. 00-1072.

**Decided:** March 19, 2002.

##### A timely charge may receive later verification

**Holding:** An otherwise sufficient Title VII charge filed within the applicable filing period may receive its required verification afterward through 29 C.F.R. section 1601.12(b), with the verification relating back to the timely charge. Verification remains mandatory and must protect the employer before it is obliged to answer the substance; the cure does not extend the filing deadline or turn a document that otherwise is not a charge into one.

**Proposition-level authority:** Ginsburg's opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Breyer, giving seven direct votes for this independent statutory construction. O'Connor and Scalia support the same operative cure and judgment on their distinct delegated-choice ground; that rationale is not added to the Court's reasoning through vote aggregation.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The procedural regulation is reasonable, consistent with Title VII and authorized by section 713(a), with Administrative Procedure Act compliance. Broader deference questions need not be resolved. Timely initiation, eventual oath and independent charge sufficiency remain separate requirements. Agency delay alone establishes neither a bar nor that the original document was insufficient.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Reverse unanimously. Whether the timely November letter actually was a charge, the significance of later correspondence and the EEOC's ten-day notice failure, and other preserved defenses require the appropriate lower-court analysis.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

Becker v. Montgomery is applied by analogy to distinguish completion of an existing timely filing from initiation of a late proceeding; its express cure rule is not transplanted into Title VII as independent authority.

Zipes v. Trans World Airlines, Inc. supplies the distinction between the timely-charge condition and subject-matter jurisdiction, without deciding every processing requirement's classification.

Love v. Pullman Co. and EEOC v. Commercial Office Products Co. inform the lay-initiated administrative process; they do not authorize disregard of the actual filing deadline or oath requirement.

United States v. Mead Corp. and Christensen v. Harris County retain their distinctions between delegated legal force and persuasive interpretation. The Court's independent construction makes a broader deference ruling unnecessary; O'Connor and Scalia rely on the actual procedural delegation for their separate ground.

Leatherman v. Tarrant County Narcotics Intelligence and Coordination Unit retains its source-based rejection of invented pleading requirements in its own field. Swierkiewicz v. Sorema N.A. governs the later federal complaint, not what constitutes a timely administrative charge.

EEOC v. Waffle House, Inc. retains the distinction between statutory enforcement authority and the actual prerequisites for relief; it supplies no independent verification-timing rule.

#### Ardestani v. INS, 502 U.S. 129 (1991)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 90-1141.  
**Decided:** December 10, 1991.

##### Whether an INA deportation hearing is an adjudication under section 554 for administrative EAJA fees

**Holding:** An administrative deportation hearing governed by the INA's exclusive procedure rather than by 5 U.S.C. §554 is not an adjudication under section 554 and therefore falls outside EAJA §504's administrative fee authorization, even if the hearing resembles formal APA adjudication.

**Proposition-level authority:** O'Connor's opinion joined by Stone, White, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter; six of eight participants.

**Material application:** The exclusive INA procedure supplies the hearing's governing authority. Functional similarity, agency regulations, and remedial purpose cannot enlarge the enacted fee cross-reference.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Substantial justification is not reached because coverage fails. Covered judicial EAJA claims and separately presented constitutional claims are not decided; the asylum grant and its factual basis are undisturbed.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The Eleventh Circuit is affirmed; the administrative fee award is unavailable under §504.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Marcello supplies the INA procedure's displacement of APA hearing provisions. Sullivan v. Hudson is distinguished as involving administrative work integral to a qualifying court action. Shaw's monetary-authorization rule and Pierce's substantial-justification rule remain unchanged; neither supplies missing §504 coverage.

#### Shalala v. Schaefer, 509 U.S. 292 (1993)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 92-311.
**Decided:** June 24, 1993.

##### Sentence-four judgment ends the action and confers prevailing status

**Holding:** A sentence-four judgment reversing the Secretary’s denial of benefits with a remand ends the §405(g) civil action and makes the successful claimant a prevailing party; the court must enter judgment rather than retain the merits action pending an administrative award. Sentence-six remands instead retain jurisdiction for the statutorily specified proceedings and later return to court.

**Proposition-level authority:** Scalia’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (7 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Sullivan v. Finkelstein, 496 U.S. 617, and Melkonyan v. Sullivan, 501 U.S. 89: the two statutory remand routes applied.
- Texas State Teachers Assn. v. Garland Independent School District, 489 U.S. 782, and Farrar, December 14, 1992: enforceable judicial relief establishes prevailing status, not automatic fee amount.

##### The missing separate judgment prevents the fee deadline from expiring

**Holding:** Under the then-effective rules, EAJA’s thirty-day application period follows a properly entered judgment and expiration of the applicable sixty-day appeal period; the absence of the required Rule 58 separate judgment prevents that deadline from expiring. Schaefer’s application is timely although the sentence-four remand was itself a final merits disposition.

**Proposition-level authority:** Scalia’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (7 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- United States v. Indrelunas, 411 U.S. 216: separate-document requirement applied as then in force.
- Bankers Trust Co. v. Mallis, 435 U.S. 381: ability to take an appeal without the document distinguished from expiration of appellate time.
- Melkonyan, 501 U.S. 89: final court judgment, not agency decision, governs the EAJA clock.

##### Hudson does not preserve administrative-fee coverage after an ordinary terminated sentence-four action

**Holding:** Hudson’s integrated administrative-proceeding fee rationale does not apply to a sentence-four remand properly entered without retained jurisdiction, or to retention of jurisdiction that is challenged. It remains applicable to qualifying sentence-six proceedings in which the court retains the action, subject to EAJA’s other requirements.

**Proposition-level authority:** Scalia’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (6 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Sentence six permits a pre-answer remand on the Secretary’s good-cause motion, or new material evidence with good cause for earlier omission, retaining jurisdiction for return and later judgment. Prevailing status does not establish every EAJA condition or amount. No later deemed-entry deadline is imported.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm the timeliness determination and reviewed award; enter the required separate judgment where still absent. No new administrative-services award or reopening of unreviewed fee issues.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Sullivan v. Hudson, 490 U.S. 877: retained-jurisdiction rationale limited in light of the statutory distinction; sentence-six coverage preserved.
- Finkelstein, 496 U.S. 617, and Melkonyan, 501 U.S. 89: sentence-four termination controls; statutory retention is reserved for sentence six.

## Event

**Kontrick v. Ryan.** No. 02-819. Decided January 14, 2004, on merits submission. The Court reviews the Seventh Circuit's affirmance of denial of discharge and rejection of Kontrick's postmerits timing objection. The question is whether Bankruptcy Rules 4004 and 9006 impose a subject-matter jurisdictional limit that survives an adverse merits adjudication, or a procedural defense forfeited by failing to invoke it before that adjudication.

Ryan amended his objection to discharge to allege fraudulent family-account transfers after extensions of the filing deadline and without a further extension. Kontrick answered the theory and contested summary judgment on the merits. Only after the court decided the claim against him did he assert untimeliness. The amendment's untimeliness is conceded for the question before the Court.

**Affirmed, 9–0. Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The postmerits timing defense is forfeited. The adjudicated denial of discharge remains in force.

## Holdings

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's opinion for a unanimous Court, joined by every other participating Justice.

**A debtor forfeits this filing defense by first raising it after the claim is adjudicated on the merits.** Bankruptcy Rules 4004 and 9006 regulate claim processing and enlargement rather than subject-matter jurisdiction; a debtor who litigates an objection to discharge through an adverse merits determination before raising untimeliness forfeits that defense. The creditor retains the burden to prove the discharge objection, and the rules' restrictions continue to govern when the debtor timely invokes them; forfeiture supplies no power to grant an extension the rules forbid over a timely objection.

Congress conferred jurisdiction over bankruptcy proceedings through 28 U.S.C. §§1334 and 157, including objections to discharge under §157(b)(2)(J). Section 2075 and Rule 9030 prevent procedural rules from enlarging or restricting that jurisdiction. Rule 4004 generally requires an objection within sixty days after the first date set for the creditors' meeting and permits an extension for cause on a motion filed before the period expires. Rule 9006(b)(3) confines enlargement to the conditions Rule 4004 permits. Those provisions regulate the orderly assertion of claims and defenses; they do not make an omitted procedural defense a perpetual challenge to statutory competence.

Carlisle enforced a timely invoked national-rule deadline while distinguishing remedial authority from jurisdiction and leaving forfeiture open. Henderson likewise examines the source, placement and function of a timing command. Here the separate jurisdictional statutes and procedural rules make the distinction clear. Kontrick's strict-enlargement argument concerns the court's authority to extend a deadline over a timely objection, which differs from a debtor's ability to insist on an unraised defense after losing on the merits. He answered the family-account theory and litigated it through judgment. That conduct forfeited the timing defense without a finding that he intentionally surrendered it or an equitable extension of the deadline.

Equitable tolling, relation back and the precise final point for asserting the defense before merits adjudication lie outside the accepted postmerits question. The rule decided here resolves Kontrick's attempt to undo the completed merits determination.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Carlisle v. United States:** Applied for the distinction between mandatory procedural limits and subject-matter jurisdiction. The present decision resolves postmerits forfeiture of this bankruptcy defense while preserving Carlisle's timely-objection holding.
- **Henderson v. United States:** Applied for classification of a timing command through its source, placement and function rather than a jurisdictional label; the different statutory timetable at issue there retains its own treatment.

## Law After Decision

The specified bankruptcy filing and enlargement rules remain mandatory procedural prescriptions, but their protection is lost when a debtor first invokes untimeliness after adjudication of the discharge objection's merits. Statutory subject-matter jurisdiction survives that filing defect, and a timely invoked objection continues to receive the rules' prescribed treatment.

§2412. Costs and fees

(a)(1) Except as otherwise specifically provided by statute, a judgment for costs, as enumerated in section 1920 of this title, but not including the fees and expenses of attorneys, may be awarded to the prevailing party in any civil action brought by or against the United States or any agency or any official of the United States acting in his or her official capacity in any court having jurisdiction of such action. A judgment for costs when taxed against the United States shall, in an amount established by statute, court rule, or order, be limited to reimbursing in whole or in part the prevailing party for the costs incurred by such party in the litigation.
(2) A judgment for costs, when awarded in favor of the United States in an action brought by the United States, may include an amount equal to the filing fee prescribed under section 1914(a) of this title. The preceding sentence shall not be construed as requiring the United States to pay any filing fee.
(b) Unless expressly prohibited by statute, a court may award reasonable fees and expenses of attorneys, in addition to the costs which may be awarded pursuant to subsection (a), to the prevailing party in any civil action brought by or against the United States or any agency or any official of the United States acting in his or her official capacity in any court having jurisdiction of such action. The United States shall be liable for such fees and expenses to the same extent that any other party would be liable under the common law or under the terms of any statute which specifically provides for such an award.
(c)(1) Any judgment against the United States or any agency and any official of the United States acting in his or her official capacity for costs pursuant to subsection (a) shall be paid as provided in sections 2414 and 2517 of this title and shall be in addition to any relief provided in the judgment.
(2) Any judgment against the United States or any agency and any official of the United States acting in his or her official capacity for fees and expenses of attorneys pursuant to subsection (b) shall be paid as provided in sections 2414 and 2517 of this title, except that if the basis for the award is a finding that the United States acted in bad faith, then the award shall be paid by any agency found to have acted in bad faith and shall be in addition to any relief provided in the judgment.
(d)(1)(A) Except as otherwise specifically provided by statute, a court shall award to a prevailing party other than the United States fees and other expenses, in addition to any costs awarded pursuant to subsection (a), incurred by that party in any civil action (other than cases sounding in tort), including proceedings for judicial review of agency action, brought by or against the United States in any court having jurisdiction of that action, unless the court finds that the position of the United States was substantially justified or that special circumstances make an award unjust.
(B) A party seeking an award of fees and other expenses shall, within thirty days of final judgment in the action, submit to the court an application for fees and other expenses which shows that the party is a prevailing party and is eligible to receive an award under this subsection, and the amount sought, including an itemized statement from any attorney or expert witness representing or appearing in behalf of the party stating the actual time expended and the rate at which fees and other expenses were computed. The party shall also allege that the position of the United States was not substantially justified. Whether or not the position of the United States was substantially justified shall be determined on the basis of the record (including the record with respect to the action or failure to act by the agency upon which the civil action is based) which is made in the civil action for which fees and other expenses are sought.
(C) The court, in its discretion, may reduce the amount to be awarded pursuant to this subsection, or deny an award, to the extent that the prevailing party during the course of the proceedings engaged in conduct which unduly and unreasonably protracted the final resolution of the matter in controversy.
(D) If, in a civil action brought by the United States or a proceeding for judicial review of an adversary adjudication described in section 504(a)(4) of title 5, the demand by the United States is substantially in excess of the judgment finally obtained by the United States and is unreasonable when compared with such judgment, under the facts and circumstances of the case, the court shall award to the party the fees and other expenses related to defending against the excessive demand, unless the party has committed a willful violation of law or otherwise acted in bad faith, or special circumstances make an award unjust. Fees and expenses awarded under this subparagraph shall be paid only as a consequence of appropriations provided in advance.
(2) For the purposes of this subsection—
(A) “fees and other expenses” includes the reasonable expenses of expert witnesses, the reasonable cost of any study, analysis, engineering report, test, or project which is found by the court to be necessary for the preparation of the party's case, and reasonable attorney fees (The amount of fees awarded under this subsection shall be based upon prevailing market rates for the kind and quality of the services furnished, except that (i) no expert witness shall be compensated at a rate in excess of the highest rate of compensation for expert witnesses paid by the United States; and (ii) attorney fees shall not be awarded in excess of $125 per hour unless the court determines that an increase in the cost of living or a special factor, such as the limited availability of qualified attorneys for the proceedings involved, justifies a higher fee.);
(B) “party” means (i) an individual whose net worth did not exceed $2,000,000 at the time the civil action was filed, or (ii) any owner of an unincorporated business, or any partnership, corporation, association, unit of local government, or organization, the net worth of which did not exceed $7,000,000 at the time the civil action was filed, and which had not more than 500 employees at the time the civil action was filed; except that an organization described in section 501(c)(3) of the Internal Revenue Code of 1986 (26 U.S.C. 501(c)(3)) exempt from taxation under section 501(a) of such Code, or a cooperative association as defined in section 15(a) of the Agricultural Marketing Act (12 U.S.C. 1141j(a)), may be a party regardless of the net worth of such organization or cooperative association or for purposes of subsection (d)(1)(D), a small entity as defined in section 601 of title 5;
(C) “United States” includes any agency and any official of the United States acting in his or her official capacity;
(D) “position of the United States” means, in addition to the position taken by the United States in the civil action, the action or failure to act by the agency upon which the civil action is based; except that fees and expenses may not be awarded to a party for any portion of the litigation in which the party has unreasonably protracted the proceedings;
(E) “civil action brought by or against the United States” includes an appeal by a party, other than the United States, from a decision of a contracting officer rendered pursuant to a disputes clause in a contract with the Government or pursuant to the Contract Disputes Act of 1978;
(F) “court” includes the United States Court of Federal Claims and the United States Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims;
(G) “final judgment” means a judgment that is final and not appealable, and includes an order of settlement;
(H) “prevailing party”, in the case of eminent domain proceedings, means a party who obtains a final judgment (other than by settlement), exclusive of interest, the amount of which is at least as close to the highest valuation of the property involved that is attested to at trial on behalf of the property owner as it is to the highest valuation of the property involved that is attested to at trial on behalf of the Government; and
(I) “demand” means the express demand of the United States which led to the adversary adjudication, but shall not include a recitation of the maximum statutory penalty (i) in the complaint, or (ii) elsewhere when accompanied by an express demand for a lesser amount.

(3) In awarding fees and other expenses under this subsection to a prevailing party in any action for judicial review of an adversary adjudication, as defined in subsection (b)(1)(C) of section 504 of title 5, United States Code, or an adversary adjudication subject to the Contract Disputes Act of 1978, the court shall include in that award fees and other expenses to the same extent authorized in subsection (a) of such section, unless the court finds that during such adversary adjudication the position of the United States was substantially justified, or that special circumstances make an award unjust.
(4) Fees and other expenses awarded under this subsection to a party shall be paid by any agency over which the party prevails from any funds made available to the agency by appropriation or otherwise.
(e) The provisions of this section shall not apply to any costs, fees, and other expenses in connection with any proceeding to which section 7430 of the Internal Revenue Code of 1986 applies (determined without regard to subsections (b) and (f) of such section). Nothing in the preceding sentence shall prevent the awarding under subsection (a) of section 2412 of title 28, United States Code, of costs enumerated in section 1920 of such title (as in effect on October 1, 1981).
(f) If the United States appeals an award of costs or fees and other expenses made against the United States under this section and the award is affirmed in whole or in part, interest shall be paid on the amount of the award as affirmed. Such interest shall be computed at the rate determined under section 1961(a) of this title, and shall run from the date of the award through the day before the date of the mandate of affirmance.

Historical and Revision Notes
Based on title 28, U.S.C., 1940 ed., §§258, 931(a) (Mar. 3, 1911, ch. 231, §152, 36 Stat. 1138; Aug. 2, 1946, ch. 753, §410(a), 60 Stat. 843).
Section consolidates the last sentence of section 931(a) of title 28, U.S.C., 1940 ed., with section 258 of said title 28. For other provisions of said section 931(a), see Distribution Table.
Subsection (a) is new. It follows the well-known common-law rule that a sovereign is not liable for costs unless specific provision for such liability is made by law. This is a corollary to the rule that a sovereign cannot be sued without its consent.
Many enactments of Congress relating to fees and costs contain specific exceptions as to the liability of the United States. (See, for example, section 548 of title 28, U.S.C., 1940 ed.) A uniform rule, embodied in this section, will make such specific exceptions unnecessary.
Subsection (b) incorporates section 258 of title 28, U.S.C., 1940 ed.
Subsection (c) incorporates the costs provisions of section 931(a) of title 28, U.S.C., 1940 ed.
Words “and for summoning the same,” after “witnesses,” were omitted from subsection (b) as covered by “those actually incurred for witnesses.”
Changes were made in phraseology.
