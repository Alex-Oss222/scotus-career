## Governing rule, posture and earlier-law limits

Kansas's **Genuine choice under conditional federal funding** rule controls. Substantial financial and political difficulty in declining related assistance does not alone establish coercion. A supported structure and practical operation leaving no genuine choice can establish it. Threatened funds, their connection to the condition, notice, transition, consequences of refusal, program operation, administrative experience and feasible alternatives may matter, but form no mandatory checklist. Competent qualitative evidence can establish compulsion without an exclusively statistical showing.

There is no fixed percentage, dollar, total-resource or insolvency threshold. Dole's five-percent setting is no safe harbor. Clear notice, relatedness, the voluntary-program description and the absence of a right to unchanged grant terms do not individually defeat a supported coercion claim. ## Actual statutory condition and its distinct qualifications

The following statements preserve the statute's operative distinctions. They find no beneficiary eligible for recovery, establish no lien and decide no independent lien challenge.

1. **Correctly paid assistance and the institutional category.** Section 1396p(b)(1) generally forbids adjustment or recovery of correctly paid medical assistance except the identified categories. For an individual described in subsection (a)(1)(B), the State shall seek adjustment or recovery from the estate or upon sale of property subject to the institutional lien. The incorporated class consists of an inpatient in a nursing facility, intermediate care facility for the mentally retarded or other medical institution who must, as a condition of institutional services under the plan, spend all income except a minimal personal-needs amount on medical care. The State must determine after notice and an opportunity for hearing under state procedures that discharge and return home cannot reasonably be expected. A command to seek recovery is not a finding that recovery succeeds or that any asset is available.

2. **Age and covered services.** For assistance received at age 55 or older, subsection (b)(1)(B) requires estate recovery for nursing-facility services, home-and-community-based services and related hospital and prescription-drug services. The State may elect recovery for other items or services under its plan. Age is measured when assistance was received, and optional additional services do not become a universal recovery duty.

3. **Long-term-care insurance.** Subsection (b)(1)(C) separately requires estate recovery for nursing-facility and other long-term-care assistance for a person receiving or entitled to insurance benefits connected to the specified asset or resource disregard. The requirement does not apply when assistance was received under a State plan whose amendment was approved as of May 14, 1993 and provided disregard to the extent of payments under long-term-care insurance, or because the individual received or was entitled to its benefits. That grandfathered exception is distinct from hardship.

4. **Survivor and home-occupant restrictions.** Recovery under paragraph (1) may occur only after any surviving spouse dies and when there is no surviving child under 21 or within the protected blindness/disability category. For States eligible to participate in the State program under subchapter XVI, the latter category is a child who is blind or permanently and totally disabled; for States not eligible, it is a child who is blind or disabled as defined in ?1382c. The disability protection is not confined to children under 21. Recovery under an institutional home lien must also wait while a qualifying sibling or caregiving son or daughter lawfully lives there and has continuously lawfully resided there since admission. The sibling must have lived there for at least the immediately preceding year. The son or daughter must have lived there for at least the immediately preceding two years and establish to the State's satisfaction that the care provided allowed the individual to remain home instead of entering an institution. This recovery-stage sibling protection does not require equity ownership and does not protect every adult heir.

5. **Hardship and estate definition.** Subsection (b)(3) requires procedures conforming to the Secretary's standards under which the agency shall waive application when it would cause undue hardship under the Secretary's criteria, except for paragraph (1)(C). This is a mandatory beneficiary-level safeguard with its stated exception, not optional implementation of the entire State recovery program. Under subsection (b)(4)(A), estate includes all real and personal property and other assets in the State-probate estate. Subsection (b)(4)(B) permits additional inclusion of property or assets in which the individual held legal title or an interest at death, only to that extent, including transfers through joint tenancy, tenancy in common, survivorship, life estate, living trust or another arrangement. That broader inclusion is mandatory for an individual to whom paragraph (1)(C)(i) applies; otherwise it remains the State's option.

6. **Separate lien predicates.** Subsection (a)(1) generally prohibits a predeath lien for assistance paid or to be paid. Its exceptions are a court judgment concerning incorrectly paid benefits and the institutional real-property lien under the predicates stated in item 1. The former is not the correctly paid estate-recovery rule. Under subsection (a)(2), the institutional home lien may not be imposed while a spouse, a child in the age or blindness/disability categories just stated, or a sibling with an equity interest who resided there for at least the year immediately preceding admission lawfully resides in the home. The equity requirement belongs to this pre-lien sibling protection, not the recovery-stage sibling provision. The institutional lien dissolves upon discharge from the institution **and** return home. Supplying these incorporated predicates does not enlarge the grant to an independent lien claim.

7. **The State-funding mechanism.** Section 1396c requires reasonable notice and an opportunity for hearing to the agency administering or supervising the approved State plan. The Secretary must find either that the plan has changed so that it no longer complies with ?1396a, or that plan administration fails to comply substantially with a provision of that section. Upon the finding, the Secretary shall notify the agency that further payments will not be made, or, in the Secretary's discretion, that continued payments will be limited to categories under or parts of the plan **not affected** by the failure. Payment cessation or that limitation continues until the Secretary is satisfied that there will no longer be the failure. The discretionary alternative preserves payments to unaffected categories or parts; it does not mean withholding from those categories while paying the affected ones. The statute requires no particular lesser selection, supplies no administrative-funds-only cap and establishes no exercised discretion here. Its cessation endpoint supplies no adjudicated retroactive-payment amount or new payment date.

The estate hardship waiver and discretion about State payments operate at different levels. Survivor and hardship protections describe the recovery obligation, but cannot themselves prove a State free to refuse a threatened funding condition. Section 1396c's procedure is a statutory command, not proof that any hearing, finding, notice of cessation, satisfaction of compliance or payment has occurred. Its discretionary alternative is legally available, not a guaranteed future cap.

## Why the facial judgment survives and what it leaves open

The supplied facts are material: federal Medicaid receipts exceed one billion dollars annually; recovery receipts are much smaller; the State resisted; HHS warned of loss of all or part; and the State complied. Those facts show substantial dependence and pressure. The disparity is relevant to practical consequences, but creates no constitutional ratio between the recovery yield and permissible funds at risk. Political unpopularity does not alone show compulsion, and the State's eventual compliance does not prove free choice by itself.

Estate recovery concerns the funded medical-assistance program's own expenditures. That direct relationship supports the condition's character as a program term while leaving coercion separately to be assessed. The statutory mechanism permits continuing payment to unaffected categories or parts instead of total cessation. On the supplied facial record, the warning remains all-or-part: no selected total sanction, binding construction closing the lesser route or otherwise supported present demand eliminating genuine choice is established. The condition and mechanism therefore are not shown constitutionally invalid throughout their operation on this record.

That conclusion does **not** adopt a rule that the mere presence of administrative discretion defeats every facial or practical-compulsion claim. Nor does it presume that every partial consequence is harmless, or that HHS will choose a small sanction. The legally available narrower applications and the actual supplied demand matter together. Neither an invented administrative ceiling nor a supposed assurance of benevolent administration supports the result.

The strongest opposing path is that a credible threat to terminate an established health program can eliminate genuine choice before any money is withheld, and a theoretical lesser alternative cannot answer an operationally unavoidable demand. All eight accept that proposition in principle under Kansas. It does not convert statutory authorization of total cessation into a fact that total cessation has been selected or made the only actual route here. Competent qualitative evidence of disrupted health services, program operation or practical inability to refuse could matter without proof of insolvency or a mandatory statistical threshold. A lesser threatened consequence could also be coercive in its actual operation.

A supported present no-choice demand could therefore justify relief without waiting for a completed cutoff. A disposition based on such a demand would require its actual record and temporal relief; it cannot be manufactured from the size of the grant or the operator's limited source access. The present record does not require a remand to search for a different sanction theory. No unknown briefing is declared missing, no litigant is found in default, and no record-development entitlement is inferred from research limits.

The other offered shortcuts fail for independent reasons. Relatedness alone does not resolve coercion. A voluntary label does not resolve coercion. Beneficiary safeguards do not resolve State-level funding pressure. Dole's numerical setting and Kansas's TANF graduated penalties cannot be inserted into Medicaid. Section 1396c cannot fairly be rewritten to impose an administrative-funds-only ceiling. The bounded facial affirmance avoids each shortcut while preserving actual coercion review.
