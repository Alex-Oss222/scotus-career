## Legal fork and components

Congress's enactment does more than authorize officials to administer a federal offense: §1301(2) recognizes and affirms inherent tribal criminal jurisdiction over all Indians, with subsection (4)'s Major Crimes Act-linked definition. Duro treated nonmember criminal jurisdiction as implicitly lost through dependent status absent affirmative congressional authorization and invited Congress to address the practical jurisdictional problem. Wheeler identifies the ultimate source of prosecutorial authority, not federal supervision alone, as decisive, and expressly reserved whether sovereignty lost and restored by Congress would be federal delegation. The current Strate, Atkinson and Hicks holdings require the actual governing source and preserve superior federal authorization; none constitutionalizes an unalterable tribal criminal boundary.

**Restoration path:** Congress may adjust this federal-law restriction on the retained sovereignty of an existing tribe over nonmember Indians; exercise of the restored power remains tribal in source. Reversal permits the federal assault prosecution and remands for ordinary proceedings. The source question does not decide an independent due-process challenge to the tribal proceeding, not presented here, nor the validity of tribal prosecution of non-Indians.

**Delegation path:** Duro's membership boundary describes what inherent authority survived, with criminal jurisdiction over outsiders necessarily coming from Congress. A statutory declaration cannot convert new federally conferred prosecution authority into independent tribal power for the Double Jeopardy Clause. Affirmance bars the successive federal prosecution on this same-sovereign ground. That conclusion need not invalidate Congress's authorization of tribal prosecution itself.

## Enhanced adversarial comparison

The strongest argument for restoration is that implicit divestiture arose from the federal relationship to the tribes, which Congress is constitutionally empowered to adjust; a court's federal common-law inference should not freeze that relationship after a direct legislative choice. Wheeler's footnote 28 deliberately left restored power open. For Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer, that separation between surviving sovereign and federal restriction supports full adoption of the bounded rule. Kennedy accepts the source answer while retaining the independent citizenship concern expressed in Duro. Thomas accepts removal of this judicial restriction but withholds a general plenary-power proposition. These are genuine limits on rationale scope, not additional judgment votes assumed to join all reasoning.

The strongest objection is Duro's express statement that external criminal power could come only through congressional delegation and its treatment of membership as central to retained self-government. Scalia and Souter regard that source determination as decisive; their path affirms without adjudicating the facial validity of §1301. The restoration supporters answer that Duro resolved no affirmative recognition statute and did not identify the membership limitation as an independently adjudicated constitutional prohibition. They must explain that distinction; merely repeating Congress's chosen label is insufficient to secure any of their joins.

Conversely, the delegation path's strongest weakness is that it treats the Court's implied federal-law restriction as less revisable than a statutory surrender and conflates the sovereign's exercise with federal permission to exercise it. Wheeler says pervasive federal control alone does not change the source. Scalia and Souter distinguish general control from an affirmative conferral of power the Tribe lacked. The disagreement is therefore about the effect of restoration on the source of this prosecution, not whether Congress has any Indian-affairs authority.

## Result and assembly limits

Six Associates support reversal: Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Scalia and Souter support affirmance. The four full restoration commitments plus Kennedy's bounded source join and Thomas's narrower removal-of-restriction join support the operative proposition that §1301's restoration produces tribal, not federal, authority for this nonmember-Indian prosecution. They do not automatically support every broader formulation of congressional plenary power. Kennedy's independent citizenship concern and Thomas's limited source basis have substantive separate-writing functions if the lead explanation goes beyond their accepted grounds.

Reversal remands for the federal prosecution to proceed; no guilt, sentence or disposition of a tribal habeas claim follows. Affirmance would retain the double-jeopardy dismissal.
