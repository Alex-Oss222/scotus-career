## Components and competing sufficient grounds

The judgment component is TSAC's immunity objection to this hardship-dischargeability proceeding. It is distinct from whether Hood proves the conditions for discharge. The requested relief changes enforceability of Hood's debt; it demands no affirmative payment from the State. TSAC's nonfiling of a proof of claim establishes no proof-of-claim waiver under section 106(b). The actual section 106(a) text unmistakably includes sections 523 and 524, authorizes determination of their application, and permits necessary process. Statutory authorization is not consent and remains distinct from constitutional power.

**Discharge-jurisdiction ground.** Determining the effect of the debtor's discharge on an education debt is an exercise of bankruptcy jurisdiction over the debtor's obligations and status. The creditor's State identity does not make that determination an affirmative personal recovery against the State. The statutory requirement that hardship be adjudicated, and the procedural use of a complaint and summons to provide notice and an opportunity to dispute it, do not convert the relief into State-treasury liability. Local Loan supplies the status and ancillary-jurisdiction principle; Nordic Village preserves the distinction between actual bankruptcy adjudication and a personal payment demand merely labeled property relief. Irving Trust supplies the separate principle that State participation does not displace bankruptcy administration, without supplying a waiver here.

The strongest objection is that a debtor-specific hardship action requires the State to defend a claim that is presumptively outside the discharge, so compulsory judicial process rather than ordinary estate administration is the operative burden. The six discharge-ground supporters answer that hardship determines the scope of this debtor's statutory discharge; it creates no affirmative State obligation to pay or perform independent relief. Their ground depends on the actual relief, not the title of the pleading or a universal exception for every proceeding denominated bankruptcy. The bankruptcy court may determine hardship through the governing procedure while the State remains free to contest it; immunity supplies no veto of that determination.

**Article I ground.** Independently, Florida Prepaid and Garrett authorize private enforcement of valid Article I obligations when the enforcement measure lies within the same power and Congress unmistakably authorizes State exposure. The Bankruptcy Clause covers determining dischargeability, and ordinary adjudication of the express education-debt exception is an appropriate implementing measure. Sections 106(a), 523 and 524 satisfy the actual authorization inquiry. The strongest objection distinguishes power to discharge a debt from power to compel a nonconsenting State to defend private judicial process. Under entering law that objection requires reconsidering the existing rule; it cannot be treated as an unanswered categorical prohibition.

The four Article I supporters adhere to that rule. O'Connor and Kennedy retain their structural disagreement and rely instead on the discharge-specific ground. Scalia and Thomas would reconsider the Article I rule to the extent necessary to preserve immunity from this coercive adversary proceeding, and reject the discharge ground as insufficient to remove its personal-suit burden.

## Coalition, remedy and limits

Among the eight Associates, six support affirmance: Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Those six support the discharge-specific rationale. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer additionally support the independent Article I ground. Scalia and Thomas support reversal on the reconsideration and compulsory-suit grounds stated above. This records supported scopes rather than assigning an author or stating a final Court tally.

The affirming remedy permits the bankruptcy court to adjudicate the actual section 523(a)(8) education-debt exception. The statutory category includes the specified governmental or nonprofit-funded education obligations and requires undue hardship to the debtor and dependents before the debt is excepted from nondischargeability. The facts needed for that determination remain ordinary merits work. The dissenting remedy reaches the adversary action against TSAC, not the existence of Hood's underlying Chapter 7 proceeding.

Section 106(a)'s independent restrictions remain: no punitive damages, its costs-and-fees limitation, enforcement consistent with appropriate nonbankruptcy law, and no new substantive cause of action. No proposition here infers consent or expands the proceeding into personal treasury recovery. Later collection or coercive relief is outside the question actually presented and requires its own legal basis.

No material fact has been reconstructed. No unsupported service defect, litigant default, or hardship finding supplies a vote. The existing dispute over whether the adversary procedure itself implicates immunity is resolved in each row rather than left as an undefined factual gap.
