## Holdings

### I. The challenged federal position is sufficiently final

**Controlling proposition:** A claimant need not repeat an application when the responsible government has fixed its position and the established remaining use sufficiently defines the alleged restriction. The fixed opposition and absence of a further useful administrative step make this compensation claim sufficiently final for merits review.

**Authority:** Thomas's opinion, joined in full by all eight other Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Palazzolo requires an adequately defined governmental restriction rather than repetitive applications for their own sake. Here federal processing depended on concurrence from the Army and tribe, whose objections were fixed, and the retained agricultural use was known. The Government identified no remaining administrative step that could materially alter the position; a separate action to compel agency action is not another application necessary to understand the alleged burden. This is a record-based finality determination, not a general excuse from useful application requirements.

### II. Refusal of an unowned route did not appropriate the water right

**Controlling proposition:** Refusing a new right-of-way across federal land does not physically take a claimant's water right where the claimant owns no crossing entitlement and the refusal neither appropriates the water nor restricts the existing lawful water use. Nevada's permission to change the place and manner of use does not itself grant an interest in federal land.

**Authority:** The same unanimous opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** The Nevada right is a protected right to use water. The State Engineer authorized a change in its use and location, while agricultural use remained available and was exercised pending perfection of the proposed municipal use. The United States did not divert water, reduce the quantity available from the ranch, restrict pumping or cut off access to the wells. Dugan and International Paper concern actual interference with water entitlements; they do not make a neighboring owner's refusal to convey a needed route an appropriation. The claimants emphasize that this was the only feasible municipal route and that the federal position preserved regional water conditions. Those circumstances explain the project's dependence on federal land, but establish neither an existing crossing right nor seizure of a quantity of the claimants' water. The property cannot be redefined as a municipal-only entitlement already incorporating that route.

### III. The regulatory and complete-deprivation theories also fail

**Controlling proposition:** This refusal to convey a new federal-land interest establishes no compensable regulatory interference with the claimants' existing water entitlement, and their retained agricultural use defeats the asserted complete-deprivation category. Retained value alone does not defeat a genuine partial-taking claim; here the independent absence of appropriation or a supported restriction on the owned entitlement resolves that claim.

**Authority:** The same unanimous opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** The challenged action concerns access to land the United States owns. The claimants retained pumping, well access, available quantity and lawful agricultural use; the asserted loss arose from inability to obtain the new federal route needed for the more valuable project. The Fifth Amendment does not require another landowner to furnish the missing interest or compensate for refusing it. That actual property-and-action determination is narrower than immunity for everything government calls proprietary.

Palazzolo preserves contextual review when a cognizable regulatory burden exists, and Tahoe-Sierra prevents turning a selected burdened strand into the entire property. Penn Central's combined economic-impact, reasonable-expectations and governmental-character inquiry therefore remains controlling for such claims. The proposed narrower remand correctly observes that useful agriculture alone would not answer every partial-taking theory. It fails here because valuation cannot create the absent crossing entitlement or an unestablished restriction on the water right. The separate lawfulness of permit processing under the APA is outside this compensation question.

## Precedent Treatment

- Palazzolo v. Rhode Island: applied to a definitive governmental position and preserved for the distinction between actual property, complete deprivation and contextual compensation.
- Tahoe-Sierra Preservation Council, Inc. v. Tahoe Regional Planning Agency: applied against defining the hoped-for municipal-use strand as the whole protected interest; its preservation of contextual review remains effective.
- Penn Central Transportation Co. v. New York City: retained for the combined inquiry where a cognizable regulatory burden is established; useful remaining property alone supplies no categorical defense.
- Dugan v. Rank: distinguished because it concerns actual diversion or interference with an owned water entitlement.
- International Paper Co. v. United States: distinguished because it concerns actual diversion or interference with an owned water entitlement.
- Lucas v. South Carolina Coastal Council: its complete-deprivation category is inapplicable to the retained lawful agricultural use.

## Law After Decision

The claim is final enough for adjudication but fails on the owned entitlement and federal action actually established. State authorization of a new water use does not include a federal crossing right. A project dependent on an ungranted route does not create compensation merely from the route's refusal, while ordinary physical and contextual takings protections remain for established burdens on property.
