## Controlling holdings

**1. Concealed facts may be considered under the applicable hearing rule.** For a properly presented habeas claim governed by former law, ordinary negligent failure to develop facts does not bar a necessary federal hearing; Townsend's deliberate-bypass qualification governs. A distinct state claim default or statutory restriction requires its own predicate, and an incomplete state record does not itself establish either.

Keeney v. Tamayo-Reyes retained that former-law rule. Banks presented the Farr suppression claim to the state courts; the later evidence supplied concealed facts supporting it. He questioned Farr and sought disclosure, while the State's assurances and uncorrected denials concealed Farr's paid relationship. This is not deliberate bypass. Texas's diligence argument identifies neither an independent adequate state claim bar nor a transformation of the existing claim into a different one. Vasquez v. Hillery distinguishes factual supplementation from such a new claim, and Coleman v. Thompson concerns an actual independent procedural default.

Carriger v. Stewart preserves former merits law for this pending capital application while reserving the temporal reach of section 2254(e)(2). That general temporal question remains unnecessary here: Michael Wayne Williams v. Taylor requires applicant-attributed lack of diligence before the statutory restriction operates, and the supported concealment and reasonable efforts defeat that trigger as well. Strickler v. Greene explains why reasonable reliance on disclosure representations is relevant; it does not require a freestanding cause ruling where no separate default is established.

**2. Farr's concealed paid role is material to the death sentence.** Suppressed favorable evidence is material when its supported lawful uses, considered with the whole relevant record, create a reasonable probability of a different lawful result sufficient to undermine confidence in that adjudication. The prisoner must make that showing; once a material Brady violation is established, no additional harmless-error showing is required.

Farr's paid cooperation and role in bringing about the gun-retrieval encounter change the future-dangerousness presentation. Timely disclosure would have supported investigation, preparation and examination about why the encounter occurred, as well as impeachment of the denials of cooperation. It would have undermined the impression that Farr independently corroborated Banks's willingness to commit future armed robberies. The denials and disclosure assurances reinforced the misleading account, while the defense's attempted impeachment was weakened by the limited credibility of its witnesses.

Kyles v. Whitley requires consideration of these lawful uses together, rather than an isolated credibility deduction or a test of whether enough aggravating evidence remains. Strickler requires separate attention to confidence in the penalty decision. The murder, the pistol assault described by Jefferson and Banks's acknowledged willingness to obtain a gun remain serious evidence. They do not make the concealed origin and purpose of the Farr encounter merely cumulative. The inquiry concerns the jury's lawful death-selection decision, not simply eligibility or proof of guilt. The complete record establishes materiality and requires a conditional sentence writ.

**3. Cook's transcript claim meets both certificate requirements.** When a habeas claim is rejected on a procedural pleading ground, an issue-specific certificate requires reasonable debate both about the validity of the constitutional claim and about the procedural ruling. Banks satisfies both requirements concerning the concealed Cook rehearsal transcript and whether the federal litigation tried that claim by implied consent.

Slack v. McDaniel supplies both conditions, and Miller-El v. Cockrell distinguishes threshold debatability from ultimate entitlement. The transcript raises a substantial suppression question about the preparation of an important guilt witness. Its receipt in evidence, the prosecutor's examination about coaching and disclosure, and the ensuing suppression submissions support reasonable disagreement about the pleading ruling. Rule 15(b) requires examination of what the parties actually litigated; admission of evidence without objection alone is not consent to try every claim involving it. The appellate court must decide the actual pleading and constitutional questions under the governing rules.

## II. Farr suppression and sentence relief

**Rule common to all eight:** Brady, Bagley and Kyles require favorable suppression and a reasonable probability sufficient to undermine confidence in the relevant lawful result. The evidence's legitimate uses in investigation, preparation, corroboration and impeachment are considered together, rather than each item being required to carry the burden alone. Strickler requires a separate penalty inquiry; sufficient proof of capital guilt or an eligibility circumstance does not itself settle death selection. Once a material Brady violation is established, Kyles permits no second duplicative harmlessness hurdle.

**Supported record application for the six granting relief:** Farr portrayed the Dallas trip as a shared plan for robberies and said Banks would take care of trouble. His later account identifies payment by the lead investigator, concern over drug charges and his own instigation of a supposed pharmacy robbery to obtain Banks's gun for the investigation. The investigator later acknowledged the informant relationship and $200 payment. The undisclosed facts support more than another general attack on character: they permit the defense to challenge the source, purpose and apparently independent corroborative force of the asserted plan for future violence. Farr's denials that he told the investigator about the Dallas trip remained uncorrected, while the State attacked the two defense impeachment witnesses for possible bias. Disclosure would have permitted a materially different presentation about Farr's own incentives and creation of the encounter.

The remaining record is serious. Whitehead was shot three times; the conviction and crime circumstances remain part of the penalty assessment. Jefferson described Banks striking him with a gun and threatening him. Banks personally admitted striking Jefferson and traveling to obtain the gun; he denied an intention to participate in robberies but conceded willingness to supply Farr a means and possible weapon for robbery. Those concessions are not ignored. They nevertheless do not make Farr's disputed account of a continuing shared robbery plan cumulative. The six find that effective disclosure of the specific governmental relationship and instigation creates a reasonable probability of a different lawful penalty result. That conclusion stands on the Farr claim's own permissible disclosure uses and the sentencing record, without adding an independently alleged deficient-assistance violation to complete the constitutional prejudice showing.

**The two denying relief:** Scalia and Thomas regard the admitted gun assistance, the crime circumstances and the separate pistol assault as sufficient to retain confidence after the complete lawful impeachment is considered. Their conclusion is a substantive confidence assessment, not a demand for proof of innocence, legal insufficiency of the remaining evidence or a second harmlessness test. They do not equate eligibility with death selection.

**Historical departure:** Historical Banks used historical Keeney's cause-and-prejudice rule for undeveloped state facts, while the controlling Keeney in this timeline retains Townsend's deliberate-bypass qualification for a properly presented claim. O'Connor authored that rule and Stevens and Kennedy joined it; Souter, Scalia, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer now apply its binding former-law domain alongside their Michael Wayne Williams distinctions. No deliberate bypass or independent adequate state claim default is established. Consequently all eight permit consideration of the developed Farr evidence without importing historical cause as a required holding; Scalia and Thomas's historical reservation and doubts about cause do not obstruct this distinct controlling route.

**Farr merits and principal objection:** The six granting relief preserve the model's complete account: paid cooperation, the witness's instigation to obtain the gun, uncorrected denials and disclosure assurances alter investigation, preparation, impeachment and the apparent independent basis of the future-robbery account. They expressly confront the murder, pistol assault and Banks's gun-facilitation admission. The remaining evidence does not make the concealed explanation of how the encounter arose merely cumulative. Scalia and Thomas accept that legitimate disclosure use is broader than character attack, but find no reasonable probability of a different lawful penalty outcome on the complete record. Neither side equates death eligibility with selection, demands innocence, or adds a second harmlessness burden after materiality.

**Assistance and source limits:** The six receive the entire requested sentence remedy under Farr, so the independent assistance theory cannot enlarge relief. Scalia and Thomas must reach it because they reject Farr, and do so on assumed deficient performance and the complete supported mitigation, including family violence, harsh discipline, skin disease, developmental effects, asserted learning limitations and the later Jefferson account. Their denial reflects whole-record prejudice, not rejection of federal evidence as categorically unexhausted or amended statutory deference. The narrower proved/admitted facts remain those in the model: Jefferson's threat account is attributed testimony; no vote depends on claiming Banks personally admitted every threat. Separate Brady and Strickland analyses are not converted into a new combined constitutional claim.

**Cook:** All eight require the issue-specific certificate because both the constitutional significance of the concealed rehearsal transcript and the procedural implied-consent question are reasonably debatable. The hearing's actual examination about coaching and disclosure and the subsequent suppression submissions support that conclusion; mere admission without objection would not itself establish consent. The certificate does not resolve Rule 15(b), guilt-phase materiality, an alleged bargain or guilt relief. Historical agreement confirms this bounded disposition and does not supply a final amendment holding.

**Remedy:** Six support reversal of the Farr sentence-relief denial and a conditional sentence writ allowing a reasonable period fixed below for a lawful penalty proceeding or replacement with a lawful noncapital sentence. The conviction remains intact on that penalty claim. Scalia and Thomas would affirm denial of both sentence routes but join reversal of the Cook certificate denial. All eight direct the Cook appeal on the identified issue. No invented fixed period, execution date, interim stay or new guilt trial follows. Aside from the identified changed factual-development ground, no material historical departure is retained; no unresolved reconciliation blocker remains.

## Holdings

**1. Concealed facts may be considered under the applicable hearing rule.** For a properly presented habeas claim governed by former law, ordinary negligent failure to develop facts does not bar a necessary federal hearing; Townsend's deliberate-bypass qualification governs. A distinct state claim default or statutory restriction requires its own predicate, and an incomplete state record does not itself establish either.

Keeney v. Tamayo-Reyes retained that former-law rule. Banks presented the Farr suppression claim to the state courts; the later evidence supplied concealed facts supporting it. He questioned Farr and sought disclosure, while the State's assurances and uncorrected denials concealed Farr's paid relationship. This is not deliberate bypass. Texas's diligence argument identifies neither an independent adequate state claim bar nor a transformation of the existing claim into a different one. Vasquez v. Hillery distinguishes factual supplementation from such a new claim, and Coleman v. Thompson concerns an actual independent procedural default.

Carriger v. Stewart preserves former merits law for this pending capital application while reserving the temporal reach of section 2254(e)(2). That general temporal question remains unnecessary here: Michael Wayne Williams v. Taylor requires applicant-attributed lack of diligence before the statutory restriction operates, and the supported concealment and reasonable efforts defeat that trigger as well. Strickler v. Greene explains why reasonable reliance on disclosure representations is relevant; it does not require a freestanding cause ruling where no separate default is established.

**2. Farr's concealed paid role is material to the death sentence.** Suppressed favorable evidence is material when its supported lawful uses, considered with the whole relevant record, create a reasonable probability of a different lawful result sufficient to undermine confidence in that adjudication. The prisoner must make that showing; once a material Brady violation is established, no additional harmless-error showing is required.

Farr's paid cooperation and role in bringing about the gun-retrieval encounter change the future-dangerousness presentation. Timely disclosure would have supported investigation, preparation and examination about why the encounter occurred, as well as impeachment of the denials of cooperation. It would have undermined the impression that Farr independently corroborated Banks's willingness to commit future armed robberies. The denials and disclosure assurances reinforced the misleading account, while the defense's attempted impeachment was weakened by the limited credibility of its witnesses.

Kyles v. Whitley requires consideration of these lawful uses together, rather than an isolated credibility deduction or a test of whether enough aggravating evidence remains. Strickler requires separate attention to confidence in the penalty decision. The murder, the pistol assault described by Jefferson and Banks's acknowledged willingness to obtain a gun remain serious evidence. They do not make the concealed origin and purpose of the Farr encounter merely cumulative. The inquiry concerns the jury's lawful death-selection decision, not simply eligibility or proof of guilt. The complete record establishes materiality and requires a conditional sentence writ.

**3. Cook's transcript claim meets both certificate requirements.** When a habeas claim is rejected on a procedural pleading ground, an issue-specific certificate requires reasonable debate both about the validity of the constitutional claim and about the procedural ruling. Banks satisfies both requirements concerning the concealed Cook rehearsal transcript and whether the federal litigation tried that claim by implied consent.

Slack v. McDaniel supplies both conditions, and Miller-El v. Cockrell distinguishes threshold debatability from ultimate entitlement. The transcript raises a substantial suppression question about the preparation of an important guilt witness. Its receipt in evidence, the prosecutor's examination about coaching and disclosure, and the ensuing suppression submissions support reasonable disagreement about the pleading ruling. Rule 15(b) requires examination of what the parties actually litigated; admission of evidence without objection alone is not consent to try every claim involving it. The appellate court must decide the actual pleading and constitutional questions under the governing rules.

**Authority:** Souter's opinion is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer. Scalia and Thomas join Holdings 1 and3 and their procedural consequences. Holding 2 and the Farr sentence remedy therefore have seven direct joins; the factual-development and Cook certificate holdings have nine. The seven granting full sentence relief do not reach the alternative penalty-assistance ground because it seeks the same complete remedy.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Townsend v. Sain and Keeney v. Tamayo-Reyes:** Applied for the deliberate-bypass qualification governing necessary factual development of a properly presented former-law claim.
- **Lindh v. Murphy and Carriger v. Stewart:** Applied to the pending application's former merits framework; Carriger's separate capital hearing-transition question retains its reserved status.
- **Michael Wayne Williams v. Taylor:** Applied for the applicant-attributed-failure trigger, which concealment and reasonable efforts do not establish merely because later discovery succeeds.
- **Vasquez v. Hillery and Coleman v. Thompson:** Applied to distinguish supplementation of a presented claim from a new claim or an actual independent state procedural default.
- **Brady v. Maryland, Kyles v. Whitley and Strickler v. Greene:** Applied to favorable suppression, cumulative lawful uses, phase-specific materiality and reasonable reliance on disclosure representations.
- **Slack v. McDaniel, Miller-El v. Cockrell and Hohn v. United States:** Applied for the two-part procedural certificate standard, its threshold character and review of certificate denial.
- **Strickland v. Washington and Terry Williams v. Taylor:** Retain their independent assistance standards; the Court's complete Farr sentence remedy makes an additional ruling on that alternative unnecessary.

## Law After Decision

Former-law factual development is governed by the actual deliberate-bypass rule, with separate defaults and statutory restrictions assessed under their own triggers. Concealed informant evidence must be evaluated through all supported lawful uses in the relevant penalty record. A material suppression violation entitles Banks to the stated sentence remedy. A procedural certificate requires both constitutional and procedural debatability, and grants appellate access at the identified issue-specific scope.

## Separate Writings

**Thomas, joined by Scalia, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** They join the ruling permitting consideration of the developed evidence and the Cook certificate disposition. They reject Farr materiality after considering disclosure's investigative and impeachment uses: the murder, the independent pistol-assault account and Banks's admitted gun assistance leave them confident in the penalty result despite Farr's concealed role.
