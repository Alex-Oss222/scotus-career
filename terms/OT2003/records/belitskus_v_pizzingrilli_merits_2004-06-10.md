### Holdings

### I. Recurring candidate and party injuries remain justiciable

**Holding and operative rule:** Completion of an election does not moot a challenge to a ballot-access condition when the injury is sufficiently likely to recur against the claimant and the election cycle is too short for full review. The established recurring injuries support adjudication for Stith, Linzey and the Pennsylvania Green Party; Donovan's individual claim must be dismissed because the required likelihood of recurrence to him is absent.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Part I, joined by all nine Justices, controls the distinct threshold dispositions.

**Controlling explanation:** Candidate-specific exclusion and the party's continuing nomination interests supply concrete injuries, rather than a generalized objection to the fee policy. The record supports recurrence for the two candidates and the continuing party. Schrader v. Blackwell applies the capable-of-repetition exception to continuing party injury in elections too short for complete litigation, and Timmons v. Twin Cities Area New Party recognizes the continuing nomination interest. Donovan's completed-election injury lacks the corresponding likelihood of personal recurrence. The live party claim therefore preserves adjudication of its own injury without preserving every voter's past claim.

### II. Actual inability to pay requires a usable alternative

**Holding and operative rule:** A State may not make payment of a filing fee the exclusive ballot-access route for an otherwise qualified candidate who cannot pay without financial hardship. Actual exclusion on that basis imposes a severe burden under Anderson–Burdick and Cartwright and requires narrow tailoring to a compelling interest; a realistically usable nonmonetary route or hardship mechanism must preserve access for the financially excluded candidate.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Part II is joined by all nine Justices and controls the constitutional rule and its application.

**Controlling explanation:** Bullock v. Carter rejects wealth-based exclusion unsupported by the proposed screening rationale, and Lubin v. Panish requires another route for candidates unable to pay. Anderson v. Celebrezze and Burdick v. Takushi direct attention to the actual burden and the State's precise interests; Cartwright v. Barnes states the calibrated framework within which outright financial exclusion is severe. Ability to pay does not establish electoral support, and administrative expense does not justify excluding a qualified candidate where a meaningful support-based or hardship route serves the legitimate objective. The hardship affidavits and resulting preliminary access support the claims of Stith and Linzey, and the mandatory policy affects the party's established recurring interests.

Reasonable evidence of financial hardship may be required. Actual inability to pay differs from inconvenience or a preference to avoid the fee. A proposed alternative must be usable in its real deadlines and requirements. Reasonable fees for candidates able to pay and independent lawful qualification or support requirements remain available. Those qualifications answer the concern that the inability-to-pay rule prevents ordinary screening; they do not allow the screening rationale to substitute wealth for support.

### III. The injunction must cure the proved candidate and party injuries

**Holding and operative rule:** In this as-applied action, the injunction must specify concrete hardship conditions and official duties sufficient to remedy the established injuries of the candidates and the continuing party. A categorical cap at Stith and Linzey cannot stand without securing the party's proved recurring injury; the district court must fit the order's scope to that remedial need and allow a meaningful lawful alternative selected by the State.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Part III, joined by Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, controls both the five-vote vacatur and the remedial rule by direct majority support.

**Controlling explanation:** The injunction's enforceable reach differs from the constitutional rule's precedential reach. Rule 65(d) requires an order stating the actual protected conditions and the acts officials must perform, so the district court's general command requires precise replacement. But limiting relief to two names may leave the successful party subject to the same established nomination injury. The contrary remedial position treats those two candidacies as adequate protection on the current record. The party's independently established recurring claim makes that categorical restriction insufficient without an injury-specific inquiry into the effective cure.

The district court must determine the concrete reach needed for those successful plaintiffs and state a workable hardship or alternative-access mechanism in definite terms. Relief extending to other candidates requires a demonstrated connection to curing the proved candidate and party injuries. The State retains the choice among meaningful lawful alternatives. This remand performs factual and remedial work under the settled inability-to-pay rule and Rule 65(d), with the resulting injunction defined by the proven operation of the policy and the cure that operation requires.

## Holdings

### I. Recurring candidate and party injuries remain justiciable

**Holding and operative rule:** Completion of an election does not moot a challenge to a ballot-access condition when the injury is sufficiently likely to recur against the claimant and the election cycle is too short for full review. The established recurring injuries support adjudication for Stith, Linzey and the Pennsylvania Green Party; Donovan's individual claim must be dismissed because the required likelihood of recurrence to him is absent.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Part I, joined by all nine Justices, controls the distinct threshold dispositions.

**Controlling explanation:** Candidate-specific exclusion and the party's continuing nomination interests supply concrete injuries, rather than a generalized objection to the fee policy. The record supports recurrence for the two candidates and the continuing party. Schrader v. Blackwell applies the capable-of-repetition exception to continuing party injury in elections too short for complete litigation, and Timmons v. Twin Cities Area New Party recognizes the continuing nomination interest. Donovan's completed-election injury lacks the corresponding likelihood of personal recurrence. The live party claim therefore preserves adjudication of its own injury without preserving every voter's past claim.

### II. Actual inability to pay requires a usable alternative

**Holding and operative rule:** A State may not make payment of a filing fee the exclusive ballot-access route for an otherwise qualified candidate who cannot pay without financial hardship. Actual exclusion on that basis imposes a severe burden under Anderson–Burdick and Cartwright and requires narrow tailoring to a compelling interest; a realistically usable nonmonetary route or hardship mechanism must preserve access for the financially excluded candidate.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Part II is joined by all nine Justices and controls the constitutional rule and its application.

**Controlling explanation:** Bullock v. Carter rejects wealth-based exclusion unsupported by the proposed screening rationale, and Lubin v. Panish requires another route for candidates unable to pay. Anderson v. Celebrezze and Burdick v. Takushi direct attention to the actual burden and the State's precise interests; Cartwright v. Barnes states the calibrated framework within which outright financial exclusion is severe. Ability to pay does not establish electoral support, and administrative expense does not justify excluding a qualified candidate where a meaningful support-based or hardship route serves the legitimate objective. The hardship affidavits and resulting preliminary access support the claims of Stith and Linzey, and the mandatory policy affects the party's established recurring interests.

Reasonable evidence of financial hardship may be required. Actual inability to pay differs from inconvenience or a preference to avoid the fee. A proposed alternative must be usable in its real deadlines and requirements. Reasonable fees for candidates able to pay and independent lawful qualification or support requirements remain available. Those qualifications answer the concern that the inability-to-pay rule prevents ordinary screening; they do not allow the screening rationale to substitute wealth for support.

### III. The injunction must cure the proved candidate and party injuries

**Holding and operative rule:** In this as-applied action, the injunction must specify concrete hardship conditions and official duties sufficient to remedy the established injuries of the candidates and the continuing party. A categorical cap at Stith and Linzey cannot stand without securing the party's proved recurring injury; the district court must fit the order's scope to that remedial need and allow a meaningful lawful alternative selected by the State.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Part III, joined by Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, controls both the five-vote vacatur and the remedial rule by direct majority support.

**Controlling explanation:** The injunction's enforceable reach differs from the constitutional rule's precedential reach. Rule 65(d) requires an order stating the actual protected conditions and the acts officials must perform, so the district court's general command requires precise replacement. But limiting relief to two names may leave the successful party subject to the same established nomination injury. The contrary remedial position treats those two candidacies as adequate protection on the current record. The party's independently established recurring claim makes that categorical restriction insufficient without an injury-specific inquiry into the effective cure.

The district court must determine the concrete reach needed for those successful plaintiffs and state a workable hardship or alternative-access mechanism in definite terms. Relief extending to other candidates requires a demonstrated connection to curing the proved candidate and party injuries. The State retains the choice among meaningful lawful alternatives. This remand performs factual and remedial work under the settled inability-to-pay rule and Rule 65(d), with the resulting injunction defined by the proven operation of the policy and the cure that operation requires.

## Precedent Treatment

- Bullock v. Carter and Lubin v. Panish are reaffirmed at their actual inability-to-pay scope: electoral access cannot be conditioned exclusively on an unaffordable fee without a realistic alternative.
- Anderson v. Celebrezze, Burdick v. Takushi and Cartwright v. Barnes are applied to the actual burden, justification and fit; the exclusion here falls within their severe-burden rule.
- Timmons v. Twin Cities Area New Party is applied to the party's concrete nomination interest and the need for effective protection of it.
- Schrader v. Blackwell is applied to the continuing party's recurring election injury and distinguished from Donovan's unsupported recurrence claim.

## Law After Decision

The inability-to-pay protection governs otherwise qualified candidates actually excluded by an unaffordable mandatory fee, together with the associated party and voter interests. Reasonable proof and lawful qualifications remain enforceable, but the alternative must work in practice. A remedy in an as-applied action must be concrete and fitted to the successful plaintiffs' actual injuries, including an established recurring party injury; its enforceable reach requires that remedial connection.

## Separate Writings

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela joins the threshold and inability-to-pay holdings and agrees that the two-name cap must be set aside, but would reverse it and require a policy remedy for all otherwise qualified candidates who establish inability to pay without material financial hardship. He would place on officials the burden to show that a narrower remedy fully cures the proved mandatory policy.

Under his proposed proof rule, a claimant establishes inability by a preponderance through a sworn account of available income, reasonably available assets, necessary living expenses and dependent obligations. Material hardship means sacrificing ordinary necessities or assuming an unreasonable financial burden, rather than merely preferring not to pay. Poverty-line status would be neither necessary nor conclusive. Officials could verify a material factual dispute but could not demand unavailable records or postpone a decision beyond the relevant ballot deadline. Bullock and Lubin supply the inability-to-pay protection, while Cartwright's calibrated review governs the resulting burden and justification; the preliminary hardship procedure shows that an administrable mechanism is possible without dispensing with evidence for a disputed claim.

He would require a published hardship application and objective decision criteria before the next relevant filing deadline, identification of the deciding official, written reasons for denial and prompt review early enough for a successful candidate to reach the ballot. A petition alternative would count only if its deadlines and support requirements were realistically usable. The district court would set dates tied to the election calendar and provide provisional filing when official delay would otherwise defeat relief. Officials would accept qualifying filings and maintain reasons sufficient for review, while the court retained enforcement authority. Independent lawful qualifications would remain controlling, and candidates able to pay would receive no automatic exemption. He would preserve the claimant-specific dispositions and any independently available fee award. These are his proposed additional proof and remedial rules; the operative vacatur and instructions are those in the Court's Part III.

Justice O'Connor, joined by Justices Scalia and Thomas, joins Parts I and II but would affirm the present injunction limiting enforceable relief to Stith and Linzey upon demonstrated hardship. The constitutional rule protects every qualifying financial-exclusion claim through precedent, but this action was litigated as applied. In their view the current candidacies define the established need for an injunction; the party's standing alone does not establish that a wider order is necessary. They would permit enlargement after a concrete determination that distinct proved party injury remains unremedied by the existing terms. They agree that Rule 65(d) requires specific conditions and official duties and that a realistic alternative must protect a candidate whose inability is shown.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns for a specific injunction protecting the proved candidate and party interests through a usable hardship or nonmonetary route. The district court must determine the necessary scope, define the financial-hardship condition and responsible officials' concrete duties, and preserve the State's choice among effective lawful alternatives. Donovan's individual claim is dismissed as moot.
