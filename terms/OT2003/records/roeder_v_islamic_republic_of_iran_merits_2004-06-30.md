## Public Projection

## Event

Roeder v. Islamic Republic of Iran; No. 03-1147. Decided June 30, 2004, on review of the D.C. Circuit's affirmance of dismissal, 333 F.3d 228. Former hostages and their spouses and children seek recovery against Iran and its Ministry of Foreign Affairs for the November 1979 seizure and 444-day detention. The January 19, 1981 Algiers Accords secured release and included the United States' undertaking to bar the covered claims. An August 2001 default addressed liability, while damages remained unresolved. The United States intervened in October 2001 to protect its agreement-related interest. Congress then amended the sovereign-immunity exception for this litigation and corrected its case designation. The question is whether those enactments also displaced the Accords' independent bar and required preservation of the default, including the necessary application of independent-adjudication principles to this pending case.

## Holdings

### I. The immunity amendments leave the independent claim bar in place

**Holding and operative rule:** A statute removing a specified sovereign-immunity obstacle extinguishes an independent bar to the plaintiff's claim only if its enacted text or necessary legal effect accomplishes that further displacement; legislative explanations cannot supply an omitted operative rule or direct entry of judgment. Section 626(c) changes the state-sponsor-designation condition for this identified litigation and §208 corrects its designation, but neither displaces the Accords' covered-claim prohibition; the claims against Iran and its Ministry therefore remain barred.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's unanimous opinion directly controls this holding and the following two holdings.

**Controlling explanation:** The amendment does real legal work. Section 626(c) of Public Law 107-77 inserts this designated litigation into former §1605(a)(7)(A), removing the state-sponsor-designation obstacle. Section 208 of Public Law 107-117 corrects the judge's initials in that case designation. Neither enactment supplies a rule inconsistent with the Accords' separate prohibition of these hostage-related claims.

The plaintiffs emphasize that Congress knew of the Government's objection, deliberately identified this suit and expressed a desire to preserve their judgment. That context supports taking the enactments seriously; it cannot change their subject from immunity to every obstacle to recovery. Removal of one legal barrier is meaningful even when another remains decisive. An explanatory statement favoring recovery does not itself enact abrogation, liability or a judgment.

Trans World Airlines v. Franklin Mint, Cook v. United States and Weinberger v. Rossi require a genuine legislative basis for displacing international commitments. No talismanic phrase is necessary: courts examine the operative text and its necessary legal effect. Congress may change domestic law concerning an executive agreement within its constitutional authority. These enactments simply do not accomplish the additional displacement asserted here. Dames & Moore v. Regan sustains the relevant claims-settlement framework within its authorization and historical-practice setting, rather than establishing an executive power to disregard later legislation.

The Ministry is Iran itself for these covered claims, so naming it does not avoid the same bar. That independent ground completely resolves the presented statutory-abrogation question. The broader Flatow cause-of-action objection is unnecessary because resolving it could not overcome the surviving bar.

### II. Intervention, default and payment each retain their independent conditions

**Holding and operative rule:** The United States may protect its own concrete agreement-related interest through authorized intervention and judicial reconsideration; the foreign state's nonappearance neither forfeits that interest nor establishes entitlement to judgment. Under §1608(e), the claimant must establish the claim or right to relief by evidence satisfactory to the court, and a payment provision conditioned on a qualifying judgment supplies payment authority only after a legally supportable judgment exists.

**Controlling explanation:** The Government undertook its own obligation to bar these claims. Its intervention after learning of the conflict protects that obligation, and Iran's nonappearance does not adequately protect the Government's interest. On this record, its timely intervention and request for judicial reconsideration were proper. The courts independently considered the legal bar under the procedural rules, including the Rule 60(b) motion as presented.

Argentine Republic v. Amerada Hess and Republic of Austria v. Altmann distinguish the FSIA's adjudicative-immunity framework from substantive entitlement. The claimant must establish the invoked exception and the separate right to relief. Section 1608(e) preserves that latter inquiry even upon default. The Accords defeat the covered claim despite the amended immunity condition. Public Law 106-386's judgment-payment mechanism presupposes the qualifying judgment and does not create the missing entitlement to obtain one.

### III. The amendments change law for a pending case and leave adjudication to the courts

**Holding and operative rule:** Congress may enact a new legal rule governing a pending controversy, including an identifiable controversy, subject to its lawful temporal reach and constitutional limits, while courts independently apply the rule to facts and claims. The present amendments fall on that pending-case side of Plaut's distinction: the unresolved-damages liability default did not finally dispose of the action, and the enacted immunity change prescribes neither findings nor liability. Unenacted explanations cannot become binding adjudicative commands.

**Controlling explanation:** Robertson v. Seattle Audubon Society permits genuine governing-law changes in pending litigation while preserving independent judicial application. United States v. Klein protects the adjudicative function from legislative prescription of findings or liability. The distinction concerns what a statute does, not merely whether its beneficiaries or case can be identified.

Plaut v. Spendthrift Farm supplies the completed-judgment rule: once a private judgment becomes final through the ordinary judicial process, Congress may not, solely by changing the previously applicable law, require its vacatur and reinstate the same litigation. Later independent adjudication of eligibility and merits cannot restore the conclusiveness of the displaced judgment. That rule preserves Congress's authority to change law while a case remains pending and preserves constitutionally permissible judicial relief, including valid Rule 60(b) grounds; Klein and McCullough v. Virginia continue to forbid dictated findings and predetermined liability.

Here damages remained unresolved when Public Laws 107-77 and 107-117 were enacted. The liability default therefore was not the completed private judgment Plaut protects. The provisions remove the specified immunity obstacle and correct the designation while leaving courts to apply the remaining law. Judicial reconsideration on the Government's motion is likewise distinct from a legislative command reopening a completed action.

Miller v. French and Plaut's Wheeling Bridge qualification distinguish new law governing future enforcement of continuing relief from displacement of completed private judgments; meaningful adjudication of the governing predicates remains necessary. Landgraf v. USI Film Products separately addresses temporal operation: express retrospectivity does not itself establish constitutional validity. Morgan Stanley's affirmance by an equally divided Court supplies no contrary rule.

The asserted broader command to preserve liability appears in explanations, not the enactments. Applying the actual amendments therefore preserves independent adjudication and leaves the Accords bar decisive.
