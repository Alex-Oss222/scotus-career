## Controlling holdings

**1. The warrant must identify what the officers are authorized to seize.** A warrant for a home search that identifies no things to be seized and incorporates no document identifying them fails the Fourth Amendment's particularity requirement, and conducting this home search under that wholly deficient authorization violates the Fourth Amendment. The preparing officer's knowledge of an application does not supply the judicial limitation omitted from the warrant.

The warrant described the premises where its form called for the objects of the search. It listed no items. The separate application and affidavit described them, but the warrant neither incorporated those documents nor brought their terms into the authorization. The warrant therefore failed on its face. Particularity protects the homeowner and limits execution through a judicial command; an officer's intended restraint is not a substitute for that command.

Wilson v. Layne explains that a warrant's authorized scope limits execution. The particularity text itself makes the omitted description essential. The contrary contention that a reasonable search can be reconstructed from the application and actual restraint would replace the warrant's missing limitation with the officer's private understanding. The disputed assertion of an oral explanation cannot support summary judgment against the homeowners and would not rewrite the judicial authorization in any event.

**2. The preparing officer cannot reasonably rely on this obvious complete omission.** Qualified immunity does not protect the officer who prepared and executed this warrant when its complete failure to identify any items made the constitutional defect apparent under law existing at the time of the search. The magistrate's signature does not make objectively reasonable the preparer's reliance on an omission he was responsible for supplying.

United States v. Leon had already identified a warrant so facially deficient in describing things to be seized as outside reasonable reliance. The Fourth Amendment's express demand and that preconduct authority supplied notice in 1997. Massachusetts v. Sheppard involved reliance on a judge's undertaking to correct a defective form after the officer brought the problem to the judge; it does not excuse this complete omission by the preparing officer. The apparent clerical origin of an error does not determine whether executing the resulting warrant was objectively reasonable.

Hope v. Pelzer describes the current notice inquiry, but its later date supplies no retroactive warning for 1997 conduct. The controlling notice here comes from the text and existing authority, not a requirement that an earlier case reproduce every feature of this form. The refusal to grant immunity permits ordinary adjudication of the reviewed individual claim.

## Precedent Treatment

- **United States v. Leon:** Applied for the preconduct proposition that an obvious facial failure to specify items defeats objectively reasonable reliance.
- **Massachusetts v. Sheppard:** Distinguished by the officer's reliance there on the judge's undertaking to correct the form; Groh prepared the complete omission at issue here.
- **Wilson v. Layne:** Applied for the relationship between judicial authorization and execution, without treating its later date as notice for 1997 conduct.
- **Hope v. Pelzer:** Applied as the governing notice method, while the required warning is supplied by the constitutional text and preconduct authority.

## Law After Decision

The judicial warrant must supply its particularized authorization. An unincorporated application and the preparer's private knowledge do not cure a complete omission of items to be seized. The responsible preparer receives no qualified immunity for executing the obviously deficient warrant on these accepted facts; the individual damages claim proceeds under ordinary liability and remedy rules.

## Separate Writings

**Kennedy, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** He agrees that the warrant violated particularity. He would nevertheless grant immunity because the detailed application, magistrate's approval and clerical character of the omission made the officer's mistaken reliance objectively reasonable in the legal circumstances. He distinguishes the existence of a defect from sufficiently clear notice imposing individual liability.

**Thomas, joined by Scalia, dissenting.** They resist treating a defective warrant as automatically making the execution an unreasonable warrantless search. On the officer's asserted account of a confined search following the detailed application, they would examine the actual reasonableness of execution rather than make the form defect conclusive. That account remains an assertion, including the disputed oral explanation. Independently, they would grant qualified immunity even assuming a constitutional violation, because the application and judicial approval leave sufficient uncertainty about individual liability. The Court's denial of immunity therefore conflicts with both their alternative merits approach and their separate notice ground.
