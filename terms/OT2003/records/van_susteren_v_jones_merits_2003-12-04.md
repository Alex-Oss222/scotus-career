## Controlling holdings

### I. This prospective separation requirement reasonably regulates primary access

**Controlling proposition:** A State may require a reasonable prospective period of disaffiliation before entry into another party’s official primary when the complete system preserves realistic access and the condition proportionately serves identified interests in party integrity and orderly selection. On this record, California’s twelve-month prefiling requirement imposes a real but less-than-severe, nondiscriminatory burden justified by its direct connection to stable nomination routes and prevention of opportunistic switching.

**Authority:** Thomas’s opinion, joined by all eight other Justices, controls both holdings.

**Controlling explanation:** Burdick requires assessment of the actual injury, the State’s precise interests and their fit, and Cartwright states the levels of that calibrated scale: severe burdens require narrow tailoring to a compelling interest; substantial burdens require an important objective and a demonstrated substantial relationship; reasonable, nondiscriminatory restrictions may rest on proportionate important regulatory interests. Van Susteren loses his desired immediate nomination opportunity. Continued advocacy or a theoretical alternative route does not erase that injury. But the prospective separation rule permits ordinary entry after the neutral period and structures movement into a primary before its competitive selection. Its operation does not make the official process realistically inaccessible in the system presented.

Storer’s approval of a one-year separation condition for independent candidates supplies a close preventive analogy, rather than an automatic answer about party primaries. Like Storer’s sore-loser and disaffiliation rule, this neutral prospective period regulates movement between nomination routes before selection rather than denying an existing nomination or access to the ballot, so it falls at the ordinary level and needs no demonstrated substantial relationship. Timmons is distinct: it requires meaningful official recognition of a consenting candidate whom a qualified party has already nominated, and it left sore-loser rules undecided. Tashjian and Eu protect actual associational choice, while Jones protects an objecting party from compelled outsider control of its nominee selection. The present condition does not replace that choosing electorate or dictate party governance. The objection that a willing candidate and party should be able to associate immediately therefore does not eliminate every proportionate entry rule. The direct relationship between prior separation and stable selection supports this condition without a finding that Van Susteren personally planned a raid. The holding concerns this duration and nomination system; materially different periods, discriminatory waivers or restrictions imposed after candidacy has matured require their own burden-and-fit assessment. A materially different party-endorsed candidacy record and the separate Qualifications Clause claim are outside the granted question.

### II. The calendar difference follows the distinct selection routes

**Controlling proposition:** The approximately twenty-three-month and thirteen-month intervals before the general election asserted for partisan and independent candidates do not establish an equal-protection violation here. The primary and independent routes have distinct operative selection stages, and requiring separation before the relevant prefiling stage reasonably serves the rule’s identified nomination interests.

**Authority:** The same unanimous opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** The comparison identifies a genuine calendar difference. It cannot be rejected merely by labeling one candidate partisan and the other independent. But measuring solely backward from the general election omits the earlier primary at which the party’s official selection occurs. The requirement’s relation to that selection and its filing process explains the different general-election intervals. **P1 — association.** Apply Anderson–Burdick to the actual burden, precise state interests and fit in the whole nomination system. ## Holdings

### I. This prospective separation requirement reasonably regulates primary access

**Controlling proposition:** A State may require a reasonable prospective period of disaffiliation before entry into another party’s official primary when the complete system preserves realistic access and the condition proportionately serves identified interests in party integrity and orderly selection. On this record, California’s twelve-month prefiling requirement imposes a real but less-than-severe, nondiscriminatory burden justified by its direct connection to stable nomination routes and prevention of opportunistic switching.

**Authority:** Thomas’s opinion, joined by all eight other Justices, controls both holdings.

**Controlling explanation:** Burdick requires assessment of the actual injury, the State’s precise interests and their fit, and Cartwright states the levels of that calibrated scale: severe burdens require narrow tailoring to a compelling interest; substantial burdens require an important objective and a demonstrated substantial relationship; reasonable, nondiscriminatory restrictions may rest on proportionate important regulatory interests. Van Susteren loses his desired immediate nomination opportunity. Continued advocacy or a theoretical alternative route does not erase that injury. But the prospective separation rule permits ordinary entry after the neutral period and structures movement into a primary before its competitive selection. Its operation does not make the official process realistically inaccessible in the system presented.

Storer’s approval of a one-year separation condition for independent candidates supplies a close preventive analogy, rather than an automatic answer about party primaries. Like Storer’s sore-loser and disaffiliation rule, this neutral prospective period regulates movement between nomination routes before selection rather than denying an existing nomination or access to the ballot, so it falls at the ordinary level and needs no demonstrated substantial relationship. Timmons is distinct: it requires meaningful official recognition of a consenting candidate whom a qualified party has already nominated, and it left sore-loser rules undecided. Tashjian and Eu protect actual associational choice, while Jones protects an objecting party from compelled outsider control of its nominee selection. The present condition does not replace that choosing electorate or dictate party governance. The objection that a willing candidate and party should be able to associate immediately therefore does not eliminate every proportionate entry rule. The direct relationship between prior separation and stable selection supports this condition without a finding that Van Susteren personally planned a raid. The holding concerns this duration and nomination system; materially different periods, discriminatory waivers or restrictions imposed after candidacy has matured require their own burden-and-fit assessment. A materially different party-endorsed candidacy record and the separate Qualifications Clause claim are outside the granted question.

### II. The calendar difference follows the distinct selection routes

**Controlling proposition:** The approximately twenty-three-month and thirteen-month intervals before the general election asserted for partisan and independent candidates do not establish an equal-protection violation here. The primary and independent routes have distinct operative selection stages, and requiring separation before the relevant prefiling stage reasonably serves the rule’s identified nomination interests.

**Authority:** The same unanimous opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** The comparison identifies a genuine calendar difference. It cannot be rejected merely by labeling one candidate partisan and the other independent. But measuring solely backward from the general election omits the earlier primary at which the party’s official selection occurs. The requirement’s relation to that selection and its filing process explains the different general-election intervals. Burdick’s complete-system inquiry and Storer’s separation rationale support that functional comparison. The Court sustains the difference because of its role in the actual routes, rather than assuming equal electoral prospects or treating the association interest as unprotected.
