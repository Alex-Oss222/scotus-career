## Controlling holdings

**A discovery request does not change the rule for evidence of unknown exculpatory value.** When destroyed evidence was only potentially useful, due process requires a showing of police bad faith. A pending discovery request or the evidence's importance to the prosecution does not dispense with that requirement; evidence whose exculpatory character was apparent before destruction and for which reasonably available comparable evidence is lacking remains governed by Trombetta's distinct rule.

**Authority:** The per curiam opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Those eight Justices adopt the rule, its application and the remand; Stevens concurs in the judgment on the ground stated separately.

Arizona v. Youngblood distinguishes evidence known to be exculpatory from material that might help the defense if further tested. California v. Trombetta supplies the former category's apparent-exculpatory-value and comparable-evidence requirements. The material here tested positive for cocaine four times before its destruction. That history does not establish that a later defense test would have been exculpatory, and the record contains no allegation or finding of bad faith. Fisher therefore has not satisfied the federal condition applicable to the lost testing opportunity.

The contrary rule would treat the request itself, or the central place of the evidence in the prosecution, as a substitute for the missing constitutional condition. Neither fact supplies the material's unknown exculpatory character. A request may bear on an actual inquiry into knowledge and purpose; it creates no automatic exception. Kyles v. Whitley addresses suppression of favorable evidence and its cumulative lawful uses, not a presumption that destroyed testing material had favorable contents. The exceptional-unfairness concern expressed in the concurrence supplies no basis for sustaining this federal dismissal on the present facts.

## Event

**Illinois v. Fisher, No. 03-374.** Decided February 23, 2004, on the submitted record. Review of the Illinois Appellate Court's reversal of Fisher's cocaine-possession conviction. The question is whether a pending discovery request or the central importance of destroyed, potentially useful evidence excuses the absence of police bad faith.

The police destroyed the substance under their ordinary procedures after four positive cocaine tests. Fisher had requested production before his prolonged absence from the proceedings. The state appellate judgment relied on federal due process.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated in submission and decision.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Arizona v. Youngblood:** Applied to potentially useful evidence; a request or centrality alone does not displace its bad-faith requirement.
- **California v. Trombetta:** Preserved for apparently exculpatory evidence lacking reasonably available comparable evidence.
- **Kyles v. Whitley:** Distinguished because its suppression and cumulative-materiality rules presuppose favorable evidence, rather than establish the result of an unperformed test.

## Law After Decision

The federal preservation inquiry continues to turn on the evidence category and its applicable conditions. A discovery request may be relevant proof of bad faith, but neither the request nor importance automatically changes the potentially-useful-evidence rule. States may enforce independent preservation and discovery protections under their own law.

## Separate Writings

**Stevens, concurring in the judgment.** Stevens retains the position expressed in his Youngblood concurrence that destruction of evidence could make a trial fundamentally unfair in an exceptional case despite the absence of bad faith. He does not join an unconditional bad-faith rule covering every conceivable loss. Four inculpatory tests and the ordinary destruction shown here do not establish the exceptional unfairness he would require, so he agrees that this federal ground cannot support the judgment. Independent state discovery law remains available on its own terms.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns to the Illinois courts for the preserved state-law inquiry and further proceedings consistent with the federal rule.
