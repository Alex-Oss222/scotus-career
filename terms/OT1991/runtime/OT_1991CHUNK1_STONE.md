# CASE 1 of 10: UNITED STATES v. IBARRA
# SECTION II: STONE POSITION SUPPLEMENT

## A. STATUS

* **Version:** 1.0
* **Approval Status:** Proposed and unapproved. No approved case-specific Stone position exists for this matter; this is newly drafted material and is not represented as previously approved.
* **Current-Through Baseline:** Historical law effective immediately before October 15, 1991.
* **Conditions:** None.

## B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

* **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Grant the petition; grant respondent's motion to proceed in forma pauperis; vacate the judgment of the Court of Appeals; remand.
* **Stone's Remedy and Remand Position:** Remand for the Tenth Circuit to hear and decide the government's suppression appeal. No remedial doctrine is required or reached. Stone expresses no view on the suppression ruling.

## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Date from which the government's 30-day appeal period runs

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** This is the only question presented, and it disposes of the case. Nothing beyond it is reached.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply the received doctrine unchanged. The governing procedural sources, Rule 4(b) and § 3731, fix timing, decisionmaker, and finality, and under Stone's treatment of procedural questions the form of the rule is supplied by the procedural source rather than by a balance of interests. Healy and Dieter supply a firm rule keyed to the filing of a timely motion. A rule of this kind, marking an appellate boundary and giving notice of when a clock starts, is precisely the occasion for a definite line rather than a standard.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** A timely motion asking the district court to reconsider a question it has decided, in order to alter the rights adjudicated, renders the order nonfinal for appeal purposes until the motion is denied, and the full 30-day period then runs from the denial. The character or apparent strength of the ground asserted in the motion, including that the ground was earlier abandoned, does not affect when the period begins. Applied here, the notice of appeal filed 27 days after the January 3, 1990 denial was timely, and the dismissal cannot stand.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The efficiency rationale recited in Dieter explains why the rule exists; it is not an additional element a movant must satisfy. Converting it into one would require a court of appeals to assess the merits of a reconsideration motion before it could know whether it had an appeal before it, which inverts the order of decision and makes the boundary depend on the answer to the question the boundary exists to reach. Litigants would be driven either to file protective notices while the district court still had the motion or to litigate the sufficiency of their own motion's merit. The tolling vocabulary used below is also inapt: equitable tolling subtracts elapsed time, whereas Healy and Dieter give the appellant the full period from the denial. Stone adopts the restart description because it is what the authorities hold, not as a new rule.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Bad faith abandonment and reassertion of a ground is expressly reserved; no party asserts it and Healy itself left the point open. Successive motions for reconsideration are expressly reserved; only one motion was filed. Nothing is said about the validity of the suppression order or about the lawfulness of the second search.
* **Approved Conditional Position or Fallback:** None exists.

Nothing in this position changes what activates the appeal period, what must be shown, who bears any burden, or what remedy is available. It applies the rule the sources already establish.

# CASE 2 of 10: MIRELES v. WACO
# SECTION II: STONE POSITION SUPPLEMENT

## A. STATUS

* **Version:** 1.0
* **Approval Status:** Proposed and unapproved. Newly drafted; not represented as previously approved.
* **Current-Through Baseline:** Historical law effective immediately before October 21, 1991.
* **Conditions:** The disposition in B has two components, ordered. The second is reached only if the Court declines the first.

## B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

* **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** First, deny summary reversal. Stone would grant the petition and set the case for briefing and argument on the judicial-capacity boundary, because the governing law is not settled on the question the case actually turns on. Second, if the Court instead disposes of the matter on the present papers, Stone would affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals on a narrowed rationale and remand.
* **Stone's Remedy and Remand Position:** On either component, no remedial ruling issues. Affirmance on the narrowed rationale returns the case to the district court with the claim against the judge surviving only as to the alleged direction to use excessive force and its alleged ratification, with qualified immunity and every element of the claim open. The Rule 54(b) posture means nothing decided here touches the pending claims against the officers.

## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Whether the alleged conduct was taken in a judicial capacity

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the judicial-capacity branch because it is the ground of the decision below and the only branch the Court of Appeals decided.
* **Question Stone Resolves:** Narrowed to this: where a complaint alleges that a judge in one communication both directed officers to produce counsel and directed them to use excessive force in doing so, does absolute judicial immunity extend to the second direction because of its connection to the first.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply the received doctrine, and decide the open boundary it leaves. Stone does not propose changing Stump, Pierson, Forrester, or Mitchell. He identifies what they hold: absolute immunity is function-specific; it runs to the adjudicative function and not to the office; it is not defeated by error, excess of authority, malice, or corrupt motive; and it is immunity from suit. He then answers the question none of them answers.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Absolute judicial immunity attaches to a judge's direction to court officers to bring a person who is in the courthouse before the court, including where that direction is erroneous, unauthorized, or maliciously motivated. It does not attach to a separate direction to inflict unnecessary force on that person. Where a complaint alleges both, the second direction is analyzed on its own; it acquires no absolute immunity from its proximity to, or its stated purpose of executing, the first. Applied here, the Rule 54(b) dismissal of the entire claim against the judge was error, and the judgment reversing it is affirmed to that extent and no further.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Stone keeps legal consequences in the doctrines that produce them. The judicial character of the command to produce counsel is a conclusion about that command. Extending that protection to an alleged direction to inflict unnecessary force requires deciding whether the directions perform one function or two. Stump and Forrester supply the functional principles without resolving that precise boundary. Stone reads them to protect the adjudicative direction without extending absolute immunity to a separately alleged operational command merely because the same judge issued both. The competing approach treats the force direction as part of the general judicial function of producing counsel. Stone considers that approach insufficiently confined because it risks protecting independently nonjudicial conduct whenever the judge describes it as executing an order. That unresolved question about the scope of the immunity warrants briefing before summary reversal.
  The strongest form of the opposing position deserves statement. If courts examine each act in isolation, every ultra vires judicial act becomes nonjudicial, since nothing improper is normally performed by a judge, and the immunity collapses into a merits inquiry, which is what Stump forbids. Stone's answer is that the excess-of-authority principle protects the erroneous or unauthorized performance of an adjudicative function, not conduct falling outside any adjudicative function. Ordering counsel produced under a mistaken view of the court's power is the first. Ordering a battery is the second. The distinction is administrable because it asks what function the challenged direction performed, which is the question Forrester already requires.
  Stone also declines to treat the Ninth Circuit's rationale as correct as stated. That court framed the test as whether the judge requested and authorized excessive force, which risks turning on characterization of a single instruction. The narrowed rationale states the severance expressly and confines it.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** At the dismissal stage, Stone reads the complaint favorably to Waco as sufficiently alleging a direction to inflict unnecessary force distinct from the direction to produce counsel. Whether the judge actually gave such a direction, or whether the officers exceeded a direction confined to producing counsel, remains for further proceedings. He reserves the ratification allegation and the requirements for making that conduct actionable. He decides nothing about qualified immunity, causation, or the merits of the § 1983 claim, and failure of absolute immunity establishes neither a cause of action nor liability. Stone need not decide whether the alleged abuse satisfies Section 14: those commitments supply no exception to a separate absolute immunity, and his disposition rests on the function-specific boundary.

### Issue 2: Complete absence of all jurisdiction

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Not reached. The Court of Appeals did not decide it, no party has briefed it as an independent ground, and it is unnecessary on Stone's disposition, since the case survives on the judicial-capacity branch whether or not jurisdiction was wholly absent. Stone notes only that a direction issued in aid of the court's jurisdiction over a matter actually pending before it is a poor candidate for that branch, and he does not resolve it.
* **Approved Conditional Position or Fallback:** None exists. The ordered components in B are Stone's own positions, each of which he regards as independently lawful, not alternatives offered to attract votes.

# CASE 3 of 10: CAMPOS v. CITY OF HOUSTON (IN CHAMBERS)
# SECTION II: STONE POSITION SUPPLEMENT

## A. STATUS

* **Version:** 1.0
* **Approval Status:** Proposed and unapproved. Newly drafted; not represented as previously approved.
* **Current-Through Baseline:** Historical law effective immediately before October 29, 1991.
* **Conditions:** Conditional on participation. Under the allotment in effect, the Fifth Circuit was not assigned to the office Stone occupies. This position operates only if the simulation allots the Fifth Circuit to Stone or the application is referred to the full Court. If neither condition holds, Stone has no role and no position is engaged.

## B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

* **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Deny the requested original injunction stopping the entire City election process. Grant the alternative stay of the October 12 order insofar as it directs use of Plan #2 for the November 5 district council elections, pending disposition of the Fifth Circuit appeal or further order of the Court or the Justice lawfully considering the application.
* **Stone's Remedy and Remand Position:** The stay suspends the challenged judicial command; it neither selects a replacement plan nor independently enjoins unaffected elections. The parties may seek lawful interim relief in the appropriate court, including a supported election schedule and a plan meeting the governing requirements. The stay does not reinstate the malapportioned prior plan, preclear Plan #2, resolve the section 2 claim, or determine the merits of the separate enforcement action. It leaves lawful remedial choices to the courts possessing jurisdiction over those matters and imposes no unsupported deadline or substitute map.

## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Authority to grant the requested original injunction

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Reached first to determine the source and scope of the requested relief.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply the governing procedural sources. The application seeks an original order halting an entire municipal election process. The 1990 Rules no longer contain the former express provision for a single Justice to grant a writ of injunction, and this application establishes no adequate source or necessity for the blanket relief requested. Section 1651(a) supports the distinct, narrower stay described below in aid of potential appellate jurisdiction. Stone need not decide the full residual scope of original injunctive authority to reject relief extending beyond the challenged judicial command.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Deny the requested original injunction. If the application is referred to the full Court, Stone likewise rejects blanket relief because the narrower stay addresses the challenged command without reaching unaffected elections; his position does not depend on attributing a single Justice's limitations to the full Court.

### Issue 2: Stay pending appeal

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Reached because the applicants seek the lesser relief, have sought relief below, and a stay of the challenged judicial command falls within the authority recognized in Ripon Society under 28 U.S.C. § 1651(a). The appeal is pending and potential Supreme Court review supplies the jurisdiction the stay protects. Section 2101(f) is not the source for this interlocutory application.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply the received in-chambers considerations from Ripon Society: threatened irreparable injury, probability of error below, and the public interest. The applicants must establish the grounds for emergency relief. Stone adds no new scrutiny tier, proof quantum, or independent merits requirement. Prior denials receive respectful consideration without transferring responsibility for the legal judgment.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** The applicants have shown sufficient probability of error and threatened irreparable injury to warrant the limited stay. Plan #2 lacks the preclearance required of the City's legislative plan. Judicial adoption does not itself supply clearance or convert that plan into one independently devised by the court. Even assuming the district court possessed authority to provide some interim relief, its stated reasons for preferring Plan #2 to the already precleared Plan #3 do not adequately explain why the objected-to plan was necessary. The plaintiffs also objected to Plan #2, and the possibility that the Attorney General might reconsider Plan #3 did not itself withdraw its clearance. The jurisdictional objections remain for resolution below; the stay rests on a provisional assessment of the challenged relief rather than a final adjudication of those objections.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The statutory duty follows coverage and compliance, without an additional requirement that these applicants prove the City's unconstitutional purpose. Good faith therefore does not answer the absence of preclearance. The validity of the preventive statutory requirement is not presented, and Stone does not replace its conditions with his own racial-equality framework. McDaniel keeps a legislative plan within section 5 despite its adoption in litigation. Clark establishes the ordinary duty to prevent implementation and reserves, rather than supplies, a possible extreme-circumstance exception. An interim label and a prospect of later correction do not establish that exception.
  Stone gives real weight to ballots already cast, administrative disruption, and the election officials' testimony that another plan could not be implemented by November 5. Those consequences are concrete; he does not assume that Plan #3 can simply be substituted on the existing timetable. Nevertheless, the City received the objection on October 4, and the district court selected Plan #2 after Plan #3 had received clearance. The record does not establish an exceptional justification for allowing the objected-to legislative change to govern the election. Holding that election would defeat the statutory protection against implementation before clearance, an injury a later special election cannot fully undo. Responsibility for the compressed timetable may bear on equity but supplies no independent exemption from section 5.
  The public interest includes lawful electoral participation as well as orderly administration. Stone's preference for stable election rules does not preserve a rule shown to lack the statutory clearance necessary for its implementation. He therefore limits relief to the direction to use Plan #2 and leaves the proper courts to determine a lawful plan and schedule on a developed record. The stay addresses the identified legal defect without deciding the at-large dilution claim, choosing the City's council structure, or interrupting contests unaffected by Plan #2.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Stone does not finally decide whether the City's counterclaim required a three-judge court or whether that counterclaim presented a justiciable controversy. Provisional assessment of the challenged order for emergency relief does not adjudicate the underlying merits without jurisdiction. He neither recognizes a general exigency exception to section 5 nor holds that no extreme circumstance could ever justify temporary nonintervention. The underlying section 2 dilution claim, the merits of the at-large scheme, and the twenty-two-member council sought by the plaintiffs remain outside this disposition.
* **Approved Conditional Position or Fallback:** None exists.

# CASE 4 of 10: ZATKO v. CALIFORNIA
# SECTION II: STONE POSITION SUPPLEMENT

## A. STATUS

* **Version:** 1.0
* **Approval Status:** Proposed and unapproved. Newly drafted; not represented as previously approved.
* **Current-Through Baseline:** Historical law effective immediately before November 4, 1991.
* **Conditions:** Petition-specific action under Rule 39.8 requires examination of the petitions, which are not reproduced in the supplied record. No finding of frivolousness or malice is supplied by this supplement.

## B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

* **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Following examination of the petitions, deny leave to proceed in forma pauperis under Rule 39.8 as to each petition the Court determines to be frivolous or malicious, stating that determination as to each such petition. Any remaining petition receives ordinary consideration; Stone would deny certiorari where that review warrants denial. On the supplied record, no petition-specific Rule 39.8 denial can yet be entered. Decline to rest the denial on the petitioners' aggregate filing history as an independent ground. Decline to announce that future similar filings will merit additional measures.
* **Stone's Remedy and Remand Position:** No underlying remedy is at issue. If leave is denied, Stone would allow twenty-one days for payment of the docketing fee and submission of conforming petitions, preserving an opportunity to use the paid docket without assuming the petitioners can afford it.

## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Operation of Rule 39.8

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Reached; it is the only question. No underlying merits question is reached, and none is presented.
* **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether Rule 39.8's trigger is the character of the particular petition or the petitioner's filing pattern.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply the rule as written. The Court's authority to adopt and enforce Rule 39.8 is not in doubt; it controls its own docket, and abusive filing is not a protected exercise of anything. Stone does not propose narrowing the rule. He reads its trigger, which is textual: the Court must be satisfied that a petition is frivolous or malicious. The subject of that sentence is the petition, not the petitioner.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Rule 39.8 authorizes denial of leave to proceed in forma pauperis where the Court is satisfied that the particular petition before it is frivolous or malicious. A petitioner's history of repetitious frivolous filing is admissible and often decisive evidence bearing on that determination, and it bears on malice and on the appropriate response; it is not a substitute for the determination the rule requires. Applied here, leave would be denied only after examination establishes that the particular petition satisfies the rule, and the denial would state that determination. The supplied record does not permit Stone to identify which petitions meet that condition.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Two points carry the position. First, the rule's own words condition denial on the character of a petition, and Stone does not read a per-petitioner authority into a per-petition trigger; a rule whose stated trigger is quietly replaced by a different one is no longer the rule that was adopted. Second, the consequence is not nominal. For these petitioners the paid docket requires a fee and conforming printing, and their applications assert that they cannot pay; a denial of leave therefore operates, as to a petition that might later prove colorable, as a refusal to consider it. Where the consequence runs that way, a stated petition-specific ground is the minimum the rule and ordinary institutional candor require, and it is also what makes the order reviewable and criticizable.
  Stone states the strongest opposing position rather than dismissing it. Requiring an individualized determination in each of seventeen matters spends precisely the resource the rule was adopted to conserve, and the pattern is itself powerful evidence that the next filing will be frivolous too. Stone's answer is that the rule already accommodates this: the pattern may supply the evidence, the determination may be brief, and the Court remains free to deny the great mass of frivolous in forma pauperis petitions in the ordinary manner, as it has been doing. What it should not do is convert an evidentiary fact about a litigant into the operative ground of an order the rule keys to a document.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Stone would not announce prospectively that future similar filings will merit additional measures. Whatever such measures might be, they are not necessary to disposing of these motions, and a statement about filings not yet made prescribes a consequence in advance of the case that would present it. He expresses no view on whether any further sanction, filing restriction, or condition would be lawful; those questions await a matter that raises them. He also reaches no question about the merits of any underlying judgment, and nothing here bears on whether any petition would be granted.
* **Approved Conditional Position or Fallback:** None exists.

# CASE 5 of 10: HAFER v. MELO
# SECTION II: STONE POSITION SUPPLEMENT

## A. STATUS

* **Version:** 1.0
* **Approval Status:** Proposed and unapproved. Newly drafted; not represented as previously approved.
* **Current-Through Baseline:** Historical law effective immediately before November 5, 1991, subject to incorporation of any material earlier effective simulated holding identified in Section I.B.
* **Conditions:** None.

## B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

* **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals.
* **Stone's Remedy and Remand Position:** The case returns for further proceedings. No remedy is awarded or prescribed. Qualified immunity, the merits of the patronage-discharge claims, causation, and damages all remain open, and nothing in the disposition bears on the official-capacity reinstatement claims beyond leaving them as the Court of Appeals left them.

## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Whether state officials sued in their individual capacities are persons under § 1983

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Reached. It is the question presented and it is dispositive of the appeal from the dismissal.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply the enacted text and the received construction unchanged. Stone proposes no change to Will, Graham, or Monell.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** State officials sued in their individual capacities are persons within the meaning of § 1983. The Eleventh Amendment does not bar such suits, and state officers are not absolutely immune from personal liability under § 1983 solely because the acts complained of were official in nature. Applied here, respondents' damages claims against Hafer personally are not barred by Will, and the judgment of the Court of Appeals is affirmed.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The statute reaches persons who act under color of state law. Misuse of authority possessed by virtue of state law may satisfy that requirement; official action and lawful action are different questions. A construction under which official character defeats personal liability would remove from the statute the class of defendants it was written to reach, which is a reading no enacted text supports. Will resolved a question about the identity of the defendant: a State is not a person, and an official-capacity damages claim is in substance a claim against the State. It did not hold that an official who acts is immune from a claim against him. Stone keeps the three things separate: the defendant sued, the liability imposed, and the defense available. These individual-capacity claims seek to impose liability on Hafer personally, not on the State; whether she is liable still depends on the merits and applicable defenses.
  Petitioner's strongest argument is that pleading in the personal capacity will now be routine and that officials performing necessary governmental functions will bear the risk of personal ruin for reasonable errors. Stone takes that concern seriously and identifies where it belongs. Lawful discretion and reasonable factual mistakes receive the protection the substantive rule provides. Where that complete rule already accounts for reasonableness or culpability, he would not repeat the same factual allowances as a second defense after a violation is established. His preferred personal defense would require the official to identify binding precedent or enacted authority directly authorizing the challenged conduct, establish genuine reliance, and show a reasonable basis for treating that authority as valid and applicable to the material facts. Policy, customary practice, a supervisor's assurance, or legal silence would supply no independent protection. That is Stone's preferred rule, not a rule adopted in this capacity appeal; the governing immunity law and Hafer's entitlement under it remain for proper adjudication. Relocating the concern into the definition of person would extinguish the remedy for culpable misuse of public power along with the claim against the reasonable official, which is not a trade the statute authorizes a court to make.
  Stone also notes what the holding does not do. Failure of the Will defense establishes no cause of action, no causation, and no relief. The merits of the discharges are governed by their own law and are untouched.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Stone reserves whether Hafer is entitled to qualified immunity, which no court below resolved. His preferred merits-first sequence and narrower legal-reliance defense do not become remand instructions or governing law through this decision. Any departure from the controlling immunity rule would require a properly presented issue and an express justification under Section 16. He reserves the substance of the patronage-discharge claims. He reserves whether every respondent in fact pleaded a personal-capacity claim, that characterization having been made below on the proceedings as a whole and not being disputed here.
* **Approved Conditional Position or Fallback:** None exists.

# CASE 6 of 10: BOARD OF GOVERNORS OF THE FEDERAL RESERVE SYSTEM v. MCORP FINANCIAL, INC.
# SECTION II: STONE POSITION SUPPLEMENT

## A. STATUS

* **Version:** 1.0
* **Approval Status:** Proposed and unapproved. Newly drafted; not represented as previously approved.
* **Current-Through Baseline:** Historical law effective immediately before December 3, 1991.
* **Conditions:** None.

## B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

* **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** No. 90-913, reversed. No. 90-914, affirmed. The district court lacked jurisdiction to enjoin either administrative proceeding.
* **Stone's Remedy and Remand Position:** Both injunctions fall, together with the procedure the district court prescribed for the Board's future filings, which rests on the same absent jurisdiction. No remedial ruling issues. The regulation's validity and application remain reviewable under § 1818(h)(2) if and when the Board enters a final order, and Stone would say so in terms.

## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Effect of § 1818(i)(1)

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Reached first, because jurisdiction precedes everything.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply the enacted text. Stone does not propose changing anything.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Section 1818(i)(1) withdraws jurisdiction to affect by injunction the issuance or enforcement of a notice or order under the section, and it does so in terms Congress attached to the court's power rather than to claim processing. The district court therefore had no jurisdiction to enjoin the prosecution of either proceeding.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The provision states that no court shall have jurisdiction. That is a clear statement, and clear-statement presumptions, including the presumption against preclusion of review, do not defeat clear language. MCorp moved at the notice-of-charges stage, before any order existed, which is the moment the provision most plainly covers.

### Issue 2: The Bankruptcy Code

* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Section 1818(i)(1) is neither qualified nor superseded by 11 U.S.C. § 362. The Board's planned actions fall within § 362(b)(4), which excepts proceedings to enforce a governmental unit's police or regulatory power. Sections 362(a)(3) and (a)(6) do not apply to ongoing, nonfinal administrative proceedings. Section 1334(b) does not help, because the Board is not another court within the meaning of that provision, and because the prosecution of the proceedings, before any final order and before any enforcement action, is unlikely to impair the exclusive jurisdiction over property of the estate that § 1334(d) protects.
* **Essential Reasoning:** Statutes are read to coexist, and implied repeal requires irreconcilability. Nothing here is irreconcilable: the police and regulatory exception is the provision Congress wrote for precisely this collision, and reading the stay to reach a regulator's pending proceedings would make the exception do no work.

### Issue 3: Leedom v. Kyne

* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** The exception is unavailable. Kyne addressed agency action taken in excess of delegated power where preclusion would leave the affected party no means of protection. Here § 1818(h)(2) expressly supplies review of the regulation's validity and its application if and when the Board finds a violation, and § 1818(i)(1) states the preclusion of interim review clearly and directly. Where both conditions hold, a court has no jurisdiction to review and enjoin the ongoing proceedings.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Stone states the strongest contrary position and answers it. If a statute barred all review of a regulation the agency had no power to adopt, the right to contest it would survive in form while being unavailable in fact, and Stone does not treat a nominally available remedy as adequate merely because a statute names it. That is why the presence of § 1818(h)(2) is doing the work, not the bare existence of the bar. Court of appeals review after a final order is a real route: it reaches validity and application, it is available to this party, and it can set the order aside. The cost of waiting is the ordinary cost of defending an administrative proceeding, which is not the kind of harm that converts a real remedy into a nominal one.
  Stone adds a limit, and labels it as his own. He would not read today's holding to mean that § 1818(i)(1) forecloses review where the statutory route is in fact unavailable to the party, or where the agency's asserted authority is not merely contestable but absent on the face of the enabling text with no order in prospect against which review could run. Those cases are not this one, and he would leave them.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Stone expresses no view on the validity of the source of strength regulation, on whether a holding company's failure to transfer assets to a troubled subsidiary can be an unsafe or unsound practice under § 1818(b), or on the section 23A charge. A ruling that a court cannot act now says nothing about whether the Board is right, and Stone does not let the jurisdictional holding carry an implication about the merits it cannot support.
* **Approved Conditional Position or Fallback:** None exists.

# CASE 7 of 10: GRIFFIN v. UNITED STATES
# SECTION II: STONE POSITION SUPPLEMENT

## A. STATUS

* **Version:** 1.0
* **Approval Status:** Proposed and unapproved. Newly drafted; not represented as previously approved.
* **Current-Through Baseline:** Historical law effective immediately before December 3, 1991.
* **Conditions:** None.

## B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

* **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals.
* **Stone's Remedy and Remand Position:** None. No remedial ruling is necessary or reached.

## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Due process and the general verdict

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Reached. Stone would take the narrowest adequate ground first, which requires identifying what § 371 makes an element.
* **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether the Due Process Clause requires reversal of a general verdict on a single conspiracy count where the evidence sufficed as to one alleged object and not the other.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply the received doctrine unchanged, on a sequence that puts the enacted offense first. Stone proposes no change to Yates, Turner, or Stromberg, and he declines to extend Yates.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Section 371 punishes the unlawful agreement together with the required overt act, and the two objects alleged in this single count are alternative means of committing that conspiracy rather than separate elements each of which must be separately proved. Where the evidence suffices to establish the defendant's knowing participation in the charged conspiracy with at least one alleged unlawful object, and the remaining elements are satisfied, failure of proof as to another alleged object does not invalidate the general verdict. Applied here, the evidence connected petitioner to the IRS object, the sufficiency of the remaining elements is not challenged, and the conviction stands. Where, by contrast, one submitted basis of conviction is legally inadequate, whether because the Constitution forbids conviction on it or because it falls outside the statute, Yates and Stromberg govern when the general verdict does not disclose which basis the jury selected.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Stone begins with the statute because a constitutional claim about an unproved element cannot be assessed until the elements are identified. Section 371 punishes conspiring to commit any offense against the United States or to defraud any agency in any manner or for any purpose. The conjunctive pleading of two objects in one count is the ordinary and long-sanctioned method of charging alternative means, and Rule 7(c)(1) says so. Nothing in the enacted text makes each object an element.
  The constitutional question then answers itself on the received law. The prosecution must prove every element beyond a reasonable doubt, and it did. The distinction between legal inadequacy and evidentiary insufficiency supported by Yates and Turner also holds independently, and Stone adopts it as the further ground: jurors cannot be expected to detect that a submitted theory is contrary to law, because that is not a question their competence reaches, whereas weighing evidence is the whole of what a jury is for. That asymmetry, not a preference for finality, is why Yates and Turner sit where they do.
  Stone declines to make the Government's historical-practice argument dispositive. Historical practice is evidence of what due process meant and of the principle the guarantee protects; it is not a veto, and a long line of citations for an unexamined practice does not by itself establish a constitutional rule. Stone therefore treats the common-law materials as confirming what the statutory analysis and the jury-competence distinction independently establish, rather than as the load-bearing ground. He would also not characterize the dispute between legal error and insufficiency as merely semantic: the two are distinguishable because they call on different institutional capacities, which is a substantive reason and the one worth stating.
  On the record, Stone notes the danger of jury confusion in a 23-count, multiple-defendant trial lasting five to six weeks. It is real, and it is why the better practice is to remove an unsupported theory from the jury's consideration, and why charging the objects in separate counts or agreeing to special interrogatories would have avoided the question entirely. Stone commends those practices without converting any of them into a requirement. He invents no rule that a trial court must give such an instruction, because no source supplies one, and the refusal to give it is not an independent ground for reversing an otherwise valid conviction.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Stone expressly reserves the Sixth Amendment theory. It appears in the petition but was not developed in the briefing or argument, and the alleged defect is that a jury determination was permitted rather than withheld, which does not present the jury-trial question in a form the Court can decide. The due process conclusion does not resolve the reserved Sixth Amendment theory or determine whether jurors must agree on an object. He also reserves whether unanimity as to the object could be required by statute or rule. He declines to exercise supervisory power, because the practice he would commend does not require compulsion to be adopted and a supervisory rule announced here would extend well beyond conspiracy counts without the benefit of argument about those consequences.
* **Approved Conditional Position or Fallback:** None exists.

# CASE 8 of 10: ESTELLE v. McGUIRE
# SECTION II: STONE POSITION SUPPLEMENT

## A. STATUS

* **Version:** 1.0
* **Approval Status:** Proposed and unapproved. Newly drafted; not represented as previously approved.
* **Current-Through Baseline:** Historical law effective immediately before December 4, 1991, subject to incorporation of any material earlier effective simulated holding identified in Section I.B.
* **Conditions:** None.

## B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

* **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Vacate the judgment of the Court of Appeals and remand. Stone would reverse the Court of Appeals insofar as it held the prior injury evidence improperly admitted and insofar as it rested on state-law error, and would hold the prior act instruction constitutionally defective as to identity.
* **Stone's Remedy and Remand Position:** Remand to the Court of Appeals to determine whether the State has proved beyond a reasonable doubt that the instructional error did not contribute to the verdict. Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18, 24 (1967), supplies that burden and standard, and Rose v. Clark, 478 U.S. 570, 582-584 (1986), applies it to burden-shifting instructional error on federal habeas review. Stone would not decide harmlessness in the first instance, because the court below has not yet applied that inquiry to the whole trial record. This is trial error subject to harmless-error review, not an error requiring automatic reversal. No writ issues from this disposition; entitlement to habeas relief remains to be decided on remand. Stone would apply no later-developed habeas harmless-error standard.

## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Admission of the prior injury evidence

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Reached, because it is the first of the two grounds on which relief was granted.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply received doctrine unchanged.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Evidence that the child had sustained serious repeated injuries by nonaccidental means was relevant, and its admission did not violate federal due process. It bore on causation and on the intent with which the person who inflicted the fatal injuries acted, because it showed the death was the result of an intentional act by someone rather than an accident, and it remains relevant although no direct evidence linked respondent to the earlier injuries. The absence of a defense claim of accident did not relieve the State of proving every element beyond a reasonable doubt. A federal habeas court may not grant relief on the ground that a state court misapplied its own evidence law, and the Court of Appeals erred in resting on that ground.
* **Essential Reasoning:** The relevance of the syndrome evidence does not depend on the identity of the abuser. That is the whole of the point, and it is also the reason the instructional question is separate.

### Issue 2: The prior act instruction

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Reached. It is an independent ground of the judgment below, it presents a federal question, and it is not disposed of by the ruling on Issue 1.
* **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether McGuire establishes a reasonable likelihood that the jury understood the instruction to treat his commission of the earlier injuries as already established or the conclusion that he committed the killing as required, thereby relieving the State of proving identity beyond a reasonable doubt.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply received doctrine. Under Boyde, McGuire bears the burden of establishing a reasonable likelihood of constitutionally impermissible application of the ambiguous instruction in the context of the charge and trial as a whole. A mere possibility does not suffice; he need not prove that misapplication was more likely than not. Stone proposes no change to Boyde, Winship, Allen, Sandstrom, or Francis, and he does not reach the question whether a state rule authorizing propensity use of prior crimes would itself violate due process.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** An instruction telling the jury that evidence was admitted to show similar offenses committed by the defendant, and that upon finding a clear connection between them it may be logically concluded that if he committed the other offenses he also committed the charged crime, creates a reasonable likelihood in the context of this charge and record that the jury treated his commission of the earlier injuries as already established and the further identity conclusion as required. McGuire has met his burden of showing that reasonable likelihood. So understood, the instruction relieves the State of proving identity beyond a reasonable doubt. The instruction was therefore constitutional error, and the case is remanded for a determination whether the State has proved the error harmless under Chapman and Rose.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Stone's route to this conclusion runs through the element and the burden, not through a general sense of unfairness. The State had to prove that this defendant killed the child. The syndrome evidence supported a finding that the fatal injuries were inflicted intentionally by someone. Circumstantial evidence could also support a finding that respondent inflicted the earlier injuries, including the evidence that he and his wife were the only regular caregivers. A permissible inference may help prove an element; its use is not unconstitutional merely because it supplies part of the prosecution's proof. The defect here concerns who determined the predicate and whether the further inference remained permissive. The instruction grouped respondent's identified rough handling with the unattributed rib fractures and rectal tearing, described the evidence as showing similar acts he committed, and then linked a clear connection to a logical conclusion of guilt. Read together, those directions created a reasonable likelihood that jurors understood the prior-abuser predicate as settled and the identity conclusion as prescribed, rather than making the factual determinations while holding the State to its burden.
  Stone states the strongest contrary reading and why it does not prevail. The phrase conditioning the inference on whether the defendant committed the other offenses supports a reading that leaves that question to the jury, and the separate instruction forbidding bad-character or disposition use is a genuine safeguard against propensity reasoning. Stone accepts the force of both points. He nonetheless concludes that the conditional phrase did not adequately correct the instruction's opening attribution of the similar acts to respondent or clarify that the proposed identity conclusion remained for the jury to accept or reject. The anti-propensity direction addressed character reasoning without resolving those ambiguities. The wife's testimony claiming responsibility for the fatal injuries and the three days of deliberation provide context for the disputed identity question, without establishing what any juror actually believed. Winship, Sandstrom, Francis, and Allen require the jury to make the necessary factual determinations under instructions preserving the prosecution's burden. Stone concludes that this charge did not preserve that division of responsibility.
  Stone also keeps the doctrines separate. The error is constitutional and therefore cognizable on habeas; it is not cognizable because the instruction may have violated state law or because the state judge may have misread the state evidence code, neither of which is a ground for federal relief.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Stone expresses no view on whether a state law permitting the use of prior crimes evidence to show propensity would violate the Due Process Clause; the question is not presented. He reserves harmlessness for the court below. He decides nothing about the sufficiency of the evidence of guilt, about the admissibility of the syndrome testimony under state law, or about any other aspect of the trial.
* **Approved Conditional Position or Fallback:** None exists.

# CASE 9 of 10: SOUTHWEST MARINE, INC. v. GIZONI
# SECTION II: STONE POSITION SUPPLEMENT

## A. STATUS

* **Version:** 1.0
* **Approval Status:** Proposed and unapproved. Newly drafted; not represented as previously approved.
* **Current-Through Baseline:** Historical law effective immediately before December 4, 1991.
* **Conditions:** None.

## B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

* **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals.
* **Stone's Remedy and Remand Position:** The case returns for resolution of the disputed factual questions bearing on seaman status. No remedy is reached, and Stone expresses no view on negligence, unseaworthiness, or maintenance and cure. Any Jones Act recovery is credited against LHWCA liability by the statute itself.

## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Whether an enumerated occupation forecloses Jones Act seaman status

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Reached; it is the question presented and it disposes of the exclusivity ground on which summary judgment principally rested.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply the enacted text and Wilander unchanged. This is an ordinary statutory case decided under the field's own sources, with no overlay.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** A maritime worker whose occupation is one of those enumerated in the LHWCA may be a seaman within the meaning of the Jones Act. The definition of employee expressly excludes a master or member of a crew of any vessel, and the exclusive remedy provisions of §§ 905(a) and 905(b) reach only a person covered under the chapter; a worker who is a crew member is therefore outside both. The status inquiry is a mixed question of law and fact turning on the nature of the vessel and the employee's precise relation to it, and a worker is confined to LHWCA remedies only where no genuine issue of fact exists as to seaman status. Applied here, the district court's ruling on the basis of job title alone was error and summary judgment was inappropriate.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The text settles it. Congress enumerated ship repairmen within the definition and then carved out crew members, and the placement of the small-vessel and recreational-vessel repairer exclusions on either side of subparagraph (G) confirms that the exclusions do real work rather than describing categories the enumeration had already excluded. A construction under which enumeration alone is conclusive would read subparagraph (G) out of the statute. The legislative materials Congress produced in 1972 point the same way, describing coverage of harbor workers and ship repairmen while excluding masters and members of the crew, and Stone uses them only to confirm a reading the text already supports.
  Stone notes what the holding does not decide. It does not hold that respondent is a seaman, and many ship repairmen will not be, because it is the employee's connection to a vessel rather than his particular job that governs. Whether these floating platforms, which have no power, steering, navigation aids, or living facilities, are vessels in navigation, and whether a rigging foreman's relation to them suffices, are contested questions for the trier of fact under the proper standard.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Stone leaves the two factual questions open. He expresses no view on whether aiding in navigation, which Wilander held unnecessary, retains any evidentiary significance, and no view on the merits of any claim.

### Issue 2: Primary jurisdiction, preclusion, and estoppel

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Reached because each, if accepted, would foreclose the suit without regard to Issue 1.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** None of the three forecloses the action. The railroad cases give no guidance, because the statute contains no exclusion for railroad workers comparable to the exclusion for crew members. No stay pending administrative determination is required: nothing in the statute indicates that Congress meant to preclude or stay traditional Jones Act suits in the district courts, the statute anticipates that such suits will be brought and tolls the time to file where recovery is denied on coverage grounds, and it contains no provision barring judicial review of coverage comparable to the one governing the Federal Employees' Compensation Act. The administrative proceedings under this statute do not approach the complex and interrelated federal scheme of law, remedy, and administration that supports preemption in the labor context, and resolution of coverage in the first instance is not essential to the statute's administration. Receipt of voluntary benefits without a formal award does not preclude a later Jones Act suit, because coverage has never been litigated and the statute credits Jones Act amounts against its own liability. For the same reason estoppel fails: where full credit removes the threat of double recovery, the detrimental reliance the doctrine requires is absent.
* **Essential Reasoning:** Stone keeps the forum question separate from the substance. A procedural device cannot extinguish a nonwaivable statutory entitlement by relocating it, and the election of remedies petitioner and its amicus press would force an injured maritime worker to choose between accepting interim compensation and preserving a statutory action Congress gave him, a choice nothing in the statute imposes. Stone does not reach that observation as a freestanding principle; the statute's own crediting and tolling provisions decide the question.
* **Approved Conditional Position or Fallback:** None exists.

# CASE 10 of 10: WOODDELL v. INTERNATIONAL BROTHERHOOD OF ELECTRICAL WORKERS, LOCAL 71
# SECTION II: STONE POSITION SUPPLEMENT

## A. STATUS

* **Version:** 1.0
* **Approval Status:** Proposed and unapproved. Newly drafted; not represented as previously approved.
* **Current-Through Baseline:** Historical law effective immediately before December 4, 1991.
* **Conditions:** None.

## B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

* **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Reverse the judgment of the Court of Appeals and remand.
* **Stone's Remedy and Remand Position:** The case returns for further proceedings, with the LMRDA damages claim triable to a jury and the § 301 claim within the district court's subject-matter jurisdiction. No remedy is reached and no view is expressed on the merits of any claim.

## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Jury trial right on the LMRDA claim

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Reached; it is the first question presented and is independent of the second.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply Terry unchanged. The controlling decision was available to the court below and was neither cited nor discussed there.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** A union member suing his local for money damages under Title I of the LMRDA is entitled to a jury trial. The remedy sought is legal: the damages are neither analogous to equitable restitution nor incidental to or intertwined with injunctive relief, the claimed lost wages being pay for jobs to which the union failed to refer him rather than a restitutionary incident of reinstatement to a job from which he was terminated, and the injunctive relief sought is incidental to the damages. An LMRDA action is also closely analogous to a personal injury action, the prototypical action at law.
* **Essential Reasoning:** The two-part inquiry asks what the action resembles historically and whether the remedy is legal or equitable, with the second the more important. Both point one way here, and respondents now concede the point.

### Issue 2: Section 301(a) jurisdiction

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Reached. It is jurisdictional and was the ground of dismissal.
* **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether the jurisdiction conferred by § 301(a) extends to a suit by an individual union member alleging violation of a union constitution, where the breach alleged is of a contract between two labor organizations.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply the enacted text as construed in Smith v. Evening News and Plumbers and Pipefitters. Stone proposes no change.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** The subject-matter jurisdiction conferred by § 301(a) extends to a suit on a union constitution brought by an individual union member, where the member alleges violation of a contract between labor organizations and is a beneficiary of that contract. Applied here, petitioner alleged that the IBEW Constitution requires locals to honor collective bargaining agreements and that the Constitution and bylaws bind Local 71, allegations the local admitted, and that the defendants breached those contracts by discriminating against him in hiring-hall referrals. That is an allegation of violation of a contract between two labor organizations, and the courts below erred in holding federal jurisdiction unavailable.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The statute's word between attaches to contracts, not to suits, so the jurisdictional grant turns on the character of the contract and not on the identity of the plaintiff. Union constitutions were the most commonplace form of contract between labor organizations when the section was enacted and remain so, and Congress would not have used the unqualified term contract meaning to exclude them. Smith v. Evening News establishes that § 301 suits are not confined to contracting parties, and the reason it gives applies directly here: if a member must go to state court and state law to enforce an interunion contract that the international itself could enforce in federal court under federal law, the same contract terms would bear different meanings according to who sued, which is the disruptive influence the section was written to prevent.
  Stone takes respondents' two objections in turn. The intrusion argument asks the Court to read a later statute as narrowing an earlier one; but if § 301, fairly construed and apart from the LMRDA, covers this suit, the later statute changes that only if something in it shows Congress meant to narrow the section, and nothing does. Silence is neither repeal nor approval, and a partial implied repeal requires more than the observation that Congress has legislated nearby. The floodgates argument is a prediction, and it is one the record contradicts: several circuits have followed this construction for years, one of them a decade earlier in a case involving this very union constitution, and respondents identify no resulting flood. Stone would not let an interpretation of enacted language turn on a forecast in any event.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Stone reaches no question about the merits of the contract claims. He notes that the record omits the local's charter, constitution if any, and bylaws, which may matter on remand. Personal standing is not disputed and is not decided. Jurisdiction is not a cause of action, and the holding establishes neither that any defendant breached any obligation nor that the individual officers are proper defendants nor that any particular remedy is available. The duty of fair representation claim and the pendent state claims are untouched.
* **Approved Conditional Position or Fallback:** None exists.
