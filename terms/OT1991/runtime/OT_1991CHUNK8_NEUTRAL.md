One cannot infer a punitive award merely from an enforceable permitting obligation, or dispose of the stronger CWA text solely by quoting RCRA. If no provision reaches the requested fines, immunity bars that relief while conceded compliance obligations remain.

### Issue 1: Does negligent failure to develop facts in state postconviction proceedings require cause and prejudice before a federal habeas hearing?

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** No case-specific simulated holding supplied. Historical baseline authorities remain eligible unless actually displaced.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** No prior Standards & Tests tracker exists; the complete material entering framework is reconstructed below.
* **Other Governing Law:** Pre-AEDPA 28 U.S.C. §2254 and federal habeas hearing law; no later statutory hearing restriction applies.
* **Material Other Authority:** Townsend v. Sain, 372 U.S. 293 (1963), requires a federal hearing where a material factual dispute was not resolved, the state determination lacks fair record support, the state procedure was inadequate, substantial newly discovered evidence exists, material facts were inadequately developed, or the applicant otherwise lacked a full and fair fact hearing. Its inadequate-development branch excludes inexcusable neglect through the deliberate-bypass framework, rather than treating ordinary counsel negligence as an automatic hearing bar. The then-effective 28 U.S.C. §2254(d) presumes qualifying state factual determinations correct, subject to eight statutory exceptions concerning unresolved disputes, inadequate procedure or development, jurisdiction, denial of counsel, absence of a full and fair hearing or due process, and lack of fair record support. It does not presume the correctness of a fact never reliably determined. Wainwright v. Sykes, 433 U.S. 72 (1977), Murray v. Carrier, 477 U.S. 478 (1986), and Coleman, 501 U.S. 722 (1991), govern procedural default of claims through cause and actual prejudice; ordinary attorney error generally is attributable to the litigant, while external impediment can provide cause. McCleskey, 499 U.S. 467 (1991), extends that framework to abuse of the writ. The recognized miscarriage-of-justice exception remains distinct. Whether those rules should replace Townsend for factual development is open here, not already answered by their existence.
* **Open Point:** Whether cause and prejudice should replace Townsend’s specific hearing rule for negligent factual-development failure.

The State argues that claim defaults and neglected factual development equally frustrate state adjudication and should use the same cause-and-prejudice standard. Tamayo-Reyes relies on Townsend’s directly applicable hearing rule and the difference between losing a claim and supplying facts needed to decide a properly presented one. The Ninth Circuit applied deliberate bypass.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

The Court may retain Townsend and require a hearing without deciding the plea claim. Replacing its inadequate-development rule requires an express precedent decision, a stated negligence threshold, exceptions, and instructions for applying the new rule. A remand to examine cause under a new standard cannot be presented as mere application of unchanged law.

* **Other Governing Law:** Fifth Amendment grand-jury structure, federal supervisory authority, and the governing harmless-error limitation.
* **Material Other Authority:** Costello, 350 U.S. 359 (1956), forbids dismissing an otherwise valid indictment merely because it rests on hearsay and rejects routine adequacy review of grand-jury evidence. Calandra, 414 U.S. 338 (1974), limits application of the exclusionary rule in grand-jury proceedings. Department of Treasury, 500 U.S. 1 (1991), recognizes review of an issue pressed or passed upon below. Under the asserted supervisory route, the defendant must establish the relevant misconduct and resulting prejudice; mere omission of a defense theory is not enough.
* **Open Point:** Whether federal supervisory authority supports the confined exculpatory-disclosure obligation and whether established prejudice supports dismissal.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

The Government argues that courts cannot add evidence-presentation duties to an independent accusatory body, and that disclosure litigation would create preliminary trials. Williams defends the narrow Page rule as protection against materially misleading unilateral presentations and relies on the district court’s prejudice findings. The appellate court treated the findings as supported under clear-error review.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

The Court may reach the passed-upon issue, reject supervisory authority for this duty, and restore the indictment without deciding guilt. It may sustain a carefully confined supervisory rule and then assess prejudice. Dismissal as improvidently granted on preservation grounds remains a vehicle option, but would decide no disclosure rule.
