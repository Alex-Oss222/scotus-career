# CASE 1 of 10: UNITED STATES v. IBARRA
# SECTION I: NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

* **Case Name:** United States v. Ibarra
* **Citation or Docket:** 502 U.S. 1 (1991); No. 90-1713
* **October Term:** 1991
* **Entry Stage and Event:** Petition for certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit, presented for disposition on the papers without oral argument. Respondent's motion for leave to proceed in forma pauperis is pending with the petition.
* **Simulated Event Date:** October 15, 1991 (historical decision date)
* **Question Presented:** Whether the 30-day period for a government appeal from a suppression order begins to run on the date of the district court's original order or on the date the district court denies a timely motion for reconsideration of that order, where the sole ground of the motion is a theory the government had earlier abandoned.
* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit, 920 F.2d 702 (1990), dismissing the government's appeal as untimely by a divided panel.
* **Relief Requested:** Grant of certiorari; vacatur of the judgment of dismissal; remand for consideration of the suppression appeal on the merits.
* **Material Record:** Respondent was indicted for possession of cocaine with intent to distribute. Officers stopped his car for a suspected operating violation, obtained consent, and searched briefly without finding cocaine. Neither respondent nor his passenger held a valid operator's license; the officers impounded the car, transported the occupants to a Western Union office, then searched the car a second time at the towing lot and found cocaine in the trunk. Respondent moved to suppress the fruits of the second search. The government initially asserted continuing consent, then expressly abandoned that theory in papers filed before the ruling, citing lack of legal support. The circumstances leading to the indictment are largely uncontested. The suppression ruling itself is not before the Court and its correctness is unresolved.
* **Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** The entire matter is a threshold timing question. Whether the 30-day limit of Fed. R. App. P. 4(b) and 18 U.S.C. § 3731 is jurisdictional in character or a claim-processing rule is treated in the panel opinions as significant to whether a merits-sensitive exception may be engrafted onto it; no party is recorded as contesting that the limit is mandatory. Two related questions are outside the record: whether the abandonment and reassertion of the consent theory was in bad faith, which no one asserts, and what rule governs successive motions for reconsideration, of which only one was filed here.
* **Relevant Dates:** Suppression order entered November 15, 1989 (725 F. Supp. 1195). Motion for reconsideration of the suppression order filed December 13, 1989. Motion denied January 3, 1990 (731 F. Supp. 1037). Notice of appeal filed January 30, 1990, within 30 days of the denial and 76 days after the original order.
* **Companion or Related Matters:** None material.
* **Participation Matter:** Eight-Member Court. Justice Thomas was not yet seated.

## B. LAW ENTERING THE CASE

* **Current Through:** Historical United States law effective immediately before October 15, 1991.
* **Earlier Same-Term Decisions:** None. This is the first matter scheduled in the term.
* **Unresolved Same-Term Dependencies:** None identified.
* **Institutional or Procedural Law:** Disposition of a certiorari petition on the papers, including summary vacatur and remand, is available where the governing rule is settled and the decision below departs from it.

### Issue 1: Date from which the government's 30-day appeal period runs

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** None. First term; no prior simulated holdings.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** None yet operating in the simulation.
* **Other Governing Law:** Fed. R. App. P. 4(b), requiring a government notice of appeal within 30 days after entry of the judgment or order appealed from or a defendant's notice of appeal. 18 U.S.C. § 3731, authorizing the appeal of a suppression order and requiring that it be taken within 30 days after the decision, judgment, or order has been rendered and diligently prosecuted.
* **Material Other Authority:** United States v. Healy, 376 U.S. 75 (1964), holding that a timely petition for rehearing filed within the appeal period renders an otherwise final decision nonfinal until the petition is decided, so that the 30-day period runs from the denial. United States v. Dieter, 429 U.S. 6 (1976) (per curiam), rejecting an exception to Healy for motions asserting mistake or inadvertence rather than error of law, and confirming that the full 30 days runs from the denial. Department of Banking of Neb. v. Pink, 317 U.S. 264, 266 (1942), supplying the working description of a motion for reconsideration as one seeking to reconsider a question decided in the case in order to effect an alteration of the rights adjudicated. Healy, 376 U.S. at 80 n.4, reserving bad faith. United States v. Marsh, 700 F.2d 1322 (10th Cir. 1983), on successive motions. United States v. Ladson, 774 F.2d 436, 438-439 n.3 (11th Cir. 1985), on protective notices of appeal.
* **Open Point:** Whether bad faith in abandoning and reasserting a theory, or the filing of successive motions for reconsideration, can withhold the Healy rule. Neither is presented.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

Issue 1.

* **Controlling reasoning below:** The panel majority read Healy and Dieter as serving judicial efficiency, reasoned that a motion reasserting a previously disavowed theory cannot serve the function of letting a district court correct matters it initially overlooked, and therefore created an exception for such motions. It added that motions to reconsider a conceded position are viewed with disfavor on appeal, citing United States v. Smith, 781 F.2d 184 (10th Cir. 1986). The panel framed the issue as whether the motion "tolled" a period assumed to have begun with the first order. An alternative strand of the opinion treats the motion as not a "true" motion for reconsideration because the ground had not been urged initially.
* **Material alternative or unreached grounds:** The dissenting judge below objected that the majority grafted a merits inquiry onto what should be a bright-line jurisdictional inquiry, 920 F.2d at 710 (Baldock, J., dissenting).
* **Petitioner's strongest supported position:** Healy states a general rule keyed to the filing of a timely motion, not to the motion's quality. Dieter already rejected one attempt to subdivide the category. A merits screen at the timing stage is unadministrable and forces litigants to guess, encouraging protective notices of appeal while a reconsideration motion is pending. The motion here sought reconsideration of a question decided in order to alter the rights adjudicated, which is what Healy and Dieter describe.
* **Respondent's strongest supported position:** The rationale of Healy and Dieter is efficiency, and a motion resting only on a ground the government deliberately abandoned advances no efficiency interest; a rule keyed to that rationale limits itself. Extending the appeal period on such a motion lets the government manufacture time by refiling a theory it discarded. This is the strongest available position; it does not answer the administrability objection, and respondent does not appear to contest that the tolling framing is inapt.
* **Necessary concessions or limiting principles:** The government does not assert that bad faith would be immaterial; the question is reserved. Only one motion was filed, so no position on successive motions is required.
* **Solicitor General or amicus position:** The United States is petitioner. No separate amicus position is material.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

* **Threshold paths:** Deny certiorari, leaving the circuit rule in place. Grant and set for argument. Grant and dispose summarily on the papers.
* **Merits paths:** (i) The timely motion for reconsideration renders the suppression order nonfinal, and the full 30 days runs from the denial, without a merits screen. (ii) The same rule, with an express exception for bad faith or for successive motions. (iii) The Tenth Circuit's exception for motions resting on abandoned theories. (iv) A narrower holding that the tolling framing is wrong and the period restarts, without resolving whether any exception exists.
* **Judgment consequences:** Paths (i), (ii), and (iv) require vacatur or reversal of the dismissal. Path (iii) requires affirmance and ends the government's appeal. Disposition of respondent's in forma pauperis motion accompanies any grant.
* **Remedial or remand consequences:** Vacatur returns the case to the Tenth Circuit to decide the suppression appeal on the merits; the underlying criminal trial remains pending.
* **Uncertainty:** The correctness of the suppression order is undecided and unreviewed. Whether the appeal period is properly characterized as jurisdictional is assumed rather than adjudicated in the papers. Nothing in the record bears on bad faith.

# CASE 2 of 10: MIRELES v. WACO
# SECTION I: NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

* **Case Name:** Mireles v. Waco
* **Citation or Docket:** 502 U.S. 9 (1991); No. 91-311
* **October Term:** 1991
* **Entry Stage and Event:** Petition for certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, presented for disposition on the papers. Petitioner seeks summary reversal.
* **Simulated Event Date:** October 21, 1991 (historical decision date)
* **Question Presented:** Whether a state judge who is alleged to have ordered police officers to seize an attorney and bring him into the courtroom forcibly and with excessive force is absolutely immune from a damages action under 42 U.S.C. § 1983, where the allegations are taken as true on a motion to dismiss.
* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** Ninth Circuit, Waco v. Baltad, 934 F.2d 214 (1991), reversing the district court's Rule 54(b) final judgment dismissing the claim against the judge on grounds of complete judicial immunity.
* **Relief Requested:** Reversal of the Ninth Circuit's judgment and reinstatement of the dismissal as to the judge.
* **Material Record:** Respondent Waco, a Los Angeles County public defender, sued a California Superior Court judge and two police officers under § 1983 for damages arising from an incident in November 1989 at the Van Nuys Superior Court building. The complaint alleges that after Waco failed to appear for the initial call of the judge's morning calendar, the judge, angered by the absence of attorneys, ordered the officers to seize Waco forcibly and with excessive force and bring him into the courtroom; that the officers seized him with unreasonable force and violence, removed him backwards from another courtroom where he was waiting to appear, cursed him and called him offensive names, and without necessity slammed him through the doors and gates into the judge's courtroom; and that the judge knowingly and deliberately approved and ratified each of those acts. General and punitive damages are demanded. Because the case arises on a Rule 12(b) motion, no facts are found; the allegations are assumed. Waco was called into the courtroom for purposes of a pending case.
* **Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** The judge moved under Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(b)(1) and 12(b)(6); the district court entered final judgment as to him under Rule 54(b), so the immunity ruling is separately reviewable. The Ninth Circuit decided only the judicial-capacity branch of the immunity inquiry and did not reach the complete-absence-of-jurisdiction branch. Whether the unsettled judicial-capacity boundary warrants summary disposition rather than briefing and argument is a separate procedural question.
* **Relevant Dates:** Incident November 1989. Ninth Circuit decision 1991. Petition disposed of October 21, 1991.
* **Companion or Related Matters:** Claims against the two officer defendants remain pending and are unaffected by the Rule 54(b) judgment.
* **Participation Matter:** Eight-Member Court. Justice Thomas was not yet seated.

## B. LAW ENTERING THE CASE

* **Current Through:** Historical United States law effective immediately before October 21, 1991.
* **Earlier Same-Term Decisions:** None supplied. Ibarra precedes this matter on the calendar; no material dependency is identified.
* **Unresolved Same-Term Dependencies:** None identified.
* **Institutional or Procedural Law:** Summary reversal is an exceptional disposition, conventionally reserved for cases in which the governing law is settled and stable, the facts are not in dispute, and the decision below is clearly in error.

### Issue 1: Whether the alleged conduct was taken in a judicial capacity

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** None.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** None yet operating in the simulation.
* **Other Governing Law:** 42 U.S.C. § 1983 (Rev. Stat. § 1979). Cal. Civ. Proc. Code §§ 128, 177, 187, conferring broad power on state judges over the conduct of proceedings, including power to control the conduct of ministerial officers and all other persons connected with a proceeding before the court; Ligda v. Superior Court, 5 Cal. App. 3d 811, 826 (1970), treating a public defender as such a person who may be ordered to appear.
* **Material Other Authority:** Bradley v. Fisher, 13 Wall. 335 (1872). Pierson v. Ray, 386 U.S. 547, 554 (1967) (immunity applies even where the judge is accused of acting maliciously and corruptly). Stump v. Sparkman, 435 U.S. 349, 356-357, 360, 362 (1978) (two-branch structure; a judge is not deprived of immunity because the action was in error or in excess of authority; whether an act is judicial turns on the nature of the act, whether it is a function normally performed by a judge, and on the parties' expectations). Forrester v. White, 484 U.S. 219, 227-229 (1988) (immunity attaches to judicial acts; a judicial act does not become less judicial by an allegation of malice or corrupt motive; it is the nature of the function performed, not the identity of the actor, that informs the analysis; administrative acts are not protected). Mitchell v. Forsyth, 472 U.S. 511, 520-526 (1985) (immunity from suit, not merely from damages; function-specific analysis). Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800, 815-819 (1982). Burns v. Reed, 500 U.S. 478, 492 (1991) (issuance of a search warrant is a judicial act). Supreme Court of Va. v. Consumers Union, 446 U.S. 719, 736-737 (1980) (no judicial immunity when acting in an enforcement capacity). Dennis v. Sparks, 449 U.S. 24 (1980); Cleavinger v. Saxner, 474 U.S. 193 (1985); Butz v. Economou, 438 U.S. 478 (1978). Limits on the immunity: Ex parte Virginia, 100 U.S. 339, 348-349 (1880) (criminal liability); Pulliam v. Allen, 466 U.S. 522, 536-544 (1984) (prospective injunctive relief and statutory attorney's fees).
* **Open Point:** No decision squarely addresses whether a single communication alleged to contain both a judicial command and a command to commit a battery is treated as one act or two for purposes of the judicial-capacity branch. That is the contested boundary.

### Issue 2: Whether the alleged conduct was taken in the complete absence of all jurisdiction

* **Other Governing Law and Authority:** Stump, 435 U.S. at 356-357; Bradley, 13 Wall. at 351.
* **Open Point:** Unreached below. Whether acting in excess of authority in aid of jurisdiction over a matter actually before the judge can amount to the complete absence of jurisdiction.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

Issue 1.

* **Controlling reasoning below:** The Ninth Circuit held the judge not immune because his alleged actions were not taken in his judicial capacity. It stated that directing officers merely to bring Waco to the courtroom would have been judicial, but that requesting and authorizing the use of excessive force would not.
* **Material alternative or unreached grounds:** The jurisdiction branch was not reached below.
* **Petitioner's strongest supported position:** Immunity is from suit, and allegations of malice or bad faith cannot defeat it, because such allegations can be tested only through discovery and trial, which is the burden the immunity exists to prevent. Stump forecloses stripping immunity for error or excess of authority, and the Ninth Circuit's rule does exactly that: if the particular act alone is examined, every unauthorized act becomes nonjudicial, since no improper act is normally performed by a judge. The relevant function here is directing court officers to bring counsel in a pending case before the court, which is a function normally performed by a judge and is expressly authorized by state law. The officers' role does not convert the direction into executive action, since a judge's direction to an executive officer to produce counsel is no more executive than issuing a search warrant.
* **Respondent's strongest supported position:** Immunity attaches only to judicial acts. Respondent's strongest argument treats the alleged communication as two commands: produce counsel, and inflict unnecessary force. On that reading the first is judicial and the second is operational conduct outside the adjudicative function; communicating them together does not enlarge the immunity. Consumers Union and Forrester make the inquiry function-specific rather than actor-specific. Respondent must therefore establish a functional distinction between the challenged directions, not merely allege that the judge performed a judicial function unlawfully.
* **Necessary concessions or limiting principles:** Petitioner must accept that a genuinely nonjudicial act is unprotected however closely it is connected in time to a judicial one. Respondent must accept that erroneous, malicious, and ultra vires judicial acts remain immune, and that only the command to use force is arguably severable.
* **Solicitor General or amicus position:** None material.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

* **Threshold paths:** Deny certiorari. Grant and set the case for briefing and argument. Grant and dispose summarily.
* **Merits paths:** (i) The relevant unit of analysis is the particular act's relation to a general judicial function, so the entire direction is judicial and immunity applies. (ii) The two commands are analytically separate; immunity covers the direction to produce counsel and not the direction to use excessive force, so the judgment below is affirmed or affirmed on a narrowed rationale. (iii) Remand for the Ninth Circuit to address the jurisdiction branch before any final immunity ruling. (iv) Resolve the jurisdiction branch in the first instance, holding that action in aid of jurisdiction over a pending matter is not action in the complete absence of jurisdiction.
* **Judgment consequences:** Path (i) reverses and restores the dismissal, ending the damages claim against the judge while leaving the officer claims alive. Path (ii) leaves the case pending against the judge on the severed allegation, with qualified immunity, causation, and the merits all unresolved. Path (iii) vacates and remands without resolving immunity.
* **Remedial or remand consequences:** No remedial doctrine is reached on any path. A ruling against immunity decides nothing about liability, damages, or the availability of any other defense.
* **Uncertainty:** There are no findings; everything turns on allegations assumed true. The complaint's phrasing bears more than one reading: it may allege a single instruction to produce counsel by force, or an instruction to produce plus a separate instruction to use excessive force, plus subsequent ratification. Whether summary disposition is appropriate depends on whether this unresolved functional boundary can properly be decided on the present papers.

# CASE 3 of 10: CAMPOS v. CITY OF HOUSTON (IN CHAMBERS)
# SECTION I: NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

* **Case Name:** Campos v. City of Houston (in chambers)
* **Canonical Matter / Alias:** Jesse Campos, W. R. (Resendez) Morris, and Mexican American Bar Association of Houston v. City of Houston; Fifth Circuit No. 91-6100
* **Citation or Docket:** 502 U.S. 1301 (1991); Application No. A-301
* **October Term:** 1991
* **Entry Stage and Event:** Emergency application by plaintiffs-appellants to a Circuit Justice, seeking an injunction and a stay pending appeal, after denial of a stay by both the district court and the court of appeals. The application is presented in chambers to a single Justice, whose ruling would not be a holding of the full Court. The application may be referred to the full Court.
* **Simulated Event Date:** October 29, 1991 (historical in-chambers date)
* **Question Presented:** Whether to grant an injunction stopping the entire City of Houston election process for the November 5, 1991 City Council elections or, alternatively, to stay pending appeal the district court's October 12, 1991 order directing that those elections be conducted under a redistricting plan to which the Attorney General had interposed a timely objection under section 5 of the Voting Rights Act.
* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** United States District Court for the Southern District of Texas, order of October 12, 1991 (Hittner, J.), on the City's supplemental counterclaim; stay denied by that court October 17, 1991; stay denied by the Fifth Circuit October 24, 1991; appeal pending in the Fifth Circuit. The order under review is interlocutory.
* **Relief Requested:** Primarily an original injunction from the Court stopping the entire City election process. Alternatively, a stay of the district court's order.
* **Material Record:** Houston's charter prescribed a fifteen-member council, nine elected from single-member districts and six including the mayor elected at large. In April 1991 Hispanic citizens sued under section 2 of the Voting Rights Act and the Fourteenth Amendment, alleging that at-large elections dilute Hispanic voting strength, and sought to enjoin future at-large elections and to install a twenty-two-member council; the City's summary judgment motion remained pending. The 1990 census showed the nine districts significantly unequal in population. On June 5, 1991 the City adopted a redistricting plan for the nine districts (Plan 9-5-1 #2) and an alternative sixteen-district plan contingent on an August 10 charter election; the voters rejected the sixteen-district alternative. Both were submitted for preclearance on July 9. The Attorney General requested more information on August 21, the City completed its submission September 27, and on October 4 the Attorney General interposed a timely objection to Plan #2. On October 7 the City sought leave to file a supplemental counterclaim asking for a declaration that the malapportioned prior plan was unconstitutional and for entry of Plan #2 as an interim plan for the November 5 election; the Attorney General participated as amicus and objected to the court's jurisdiction. At an October 9 hearing City election officials testified that it would be physically impossible to hold the November 5 election under any plan other than Plan #2. Hours before the continued October 11 hearing the City Council adopted a different plan (Plan 9-5-1 #3) and submitted it by telefax. On October 12, before the district court ruled, the Attorney General precleared Plan #3 subject to reconsideration within the sixty-day period, and sent the letter to the court by telefax. That afternoon the district court ordered the November 5 election conducted under the unprecleared Plan #2, reasoning that Plan #3 should not be used because the Hispanic citizens objected to it and the Attorney General might change his mind. Some voters had already cast absentee ballots by the time the application was decided. The federal judges on the scene had all declined a stay.
* **Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** Whether a Circuit Justice possesses authority to issue an original injunction stopping an entire municipal election process, given that the 1990 revision of the Court's Rules removed the former express provision for writs of injunction by a single Justice. Whether the district court had jurisdiction or exceeded its authority in granting relief on the City's counterclaim, raised on two grounds: absence of a justiciable case or controversy, and the contention that the counterclaim was an action under section 5 triable only by a three-judge court. Whether stay relief was properly sought below first. The order is interlocutory, so the stay authority tied to final judgments subject to review on certiorari is not the natural source.
* **Relevant Dates:** October 4, 1991 objection; October 11, 1991 adoption and submission of Plan #3; October 12, 1991 conditional preclearance of Plan #3 and district court order; October 16-17, 1991 stay sought and denied below; October 18 and 24, 1991 stay sought and denied in the Fifth Circuit; October 29, 1991 application decided; November 5, 1991 scheduled election.
* **Companion or Related Matters:** United States v. City of Houston, No. H-91-3076 (S.D. Tex.), a separate section 5 enforcement action filed October 17, 1991 seeking to enjoin the November 5 election or, alternatively, special elections under a precleared plan; a three-judge panel refused the injunction on October 29, 1991, recording its understanding that the district court's order was a temporary expedient and that, if affirmed, it would require an election under a constitutional, precleared, or court-imposed plan in far less than two years. Watkins v. Mabus, 771 F. Supp. 789 (S.D. Miss. 1991), in which an application for injunction and stay was referred to the full Court and denied on August 20, 1991, with two Justices noting they would grant.
* **Participation Matter:** Under the circuit allotment in effect, the Fifth Circuit was assigned to Justice Scalia, and the office historically held by Rehnquist carried the District of Columbia, Fourth, and Federal Circuits. Whether this application reaches Stone at all therefore depends on facts the supplied instruction does not settle, and on whether the application is referred to the full Court. This is flagged, not resolved.

## B. LAW ENTERING THE CASE

* **Current Through:** Historical United States law effective immediately before October 29, 1991.
* **Earlier Same-Term Decisions:** None supplied. Ibarra and Mireles precede this matter on the calendar; no material dependency is identified.
* **Unresolved Same-Term Dependencies:** None identified.
* **Institutional or Procedural Law:** The Court's Rules governing applications to individual Justices and stays, as revised effective 1990; the All Writs Act, 28 U.S.C. § 1651(a); 28 U.S.C. § 2101(f) for stays of final judgments subject to review on certiorari; 28 U.S.C. § 42 on circuit allotment; the rule that an application will not ordinarily be entertained unless the relief was first sought below; and the referral power of the Justice to whom an application is submitted.

### Issue 1: Authority to issue the requested original injunction

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** None.
* **Other Governing Law:** The Court's Rules as revised in 1990, which dropped the former express authorization for a single Justice to grant a writ of injunction; 28 U.S.C. § 1651(a).
* **Material Other Authority:** None performing independent work.
* **Open Point:** The precise residual source and scope of single-Justice injunctive authority after the 1990 revision.

### Issue 2: Standard for a stay pending appeal from a Circuit Justice

* **Other Governing Law and Authority:** Republican State Central Committee of Arizona v. Ripon Society Inc., 409 U.S. 1222, 1224, 1227 (1972) (Rehnquist, J., in chambers), considers threatened irreparable injury, the probability of error below, and the public interest. Responsibility for delay bears on the equitable assessment. Ripon Society identifies 28 U.S.C. § 1651(a) and Johnson v. Stevenson, 335 U.S. 801 (1948), as authority for a Circuit Justice to stay the injunctive aspect of a district court order while an appeal is pending. Prior denials below call for circumspection without foreclosing relief. The stay preserves potential appellate jurisdiction; it does not depend on treating this interlocutory order as a final judgment under § 2101(f).
* **Open Point:** The weight to be given to the declination of a stay by the judges on the scene.

### Issue 3: Underlying authority of a section 2 court to order an interim election plan

* **Other Governing Law:** Voting Rights Act section 2, 42 U.S.C. § 1973; section 5, 42 U.S.C. § 1973c; 28 U.S.C. § 2284; Fourteenth Amendment population equality requirements.
* **Material Other Authority:** McDaniel v. Sanchez, 452 U.S. 130, 137-138, 153 (1981) (a genuinely judicial plan differs from a legislative proposal reflecting the covered jurisdiction's policy choices; judicial adoption of the latter does not remove the preclearance requirement). Upham v. Seamon, 456 U.S. 37 (1982). Clark v. Roemer, 500 U.S. 646, 652-655 (1991) (unprecleared changes are unenforceable, and section 5 plaintiffs ordinarily are entitled to an injunction against implementation; the Court reserved whether an extreme circumstance could justify permitting an election and distinguished relief after an election from permission to conduct it). Connor v. Finch, 431 U.S. 407 (1977). Perkins v. Matthews, 400 U.S. 379 (1971) (challenges to an objection are heard only in the District Court for the District of Columbia). United States v. Board of Supervisors, 429 U.S. 642, 645 (1977) (enforcement of unprecleared changes may be enjoined by a three-judge court in a suit by a voter or by the Attorney General; enforcement covers coverage, satisfaction of preclearance, and remedy).
* **Open Point:** Whether a single-judge section 2 court's entry of an unprecleared legislative plan as interim relief is an action under section 5 requiring a three-judge court, and, if not, what limits govern the choice among available interim plans. Their application to this interim order is pending in the Fifth Circuit. The distinction between judicial and legislative plans and the ordinary preclearance obligation are governed by the eligible authorities above; the possible scope of an extreme-circumstance exception remains reserved.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

* **Controlling reasoning below:** The district court identified three plans before it, the malapportioned prior plan, the objected-to Plan #2, and the just-adopted and conditionally precleared Plan #3, and chose Plan #2 on the stated grounds that the Hispanic plaintiffs objected to Plan #3 and that the Attorney General might reconsider his preclearance. The Fifth Circuit denied a stay. The three-judge panel in the separate enforcement action denied an injunction on the understanding that the order was temporary.
* **Material alternative or unreached grounds:** Three alternatives were available to the district court and were not adopted: enjoining the election pending preclearance of a new plan; devising a court plan, which would not require preclearance; or ordering the election under the conditionally precleared Plan #3, which the City supported.
* **Applicants' strongest supported position:** The order directs a municipal election under a plan the Attorney General timely objected to, which the covered jurisdiction may not enforce, and it does so while a precleared plan was available and on stated reasons that do not withstand examination, since the plaintiffs objected to Plan #2 as well. If the counterclaim is an action under section 5, a single judge could not enter it. Allowing the election to proceed places the burden of the City's delay on the voters the statute protects.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

* **Controlling reasoning below:** The district court read Will to bar the § 1983 claims because Hafer could not be held liable for employment decisions made in her official capacity as auditor general. The Third Circuit reversed as to the damages claims, finding that respondents sought damages from Hafer personally and holding that because she acted under color of state law an individual-capacity § 1983 action could be maintained; it sustained the official-capacity reinstatement claims on Will's own footnote.

* **Petitioner's strongest supported position:** Will's language about officials acting in their official capacities means that § 1983 does not authorize damages suits against state officers for damages arising from official acts. * **Respondents' strongest supported position:** Will decided who the defendant is, not what the defendant did. Section 1983 reaches persons acting under color of law, including misuse of power possessed by virtue of state law, so petitioner's reading would empty the statute of its central application. The Eleventh Amendment does not restrict suits seeking damages from an official personally. The burden on officials is the work of immunity doctrine, not of the definition of person.

* **Merits paths:** (i) State officials sued in their individual capacities are persons under § 1983 and the Eleventh Amendment does not bar such suits; affirm. * **Material Other Authority:** Leedom v. Kyne, 358 U.S. 184 (1958), permitting review of agency action taken in excess of delegated powers where preclusion would leave no other means of protecting the right at issue.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

* **Controlling reasoning below:** The Fifth Circuit read § 1818(i)(1)'s plain language to deprive the district court of jurisdiction to enjoin either proceeding, and vacated the injunction as to section 23A on that basis. It then read Leedom v. Kyne to authorize an injunction against an administrative proceeding conducted without statutory authorization, concluded that the Board's treatment of a holding company's failure to transfer assets to a troubled subsidiary as an unsafe or unsound practice under § 1818(b) was an unreasonable and impermissible construction of that term, and directed an injunction against enforcement of the source of strength regulation. The Bankruptcy Code does not qualify it: the Board's planned actions fall within the express police and regulatory exception to the automatic stay, and the stay provisions MCorp invokes have no application to ongoing nonfinal administrative proceedings. Section 1334(b) is inapposite because the Board is not another court. Leedom v. Kyne is unavailable because the statute supplies a meaningful and adequate opportunity to challenge the regulation's validity and application if and when the Board finds a violation, and expressly forecloses interim review.
* **MCorp's strongest supported position:** Preclusion of all interim review permits an agency to conduct proceedings it has no authority to conduct, and Leedom v. Kyne exists for exactly that case; where the agency's asserted authority is absent, there is no notice or order under the section for the bar to protect. Bankruptcy jurisdiction over property of the estate is exclusive, and prosecution of proceedings that would compel a debtor to transfer assets to subsidiaries reaches the estate. This is the strongest available position; it does not answer that § 1818(h)(2) supplies review of the regulation after a final order, nor that no order has issued.
* **Necessary concessions or limiting principles:** The Board concedes that the regulation's validity is reviewable after a final order. MCorp concedes that the Board's proceedings are regulatory in character.
* **Solicitor General or amicus position:** The United States appeared for the Board and pressed the plain-language jurisdictional argument.

* **Threshold paths:** Hold the district court without jurisdiction as to both proceedings. Hold jurisdiction available as to one or both. Hold the challenge to the regulation unripe pending a final order.

### Issue 1: Whether due process requires setting aside a general verdict where one alternative object lacked sufficient evidence

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** None.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** None yet operating in the simulation.
* **Other Governing Law:** Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment. 18 U.S.C. § 371, punishing a conspiracy to commit any offense against the United States or to defraud the United States or any agency in any manner or for any purpose, with an overt act. Fed. R. Crim. P. 7(c)(1), authorizing a single count to allege that an offense was committed by one or more specified means.
* **Material Other Authority:** Claassen v. United States, 142 U.S. 140, 146 (1891), restating the common-law rule that a general verdict on a multicount indictment stands if any one count is good and warrants the judgment. Yates v. United States, 354 U.S. 298, 312 (1957), setting aside a general verdict where one basis of conviction was legally inadequate and it was impossible to tell which the jury selected. Turner v. * **Open Point:** Whether Yates rested on due process, on supervisory power, or on neither, the opinion having invoked neither expressly.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

* **Controlling reasoning below:** The Seventh Circuit affirmed, reasoning that negating a verdict supported by evidence but possibly resting on an erroneous view of law differs from negating one on the remote chance that the jury convicted on a ground lacking adequate evidence when sufficient alternative grounds existed.
* **Material alternative or unreached grounds:** The Sixth Amendment theory and the supervisory-power theory.
* **Petitioner's strongest supported position:** Yates states that a verdict must be set aside where it is supportable on one ground but not another and it is impossible to tell which the jury selected, and that statement covers this case. Turner is distinguishable because assurance that the jury did not use the inadequate ground exists only where the prosecution presented no evidence at all on that theory; where some but insufficient evidence was offered, the impossible-to-tell rationale governs. The distinction between legal error and insufficiency is illusory in any event, since a judgment unsupported by the requisite minimum of proof is invalid as a matter of law and constitutionally required to be set aside.
* **Government's strongest supported position:** The common-law rule was settled in England before the Declaration of Independence and in this country long afterwards, so historical practice does not support the due process claim. Yates is the single decision extending Stromberg to a basis that violated no constitutional provision but was merely legally inadequate, and it was an unexplained extension. Turner, decided later, squarely holds that a general verdict stands if the evidence suffices as to any charged act, and a logical application of Turner requires that alternative facts in conspiracy cases be treated like alternative facts elsewhere. Petitioner's proposed distinction rewards the greater failure of proof and has no foundation in Turner or the presumption underlying it. Jurors are not equipped to detect that a theory is contrary to law, but they are well equipped to weigh evidence.
* **Necessary concessions or limiting principles:** The Government concedes the evidence was insufficient as to the DEA object. Continued adherence to Yates is not at issue; the Government asks only that it not be extended. Petitioner does not ask that Turner be overruled and offers no adequate basis for distinguishing it.
* **Solicitor General or amicus position:** The United States is respondent.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

* **Threshold paths:** Decline to reach the Sixth Amendment theory as undeveloped. Reach it.
* **Merits paths:** (i) Affirm on the ground that Turner rather than Yates governs, the line between legal inadequacy and evidentiary insufficiency being the controlling distinction. (ii) Affirm on the narrower ground that the objects of a § 371 conspiracy are alternative means of committing a single offense, so no element went unproved. (iii) Reverse, extending Yates to evidentiary insufficiency. (iv) Exercise supervisory power to require removal of unsupported theories from the jury's consideration in federal trials, without a constitutional holding.
* **Judgment consequences:** Affirmance leaves the conviction and sentence intact. Reversal would vacate the conviction on Count 20 and require a new trial, and would carry consequences well beyond conspiracy counts, reaching for example an indictment charging murder by shooting or drowning where proof of drowning failed.
* **Remedial or remand consequences:** None on affirmance.
* **Uncertainty:** The risk of jury confusion in a 23-count, multiple-defendant, five to six week trial is a matter of judgment on which the record permits differing assessments. What Yates was grounded in is genuinely unclear from its own text.

* **Case Name:** Estelle v. McGuire
* **Canonical Matter / Alias:** Estelle, Warden v. McGuire
* **Citation or Docket:** 502 U.S. 62 (1991); No. 90-1074
* **October Term:** 1991
* **Entry Stage and Event:** Certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit in a federal habeas corpus proceeding challenging a state conviction; argued October 9, 1991; submitted for decision.
* **Simulated Event Date:** December 4, 1991 (historical decision date)
* **Question Presented:** Whether the admission of evidence of a child victim's prior injuries to establish battered child syndrome, and the instruction given on the use of prior act evidence, so infused a state criminal trial with unfairness as to deny due process and warrant federal habeas relief.
* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** Ninth Circuit, 902 F.2d 749 (1990), reversing the district court's denial of the writ and granting habeas relief.
* **Relief Requested:** By petitioner warden, reversal and reinstatement of the conviction. By respondent, affirmance.
* **Material Record:** Respondent was convicted in California of the second-degree murder of his six-month-old daughter. He and his wife brought the baby to a hospital; she was bluish and not breathing. The attending physician noted a large and relatively recent chest bruise with multiple bruises around it. Two physicians testified that the child was a battered child who had suffered prior injuries, including rectal tearing at least six weeks old and partially healed rib fractures approximately seven weeks old. No direct evidence linked respondent to the prior injuries, but respondent and his wife were the only two people regularly caring for the child. Witnesses also described respondent carrying the child by one arm and roughly pinching her cheeks, acts distinct from the rib fractures and rectal tearing for which no actor was directly identified. Respondent's wife testified under a grant of immunity that she had inflicted the fatal injuries. The jury deliberated for three days. No claim was made at trial that the death was accidental. The trial court introduced its limiting instruction by describing evidence offered to show other similar acts committed by respondent, grouping the identified rough handling with the unattributed earlier injuries. It instructed that the prior injury evidence could be considered for the limited purpose of determining whether it tends to show a clear connection between the other two offenses and the one charged, so that it may be logically concluded that if the defendant committed other offenses he also committed the crime charged; the court also instructed that the evidence, if believed, was not received and could not be considered to prove that respondent is a person of bad character or that he has a disposition to commit crimes. The California Court of Appeal affirmed, holding the prior injury evidence patently proper under state law to establish battered child syndrome, which exists when a child has sustained repeated or serious injuries by nonaccidental means. The California Supreme Court denied review. The federal district court denied habeas relief.
* **Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** The scope of federal habeas review of state convictions is itself at issue. The Ninth Circuit rested in part on its conclusion that the evidence was erroneously admitted under state law. Whether the instructional question was properly within the scope of review and, if error is found, whether harmlessness should be decided in the first instance or on remand, are both live.
* **Relevant Dates:** Prior injuries approximately six and seven weeks before death. Conviction and state appellate affirmance preceding the federal petition. Ninth Circuit decision 1990. Argued October 9, 1991; decided December 4, 1991.
* **Companion or Related Matters:** None material.
* **Participation Matter:** Justice Thomas took no part. Eight Members participating. Counsel for respondent was appointed by the Court.

### Issue 2: The prior act instruction

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

* **Controlling reasoning below:** The Ninth Circuit concluded the trial was arbitrary and fundamentally unfair in violation of due process. It held the prior injury evidence erroneously admitted to establish battered child syndrome because no evidence linked respondent to the prior injuries and no claim of accident was made at trial, and held that the prior act instruction allowed a finding of guilt based simply on a judgment that respondent had committed the prior acts.
* **Material alternative or unreached grounds:** Harmlessness was not addressed below because relief was granted outright.
* **Petitioner's strongest supported position:** The battered child evidence was probative on the intent with which the person who injured the child acted, because it showed the death resulted from an intentional act by someone rather than an accident, and the absence of a defense claim of accident did not relieve the prosecution of proving every element beyond a reasonable doubt. A federal habeas court may not reexamine state-law evidentiary determinations, and the Ninth Circuit's reliance on state-law error was itself improper. The instruction's own words left to the jury whether respondent committed the prior acts and permitted use of the evidence only if the jury so believed, and the court expressly forbade use of the evidence to show bad character or disposition. Circumstantial evidence permitted identification of respondent as the prior abuser, even without direct evidence, because he and his wife were the child's only regular caregivers.
* **Respondent's strongest supported position:** The instruction told the jury that the prior injury evidence had been admitted to show similar offenses and that on finding a clear connection it could logically conclude that if respondent committed the other offenses he also committed the charged crime. So framed, it encouraged the jury to assume he inflicted the prior injuries and then directed it to conclude that the prior abuser was the murderer, which may have relieved the State of proving identity beyond a reasonable doubt. The relevance of battered child syndrome to nonaccidental injury does not establish the abuser's identity. Even if circumstantial evidence could support identifying respondent, the jury still had to determine that predicate and decide whether to draw the further inference; the charge could not treat those determinations as already made. His wife's testimony claiming responsibility for the fatal injuries makes the disputed identity question material.
* **Necessary concessions or limiting principles:** Petitioner must accept that a state instruction which relieves the prosecution of an element presents a federal question cognizable on habeas. Respondent must accept that the evidence itself was relevant and properly admitted, that state-law error is not a ground for federal habeas relief, and that the limiting instruction forbidding character and disposition use cuts against the propensity reading.
* **Solicitor General or amicus position:** The United States filed as amicus urging reversal, as did the Criminal Justice Legal Foundation; the National Association of Criminal Defense Lawyers and others filed urging affirmance. None materially alters the analysis.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

* **Threshold paths:** Confine review to federal constitutional questions and set aside the state-law ground relied on below. Decline to reach the instructional question as outside the scope of review.
* **Merits paths:** (i) Neither the admission nor the instruction violated due process; reverse outright. (ii) The admission did not violate due process, but respondent establishes a reasonable likelihood that the instruction was applied to relieve the State of proving identity; hold it constitutional error and remand for a harmless-error determination. (iii) Assume or find constitutional instructional error, but hold that the State has proved it harmless beyond a reasonable doubt on the whole record; reverse the grant of relief. (iv) Affirm the grant of habeas relief on an independently established federal constitutional error that the State has not proved harmless, without relying on state-law error as a ground for the writ.
* **Judgment consequences:** Paths (i) and (iii) reverse the grant of federal habeas relief. Path (ii) vacates the appellate judgment granting relief and leaves entitlement to the writ for resolution on remand; this disposition does not itself vacate the state conviction. Path (iv) leaves the writ granted, requiring retrial or release according to its terms.
* **Remedial or remand consequences:** On path (ii) the Court of Appeals would decide whether the State has proved beyond a reasonable doubt that the instructional error did not contribute to the verdict, applying Chapman and Rose to the whole trial record.
* **Uncertainty:** The instruction is ambiguous on its face and reasonable readers differ on whether the surrounding instructions cured it. The record does not reveal what the jury in fact concluded. The presence of circumstantial evidence identifying a possible prior abuser does not itself establish how the jury understood the instruction or whether an error contributed to its verdict.

* **Controlling reasoning below:** The district court granted summary judgment on two grounds: that as a matter of law respondent was not a Jones Act seaman because the platforms were not vessels in navigation and he was aboard to perform ship repair rather than to aid in navigation; and, more importantly for review, that he was a harbor worker precluded from suing by § 905(a). The Ninth Circuit reversed both, holding that questions of fact existed as to seaman status, including whether the platforms were vessels in navigation, whether the relationship was permanent, and whether he aided in their navigation, and holding that because the LHWCA by its terms does not cover a master or member of a crew of any vessel, a phrase equivalent to seaman under the Jones Act, the question of status should have gone to a jury. It rejected the notion that any employee whose work involved ship repair was necessarily restricted to the LHWCA, reasoning that coverage turns on the nature of the work and congressional intent rather than on job title.
* **Material alternative or unreached grounds:** The merits of negligence, unseaworthiness, and maintenance and cure are untouched.
* **Petitioner's strongest supported position:** Where a claimant's job fits within one of the occupations enumerated in the definition of employee, the fact-intensive status inquiry may be resolved as a matter of law, which is the Fifth Circuit's approach; longshoremen, shipbuilders, and ship repairers meeting the situs requirement are unqualifiedly covered and thereby ineligible for consideration as seamen. Decisions holding the LHWCA the exclusive remedy for certain injured railroad workers otherwise able to sue under the Federal Employers' Liability Act show that an enumerated relationship can displace a negligence action. Where a worker is arguably covered, the district court should stay the Jones Act suit pending a final administrative determination, as the relation of the Federal Employees' Compensation Act to the Federal Tort Claims Act illustrates. Receipt of LHWCA benefits should preclude a later Jones Act suit, and equitable estoppel should bar it.
* **Respondent's strongest supported position:** The definition of employee expressly excludes a master or member of a crew of any vessel, so the exclusive remedy provision cannot cover a harbor worker who is also a crew member; the excluded phrase is a refinement of seaman. It is not the employee's particular job that is determinative but his connection to a vessel, and a ship repairman may spend all his working hours aboard a vessel in furtherance of its mission, even one used exclusively in repair work. The statute anticipates that such suits will be brought and provides for tolling where recovery is denied on coverage grounds, and it credits Jones Act recoveries against LHWCA liability, which removes any double-recovery concern.
* **Necessary concessions or limiting principles:** Respondent must accept that in many cases a ship repairman will lack the requisite connection to a vessel in navigation, and that a worker is limited to LHWCA remedies where no genuine issue of fact exists on seaman status. Petitioner has no answer to the express subparagraph (G) exclusion, which is fatal to the categorical position, and the railroad cases are inapposite because the statute contains no comparable exclusion for railroad workers.
* **Solicitor General or amicus position:** The United States appeared as amicus urging affirmance. Amici urging reversal included Global Marine and the Shipbuilders Council of America, the latter pressing the equitable estoppel and election of remedies arguments; the United Brotherhood of Carpenters and Joiners filed urging affirmance.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

* **Threshold paths:** Hold the LHWCA exclusive as a matter of law for enumerated occupations. Hold that exclusivity depends on the absence of a genuine factual issue as to crew membership. Stay the action pending administrative determination.
* **Merits paths:** (i) A worker in an enumerated occupation may be a Jones Act seaman; the exclusive remedy provisions do not bar the suit unless no genuine issue of fact exists on crew membership; affirm. (ii) Enumerated occupation forecloses seaman status; reverse. (iii) Affirm on the exclusivity question but hold that a stay pending administrative determination is required.
* **Judgment consequences:** Affirmance leaves summary judgment reversed and returns the case for resolution of the factual questions bearing on seaman status, with the Jones Act, unseaworthiness, and maintenance and cure claims proceeding. Reversal ends the Jones Act suit and confines respondent to LHWCA remedies.
* **Remedial or remand consequences:** No remedy is reached. Any Jones Act recovery would be credited against LHWCA liability under § 903(e).
* **Uncertainty:** Whether the platforms are vessels in navigation and whether respondent's connection to them suffices are genuinely disputed and unresolved. The Fifth Circuit's contrary rule was in some question following a later decision in that circuit, and the en banc proceeding that might have settled it was dismissed on settlement.

## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

* **Case Name:** Wooddell v. International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers, Local 71
* **Citation or Docket:** 502 U.S. 93 (1991); No. 90-967
* **October Term:** 1991
* **Entry Stage and Event:** Certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit, granted to address two questions; argued October 16, 1991; submitted for decision.
* **Simulated Event Date:** December 4, 1991 (historical decision date)
* **Question Presented:** Whether a union member who sues his local union for money damages under Title I of the Labor-Management Reporting and Disclosure Act is entitled to a jury trial; and whether the subject-matter jurisdiction conferred by § 301(a) of the Labor-Management Relations Act extends to a suit by an individual union member alleging violation of a union constitution.
* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** Sixth Circuit, judgment order reported at 907 F.2d 151 (1990), reversing the dismissal of the LMRDA free speech and job discrimination claim but otherwise affirming, including the holding that there was no right to a jury trial on the LMRDA claim, and holding on the basis of prior circuit precedent that § 301 did not authorize a breach-of-contract action by an individual union member for violation of a union constitution.
* **Relief Requested:** By petitioner, reversal on both questions and remand. By respondents, affirmance.
* **Material Record:** Petitioner is a member of Local 71 of the International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers. Following a dispute arising from his opposition to an announced dues increase and to the appointment of a union representative, the local's president, who is petitioner's brother, filed internal disciplinary proceedings against him; no decision was finally rendered on the charges. Petitioner alleges that the union then discriminated against him in job referrals in the operation of a hiring hall provided for in the local's collective-bargaining contracts with electrical contractors. His complaint alleged violation of LMRDA-protected rights through discrimination in referrals because of his opposition to proposed union policy, violation of his LMRDA right to a fair hearing, violations of the IBEW Constitution and the local's bylaws alleged to be breaches of contract redressable under § 301 and state law, breach of the duty of fair representation, and pendent state claims for interference with contractual relations and intentional infliction of emotional distress. He sought injunctive relief, lost wages and benefits, additional compensatory damages, punitive damages, and attorney's fees. The amended complaint alleged that the IBEW Constitution requires all local unions to live up to all collective bargaining agreements, and that the Constitution and the local's bylaws are contracts binding on Local 71; in its amended answer the local admitted those allegations. The record sets out selected provisions of the IBEW Constitution, including rules for local unions requiring locals to honor their contracts and requiring IBEW approval of local bylaws and agreements; the local's charter, constitution if any, and relevant bylaws are not in the record. That the IBEW and Local 71 are labor organizations representing employees in an industry affecting commerce is undisputed.
* **Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** The § 301 question as presented is one of subject-matter jurisdiction; petitioner's personal standing is not disputed before the Court. The case was briefed and argued below before Teamsters v. Terry was decided, and the decision below neither cites nor discusses it. There is a circuit conflict on the § 301 question.
* **Relevant Dates:** Internal disciplinary proceedings and referral discrimination alleged; district court dismissal of all claims against all defendants in the course of acting on two summary judgment motions; Sixth Circuit judgment order 1990; Teamsters v. Terry decided March 20, 1990, before the decision below; certiorari granted at 498 U.S. 1082 (1991); argued October 16, 1991; decided December 4, 1991.
* **Companion or Related Matters:** None material.
* **Participation Matter:** Justice Thomas took no part. Eight Members participating.

### Issue 2: Section 301(a) jurisdiction over an individual member's suit on a union constitution

* **Other Governing Law:** LMRA § 301(a), 29 U.S.C. § 185(a), providing that suits for violation of contracts between an employer and a labor organization representing employees in an industry affecting commerce, or between any such labor organizations, may be brought in any district court of the United States having jurisdiction of the parties, without respect to the amount in controversy or the citizenship of the parties.
* **Material Other Authority:** Smith v. Evening News Assn., 371 U.S. 195, 198, 200-201 (1962) (the word between refers to contracts rather than suits; § 301 suits are not limited to the contracting parties, and an individual employee may sue under § 301 for violation of an employer-union contract; individual claims lie at the heart of the grievance and arbitration machinery, and a contrary holding would allow individual contract terms to bear different meanings under state and federal law, exerting a disruptive influence on the negotiation and administration of collective agreements). Plumbers and Pipefitters v. Plumbers and Pipefitters, Local 334, 452 U.S. 615, 624 (1981) (union constitutions were at the time of enactment, and remain, probably the most commonplace form of contract between labor organizations, so Congress would not likely have used the unqualified term contract without intending to encompass them; § 301 was enacted to supply federal jurisdiction for enforcement of contracts made by labor organizations and to counteract jurisdictional defects in state courts). Teamsters v. Lucas Flour Co., 369 U.S. 95, 103 (1962).
* **Open Point:** Whether the merits of the alleged breaches state a claim, and whether the individual officer defendants are proper defendants, are not presented.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

* **Controlling reasoning below:** On the jury trial question the Sixth Circuit affirmed the denial without citing or discussing Terry. On the § 301 question it relied on prior circuit precedent holding that § 301 does not authorize such an action by an individual union member.
* **Material alternative or unreached grounds:** The merits of the contract claims; the duty of fair representation claim; the pendent state claims.
* **Petitioner's strongest supported position:** On the jury right, although he seeks injunctive relief as well as damages, the injunctive relief is incidental to the damages rather than the reverse, and his claim for lost wages cannot be treated as restitutionary incident to reinstatement, because the damages sought are pay for jobs to which the union failed to refer him rather than pay for a job from which he was terminated; and LMRDA actions are closely analogous to personal injury actions, the prototypical action at law. On § 301, the IBEW Constitution is a contract between labor organizations; his complaint alleges that it requires locals to honor collective bargaining agreements and that it and the bylaws bind the local, allegations the local admitted; and § 301 is not limited to suits by parties to the interunion contract but extends to individual members who are its beneficiaries.
* **Respondents' strongest supported position:** On § 301, jurisdiction reaches only suits by the parties to the interunion contract, and third-party suits to enforce it lie outside the grant; construing the section to reach member suits is an unwarranted intrusion on state contract law, since the federalization of union-member relations should be confined to the provisions Congress enacted in the later LMRDA; and the construction will inundate the federal courts with trivial intraunion litigation. On the jury trial question, respondents now concede that Terry controls.
* **Necessary concessions or limiting principles:** Respondents concede Terry's application. Petitioner must accept that the answer on § 301 is affirmative only where the breach alleged violates a contract between two labor organizations, and that personal standing and the merits are separate questions. Respondents' intrusion and floodgates arguments have no supporting evidence, the construction having been the law in several circuits for years, including a decision a decade earlier involving this same union constitution.
* **Solicitor General or amicus position:** The Association for Union Democracy and others filed as amici urging reversal. No government position.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

* **Threshold paths:** Decide both questions. Decide the jury question and remand the § 301 question. Dismiss the § 301 question as not properly presented.
* **Merits paths:** (i) A jury trial right attaches to the LMRDA damages claim, and § 301(a) jurisdiction extends to an individual member's suit alleging violation of a contract between labor organizations; reverse and remand on both. (ii) A jury right attaches but § 301 reaches only suits by contracting parties. (iii) Neither.
* **Judgment consequences:** Path (i) reverses and remands with the LMRDA claim triable to a jury and the § 301 claim within federal jurisdiction, leaving every merits question open. Path (ii) or (iii) leaves the § 301 claim to state court and state law.
* **Remedial or remand consequences:** No remedy is reached. Nothing decided establishes breach, entitlement, or damages, or that the officer defendants may be held liable.
* **Uncertainty:** The local's charter, constitution if any, and relevant bylaws are not in the record, so the contractual structure is known only in part. The merits of the referral discrimination allegations are untested.
