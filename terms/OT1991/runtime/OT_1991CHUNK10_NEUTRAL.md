# Chemical Waste Management, Inc. v. Hunt

# SECTION I: NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

* **Case Name:** Chemical Waste Management, Inc. v. Hunt
* **Citation or Docket:** 504 U.S. 334 (1992); No. 91-471.
* **October Term:** 1991.
* **Entry Stage and Event:** MERITS, review of a discriminatory hazardous-waste disposal charge.
* **Simulated Event Date:** June 1, 1992, chronology only.
* **Question Presented:** Record-grounded formulation: Does Alabama's additional charge on hazardous waste generated outside the State violate the Commerce Clause despite asserted environmental, capacity, and fiscal interests?
* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** Alabama Supreme Court, 584 So.2d 1367, reversing the trial court's invalidation of the additional charge.
* **Relief Requested:** Invalidate the discriminatory additional charge and obtain corresponding relief in the state proceeding.
* **Material Record:** Chemical Waste Management operates a private commercial hazardous-waste facility at Emelle. Alabama imposed a base charge of $25.60 per ton on covered hazardous waste and an additional $72 per ton for waste generated outside Alabama. The trial court found no distinction between the waste streams apart from origin. Alabama invokes finite disposal capacity, environmental and transportation hazards, and future costs. Other provisions regulating total volume do not make the entire regulatory scheme the question presented.
* **Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** Hazardous-waste disposal is commercial activity; the State's environmental objective does not eliminate commerce coverage. The additional charge is facially keyed to origin. This is not a challenge to every disposal fee or a request for an unrestricted operating permit.
* **Relevant Dates:** Alabama Act No. 90-326 enacted in 1990; judgment under review in 1991.
* **Companion or Related Matters:** Fort Gratiot follows this matter in the controlling index, although both share a date; its later disposition cannot supply entering law.
* **Participation Matter:** Stone replaces Rehnquist; no other nonparticipation identified.

## B. LAW ENTERING THE CASE

* **Current Through:** Immediately before June 1, 1992.
* **Earlier Same-Term Decisions:** No effective simulated decisions supplied.
* **Unresolved Same-Term Dependencies:** Wyoming v. Oklahoma bears directly on discrimination against interstate goods if its actual simulated ruling reaches the relevant rule. Quill may affect a tax-specific proposition only if actually adopted; its collection-nexus issue is distinct. Do not import an assumed Fort Gratiot result.

### Issue 1: Origin discrimination and environmental justification

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** None supplied.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** None supplied.
* **Other Governing Law:** Philadelphia v. New Jersey, 437 U.S. 617 (1978), subjects exclusion of out-of-state waste to the Commerce Clause and rejects origin alone as a quarantine distinction. Facially discriminatory burdens are presumptively invalid. Hunt v. Washington State Apple Advertising Commission, 432 U.S. 333 (1977), places on the State the burden of establishing a legitimate local purpose that cannot adequately be served by reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives. Maine v. Taylor, 477 U.S. 131 (1986), sustains an import restriction supported by distinctive biological risks and unavailable adequate inspection alternatives. Pike v. Bruce Church, 397 U.S. 137 (1970), supplies a different balancing branch for evenhanded incidental burdens, not the presumptive standard for express origin discrimination.
* **Material Other Authority:** Complete Auto, 430 U.S. 274 (1977), independently requires nondiscrimination for state taxes. An actual compensatory-tax theory requires identification of the corresponding intrastate burden and substantial equivalence; a general claim that residents pay taxes does not establish the comparison. Constitutional review is independent; the demanding justification is not a criminal proof quantum.
* **Open Point:** Whether Alabama has demonstrated an origin-linked difference or unavailable neutral alternative sufficient to justify this additional charge.

### Issue 2: Relief after an invalid exaction

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** None supplied.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** None supplied.
* **Other Governing Law:** McKesson Corp. v. Division of Alcoholic Beverages and Tobacco, 496 U.S. 18 (1990), requires meaningful backward-looking relief where the State coerces payment first and provides only later review. State law supplies the appropriate remedial vehicle and may permit constitutionally adequate equalization alternatives; constitutional invalidity does not determine every refund computation.
* **Material Other Authority:** The state court must distinguish prospective relief against the differential from any independently available claim for recovery of payments; a refund is not assumed merely from a request for an injunction.
* **Open Point:** The necessary state remedial implementation, including payments and procedural prerequisites actually established on remand.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

The operator argues that identical hazardous waste cannot be charged differently merely because it crosses a state line, and that neutral fees and capacity rules address the asserted risks. Alabama argues that large incoming volumes threaten health, consume a limited resource, increase transport risks, and expose the State to costs that warrant discouraging outside waste. The state supreme court accepted the local purposes and absence of adequate alternatives. The operator answers that legitimate ends do not establish the required discrimination-specific justification: an imported ton and an Alabama ton consume capacity and create the same disposal hazard on the findings made.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

The Court may reverse under the discrimination branch, sustain the charge if Alabama meets that branch's demands, or remand if a material factual distinction remains unresolved. It should not apply ordinary Pike balancing merely because the State's purpose is environmental. Invalidating the differential leaves distinct neutral provisions for appropriate separate treatment and requires a remedy matching the charge actually challenged. The Court need not invent a comprehensive replacement waste program.

[Statute, findings, and proceedings, 504 U.S. 334, pp. 336-341](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/504/334/); [Philadelphia, 437 U.S. 617](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/437/617/); [Hunt, 432 U.S. 333](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/432/333/); [Maine v. Taylor, 477 U.S. 131](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/477/131/); [McKesson, 496 U.S. 18](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/496/18/).

# Fort Gratiot Sanitary Landfill, Inc. v. Michigan Department of Natural Resources

# SECTION I: NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

* **Case Name:** Fort Gratiot Sanitary Landfill, Inc. v. Michigan Department of Natural Resources
* **Citation or Docket:** 504 U.S. 353 (1992); No. 91-636.
* **October Term:** 1991.
* **Entry Stage and Event:** MERITS, review of dismissal of a Commerce Clause challenge to waste-import restrictions.
* **Simulated Event Date:** June 1, 1992, chronology only.
* **Question Presented:** Record-grounded formulation: May Michigan authorize counties to exclude waste originating outside the county from private disposal facilities unless the county plan expressly permits importation?
* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** Sixth Circuit, 931 F.2d 413, affirming dismissal of the operator's complaint.
* **Relief Requested:** Declaratory and prospective injunctive relief against the origin restrictions.
* **Material Record:** Michigan's Solid Waste Management Act requires county planning for disposal needs and facility approval. The 1988 Waste Import Restrictions bar a facility from accepting waste generated outside its county unless the county plan explicitly authorizes it. The restriction covers other Michigan counties as well as other States and countries. St. Clair County denied this private landfill operator's 1989 request to accept outside waste. No distinct danger inhering in the proposed waste because of its origin was found below.
* **Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** This is state regulation of a private disposal business, not a State selling space at its own facility. The complaint challenges an operative import prohibition, not an abstract objection to county planning. No need to prove that every Michigan county excludes all outside waste arises merely from the existence of multiple county plans.
* **Relevant Dates:** Original state planning statute in 1978; import restrictions in 1988; application denied in 1989.
* **Companion or Related Matters:** Chemical Waste Management precedes this matter in the controlling index. Integrate its actual effective discrimination holding when supplied; its proposed or historical result is not simulated law.
* **Participation Matter:** Stone replaces Rehnquist; no other nonparticipation identified.

## B. LAW ENTERING THE CASE

* **Current Through:** Immediately before June 1, 1992.
* **Earlier Same-Term Decisions:** No effective simulated decisions supplied.
* **Unresolved Same-Term Dependencies:** Wyoming v. Oklahoma is material to discrimination against interstate commerce to the extent of its actual simulated holding. Quill's collection-nexus dispute is distinct. The index supplies the sequence, including the earlier Chemical Waste Management matter; its actual effective holding must be checked before this case is simulated.

### Issue 1: County-origin exclusion

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** None supplied.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** None supplied; the historical authorities below remain the opening baseline.
* **Other Governing Law:** The Commerce Clause reaches commerce in waste. Philadelphia v. New Jersey, 437 U.S. 617 (1978), rejects exclusion of out-of-state waste on origin alone. Brimmer v. Rebman, 138 U.S. 78 (1891), and Dean Milk v. Madison, 340 U.S. 349 (1951), demonstrate that a local geographical line can discriminate against interstate trade despite also disadvantaging some intrastate commerce. Under Hunt v. Washington State Apple Advertising Commission, 432 U.S. 333 (1977), the State must justify discriminatory means by a legitimate local purpose that reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives cannot adequately serve. Maine v. Taylor, 477 U.S. 131 (1986), illustrates satisfaction through distinctive risks and unavailable adequate inspection alternatives. Evenhanded incidental regulation instead receives Pike balancing, 397 U.S. 137 (1970).
* **Material Other Authority:** Hughes v. Alexandria Scrap, 426 U.S. 794 (1976), and Reeves v. Stake, 447 U.S. 429 (1980), distinguish market participation from regulation. A state mandate controlling private facilities cannot acquire that exception simply because counties plan public services. Congressional authorization must actually encompass the discrimination; no identified federal provision expressly authorizes this origin rule.
* **Open Point:** Whether evenhanded treatment of all out-of-county sources removes discrimination, or whether the interests in planning and responsibility justify the restriction under the demanding branch.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

The operator argues that the rule closes a local market to interstate waste and protects local access to private disposal capacity. Michigan and county respondents emphasize comprehensive planning, local responsibility for capacity, environmental risks, and the identical treatment of other Michigan counties. The Sixth Circuit accepted the county-based structure as distinguishable from a state-line exclusion. The operator answers that moving the boundary inward does not remove its burden on interstate sellers and that neutral capacity, permitting, and safety regulations can preserve planning without an origin preference. Respondents' strongest position concerns the functional demands of the planning system; it does not establish a factual difference between otherwise identical tons of waste.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

The Court may reverse under the discrimination doctrine, sustain the structure as evenhanded and assess its incidental burdens, or remand for further justification if the existing record leaves a material discrimination-specific issue unresolved. A ruling against the import restriction does not require that the landfill receive an unrestricted permit, accept every kind of waste, or exceed neutral capacity limits. Remedial implementation must identify the enforcing defendants and the operative restriction rather than displace the full county planning statute.

## E. SOURCES

[Statute, request, and proceedings, 504 U.S. 353, pp. 355-359](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/504/353/); [Philadelphia, 437 U.S. 617](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/437/617/); [Brimmer, 138 U.S. 78](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/138/78/); [Dean Milk, 340 U.S. 349](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/340/349/); [Maine v. Taylor, 477 U.S. 131](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/477/131/); [Reeves, 447 U.S. 429](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/447/429/).

# Morales v. Trans World Airlines, Inc.

# SECTION I: NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

* **Case Name:** Morales v. Trans World Airlines, Inc.
* **Citation or Docket:** 504 U.S. 374 (1992); No. 90-1604.
* **October Term:** 1991.
* **Entry Stage and Event:** MERITS, review of an injunction against threatened state enforcement of airline-advertising guidelines.
* **Simulated Event Date:** June 1, 1992, chronology only.
* **Question Presented:** Record-grounded formulation: Does the Airline Deregulation Act preempt enforcement of the challenged fare-advertising guidelines through general consumer-protection law, and how far may the federal injunction extend?
* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** Fifth Circuit, 949 F.2d 141, affirming the challenged permanent injunction.
* **Relief Requested:** The state Attorney General seeks relief from the injunction; the airlines defend protection against the threatened enforcement.
* **Material Record:** The National Association of Attorneys General adopted detailed guidelines covering airline practices, including advertised fares, availability, qualifications, and restrictions. Texas threatened enforcement of its consumer-protection law against specified fare advertising. Airlines sought federal equitable relief before enforcement began. The injunction extended beyond the identified fare-advertising controversy to other advertising and airline practices relating to rates, routes, or services. The specific threatened rules and the larger abstract category must be distinguished.
* **Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** A concrete threatened enforcement carrying potentially substantial repeated sanctions supports the request for preventive relief. The record does not establish a threatened enforcement dispute concerning every conceivable airline operation. Preemption and the scope of equitable relief are independent questions.
* **Relevant Dates:** Guidelines adopted in 1987 and revised in 1988; litigation followed threatened enforcement.
* **Companion or Related Matters:** No earlier listed case directly construes this statutory preemption clause. Later term preemption disputes cannot supply entering law.
* **Participation Matter:** Souter did not participate; Stone replaces Rehnquist among the participating Justices.

## B. LAW ENTERING THE CASE

* **Current Through:** Immediately before June 1, 1992.
* **Earlier Same-Term Decisions:** No effective simulated decisions supplied.
* **Unresolved Same-Term Dependencies:** Revalidate an earlier actual simulated holding only if it changes a relevant statutory-construction or equitable-remedy principle. No concrete earlier decision is identified as settling this clause. Same-day matters supply no assumed intervening rule.

### Issue 1: Express preemption and the savings clause

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** None supplied.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** None supplied.
* **Other Governing Law:** 49 U.S.C. App. § 1305(a)(1) prohibits state enactment or enforcement of a law, rule, regulation, standard, or other provision having the force of law relating to air-carrier rates, routes, or services within its coverage. The Federal Aviation Act's § 1506 preserves existing common-law and statutory remedies. Section 1381 assigns the federal agency authority over unfair or deceptive air-carrier practices. The express preemption and savings provisions must both receive operative scope.
* **Material Other Authority:** Shaw v. Delta Air Lines, 463 U.S. 85 (1983), interprets similar relational language in ERISA but also recognizes limits for remote connections. The analogy informs ordinary meaning; it does not substitute ERISA's whole structure for this statute. Metropolitan Life Insurance v. Massachusetts, 471 U.S. 724 (1985), treats congressional intent as the preemption inquiry, including the enacted text and statutory structure. The fact that a state law is generally applicable neither automatically defeats nor automatically establishes express preemption. The legal question receives independent review.
* **Open Point:** Whether the particular state-enforced fare requirements fall within the clause and how the savings provision limits the asserted breadth.

### Issue 2: Equitable channel and injunction scope

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** None supplied.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** None supplied.
* **Other Governing Law:** Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908), permits appropriate prospective relief against a state officer enforcing allegedly unlawful restrictions when threatened sanctions make awaiting enforcement an inadequate practical remedy. 
* **Material Other Authority:** Public Service Commission v. Wycoff, 344 U.S. 237 (1952), cautions against deciding hypothetical future regulatory disputes. A successful preemption argument does not authorize an injunction beyond a concrete threatened injury.
* **Open Point:** The separable protection warranted against the identified fare rules and the unsupported remainder of the injunction.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

The airlines argue that detailed fare-marketing requirements relate directly to rates and restore state regulatory controls Congress displaced. Texas argues that prohibiting deception through generally applicable consumer law leaves airlines free to choose fares and fits the savings clause. It warns that a broad relational interpretation would eliminate ordinary consumer remedies. The airlines answer that statutory substance, not a general consumer-law label, controls, and point to the federal unfair-practices authority. The court of appeals sustained the injunction's broad formulation. Texas's objection to that breadth remains distinct from whether the specifically threatened fare requirements are preempted.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

The Court may sustain preemption of the identified fare rules while narrowing the injunction; reject preemption and dissolve the relevant protection; or differentiate particular requirements if their operation materially differs. The federal agency's authority does not itself establish that every state remedy is displaced. No First Amendment ruling is needed if the statute disposes of the threatened enforcement. Rules against unrelated harms and an actual fraud claim with materially different elements require their own analysis, not advance adjudication through an unlimited injunction.

## E. SOURCES

[Guidelines, threats, and proceedings, 504 U.S. 374, pp. 378-383, and guideline appendix](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/504/374/); [Shaw, 463 U.S. 85](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/463/85/); [Metropolitan Life, 471 U.S. 724](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/471/724/); [Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/209/123/); [Wycoff, 344 U.S. 237](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/344/237/).

# Burdick v. Takushi

# SECTION I: NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

* **Case Name:** Burdick v. Takushi
* **Citation or Docket:** 504 U.S. 428 (1992); No. 91-535.
* **October Term:** 1991.
* **Entry Stage and Event:** MERITS, review of a First and Fourteenth Amendment challenge to Hawaii's write-in prohibition.
* **Simulated Event Date:** June 8, 1992, chronology only.
* **Question Presented:** Record-grounded formulation: Does the complete prohibition on write-in voting impermissibly burden voters when considered within Hawaii's full nomination and election system?
* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** Ninth Circuit, 937 F.2d 415, reversing summary judgment and injunctive relief for Burdick.
* **Relief Requested:** Provision for casting, counting, and publishing write-in votes in primary and general elections.
* **Material Record:** In 1986 only one candidate filed for Burdick's state legislative district; Burdick wished to vote for someone who had not filed and sought relief for future elections as well. After abstention and certification, Hawaii's supreme court held in 1989 that state law prohibits write-ins and does not constitutionally require them. New parties qualify by obtaining signatures from one percent of registered voters 150 days before the primary. Party candidates file 15 signatures for legislative or county office, or 25 for statewide or federal office, 60 days before the primary. Established parties need not repeat the new-party petition if they meet statutory criteria. Nonpartisan candidates can reach the primary through the same small candidate-signature requirement and deadline. To advance, a nonpartisan must obtain ten percent of the primary vote or the vote sufficient to nominate a partisan, whichever is lower. A primary voter selects a single party or nonpartisan ballot for all offices. Respondents report that eight of twenty-six nonpartisans advanced over the preceding ten years; this is a reported litigation submission, not a new judicial demographic finding.
* **Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** The authoritative state interpretation resolves the initial state-law uncertainty. Burdick challenges the write-in prohibition, not each ballot-access provision independently; the system's operation remains relevant to the burden of the challenged rule. His demand includes a political-expression theory broader than voting for an eligible candidate.
* **Relevant Dates:** Initial dispute in 1986; state construction in 1989; court of appeals judgment in 1991.
* **Companion or Related Matters:** Norman v. Reed and Burson v. Freeman precede this date in the inventory, but their actual simulated decisions have not been supplied.
* **Participation Matter:** Stone replaces Rehnquist; no other nonparticipation identified.

## B. LAW ENTERING THE CASE

* **Current Through:** Immediately before June 8, 1992.
* **Earlier Same-Term Decisions:** No effective simulated decisions supplied.
* **Unresolved Same-Term Dependencies:** Norman is material to meaningful ballot access and the relevant degree of justification. Burson concerns political speech near polls, a different regulatory setting; any actual rule must be applied within its scope. The present case's future name cannot label an already adopted combined test.

### Issue 1: Magnitude of the electoral burden and sufficient justification

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** None supplied.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** None supplied.
* **Other Governing Law:** Anderson v. Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780 (1983), considers the character and magnitude of injury to voting and associational rights, the State's precise interests, their legitimacy and strength, and the necessity of the burden. Reasonable nondiscriminatory election regulations generally can be justified by important regulatory interests; severe restrictions demand substantially more, including narrow tailoring to compelling interests where strict scrutiny applies. Every routine election rule does not trigger strict scrutiny. The challenger identifies the injury and operation of the rule; the State supplies the interests justifying it. No special trial proof quantum follows merely from selecting the scrutiny level.
* **Material Other Authority:** Storer v. Brown, 415 U.S. 724 (1974), permits reasonable nomination and disaffiliation rules and recognizes interests in political stability; voters do not have an absolute entitlement to immediate candidacies. Jenness v. Fortson, 403 U.S. 431 (1971), evaluates meaningful alternative access rather than formal identity of all routes. Lubin v. Panish, 415 U.S. 709 (1974), rejects a prohibitive fee barrier and does not treat write-ins as an automatic cure for a defective printed-ballot scheme. Rosario v. Rockefeller, 410 U.S. 752 (1973), permits a timely enrollment requirement against party raiding; Tashjian v. Republican Party, 479 U.S. 208 (1986), protects party associational choice within its actual setting. Voting selects officials; the ballot is not a general public forum for every expressive message.
* **Open Point:** Whether the full write-in ban adds a slight administrative burden or substantially forecloses meaningful electoral choice, and whether the asserted interests justify its scope in both election stages.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

Burdick argues that he cannot vote for his preferred person and that the absence of write-ins must be assessed together with the nomination routes and deadlines. He also claims an expressive entitlement to record protest votes, including votes for fictional figures. Hawaii emphasizes low candidate-signature requirements, alternative nomination routes, predictable deadlines, party stability, prevention of general-election sore-loser candidacies, and prevention of primary raiding. It also points to provisions making certain unopposed primary victors officeholders without a further general election. The Ninth Circuit found access sufficiently easy and the interests adequate. Burdick responds that the single-ticket primary structure can make the nonpartisan route costly to voters wishing to participate in other party races, and that a total ban reaches candidates and circumstances unrelated to raiding or losing a primary. The legal validity of selecting one primary ticket does not by itself determine the combined burden of an additional prohibition.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

The Court may affirm if the full system leaves only a limited burden supported by legitimate regulatory needs, or reverse if the actual burden and breadth exceed those justifications. It may reject the protest-message theory while recognizing a narrower electoral claim. The choice is not between an absolute right to every write-in and unreviewable legislative control. Relief must leave room for lawful qualifications and administration; the Court need not independently invalidate the party, signature, deadline, or primary-ticket provisions. Hypothetical late candidate scandals illustrate a possible mechanism, not a fact that occurred in Burdick's election.

## E. SOURCES

[Record, state construction, and statutory routes, 504 U.S. 428, pp. 430-436; litigants' arguments discussed at pp. 438-440 and 442-451](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/504/428/); [Anderson, 460 U.S. 780](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/460/780/); [Storer, 415 U.S. 724](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/415/724/); [Jenness, 403 U.S. 431](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/403/431/); [Lubin, 415 U.S. 709](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/415/709/); [Rosario, 410 U.S. 752](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/410/752/).

# Eastman Kodak Co. v. Image Technical Services, Inc.

# SECTION I: NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

* **Case Name:** Eastman Kodak Co. v. Image Technical Services, Inc.
* **Citation or Docket:** 504 U.S. 451 (1992); No. 90-1029.
* **October Term:** 1991.
* **Entry Stage and Event:** MERITS, review of reversal of antitrust summary judgment.
* **Simulated Event Date:** June 8, 1992, chronology only.
* **Question Presented:** Record-grounded formulation: Does competition in the equipment market preclude, as a matter of law, the asserted power in replacement-parts and service markets, and does this record permit trial of the tying and monopolization claims?
* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** Ninth Circuit, 903 F.2d 612, reversing summary judgment for Kodak.
* **Relief Requested:** Kodak seeks restoration of summary judgment; the independent service organizations seek continuation of their antitrust action.
* **Material Record:** Independent organizations began servicing Kodak copiers and micrographic equipment. Kodak later restricted their access to replacement parts, including through arrangements with original-equipment suppliers. Respondents present evidence of distinct demand for parts and service, prior separate provision, restrictions steering customers toward Kodak service, and exceptions for customers servicing their own machines. Their evidence supports estimates near complete control of compatible parts and eighty to ninety-five percent of Kodak-equipment service, together with higher prices and exclusion of service competitors. They also present information costs in predicting lifetime service expense and substantial costs of replacing installed equipment. Kodak disputes the implications and invokes competition for new equipment and legitimate business reasons. These are summary-judgment materials and inferences, not established liability findings.
* **Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** Review follows truncated discovery and Kodak's summary-judgment motion. Draw reasonable evidentiary inferences for respondents without treating every allegation or economic possibility as proved. A single brand is not automatically a legally separate market simply because its parts are proprietary.
* **Relevant Dates:** Independent servicing began in the early 1980s; challenged restrictions followed; appellate judgment in 1990.
* **Companion or Related Matters:** No earlier listed case resolves these aftermarket questions. Holmes v. Securities Investor Protection Corp. may matter if an actual simulated causation holding applies to a later damages issue, but a RICO holding cannot replace the Sherman and Clayton Act elements here.
* **Participation Matter:** Stone replaces Rehnquist; no other nonparticipation identified.

## B. LAW ENTERING THE CASE

* **Current Through:** Immediately before June 8, 1992.
* **Earlier Same-Term Decisions:** No effective simulated decisions supplied.
* **Unresolved Same-Term Dependencies:** Revalidate any actual Holmes proposition only within a genuinely presented antitrust-remedy issue; no direct same-term aftermarket rule is supplied. The present summary-judgment question does not require a final damages determination.

### Issue 1: Summary judgment and tying

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** None supplied.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** None supplied.
* **Other Governing Law:** Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 56; Celotex v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317 (1986), and Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, 477 U.S. 242 (1986), require a genuine dispute on a material element under the applicable trial burden. The movant can identify an absence of proof on an element respondents must establish; respondents then need competent evidence, not unsupported allegations. Justifiable inferences favor the nonmovant. Matsushita v. Zenith, 475 U.S. 574 (1986), requires evidence capable of supporting a reasonable inference, with implausibility relevant in its economic and evidentiary context.
* **Material Other Authority:** Sherman Act § 1 and Jefferson Parish Hospital District No. 2 v. Hyde, 466 U.S. 2 (1984), require an agreement conditioning access to a tying product on a distinct tied product, appreciable economic power in the tying market, and a noninsubstantial affected volume of tied commerce for the asserted per se tying theory. Separate products depend on distinct demand, not merely separate physical components. The applicable civil proof burden remains on plaintiffs at trial; denying summary judgment does not shift ultimate liability to Kodak.
* **Open Point:** Whether equipment competition necessarily defeats the proposed aftermarket-power inference, despite evidence concerning installed customers, information, switching, and actual competitive effects.

### Issue 2: Monopolization and business justification

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** None supplied.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** None supplied.
* **Other Governing Law:** Sherman Act § 2; United States v. Grinnell, 384 U.S. 563 (1966), requires monopoly power in a properly defined market and willful acquisition or maintenance, distinct from success through superior product, business acumen, or historical accident. Attempted monopolization separately requires exclusionary conduct, specific intent, and dangerous probability; possession of a large share is not automatically a completed or attempted offense. Aspen Skiing v. Aspen Highlands, 472 U.S. 585 (1985), treats the actual conduct and asserted business reasons as material without establishing a universal duty to help competitors. Brunswick v. Pueblo Bowl-O-Mat, 429 U.S. 477 (1977), requires antitrust injury for private damages; competitor losses alone are insufficient.
* **Material Other Authority:** Market definition concerns actual substitutes and competitive constraints; parts compatibility, installed-equipment switching costs, and information can matter. A lawful patent or differentiated product alone does not establish unlawful exclusion. Quality assurance, inventory management, and prevention of free riding must be evaluated as actual explanations, not presumed pretexts or automatic immunity.
* **Open Point:** Whether the evidence permits relevant market, power, and exclusion findings, and whether Kodak's asserted reasons eliminate the disputed issues as a matter of law.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

Kodak argues that aftermarket exploitation would increase lifetime equipment costs and drive customers to competing equipment, defeating any durable power. It warns that allowing a branded aftermarket claim can penalize ordinary integrated distribution and deter useful service arrangements. Respondents answer with the experience of installed customers, imperfect lifecycle information, switching costs, separate service demand, and actual price and exclusion evidence. They contend that these facts prevent treating equipment competition as a conclusive legal defense. Kodak also offers quality, inventory, and free-riding justifications; respondents point to separate-service practices and restrictions inconsistent with those explanations. The Ninth Circuit found genuine disputes and rejected the proposed categorical inference. The United States appeared as amicus urging reversal; no additional position from its unexamined brief is invented here.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

The Court may affirm continuation of the action if reasonable factfinders could find the disputed elements, reverse if respondents lack proof on a necessary element, or differentiate the § 1 and § 2 theories. Neither economic theory nor a market-share percentage substitutes for a record-based market and conduct inquiry. An order allowing trial does not establish an antitrust violation, award treble damages, require compulsory licensing, or declare a general obligation to supply rivals. Specific issues beyond the motion remain for ordinary proceedings.

## E. SOURCES

# United States v. Thompson/Center Arms Co.

# SECTION I: NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

* **Case Name:** United States v. Thompson/Center Arms Co.
* **Citation or Docket:** 504 U.S. 505 (1992); No. 91-164.
* **October Term:** 1991.
* **Entry Stage and Event:** MERITS, review of a National Firearms Act tax-refund judgment.
* **Simulated Event Date:** June 8, 1992, chronology only.
* **Question Presented:** Record-grounded formulation: Does distributing the Contender pistol with a conversion kit having both lawful long-rifle and short-rifle configurations constitute making a short-barreled rifle under the Act before that configuration is assembled?
* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** Federal Circuit, 924 F.2d 1041, reversing Claims Court summary judgment for the Government, 19 Cl. Ct. 725.
* **Relief Requested:** The United States seeks restoration of tax liability; the manufacturer seeks the $200 refund for the registered unit.
* **Material Record:** The Contender is a single-shot pistol with a ten-inch barrel. In 1985 the manufacturer also offered a kit with a twenty-one-inch barrel, shoulder stock, and fore-end. The assembled kit with the long barrel produces a rifle outside the Act's short-barrel category; adding the stock while retaining the pistol barrel produces a short-barreled rifle. The parts also retain their pistol use. ATF advised that possession or distribution of the pistol and kit together constituted an NFA firearm. The company paid $200 for one identified unit, applied for approval, and filed a refund claim. It brought suit after more than six months without action. No actual assembly of the taxable short-barreled configuration is established for the unit at issue.
* **Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** The Tucker Act, 28 U.S.C. § 1491, supplies the refund suit's jurisdictional vehicle. This is civil statutory classification, not a criminal trial, a Second Amendment claim, or an exemption from all firearms regulation. The Federal Circuit's broad actual-assembly rationale and the particular dual-use package are distinct questions.
* **Relevant Dates:** Product and administrative advice in 1985; Claims Court judgment in 1990; Federal Circuit judgment in 1991.
* **Companion or Related Matters:** United States v. R. L. C. is an earlier listed criminal-construction case requiring actual simulated revalidation.
* **Participation Matter:** Stone replaces Rehnquist; no other nonparticipation identified.

## B. LAW ENTERING THE CASE

* **Current Through:** Immediately before June 8, 1992.
* **Earlier Same-Term Decisions:** No effective simulated decisions supplied.
* **Unresolved Same-Term Dependencies:** An actual R. L. C. holding may affect the sequence of interpretive tools and lenity. Draft outcomes are not law; the controlling index also orders same-date matters, whose earlier effective simulated holdings govern to the extent relevant.

### Issue 1: Statutory making and a dual-use package

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** None supplied.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** None supplied.
* **Other Governing Law:** 26 U.S.C. § 5821 imposes the making tax; §§ 5822 and 5841 govern prior approval and registration. Section 5845(a)(3) includes rifles with barrels shorter than sixteen inches in the NFA definition of firearm. Section 5845(c) defines rifle in terms of design or redesign, making or remaking, and intended shoulder firing. Section 5845(i) includes manufacturing, putting together, altering, combinations of these, and otherwise producing within “make.” The Act expressly addresses combinations of parts in certain other definitions, including machineguns; that drafting choice bears on, but does not conclusively settle, the distinct rifle definition.
* **Material Other Authority:** The ordinary tools include language, the defined terms, related provisions, and the scheme's operation. The Federal Circuit requires actual assembly; United States v. Drasen, 845 F.2d 731 (7th Cir. 1988), treats a complete unassembled short-rifle parts kit as covered. Neither lower decision supplies a binding Supreme Court resolution of this package.
* **Open Point:** Whether “manufacturing” or “otherwise producing” reaches all unassembled combinations, and whether the genuine unregulated configurations distinguish this package from one usable only to produce an NFA firearm.

### Issue 2: Criminal applications and remaining ambiguity

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** None supplied.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** None supplied.
* **Other Governing Law:** Sections 5861 and 5871 supply criminal consequences for specified violations, including unapproved making and possession of unregistered firearms. Crandon v. United States, 494 U.S. 152 (1990), applies lenity to unresolved ambiguity in a provision with criminal applications even in a civil proceeding. Lenity follows serious use of the interpretive tools; it is not a substitute for them or a new evidentiary burden.
* **Material Other Authority:** United States v. Freed, 401 U.S. 601 (1971), concerns the Act's criminal mental-state framework in its own registration setting. It does not justify importing later firearm cases or deciding every element of a hypothetical prosecution here. A civil refund claimant retains the applicable refund burden; the criminal proof standard does not become the burden for this civil action.
* **Open Point:** Whether genuine uncertainty remains about this dual-use configuration and should receive a uniform narrow construction.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

The Government argues that a ready-to-assemble firearm does not escape the Act merely through packaging, and that a set containing all necessary short-rifle components has been manufactured even if a buyer must join them. The manufacturer invokes the rifle's defined shoulder-fired configuration, the absence of assembly, the lawful pistol and long-rifle uses, and Congress's express treatment of parts elsewhere. The Government warns that an assembly-only rule undermines regulation; the company answers that the Government's reading reaches a package affirmatively configured for lawful uses. The Federal Circuit accepted the broader actual-assembly requirement and declined to follow Drasen.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

The Court may affirm the refund narrowly for the dual-use package without adopting an across-the-board actual-assembly requirement; adopt that broader requirement; or reverse because the complete short-rifle option suffices under the statutory definition. Any lenity rationale must identify what remains ambiguous after construction. No criminal culpability finding or prospective administrative permission for every parts arrangement follows from the civil refund. The Court need not prescribe how customers must store or sequence assembly of otherwise lawful configurations.

The Federal Circuit and Drasen conflict is documented in the primary case record at 504 U.S. 509.

# Sochor v. Florida

# SECTION I: NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

* **Case Name:** Sochor v. Florida
* **Citation or Docket:** 504 U.S. 527 (1992); No. 91-5843.
* **October Term:** 1991.
* **Entry Stage and Event:** MERITS, capital sentencing direct review.
* **Simulated Event Date:** June 8, 1992, chronology only.
* **Question Presented:** Record-grounded formulation: Was Florida's heinousness aggravator constitutionally applied, and did appellate review adequately cure the use of an unsupported cold, calculated, and premeditated aggravator in the penalty process?
* **Question Granted:** The primary opinion's procedural account at 504 U.S. 532 identifies a grant limited to the heinousness application and appellate treatment of the improper aggravator, citing 502 U.S. 967. That documented limitation excludes the other two petition questions; no same-day grant is inferred.
* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** Florida Supreme Court, 580 So.2d 595, affirming the death sentence after rejecting the coldness aggravator as unsupported.
* **Relief Requested:** Relief from the death sentence and a constitutionally adequate sentencing determination or appellate correction.
* **Material Record:** Sochor was convicted of first-degree murder and kidnapping after attempting rape and strangling a conscious victim. The penalty jury received four aggravating circumstances, including heinousness and coldness, and could consider statutory and nonstatutory mitigation. It recommended death ten to two without specifying which factors it found. The judge found all four aggravators and no mitigation and imposed death. Florida's supreme court held the jury-instruction vagueness challenge unpreserved and alternatively rejected it. It sustained the judge's heinousness finding based on the victim's fear and suffering, but held that the heightened premeditation required for coldness was absent. It nevertheless affirmed, emphasizing three remaining aggravators, no mitigation, and proportionality to other cases.
* **Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** Separate the procedurally barred jury-instruction claim from the judge's use of heinousness, the jury's possible use of coldness, and the judge's documented use of coldness. The advisory jury's general recommendation does not reveal its findings. The State's no-mitigation finding is the sentencing record, not a license to suppress evidence or infer that aggravator counting alone controls.
* **Relevant Dates:** Offense on New Year's Eve 1981; state supreme court decision in 1991.
* **Companion or Related Matters:** Griffin, Estelle v. McGuire, and Stringer are earlier inventory cases whose effective simulated holdings require particular attention.
* **Participation Matter:** Stone replaces Rehnquist; no other nonparticipation identified.

## B. LAW ENTERING THE CASE

* **Current Through:** Immediately before June 8, 1992.
* **Earlier Same-Term Decisions:** No effective simulated decisions supplied.
* **Unresolved Same-Term Dependencies:** Stringer directly concerns invalid aggravators in a weighing system. Griffin concerns general verdicts and unsupported factual grounds; Estelle may affect instruction review and the limits of federal error. Their historical same-term holdings cannot be imported through citations in the comparator. Revalidate the surviving rule on each relevant issue before voting.

### Issue 1: Jurisdiction over the jury's heinousness instruction

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** None supplied.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** None supplied.
* **Other Governing Law:** Michigan v. Long, 463 U.S. 1032 (1983), and Herb v. Pitcairn, 324 U.S. 117 (1945), bar Supreme Court disturbance of a judgment resting on an adequate independent state ground. A clear state procedural ruling is not erased by an alternative federal merits discussion. The petitioner must establish why the asserted ground does not foreclose the requested federal review; this is a jurisdictional inquiry, not harmless-error analysis.
* **Material Other Authority:** Preterm Coleman v. Thompson, 501 U.S. 722 (1991), distinguishes state procedural independence and adequacy. Direct review here is not a new habeas innocence-gateway proceeding.
* **Open Point:** Whether the state preservation ground is adequate and independent; the record supplies no developed basis for rejecting it.

### Issue 2: Heinousness as applied by the sentencing judge

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** None supplied.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** None supplied.
* **Other Governing Law:** Godfrey v. Georgia, 446 U.S. 420 (1980), and Maynard v. Cartwright, 486 U.S. 356 (1988), require meaningful narrowing of vague capital aggravators. Walton v. Arizona, 497 U.S. 639 (1990), permits a judge's application of an authoritative narrowing construction and presumes judicial familiarity with it. Florida's narrowing cases concerning conscious-victim strangulation bear on this application, distinct from whether every use of the statutory words is sufficiently confined.
* **Material Other Authority:** The Eighth Amendment inquiry is not a free-standing demand for a preferred instruction at every stage. Florida bears its state-law burden of proving aggravating circumstances beyond a reasonable doubt; the federal question also tests the legal boundary used.
* **Open Point:** Whether a consistent relevant narrowing principle supplies sufficient guidance here despite asserted inconsistency in other factual settings.

### Issue 3: Unsupported coldness, the jury, and appellate correction

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** None supplied.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** None supplied.
* **Other Governing Law:** Clemons v. Mississippi, 494 U.S. 738 (1990), requires correction when a weighing sentencer relies on an invalid aggravator: lawful appellate reweighing, constitutional harmless-error review, or resentencing. Parker v. Dugger, 498 U.S. 308 (1991), requires review of the actual sentencing record rather than a materially mistaken premise. Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18 (1967), places on the State the burden of showing beyond a reasonable doubt that the constitutional error did not contribute to the sentence. Proportionality comparison is not the same inquiry as either harmlessness or reweighing. Zant v. Stephens, 462 U.S. 862 (1983), addresses a nonweighing structure and cannot be transferred without that distinction.
* **Material Other Authority:** Stromberg v. California, 283 U.S. 359 (1931), and Yates v. United States, 354 U.S. 298 (1957), address general verdicts including legally invalid grounds. Whether the jury should be presumed to have used an evidentially unsupported factor requires the actual simulated Griffin rule, not automatic transplantation from a legally prohibited ground. The judge's express finding independently establishes that he considered coldness here.
* **Open Point:** Whether the jury presents an additional error; whether the appellate discussion substantively establishes harmlessness; and what correction Florida law allows. Merely using or omitting particular words is not the constitutional standard.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

Sochor argues that the vague heinousness instruction affected a jury with an important role in Florida sentencing, that inconsistent narrowing fails to guide judges, and that removal of coldness without reweighing or harmless-error review leaves an invalid death sentence. Florida invokes preservation, the consistent conscious-strangulation application, uncertainty about the jury's actual factors, and the state supreme court's three-aggravator/no-mitigation discussion as a sufficient harmlessness determination. Its strongest harmlessness argument concerns the substance of that discussion and cited cases, not a requirement of particular vocabulary. Sochor answers that the paragraph evaluates proportionality and counts factors without assessing the invalid factor's contribution to this sentence.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

The Court may decline the barred instruction claim yet decide preserved sentencing questions. It may reject the as-applied heinousness challenge and still grant relief for coldness. If the appellate treatment establishes lawful reweighing or Chapman harmlessness, affirmance remains available; otherwise the judgment requires remand for a lawful correction. The Court need not decide a separate uncertain jury-coldness theory if the judge's documented error independently requires the same remand. No automatic life sentence follows from removal of one factor, and a new jury proceeding is not invariably compelled if a constitutionally and legally adequate alternative correction exists.
