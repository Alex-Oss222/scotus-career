# United States v. Felix

## Event

**United States v. Felix** — No. 90-1599; OT1991.

Argued 1992-01-14; decided 1992-03-25. Certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1); the grant preceded argument. Review concerns the reversal of conspiracy count 1 and substantive counts 2–6.

**Question:** Does evidentiary overlap bar prosecution of the distinct Oklahoma substantive offenses or the conspiracy whose acts include conduct involved in the Missouri prosecution?

**Material record and posture:** The Missouri prosecution concerned attempted manufacture during August 26–31, 1987. Evidence of the earlier Oklahoma manufacturing operation was admitted to establish intent; that evidentiary use did not enlarge the charged Missouri offense. The later Oklahoma prosecution concerned the earlier completed acts and a broader agreement. The Tenth Circuit reversed counts 1–6 under Grady but affirmed the travel convictions, counts 9–10, with sentencing proceedings. No prior acquittal or issue-preclusion finding controls this record.

**Render form: compact.** Basis: 9–0 judgment; no material comparator change. White replaces the historical Chief as author; the same broad and narrow coalitions and disposition are preserved.

## Holdings

### 1. Reused evidence and separate substantive offenses

**Holding and operative rule.** The use of evidence of an uncharged crime in one trial does not itself place the defendant in jeopardy for that crime. The later Oklahoma substantive charges concern different acts from the Missouri attempt and are not barred merely because evidence of those acts helped prove intent in Missouri.

**Authority.** White, Part I, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas: nine votes make this an Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that evidence of one crime is not a prosecution for that crime. Dowling permits evidence to perform a legitimate function in a prosecution without treating every described act as an offense adjudicated there. Grady likewise rejects a rule forbidding the reuse of evidence; its inquiry concerns conduct used to establish an essential element of an already prosecuted offense. Missouri charged an attempted manufacture in late August. The Oklahoma manufacturing offenses occurred earlier and elsewhere. Their admission as evidence of intent in Missouri neither changed that indictment nor submitted the Oklahoma offenses for conviction. The contrary argument would make the scope of jeopardy depend on everything mentioned at trial and would erase the distinction Dowling preserves. The Court decides only the double-jeopardy objection. It does not approve every use of other-act evidence or decide an objection to the admission of particular proof.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Dowling v. United States, 493 U.S. 342 — applied: evidentiary use does not itself constitute prosecution for the described offense.
- Grady v. Corbin, 495 U.S. 508 — applied and confined to essential-conduct analysis; its express rejection of a same-evidence test remains operative.

### 2. Agreement remains a distinct offense

**Holding and operative rule.** A conspiracy and its substantive objects are distinct offenses; prosecution of the substantive crime does not bar prosecution of the agreement merely because the same acts help prove both. The overlapping Missouri conduct therefore does not bar this Oklahoma conspiracy.

**Authority.** White, Part II, joined by Stone, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: seven votes control this rationale; Stevens and Blackmun agree only with the judgment on a narrower ground.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that an agreement to commit crimes is not the same offense as the crimes committed under it. Bayer explains that the agreement has elements and dangers independent of its execution; Pinkerton and Iannelli preserve that distinction even when the offenses share proof. The conspiracy charged here embraces an agreement and additional conduct extending beyond the Missouri attempt. The presence of that attempt among the acts used to prove the conspiracy does not transform the agreement into the previously prosecuted attempt. Grady does not silently displace this settled body of conspiracy law. Garrett reinforces the need to examine a continuing or multilayered course of criminal activity with attention to its actual structure. The Court rejects the circuit’s contrary use of evidentiary overlap. It neither overrules Grady nor creates a same-transaction requirement, and it leaves ordinary evidentiary restrictions and materially different successive-prosecution records for their own cases.

**Precedent treatment.**

- United States v. Bayer, 331 U.S. 532 — applied: the agreement is distinct from its execution.
- Pinkerton v. United States, 328 U.S. 640 — applied: substantive crime and conspiracy remain separately punishable offenses.
- Iannelli v. United States, 420 U.S. 770 — applied: conspiracy’s independent character survives overlapping proof.
- Garrett v. United States, 471 U.S. 773 — applied by analogy to the treatment of multilayered criminal conduct.
- Grady v. Corbin, 495 U.S. 508 — clarified without overruling: it does not abrogate the established conspiracy/substantive-offense distinction.

### Limits and questions not reached

Distinct evidentiary use does not itself create prior jeopardy for an uncharged offense. The established agreement/substantive-offense distinction governs this conspiracy. Grady remains in force within its proper scope; ordinary evidentiary objections and a different single-incident successive prosecution remain open.

# Barnhill v. Johnson

## Event

**Barnhill v. Johnson** — No. 91-159; OT1991.

Argued 1992-01-14; decided 1992-03-25. Certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1); review of the honor-date ruling in the trustee’s preference action.

**Question:** For the preference period under 11 U.S.C. §547(b), does the transfer by ordinary check occur on delivery or honor?

**Material record and posture:** The ordinary check was delivered November 18, dated November 19, and honored November 20. The parties’ chronology places honor on the ninetieth day before the bankruptcy petition. Delivery preceded the preference window. Before payment, the drawer retained control of the account and could stop payment. The state-law rules distinguish the payee’s instrument and claim against the drawer from any interest in bank funds.

## Holdings

### 1. Honor determines the §547(b) transfer

**Holding and operative rule.** For §547(b), an ordinary check transfers the debtor’s relevant bank-account interest when the drawee honors it, because delivery gives the payee no assigned interest in that account. The November 20 honor date therefore satisfies the ninety-day timing element here, without deciding other preference elements or defenses.

**Authority.** Stone, joined by White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: seven votes support the rule and explanation.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the debtor parts with the bank funds when the bank pays the ordinary check. McKenzie makes transfer timing a federal question while looking to state law for the property transferred; Butner likewise respects state-created property interests. Under the governing commercial law, delivery creates an instrument and a claim against the drawer, but it assigns none of the account. The drawer may stop payment, and intervening events may prevent honor. The statutory word conditional does not turn the payee’s claim against the drawer into bank-account property that state law has not conveyed. Section 547(e) does not relate the transfer back to a time before that interest passed. The competing delivery rule therefore identifies the wrong property event. Wolas preserves the separate ordinary-course defense; it supplies no contrary transfer date. The Court leaves §547(c), nonordinary payment instruments and the remaining preference elements undisturbed.

**Precedent treatment.**

- McKenzie v. Irving Trust Co., 323 U.S. 365 — applied: federal transfer law operates on property interests identified by state law.
- Butner v. United States, 440 U.S. 48 — applied: state law defines the relevant property interest absent a contrary federal rule.
- Union Bank v. Wolas, current-term decision December 11, 1991 — left intact: ordinary-course eligibility and proof remain distinct from §547(b) timing.

### Limits and questions not reached

For §547(b), honor fixes transfer of the relevant account interest by ordinary check. No creditor automatically loses every payment honored within ninety days: the trustee must establish the remaining elements, and available defenses remain. Nonordinary instruments and §547(c) timing are not decided.

The honor-date ruling stands. Further administration of the preference claim remains subject to its other elements and available defenses; no additional factual finding or remedial order is invented. No subsequent event is established.

# National Railroad Passenger Corp. v. Boston & Maine Corp.

## Event

**National Railroad Passenger Corp. v. Boston & Maine Corp.** — No. 90-1419 and 90-1769; OT1991.

Argued 1992-01-13; decided 1992-03-25. Consolidated certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1); review of the circuit’s setting aside the ICC conveyance order. Both dockets receive the same judgment.

**Question:** Does §562(d), as amended, authorize this acquisition and reconveyance, and does the ICC’s actual decision adequately establish the statutory conditions and a public use?

**Material record and posture:** The Montrealer used B&M’s 48.8-mile segment. After maintenance problems and service suspension, Amtrak arranged acquisition and immediate reconveyance to Central Vermont, with rehabilitation, twenty-year passenger rights and continued freight access for B&M. Amtrak made a take-it-or-leave-it purchase offer. B&M proposed discussing upgrades while declining the sale proposal. The ICC found inability to agree, allowed Amtrak to elect the acquisition remedy, applied the statutory need presumption, and ordered conveyance with $2,373,286 compensation. The circuit rejected the transaction; Judge Ginsburg would instead remand for adequate findings. A 1990 amendment expressly authorizes service-promoting reconveyance and applies to pending cases. Its applicability here is undisputed. Maintenance allegations are not converted into independent judicial findings. Valuation, competition and other issues left unresolved by the circuit remain open.

**Render form: compact.** Basis: 6–3 with the same final non-Stone alignment, controlling grounds and remand as the verified comparator. Preassembly reconciliation is recorded internally and does not itself make a historical departure.

The same nine Justices participate at argument/submission and decision; no case-specific nonparticipation is established. Six qualified Justices constitute a quorum; five affirmative votes are required for a controlling merits proposition. 

## Public Action

The D.C. Circuit’s judgments in both dockets are reversed and the cases remanded, 6–3. The ICC acted within the acquisition authority and satisfied the challenged statutory prerequisites. The circuit must resolve the compensation challenges and other issues it did not decide.

## Holdings

### 1. Passenger-service acquisition and reconveyance

**Holding and operative rule.** Section 562(d) permits the ICC’s construction that property required for passenger service includes property usefully and appropriately acquired for Amtrak’s rail operations; it does not require indispensable permanent Amtrak ownership. The statutory need presumption and express service-promoting reconveyance authority therefore permit this transaction, subject to the statute’s independent conditions.

**Authority.** Kennedy, joined by Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia and Souter: six votes support this holding and explanation.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that planned reconveyance does not defeat the acquisition authority. Chevron requires a court to examine the statutory text and structure and to accept an administering agency’s permissible resolution of a remaining ambiguity. The word required can bear more than an indispensable-ownership meaning. Reading it that narrowly would drain the presumption of need of its force and frustrate Congress’s express authorization of reconveyance. K mart and Everhart direct attention to the whole enactment, not an isolated dictionary choice. Amtrak will use this track for passenger service, while Central Vermont will maintain it under the arrangement. The statute allows Amtrak a reasonable business judgment within those service-related limits, subject to the ICC’s oversight and the specified rebuttal. The contrary interpretation adds a permanent-ownership condition Congress did not enact. The Court leaves compensation and distinct regulatory objections for the remaining review proceedings.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. NRDC, 467 U.S. 837 — applied: permissible agency construction governs the unresolved statutory ambiguity.
- K mart Corp. v. Cartier, Inc., 486 U.S. 281 — applied: statutory text and structure constrain deference.
- Sullivan v. Everhart, 494 U.S. 83 — applied: the meaning of a disputed term is assessed within the whole statute.
- Lechmere, Inc. v. NLRB, current-term decision January 27, 1992 — preserved: no prior binding construction is displaced here.

### 2. The agency’s discernible ground

**Holding and operative rule.** A reviewing court may recognize an interpretation necessarily embodied in the agency’s own decision even when the agency did not state that interpretation in the exact words later used by counsel. That rule sustains this ICC order because its application of the need presumption and its disposition of the competing remedies disclose the statutory construction; it does not permit a court to supply a new agency ground.

**Authority.** Kennedy, joined by Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia and Souter: six votes support this holding and explanation.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that Chenery requires the agency’s ground, not a prescribed verbal formula. The ICC rejected the contention that Amtrak must seek trackage rights first, treated the passenger-service use as sufficient to invoke the presumption, and examined the two specified means of rebutting need. Its actual analysis necessarily rests on a construction that does not demand indispensable ownership. Recognizing that construction explains the decision the Commission made; it does not replace it with a court’s preferred policy. State Farm’s prohibition against counsel’s later rationalizations remains fully applicable when the agency’s reasoning cannot be found in its decision. The dissent’s objection would be decisive in that situation, but this record discloses the operative premise. The Court therefore does not remand merely to obtain the same interpretation in more explicit words. It supplies no permission to invent omitted findings or uphold action on an unrelated ground.

**Precedent treatment.**

- SEC v. Chenery Corp., 318 U.S. 80 — applied: the agency’s own ground must sustain its action; a necessarily discernible premise suffices here.
- Motor Vehicle Manufacturers Assn. v. State Farm, 463 U.S. 29 — distinguished on the record, not weakened: new appellate rationalizations remain insufficient.

### 3. Inability to agree on sale terms

**Holding and operative rule.** Section 562(d)’s inability-to-agree prerequisite permits the ICC to find from the parties’ positions that further sale negotiations will not produce agreement; it does not impose an unstated mandatory bargaining ritual. The supported finding here satisfies that condition, without making every unilateral offer sufficient.

**Authority.** Kennedy, joined by Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia and Souter: six votes support this holding and explanation.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the sale prerequisite turns on whether agreement can be reached, not on a bargaining ceremony absent from the statute. Amtrak offered to purchase the segment on fixed terms. B&M declined that proposal and offered instead to discuss upgrading the track while retaining it. The Commission determined from those positions that further efforts would not produce a sale agreement. Under Chevron, that understanding is a permissible reading of the enacted prerequisite, and the record supports its application. B&M’s demand for a separate good-faith negotiation requirement would add a condition beyond inability to agree. The Court does not hold that an unaccepted offer alone invariably establishes inability, or that the statutory presumption of need dispenses with this antecedent finding. The agency must address the parties’ actual positions. Its finding here is sufficient; compensation and the remaining challenges still require their separate consideration.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. NRDC, 467 U.S. 837 — applied to the ICC’s interpretation of the independent sale-negotiation prerequisite.

### 4. Rebuttal: significant impairment

**Holding and operative rule.** To defeat §562(d)’s presumption of need, the railroad must establish both statutory rebuttal conditions. In assessing significant impairment of its common-carrier obligations, the ICC may consider the transaction as a whole, including preserved trackage rights and compensation; the supported finding of no significant impairment independently prevents rebuttal here.

**Authority.** Kennedy, joined by Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia and Souter: six votes support this holding and explanation.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the Commission need not assess a conveyance as though its protective terms did not exist. B&M retains the right to serve its existing customers, and the conveyance provides compensation. Those terms bear directly on whether the transaction significantly impairs its common-carrier obligations. The statute does not require the ICC to disregard them and examine only the bare transfer of title. The Commission’s finding of no significant impairment is supported by substantial evidence and falls within its permissible construction of the provision. Chevron supports that construction, while the Court’s current Arkansas decision preserves the requirement to review the actual evidentiary record. Because the two rebuttal conditions are conjunctive, failure to prove significant impairment is sufficient to leave the need presumption unrebutted. This holding does not determine whether the compensation amount is correct; the valuation challenges remain for the circuit.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. NRDC, 467 U.S. 837 — applied: the statute permits consideration of mitigation within the transaction.
- Arkansas v. Oklahoma, current-term decision February 26, 1992 — consistent with, and unchanged by, review of an agency conclusion against the actual record.

### 5. Rebuttal: alternative property

**Holding and operative rule.** The ICC permissibly reads the alternative-property condition of §562(d) to ask whether an adequate alternative route is available on the statutory terms, rather than whether a lesser interest in the same segment would suffice. The absence of such an alternative independently prevents rebuttal of the need presumption here.

**Authority.** Kennedy, joined by Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia and Souter: six votes support this holding and explanation.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the alternative-property inquiry does not require the Commission to reintroduce the circuit’s indispensable-ownership rule through the rebuttal provision. The ICC considered whether Amtrak could provide the required passenger service over an adequate alternative route and found none. Its construction gives coherent effect to the acquisition remedy, the need presumption and the separate availability of trackage-rights proceedings. Chevron requires acceptance of that permissible reading, not substitution of a judicial preference for a lesser interest in the very property sought. B&M’s argument would make the rejected ownership limitation do the same work under a different clause. The Court sustains the Commission’s construction and application on this record. Because both rebuttal conditions must be established, this failure independently leaves the presumption in place, even apart from the no-impairment finding. No rule for property unrelated to passenger service is adopted.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. NRDC, 467 U.S. 837 — applied: the ICC’s route-based construction is permissible within the statutory scheme.

### 6. Public use despite private reconveyance

**Holding and operative rule.** A compensated condemnation serving a rationally supported public passenger-service purpose satisfies the Fifth Amendment’s public-use requirement even when title is reconveyed to another private railroad. The ICC’s determination that this transaction facilitates Amtrak service meets that requirement.

**Authority.** Kennedy, joined by Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia and Souter: six votes support this holding and explanation.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that transfer to a private railroad does not by itself negate public use. Midkiff sustains a condemnation rationally related to a conceivable public purpose, including a subsequent private transfer; Berman likewise recognizes that private participation can implement a public project. Here the conveyance supports restoration and maintenance of passenger service over the Montrealer route while securing Amtrak’s continuing use. The ICC’s public-purpose determination is rational, and the record supplies no basis for treating the arrangement as naked private favoritism. Carmack’s concern about limited delegations to private entities does not alter the result: the condemnation power here belongs to the ICC, which must apply the statute and provide compensation. The Court need not predict whether every operational objective will be achieved. It decides the public-use objection, leaving the amount of just compensation and the remaining statutory review issues unresolved.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Hawaii Housing Authority v. Midkiff, 467 U.S. 229 — applied: rational public purpose can support condemnation followed by private transfer.
- Berman v. Parker, 348 U.S. 26 — applied: private implementation does not defeat an otherwise public purpose.
- United States v. Carmack, 329 U.S. 230 — distinguished: the condemnation power here is exercised by a government commission.

### Limits and questions not reached

Section 562(d) allows the approved service-connected acquisition and reconveyance. Judicial deference remains tied to an interpretation actually discernible in the agency’s decision. The conjunctive rebuttal, inability-to-agree finding, compensation obligation and passenger-service boundary retain distinct work; no condemnation power for naked private favoritism follows.

Both cases return to the D.C. Circuit for the compensation challenges and other outstanding review issues. The Court does not itself fix a new valuation, resolve distinct regulatory claims, or schedule further Supreme Court proceedings. No later filing is established.

## Holdings

### 1. Partial withdrawal under an unfinished decree

**Holding and operative rule.** A court may withdraw supervision from a completed part of a school-desegregation decree while retaining jurisdiction and supervision elsewhere only after the district shows full and satisfactory compliance and elimination of the relevant vestiges to the extent practicable. The court must assess demonstrated good faith toward the whole decree and whether retaining control is necessary or practicable to achieve compliance elsewhere; release may not obstruct the remaining remedy.

**Authority.** Stone, joined by White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter: all eight participating Justices support this limited rule.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that completion can justify ending supervision of one obligation without ending the case. Green requires removal of the former dual system’s effects throughout school operations; it does not make each remedial obligation indivisible. Swann supplies equitable flexibility directed to the constitutional injury, and Dowell requires good-faith compliance and practicable elimination of vestiges. Those principles permit partial withdrawal when supported by findings. They also require attention to connections among assignments, personnel, resources and other remaining duties. The court must assess whether retaining control is necessary or practicable to complete compliance elsewhere and preserve an effective remaining remedy. The contrary categorical rule would preserve restrictions that no longer perform remedial work. The opposite shortcut would mistake progress in one area for completion of the whole decree. Rufo’s tailored approach to consent modification is consistent with this inquiry but does not displace the school-specific obligations. The Court orders findings, not final release.

### 2. Causation and the limits of the remand

**Holding and operative rule.** The district bears the burden of showing that imbalance for which it disclaims remedial responsibility is not traceable to the constitutional violation; genuinely independent demographic change does not require perpetual racial balancing. Existing factual findings receive clear-error review, but findings on one question do not decide unexamined causal or remedial connections; the remand must resolve properly open issues and define the obligations released and retained.

**Authority.** Stone, joined by White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter: eight votes; the separate opinions’ disputed factual applications are outside this holding.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that an explanation of population change must answer the question on which release depends. Spangler protects a district from endless readjustment for genuinely independent demographic change after it has performed the required remedial work. It does not establish that every residential or enrollment pattern is independent merely because migration occurred. Swann preserves the district’s responsibility to show why remaining imbalance is not an effect it must remedy. At the same time, ordinary appellate review respects factual findings actually made; a legal challenge to the completeness of an inquiry is different from disagreement with a resolved fact. It does not require relitigation of every demographic finding or endorse every causal conclusion. ## Holdings

### 1. Voluntarily created tenancies

**Holding and operative rule.** Regulating rents and continuation of voluntarily created pad tenancies does not impose a permanent physical occupation where the law permits the owner to change the land’s use and no contrary application is established. A tenant’s resale premium and the owner’s reduced ability to select incoming tenants do not themselves convert that regulation into a compelled occupation.

**Authority.** O’Connor, I, joined by Stone, White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas: seven votes control. Blackmun and Souter separately agree with rejection of the physical-taking claim.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the owners invited the occupation for which they now seek automatic compensation. Florida Power distinguishes that invitation from a government license forcing an unwanted occupant onto property; regulation of rent does not erase the distinction. Loretto requires compensation for a compelled permanent occupation, but preserves ordinary regulation of the landlord-tenant relationship. Here the challenged framework permits a change of use, and the owners have not alleged an actual unsuccessful effort to invoke that process. Their loss of rent and tenants’ ability to capitalize favorable terms describe economic effects, not governmental compulsion to surrender possession. The right to exclude remains important, but it does not turn every limit on an invited tenancy into physical appropriation. Nollan’s compelled easement presents a different premise. The Court leaves a law actually forbidding withdrawal, its particular application, and the separate regulatory-taking merits for appropriate proceedings.

**Precedent treatment.**

- FCC v. Florida Power Corp., 480 U.S. 245 — applied: invitation to occupy remains distinct from compelled occupation despite regulated rent.
- Loretto v. Teleprompter Manhattan CATV Corp., 458 U.S. 419 — applied and distinguished: its permanent-occupation rule requires compulsion absent here.
- Nollan v. California Coastal Commission, 483 U.S. 825 — distinguished: a required easement cannot substitute for proof of forced occupation here.

**Authority.** O’Connor, II-A, joined by White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas: six formal joins; Blackmun separately agrees that the claim is not properly raised. Stone’s limited merits reach supplies no additional procedural rationale.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the due-process claim is not properly presented for this review. The owners did not put that distinct federal claim in their complaint or litigate it in the Court of Appeal. # Jacobson v. United States

## Event

**Jacobson v. United States** — No. 90-1124; OT1991.

Argued 1991-11-06; decided 1992-04-06. Certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1); review of a federal conviction under 18 U.S.C. §2252(a)(2) after a jury rejected entrapment.

**Question:** Could rational jurors find beyond a reasonable doubt that Jacobson was predisposed to commit the charged crime independently of the Government’s campaign?

**Material record and posture:** Jacobson legally bought material in 1984 before the federal amendment. Beginning in January 1985, government agents used fictitious organizations, surveys, a pen pal and appeals against censorship over twenty-six months. He eventually ordered illegal material twice; the first order was unfilled, and a controlled delivery followed the second. The prosecution relies on prompt orders, requests for more, expressed interests and precautions about detection. The search found the lawful earlier magazines and government-supplied material, with no independent illicit collection. His testimony about the expected contents of the earlier purchase and the meaning of later correspondence must be assessed within the whole record. Inducement is not disputed for the question before the Court; the contested issue is independent predisposition.

## Holdings

### 1. Predisposition independent of official cultivation

**Holding and operative rule.** When the Government induces the charged crime and entrapment is at issue, it must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant’s predisposition existed before and independently of its cultivation; evidence obtained after contact may prove that earlier state of mind. Even giving the prosecution its reasonable favorable inferences, the lawful earlier purchase and responses generated during this twenty-six-month operation do not establish that independent predisposition.

**Authority.** White, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, Souter, Thomas: six votes control.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the Government cannot establish independent readiness merely by pointing to the offense its efforts eventually produced. Sorrells and Sherman distinguish offering an opportunity from creating the disposition to accept it; Russell preserves that subjective inquiry and permits legitimate undercover work. The earlier lawful purchase shows an interest, but not by itself readiness to commit the later crime. The prompt orders, expressions of interest and precautions deserve consideration, yet all arose after sustained official cultivation and do not close the evidentiary gap on this record. Jackson requires favorable reasonable inferences for the prosecution, not abandonment of proof beyond reasonable doubt. Later evidence can reveal an earlier disposition; the Court imposes no rule excluding it. Nor does the decision require reasonable suspicion before contact or knowledge that the conduct violated law. The defect is the failure to prove this defendant’s independent predisposition.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Sorrells v. United States, 287 U.S. 435 — applied: the Government may offer opportunities but may not manufacture the disposition and prosecute its product.
- Sherman v. United States, 356 U.S. 369 — applied: the record can establish entrapment as a matter of law despite an eventual willing transaction.
- United States v. Russell, 411 U.S. 423 — preserved: subjective predisposition remains the inquiry; no objective misconduct defense is substituted.
- Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 307 — applied: reasonable prosecution-favorable inferences remain subject to proof beyond reasonable doubt.
- Mathews v. United States, 485 U.S. 58 — preserved: a routine offered opportunity does not automatically support an entrapment instruction.

### 2. Insufficiency requires acquittal

**Holding and operative rule.** Because the prosecution’s proof was legally insufficient to overcome entrapment, the proper disposition is acquittal rather than a new trial. Burks bars a second opportunity to supply the missing evidence of independent predisposition.

**Authority.** White, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, Souter, Thomas: six votes support the remedial direction.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that this evidentiary failure ends the prosecution on the charge. Burks distinguishes reversal for an error in the trial process from reversal because the prosecution failed to prove what the law requires. A new trial can correct the first kind of defect, but cannot give the Government another chance to supply evidence missing from its completed case. The present judgment does not rest on an incorrect instruction or a ruling that excluded available proof. The Court has considered the prosecution’s evidence and its reasonable favorable inferences and found them legally insufficient to establish independent predisposition beyond reasonable doubt. Sherman confirms that entrapment can have that matter-of-law consequence. The case therefore returns for acquittal. The Court does not authorize another entrapment trial, determine a separate outrageous-conduct claim, or revise the elements of the underlying statutory offense.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Burks v. United States, 437 U.S. 1 — applied: insufficiency requires acquittal and bars retrial to repair the proof.
- Sherman v. United States, 356 U.S. 369 — applied: matter-of-law entrapment supports termination, not a second prosecution opportunity.

The prosecution must prove independently existing predisposition, not merely readiness after successful official cultivation. Later-collected evidence may reveal earlier readiness; neither the evidence’s date nor investigative persistence alone decides entrapment. This record is insufficient even under prosecution-favorable review, and Burks requires acquittal. The subjective defense, ordinary sting operations and statutory elements otherwise remain.

## Holdings

### 1. Preservation of the equal-protection objection

**Holding and operative rule.** A pre-Batson motion alleging a historical pattern of racial peremptory strikes, renewed after the challenged exclusions and carried forward on appeal with a Fourteenth Amendment reference, preserves the equal-protection objection despite imperfect labels. Trevino’s objection meets that standard under Ford.

**Authority.** Per curiam Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; no noted dissent.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that Trevino presented the substance of the equal-protection claim to the state courts. Ford treats a historical-pattern objection as invoking the evidentiary theory then associated with Swain, even when counsel does not name the correct constitutional clause precisely. Trevino alleged that pattern before trial, renewed the motion after each challenged strike, and referred to Swain. On appeal he renewed the same objection under a heading invoking the Fourteenth Amendment. The State understood the claim and disputed its legal basis through the asserted same-race limitation. Holland’s rejection of a different Sixth Amendment theory does not answer that preserved claim. Requiring greater precision here would impose a burden the Court did not impose in Batson itself. The holding concerns these concrete objections and renewals, not an automatic rule that any general assertion of unfairness preserves every constitutional claim.

### 2. Direct review and the remaining Batson inquiry

**Holding and operative rule.** Because Trevino’s case remained on direct review when Batson was decided, Griffith requires its application, and Powers removes the objection that Trevino and the excluded jurors are of different races. The state court must decide the preserved claim under Batson’s ordinary burdens; preservation alone establishes neither purposeful discrimination nor an automatic entitlement to a new trial.

**Authority.** Per curiam Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; no noted dissent.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the timing and racial-identity objections do not prevent consideration of the preserved claim. Griffith applies Batson to criminal cases pending on direct review when that decision issued. Trevino’s appeal was pending, so the collateral-review limitation associated with Teague does not govern. Powers rejects the further contention that a defendant can challenge racial exclusion only when the excluded jurors share his race. Those rules require consideration of the claim, but do not decide the disputed purpose of the strikes. Batson supplies the remaining sequence: the required initial showing, a race-neutral explanation when called for, and an ultimate determination of purposeful discrimination. The state court must obtain the explanations and findings appropriate to that inquiry. Relief must follow its lawful completion, rather than an assumption that the preservation ruling itself proves discrimination. The Court leaves all other asserted trial errors undecided.
