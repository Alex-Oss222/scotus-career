# Foucha v. Louisiana

## 1. Event

Foucha v. Louisiana, No. 90-5844; October Term 1991. Argued November 4, 1991; decided May 18, 1992. Certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1257 to the Louisiana Supreme Court, 563 So.2d 1138 (June 14, 1990), affirming continued psychiatric confinement after an insanity acquittal. The question is whether the State may continue that confinement on dangerousness and the acquittee's failure to prove safety after disclaiming present mental illness. Render form: full. Basis: proposition-specific joins, a noncontrolling equality plurality, and an express implementation mandate materially defining the remedy.

## 2. Participation

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participate at both argument and decision. All are seated at both milestones; no recusal or other nonparticipation is established. Nine participants satisfy the six-Justice quorum; five votes control a judgment or proposition. Thomas's October 23, 1991 oath precedes this argument. The October 23 circuit allotments and standing referral practice remain unchanged.

## 3. Public Action

The Court reverses the judgment sustaining Foucha's continued psychiatric confinement and remands for implementation.

## 6. Holdings

### 1. The insanity judgment no longer authorizes this psychiatric confinement

**Holding and operative rule.** When the State concedes that an insanity acquittee is no longer mentally ill, it may not continue his psychiatric confinement under the existing insanity-acquittee authority solely because he cannot prove that he will be harmless. The original acquittal supports neither a suspended criminal sentence nor indefinite psychiatric custody after its illness predicate disappears.

**Authority.** White's Part II-A, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter: six of nine participating Justices adopt this proposition and application.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that Louisiana must identify a present lawful basis for the restraint it imposes. Jones v. United States permits initial commitment because an insanity verdict supports an inference of illness and dangerousness; its rejection of a hypothetical criminal-sentence ceiling rests on those continuing conditions. O'Connor v. Donaldson establishes that a permissible commitment cannot continue after its supporting basis disappears. Jackson v. Indiana requires the nature and duration of custody to remain reasonably related to its purpose. Louisiana no longer claims that Foucha is mentally ill. His antisocial personality and the doctor's unwillingness to guarantee safety do not restore that conceded absence. The State's substantial interest in protecting the public therefore cannot perpetuate this particular psychiatric commitment. The original criminal conduct does not make the acquittal a conviction authorizing punishment. The Court neither finds Foucha harmless nor sets the maximum duration of an otherwise valid commitment. Any separate lawful restraint must rest on its own established authority and procedures.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Jones v. United States, 463 U.S. 354 (1983): applied to the continuing illness-and-danger basis of insanity commitment; initial verdict-based commitment and rejection of an automatic hypothetical-sentence ceiling remain intact.
- O'Connor v. Donaldson, 422 U.S. 563 (1975): applied to the requirement that confinement end when its authorizing basis disappears; the Court does not equate a conditional-release recommendation with a finding of harmlessness.
- Jackson v. Indiana, 406 U.S. 715 (1972): applied to the required relationship between confinement's nature, duration and purpose.

### 2. Psychiatric custody requires appropriate grounds and procedures

**Holding and operative rule.** Once the insanity-acquittal basis for holding Foucha as a mental patient has ended, Louisiana cannot preserve that psychiatric custody without constitutionally adequate proceedings establishing the grounds required for it. If the State invokes ordinary civil commitment, it must establish current mental illness and dangerousness by clear and convincing evidence; additional procedure cannot supply an absent substantive ground.

**Authority.** White's Part II-B, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter: five of nine participating Justices adopt this independently sufficient ground. Stone-Zsela does not join this section's separate Vitek-based procedural characterization.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that a hearing about dangerousness alone cannot authorize confinement as a mental patient after the State disclaims mental illness. Vitek v. Jones recognizes that placement in a psychiatric institution imposes a distinct deprivation requiring appropriate justification and procedure, even for someone already lawfully confined. Jackson v. Indiana requires a new lawful basis and the protections belonging to it when the original commitment purpose no longer supports custody. Addington v. Texas supplies the clear-and-convincing burden for ordinary civil commitment. Louisiana instead requires Foucha to disprove danger, although its conceded facts cannot presently establish the illness required for that civil route. The State's reliance on a counseled release hearing does not answer this defect: fairness in deciding the wrong statutory condition does not establish the missing condition. Baxstrom v. Herold reinforces that an expired original basis does not remove the safeguards of the new proceeding. The Court does not prescribe a universal burden for every possible preventive regime or disturb the distinct rules governing initial insanity commitment.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Vitek v. Jones, 445 U.S. 480 (1980): applied to psychiatric institutionalization's distinct liberty deprivation and need for appropriate grounds and procedures.
- Jackson v. Indiana, 406 U.S. 715 (1972): applied to the need for an independently authorized commitment proceeding after the initial basis expires.
- Addington v. Texas, 441 U.S. 418 (1979): applied unchanged to the clear-and-convincing burden for ordinary civil commitment; that burden is not made universal for every preventive custody channel.
- Baxstrom v. Herold, 383 U.S. 107 (1966): relied on for the ordinary protections attending a new civil commitment after the prior detention basis ends; no separate majority equal-protection holding is created here.

### 3. Public safety does not sustain this unbounded dangerousness regime

**Holding and operative rule.** Louisiana's scheme permitting indefinite psychiatric confinement of a no-longer-mentally-ill acquittee because he has not proved nondangerousness violates due process. A legitimate preventive purpose and periodic review do not cure the absence of a carefully limited substantive authority; the Court leaves materially narrower preventive regimes undecided.

**Authority.** White's Part II-C, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter: six of nine participating Justices adopt this independently sufficient rejection of Louisiana's asserted alternative preventive justification. The holding adopts no new general scrutiny tier or universal dangerousness proof standard.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the exceptional power to prevent danger cannot be detached from the limits that justify the particular restraint. United States v. Salerno sustained a narrowly focused pretrial scheme with an identified threat, government proof, an adversarial hearing, review and strict temporal limits. Louisiana's rule instead permits confinement without an endpoint while requiring the acquittee to prove safety. The doctor's reluctance to certify Foucha as harmless is enough under that rule, although the State concedes that the psychiatric illness has ended. A fair procedure for predicting danger does not supply substantive authority for an otherwise unauthorized restraint. The original verdict supplies no punitive authority under Jones. If later conduct constitutes a crime, ordinary criminal proceedings remain available; the Court makes no new finding that a particular assault occurred or warrants conviction. A better hearing alone cannot cure this regime's substantive defect. The Court reserves narrowly drawn alternatives rather than converting today's holding into a universal prohibition of preventive detention.

**Precedent treatment.**

- United States v. Salerno, 481 U.S. 739 (1987): distinguished because its confined pretrial detention authority and safeguards do not authorize Louisiana's indefinite post-acquittal psychiatric restraint.
- Jones v. United States, 463 U.S. 354 (1983): applied to the absence of authority to punish an insanity acquittee as a convicted offender.

## 8. Law After Decision

Effective May 18, 1992, Jones's distinct initial-commitment rule remains in force, but Louisiana cannot continue this psychiatric confinement after conceding the end of illness on the acquittee's failure to prove safety. Five Justices additionally require appropriate psychiatric grounds and procedures; ordinary civil commitment retains Addington's clear-and-convincing burden. Six reject the indefinite dangerousness alternative under this scheme. These are applied and clarified channel limits, not a new universal scrutiny tier or dangerousness standard. No majority adopts the plurality's equal-protection rule. Maximum duration of an otherwise valid commitment, materially narrower preventive regimes, and the scope of state insanity defenses remain undecided. No Marks inference is needed because each controlling due-process proposition has at least five direct joins.

## 9. Separate Writings

White, joined by Blackmun, Stevens and Souter, concludes in Part III that Louisiana also denies equal protection by withholding ordinary commitment safeguards from a sane acquittee while not permitting comparable indefinite dangerousness confinement for other persons whose original custody basis has ended. The four would require a particularly convincing justification for that disparity; this is a plurality position, not law.

O'Connor emphasizes that the holding concerns this indefinite psychiatric regime. She leaves open detention carefully limited in nature and duration to substantial safety concerns and questions extended confinement beyond what a conviction could have permitted, without deciding that question. No constitutional requirement that a State provide an insanity defense is adopted.

Stone-Zsela agrees that the psychiatric channel's ended predicate requires relief. He reserves independent equal protection, a new general scrutiny tier, universal proof rules and narrowly defined alternative regimes. Implementation cannot allow indefinite continuation while the State looks for a new justification; separate custody requires its own established authority and procedures.

Kennedy would affirm because the insanity adjudication follows proof of criminal conduct and belongs to a distinct legal channel. He views state definitions of criminal insanity and incapacitation as constitutionally relevant and rejects automatic transfer of ordinary civil-commitment rules to this setting.

Thomas, joined by Scalia, would affirm because acquittees differ from ordinary civil committees, the State may attach continued safety-based custody to that status, and periodic counseled review is adequate here. They reject an expansive new substantive right against this confinement and distinguish its lack of a fixed endpoint from a refusal ever to provide release review. Neither dissent controls.

## 10. Procedure After Action

Supreme Court merits review is complete. The Louisiana courts must implement reversal by ending custody under the challenged insanity-acquittee basis. The mandate permits no indefinite continuation of that custody while the State searches for a substitute rationale. Any independently lawful custody requires its own established authority and procedures; the Court orders neither automatic release from a separate lawful restraint nor a new civil commitment. No future hearing date, factual diagnosis, new charge, compliance finding or retained Supreme Court proceeding is created.

# Riggins v. Nevada

## 1. Event

Riggins v. Nevada, No. 90-8466; October Term 1991. Argued January 15, 1992; decided May 18, 1992. Certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1257 to the Nevada Supreme Court, 107 Nev.178, 808 P.2d535 (March 28, 1991), affirming murder and robbery convictions and a death sentence. The granted question concerns forced medication through trial and the Sixth and Fourteenth Amendment right to a full and fair trial. Render form: full. Basis: the mandate expressly orders a new trial and contemporaneous justification for renewed compelled medication, with separate grounds concerning the rule's limits.

## 6. Holdings

### 1. The State must justify antipsychotic medication continued over objection

**Holding and operative rule.** Once a pretrial detainee objects to continued antipsychotic medication, the State must establish medical appropriateness and a need sufficient to override the protected refusal interest, considering reasonable less intrusive means. The unexplained order here supplies neither an essential justification nor findings about alternatives and cannot authorize the compelled treatment through trial.

**Authority.** O'Connor's Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens and Souter: six of nine participating Justices adopt this rule and application.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that clinical acceptability does not itself establish legal authority to override refusal. Washington v. Harper recognizes the substantial liberty interest affected by unwanted antipsychotic medication and permits justified treatment of dangerous, mentally ill prisoners. An accused awaiting trial retains the protected refusal interest recognized in Harper. Riggins initially sought treatment, but continued administration became involuntary after his objection. Medical appropriateness is accepted for this review; necessity is not. The hearing contained testimony that he could remain competent without Mellaril and questions about the high dosage. The trial court's unexplained denial establishes neither an essential safety need nor that less intrusive means could not permit adjudication. The State's legitimate interest in a competent trial cannot replace those missing determinations. The Court does not impose a new documentary ritual, universal proof quantum or strict-scrutiny formula. Medically appropriate treatment essential to safety after considering alternatives remains possible. The complete conditions for competency-restoration medication, and whether a defendant may insist on becoming incompetent, remain undecided.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Washington v. Harper, 494 U.S. 210 (1990): applied for the protected refusal interest and requirement of overriding justification and medical appropriateness; its convicted-prisoner procedures do not automatically govern trial medication.
- Illinois v. Allen, 397 U.S. 337 (1970): relied on for the State's legitimate trial interest and the possibility of necessary courtroom measures; no comprehensive competency-medication exception is adopted.

### 2. This unsupported medication order requires a new trial

**Holding and operative rule.** When unjustified compelled antipsychotic medication through trial creates a supported, substantial risk of impairing the defense that the record cannot reliably reconstruct, the defendant need not demonstrate exactly how an unmedicated trial would have differed. The risk here extends beyond visible demeanor to testimony, comprehension and communication with counsel; explanatory expert testimony does not cure it, and the convictions and dependent sentence must be vacated for a new trial.

**Authority.** O'Connor's Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens and Souter: six of nine participating Justices adopt this record-specific rule and remedy. Kennedy concurs in the reversal and new-trial judgment on a distinct trial-integrity ground.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that this trial record cannot establish that the unsupported treatment left the defense intact. Illinois v. Allen and Estelle v. Williams recognize that compelled practices can affect an accused's trial in ways a transcript cannot measure. The doctors' testimony identifies a strong possibility that this dosage affected attention, communication and the substance of testimony, not merely the appearance the jury observed. Expert explanation may help jurors interpret demeanor, but cannot restore a conversation with counsel or a response that medication changed. The contrary demand for a precise account of the trial that would have occurred without treatment would therefore require speculation. Allen permits necessary measures to protect the trial, but this record contains no finding establishing an essential justification for this treatment. Reversal and a new trial follow from this combination of unjustified treatment and supported, unreconstructable trial risk. The Court neither finds Riggins incompetent nor decides that every medication error is structural. Renewed compelled treatment requires a constitutionally adequate contemporaneous justification.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Illinois v. Allen, 397 U.S. 337 (1970): applied by analogy to potentially prejudicial compelled trial measures and the need for an essential justification.
- Estelle v. Williams, 425 U.S. 501 (1976): applied by analogy to effects of compelled trial appearance that cannot be fully reconstructed from a transcript; its particular waiver disposition is not extended.

## 8. Law After Decision

Effective May 18, 1992, an objection to continued trial-related antipsychotic medication obligates the State to establish its medical appropriateness and overriding need with attention to reasonable alternatives. Unjustified treatment with the supported, unreconstructable trial risk present here requires reversal and a new trial. Harper remains governing in its convicted-prisoner setting; this decision adds the stated application for an objecting detainee at trial. No universal proof quantum, strict-scrutiny formula, structural-error category or comprehensive competency-medication test is adopted. Kennedy's extraordinary-showing standard and Stone-Zsela's express Chapman analysis remain noncontrolling supplemental grounds. The unpreserved Eighth Amendment sentencing-display issue and competent-defendant refusal that would produce incompetence remain open. Six direct joins resolve each holding; no Marks construction is necessary.

## 9. Separate Writings

Kennedy concurs in the judgment. He would require an extraordinary showing that involuntary medication for competency presents no significant risk of materially altering the accused's reactions, participation or assistance to counsel. He doubts that the contemporary medical evidence often permits that showing; if competence cannot be restored without such impairment, lawful civil commitment rather than trial may be the available route. This proposed standard is not the Court's rule.

Stone-Zsela joins the Court and separately applies Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18 (1967): the State bears the burden of showing constitutional trial error harmless beyond a reasonable doubt, and the medication record fails that burden. His concurrence supplies no general structural-error classification, categorical ban on competency treatment or order to stop medically necessary care. The Court's holding remains the record-specific unjustified-treatment and trial-risk rule.

Thomas, joined by Scalia except in Part II-A, would affirm. They regard expert testimony and the competence finding as adequate protection of the trial, distinguish a refusal-interest violation remediable in a civil action from an established basis to reverse a conviction, and reject reversal based on possible impairment alone. Thomas alone also disputes proof of involuntariness and whether a separate Harper liberty theory was preserved and included in the grant; Scalia does not join those additional objections.

Renewed involuntary medication must have a constitutionally adequate contemporaneous justification under the Court's holding; a new trial is not an order to withhold medically necessary treatment. The Court does not authorize trial of an incompetent defendant, fix a treatment plan, decide an unpresented sentencing claim, set a new date or retain supervision.

# Wyatt v. Cole

## 1. Event

Wyatt v. Cole, No. 91-126; October Term 1991. Merits decision, May 18, 1992. Argued January 14, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the Fifth Circuit, 928 F.2d 718 (April 17, 1991), concerning the qualified-immunity protection afforded Cole and attorney Robbins for using Mississippi's replevin procedure. The granted issue is whether private defendants in §1983 actions may claim the good-faith immunity afforded public officials; the operative controversy concerns Harlow immunity for these private litigants. The petitioner seeks reversal and continued proceedings, not a Supreme Court damages award. Source reporter citation: 504 U.S. 158. Same-day release group: May 18, 1992; no ordered dependency is supplied.

## 6. Holdings

### Private litigants and immunity from suit

**Holding and operative rule.** Private litigants who invoke state replevin, garnishment, or attachment procedures do not receive the objective immunity from suit recognized in Harlow merely because their qualifying joint action may be attributable to the State under §1983. Whether such defendants have a distinct good-faith or probable-cause defense, whether the plaintiff bears additional elements, and the allocation of any such burden remain undecided.

**Authority.** O'Connor's opinion for the Court, joined in full by White, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia and Kennedy: six of nine participating Justices adopt the proposition covering replevin, garnishment and attachment. Stone joins the opinion only as applied to private litigants invoking state replevin machinery, including its stated reservations; seven adopt that narrower proposition. He takes no position on its extension to the other procedures. Souter and Thomas dissent. The six full joins suffice for the broader rule; no fractured-decision analysis is required.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that private use of compulsory state process does not itself carry public-official immunity. Lugar v. Edmondson Oil Co. supplies the attribution requirements but expressly reserved immunity. Owen v. City of Independence requires an independent historical and functional basis for protection under §1983. The analogies to malicious prosecution and abuse of process may support defenses involving good faith or probable cause; they do not establish the immunity from litigation that Cole and Robbins obtained. Pierson v. Ray recognized a good-faith protection for police officers, while Harlow v. Fitzgerald reformulated official immunity to protect discretionary public service from disruptive inquiry. Mitchell v. Forsyth gives that protection consequences beyond a defense to liability, including immediate appeal. Cole pursued a private commercial claim, and Robbins represented it; neither held an office whose public performance required this protection. Reasonable reliance on legislation therefore does not justify the requested extension. The Court reserves any independently supported defense and its burden, and requires further consideration of each defendant's state-action status before liability can be imposed.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Lugar v. Edmondson Oil Co., 457 U.S. 922 (1982) — answers its reserved immunity question while preserving its two-part attribution inquiry; joint action does not itself establish immunity or either defendant's liability.
- Owen v. City of Independence, 445 U.S. 622 (1980) — applies its historical and functional inquiry; immunity is not assumed for every person reached by §1983.

# Wade v. United States

## 1. Event

Wade v. United States, No. 91-5771; October Term 1991. Merits decision May 18, 1992; argued March 23, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the Fourth Circuit, 936 F.2d 169 (June 12, 1991), affirming a 180-month sentence. ## 6. Holdings

### Government motion is the statutory trigger

**Holding and operative rule.** Substantial assistance alone does not authorize a sentencing court to depart under 18 U.S.C. §3553(e) or USSG §5K1.1 without the Government's required motion. Those provisions give the Government power to move, not a duty to do so whenever assistance has been useful; their independent operation is reserved on this record.

**Authority.** Part A of Souter's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. All nine adopt the statutory rule.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that Congress made a Government motion the trigger for the substantial-assistance authority at issue. Section 3553(e) permits a sentence below a statutory minimum upon that motion, and §5K1.1 uses the same condition for a Guidelines departure. A judge's finding that cooperation was valuable cannot substitute for the specified act. The statutory objective of encouraging assistance does not erase the means Congress selected to encourage it. Wade supplied information useful to another prosecution, but the Government made no motion. The sentencing court therefore could not reduce the sentence simply by reassessing that assistance. Santobello v. New York requires fulfillment of a material prosecutorial promise inducing a plea; no such promise is established here, and the Court does not resolve that distinct branch. The parties treat the two motion provisions as equivalent obstacles in this case. The Court leaves their possible independent operation open and does not classify this sentencing restriction as a limit on Article III jurisdiction.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Santobello v. New York, 404 U.S. 257 (1971): distinguished; enforcement of an established plea promise remains available on its own requirements, which are not presented here.

### Constitutional review of a refusal remains available

**Holding and operative rule.** A district court may review the Government's refusal to file a substantial-assistance motion and grant relief when the refusal rests on an unconstitutional purpose, including race or religion. This case establishes no particular corrective remedy because no unconstitutional refusal has been shown.

**Authority.** Part A of Souter's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. All nine reject categorical immunity from constitutional review.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that discretion over a substantial-assistance motion remains subject to the Constitution. Wayte v. United States recognizes both the breadth of prosecutorial choice and the constitutional limits upon it. The decision whether to reward cooperation is another exercise of public authority, and the statutory motion requirement does not authorize forbidden discrimination. A refusal because of race or religion would therefore permit judicial relief. The contrary rule would mistake the absence of a statutory entitlement to a motion for the absence of any enforceable constitutional restraint. The Court nevertheless distinguishes unlawful purpose from an ordinary disagreement about cooperation's usefulness. A prosecutor may assess the costs and benefits of moving, even when assistance has value. Wade has identified no supported unconstitutional purpose. The Court accordingly rejects the Fourth Circuit's categorical no-inquiry rationale while leaving the sentence intact and reserving the particular remedy for an established violation.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Wayte v. United States, 470 U.S. 598 (1985): applied to the departure-motion decision; broad prosecutorial discretion remains constitutionally limited.

### Refusals unrelated to a legitimate governmental end

**Holding and operative rule.** A defendant is entitled to relief if the prosecutor's refusal to file a substantial-assistance motion is not rationally related to any legitimate governmental end. Useful assistance alone does not establish that defect, because the Government may rationally assess the costs and benefits of moving; no particular corrective remedy is selected here.

**Authority.** Part B of Souter's Opinion of the Court, joined by White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. Eight Justices adopt this independent constitutional ground; Stone-Zsela does not join the separate formulation and resolves only the unsupported proffer under his stated ground.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that a departure-motion refusal unrelated to any legitimate governmental end would entitle a defendant to relief. The Government's power to choose does not authorize arbitrary treatment without a lawful governmental purpose. Wayte v. United States preserves constitutional limits upon prosecutorial discretion; the rationality requirement applies that distinction to the asserted refusal here. But constitutional review does not make the sentencing judge the prosecutor's supervisor in evaluating every instance of cooperation. Wade's assistance may have been valuable while the Government still reasonably concluded that moving would not serve its legitimate objectives. His counsel identified no contrary factual reason when invited to describe the evidence supporting a hearing. A useful contribution to another prosecution therefore does not, by itself, establish an irrational refusal. The Court rejects a demand for judicial inquiry based only on the asserted value of assistance, leaves the sentence intact, and reserves the remedy that would be appropriate after proof of a refusal actually failing this constitutional requirement.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Wayte v. United States, 470 U.S. 598 (1985): applied for constitutionally constrained prosecutorial choice; the legitimate-end requirement is adopted for the refusal at issue.

### Threshold for discovery or an evidentiary hearing

**Holding and operative rule.** A defendant seeking discovery or an evidentiary hearing about a refused substantial-assistance motion must make a substantial threshold showing of improper motive; useful assistance alone, with or without generalized accusations, is insufficient. Wade's assistance-only proffer does not justify discovery, a hearing, a remand to develop an unspecified claim, or a sentence reduction.

**Authority.** Part B of Souter's Opinion of the Court, joined by White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas: eight Justices adopt the discovery threshold and its application. Stone-Zsela agrees that this proffer fails but does not join the general threshold; his separate concurrence is not a limit on the eight-Justice rule.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that an allegation of useful cooperation does not itself open prosecutorial decisionmaking to discovery. Wayte v. United States recognizes broad prosecutorial discretion and the costs of motive inquiries; it reserved the discovery question there. A defendant must make a substantial threshold showing directed to improper motive before obtaining discovery or an evidentiary hearing. That threshold does not require the sentencing court to treat every refusal as unreviewable. It requires something beyond the assistance itself and a generalized charge of bad faith. Wade contends that the district court's view of its authority prevented him from developing his claim. The record defeats that contention: the court invited counsel to identify the evidence he would offer, and counsel described only Wade's cooperation. That proffer is consistent with a lawful assessment of the costs and benefits of moving. It identifies no suspect reason for refusal. A remand to search for an unspecified constitutional violation is therefore unwarranted, and the judgment is affirmed.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Wayte v. United States, 470 U.S. 598 (1985): its constitutional limits and concern with intrusive inquiry are applied; it reserved discovery entitlement, and the substantial-assistance threshold is adopted here.

**Limits and questions not reached.** No plea promise is found. No particular remedy for a proved unconstitutional refusal, separate §3553(e)/§5K1.1 interaction, departure amount, or independent challenge to the consecutive firearm term is decided.

## 6. Holdings

### Content-based electioneering restrictions on public sidewalks

**Holding and operative rule.** Tennessee's campaign-specific prohibition is a content-based restriction on political speech, and ordinary public sidewalks and streets within the 100-foot boundary remain public forums. The State must show that this restriction is necessary to serve a compelling interest and narrowly drawn to achieve that end; viewpoint neutrality and the availability of speech beyond the boundary do not by themselves lower that scrutiny.

**Authority.** Seven participating Justices expressly adopt this governing proposition at the same level of generality: Blackmun's plurality, joined by Stone, White and Kennedy, and Stevens's dissent, joined by O'Connor and Souter. It is an independently majority-supported proposition, not a seven-Justice judgment coalition or an Opinion of the Court. Scalia rejects the forum classification and applies a different standard.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the subject of speech determines whether Tennessee's prohibition applies, so the law requires the strict scrutiny used for content-based restrictions. Perry Education Ass'n v. Perry Local Educators' Ass'n supplies that standard in traditional public forums. United States v. Grace preserves ordinary sidewalks' public character despite proximity to a government institution. Boos v. Barry requires scrutiny of restrictions directed at political subjects; viewpoint neutrality does not make subject discrimination neutral. Mills v. Alabama protects election-day advocacy while leaving the distinct regulation of polling places open. Tennessee's protection of free voting presents a compelling interest, but identifying that interest does not dispense with necessity and tailoring. The Justices adopting this governing rule disagree about whether the history, outdoor reach and available alternatives satisfy it here. Their shared rule therefore establishes the applicable scrutiny, not a common resolution of the statute's validity or permission to suppress election advocacy generally.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Perry Education Ass'n v. Perry Local Educators' Ass'n, 460 U.S. 37 (1983) — applies its traditional-public-forum and content-based scrutiny framework to the ordinary streets and sidewalks reached here.
- United States v. Grace, 461 U.S. 171 (1983) — applies its protection of ordinary public sidewalks; government-building proximity does not alone remove that status.
- Boos v. Barry, 485 U.S. 312 (1988) — applies strict scrutiny to political subject discrimination; a claim of regulatory purpose does not automatically make this campaign restriction content neutral.
- Mills v. Alabama, 384 U.S. 214 (1966) — preserves its protection of general election-day advocacy and its reservation concerning conduct in and around polls; it does not automatically settle this boundary's validity.
- Simon & Schuster, Inc. v. Members of the New York State Crime Victims Board, December 10, 1991, simulated decision — preserves exacting review of content-based burdens; this case's divided application creates no general exception to that rule.
- Ward v. Rock Against Racism, 491 U.S. 781 (1989) — distinguishes its content-neutral time, place and manner standard; it does not replace strict scrutiny here.

### Judgment without a controlling validity rationale

**Judgment and authority.** Five Justices—Stone, White, Blackmun, Scalia and Kennedy—reverse the judgment invalidating Tennessee's 100-foot campaign boundary and remand. Blackmun, Stone, White and Kennedy sustain it under strict scrutiny; Scalia sustains it as reasonable and viewpoint neutral in a nonpublic forum. Stevens, O'Connor and Souter would affirm invalidation. No opinion speaks for the Court on the justification sufficient to uphold the restriction.

**Fractured-decision boundary.** Marks v. United States, 430 U.S. 188 (1977), does not supply a controlling application rationale here. The plurality's rule assumes a public forum and requires necessity and narrow tailoring; Scalia's rule assumes a nonpublic forum and requires reasonableness and viewpoint neutrality. Those different forum predicates are not demonstrated logical subsets. His conclusion that the same history makes the restriction reasonable does not adopt the plurality's necessity or tailoring determination. The five-vote disposition binds this case; neither the four-Justice strict-scrutiny application nor the one-Justice nonpublic-forum theory becomes a controlling exception.

**Limits and questions not reached.** The judgment concerns this 100-foot boundary during voting. It does not decide the separately classified 300-foot provisions, larger zones, restrictions beyond actual voting periods, unrelated political speech, discriminatory enforcement, hypothetical incidental bumper-sticker displays or unusual highway applications. The Court does not resolve state constitutional grounds not decided on review or approve a general election-day campaign ban.

## 9. Separate Writings

Blackmun's plurality, joined by Stone, White and Kennedy, concludes that the historical mechanism justifies this limited protection of the unavoidable approach to voting. Campaign agents could confront and observe voters in ways that enabled intimidation or fraudulent control. Starting protection only inside the door and punishing proved intimidation afterward do not equally prevent subtle or completed pressure. Campaign-specific coverage addresses the documented source of that harm; it does not prefer candidates or viewpoints. The plurality treats the 100-foot line as a confined implementation of protection while campaigning remains available beyond it. It does not demand an experiment exposing voters to coercion or proof that each foot is indispensable, and does not treat tradition alone as sufficient. This application has four votes and is noncontrolling.

Kennedy's concurrence explains that a law facially distinguishing content may call for examination of its actual justification. He regards this narrow accommodation of free voting as compatible with his objection to general censorship through compelling-interest balancing. His concurrence does not lower the standard joined in this case.

Scalia concurs only in the judgment. In his view, the longstanding exclusion of electioneering from functioning polling environs means that those areas are not traditional public forums during voting. He finds the restriction reasonable and viewpoint neutral and accepts the historical considerations as adequate for that standard. His forum theory has one vote.

A tradition adopted with secret ballots does not itself prove the continuing necessity of this outdoor exclusion. Existing laws against intimidation and more limited entrance protections are insufficiently answered; permitting other solicitation while banning campaign speech further weakens the asserted fit. The dissent would leave the federal invalidation in place.

## 6. Holdings

### 1. No independent demand or initiation element

**Holding and operative rule:** Official-right extortion under 18 U.S.C. §1951 does not require that the public official initiate, demand or separately induce the payment when he obtains money to which he is not entitled knowing that it is given in return for official acts. The required interstate-commerce element and proof beyond a reasonable doubt remain.

**Authority:** Parts I–II of Stevens’s opinion, joined by White, Blackmun, O’Connor and Souter; five Justices adopt this statutory ground. Kennedy and Stone independently support the judgment without joining those parts, and their reasons are not necessary to make this holding controlling.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an official cannot avoid this offense merely because the payer proposed the transaction. Morissette supplies the presumption that Congress carries established legal meaning into a borrowed term of art, and Taylor recognizes the same interpretive starting point. The official-right branch retains the prohibition against taking unauthorized payment for official service; the Hobbs Act’s expansion to private force and fear offenses does not narrow that branch. The text supplies no first-speaker condition, and even reading induced to modify official right does not necessarily require the official to initiate the transaction. Evans’s contrary construction confuses initiation with the knowing receipt of payment for the exercise of public power. The Government must still establish the prohibited exchange rather than rely upon lawful contributions or official status alone. This construction resolves the presented demand objection without deciding every gift, gratuity or disputed promise. The statute’s commerce element and the ordinary criminal burden remain intact.

**Precedent treatment:**
- Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S.246 — applied for the established content of a borrowed criminal-law term; no strict-liability offense is created.
- Taylor v. United States, 495 U.S.575 — applied for the common-law interpretive presumption, subject to contrary statutory direction.
- United States v. Nardello, 393 U.S.286 — distinguishes expansion of extortion beyond public officials from narrowing its official-right branch.

### 2. The charge requires an exchange; promised performance need not be completed

**Holding and operative rule:** A charge requiring a public official knowingly to accept money in exchange for a specific requested exercise of official power, while excluding a contribution standing alone even when the donor has pending business, satisfies McCormick’s exchange requirement on this record. The offense is complete upon receipt in return for the official undertaking; actual fulfillment of the promised act is not an additional element.

**Authority:** Parts I–II of Stevens’s opinion, joined by White, Blackmun, O’Connor and Souter; five Justices sustain the instruction and distinguish agreement from later performance.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this charge distinguished a purchased official act from legitimate political support. McCormick supplies the protection for campaign contributions: a contribution must be given in return for an explicit promise or undertaking of official action, rather than merely because the contributor hopes for favor. The charge here first told the jury that accepting a contribution, even from a donor with pending business, is lawful by itself. It then required demand or acceptance in exchange for a specific requested exercise of official power. Read with the knowing-and-willful instructions, that language required the prohibited undertaking and did not make the disclosed check or an official’s position an offense. Evans’s separate demand objection adds a condition the statute lacks. Nor must the Government prove completion of the promised rezoning assistance: receipt in return for the undertaking completes the offense. The Court does not substitute new credibility findings for the verdict or decide cases lacking the required exchange.

**Precedent treatment:**
- McCormick v. United States, 500 U.S.257 — applied: legitimate campaign support remains protected absent the required official exchange; completed performance is not required.
- Glasser v. United States, 315 U.S.60 — applied for the favorable-to-verdict treatment of supported factual inferences; statutory construction and charge review remain legal questions.

### 3. No false claim of lawful entitlement is indispensable

**Holding and operative rule:** Official-right extortion is not confined to obtaining payment by falsely asserting that law entitles the official to collect it. A knowing unauthorized payment in return for official acts can violate the official-right branch without deceiving the payer that the payment is a lawful fee.

**Authority:** Part III of Stevens’s opinion, joined by White, Blackmun, Kennedy and Souter; five Justices decide this construction. O’Connor does not reach it, and Stone does not join or supply an additional ground on it.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an official’s false claim to a lawful fee is one form of official-right extortion, not an indispensable feature of every form. Morissette supplies the common-law interpretive inquiry, but identifying frequent examples does not establish an exclusive element. Commonwealth v. Wilson recognizes official extortion beyond excessive fees, while the pre-enactment decisions in Commonwealth v. Brown and State v. Barts supply examples of corrupt payment for the use or withholding of public authority. Collier v. State distinguishes payment for private service from a payment exacted in an official capacity; it does not make the payer’s belief that a fee is lawful the defining condition. The contrary argument would erase the distinction between an illustration and a required element. The payment here concerned specific official assistance and was not lawfully due. The Court rejects the proposed false-pretense limitation without making all private payments to officials criminal or eliminating the exchange required for this conviction.

**Precedent treatment:**
- Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S.246 — applied to identify rather than artificially narrow the borrowed offense.
- Commonwealth v. Wilson, 30 Pa.Super.26; Commonwealth v. Brown, 23 Pa.Super.470; and State v. Barts, 132 N.J.L.74 — considered as pre-enactment evidence that official extortion was not confined to overcharged lawful fees.
- Collier v. State, 55 Ala.125 — distinguished: private-service payment is different from payment for official action; its result does not establish the asserted universal false-pretense element.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No entrapment claim is reopened. The conditional tax claim is not reached by the affirming coalition; Scalia and Thomas separately reject disturbing that count because the instructional objection was not preserved. Mere gratuities and cases lacking the required undertaking are not decided. The Court creates no new commerce element or proof standard. No Marks construction is necessary because each reached controlling proposition has an express five-Justice opinion coalition.

The pre-enactment state cases support the historical meaning of the federal term rather than become independent federal offenses. Enmons’s treatment of wrongful force or fear in its labor setting does not replace the official-right branch. R.L.C.’s juvenile ceiling remains; its separate disagreements about legislative history and lenity were never a majority methodology. Jacobson remains the independently-existing-predisposition rule for a presented entrapment issue; it does not reopen that defense in this grant. ## 1. Event

## 6. Holdings

### 1. Purposeful contacts can satisfy due process without physical presence

**Holding and operative rule:** A mail-order seller that purposefully and continuously solicits a State’s consumers may have the minimum connection required for a use-tax collection obligation even without physical presence, when the obligation is rationally related to benefits received through that market. Quill’s sustained solicitation and approximately $1 million in annual North Dakota sales satisfy due process; Bellas Hess and earlier holdings are overruled only insofar as they make physical presence indispensable for this collection duty.

**Authority:** Parts I–III of Stevens’s opinion, joined by White, Blackmun, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; eight Justices. Stone expressly does not reach due process and supplies no join.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that deliberate, continuing participation in the State’s market supplies the fair connection required here. Miller Brothers supplies the requirement of a definite link between the State and the person or transaction, while International Shoe explains why purposeful contacts matter more than a formal presence label. Burger King confirms that commercial activity deliberately directed to a State does not escape its authority solely because the seller communicates from elsewhere; Shaffer applies the same rejection of mechanical presence rules to adjudicative jurisdiction. Comparable reasoning governs this collection obligation without equating every rule for judicial jurisdiction with every rule for legislative authority. Quill repeatedly solicits North Dakota customers and conducts substantial sales there, and the use tax relates to the benefits of access to that market. The contrary physical-presence prerequisite cannot survive that reasoning. Due process and the Commerce Clause impose distinct limits, however, so satisfying this connection does not itself authorize the collection duty that the Court rejects on the other ground.

**Precedent treatment:**
- National Bellas Hess v. Department of Revenue, 386 U.S.753 — overruled only to the extent its due-process holding requires physical presence for use-tax collection; its Commerce rule is treated separately below.
- Miller Brothers v. Maryland, 347 U.S.340 — the minimum-connection requirement remains; any indispensable physical-entry implication is displaced at the stated collection scope.
- International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S.310 — applied for purposeful affiliation and fair connection.
- Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz, 471 U.S.462 — its nonphysical purposeful-contact reasoning is extended by analogy to the collection obligation; every personal-jurisdiction rule is not imported.
- Shaffer v. Heitner, 433 U.S.186 — applied by comparable reasoning against a mechanical presence prerequisite.
- Moorman Manufacturing Co. v. Bair, 437 U.S.267 — the requirement that the tax relate rationally to state-connected values remains.

### 2. The specific Commerce collection precedent remains controlling

**Holding and operative rule:** Bellas Hess’s specific Commerce Clause rule continues to bar imposing a sales-or-use-tax collection duty on a seller whose customer connections are limited to mail or common-carrier commerce without the requisite local presence. Quill’s solicitation and remote deliveries do not themselves displace that rule; Complete Auto’s separate substantial-nexus requirement is not satisfied merely by purposeful market access or the due-process showing.

**Authority:** The common, narrower ground expressly supported by Stevens, Blackmun, O’Connor and Souter in Part IV, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas in their concurrence, and Stone in his concurrence in the judgment; eight Justices retain the particular precedent. The full Part IV substantive policy analysis has four joins and is not an Opinion of the Court. The software application has the separate five-Justice authority identified below.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that its specific collection precedent remains in force. Complete Auto abandoned a formal immunity based on how a State labels its tax, but it preserved substantial nexus as a distinct condition and did not overrule Bellas Hess’s mail-order collection boundary. National Geographic and D.H. Holmes distinguished that precedent rather than announcing its abandonment. The State’s account of economic growth and easier calculation presents a substantial argument for change, but it does not remove the particular precedent or the reliance of sellers that arranged distribution and completed transactions under it. Stare decisis supports retaining that defined boundary, while Congress can make a coordinated adjustment to collection obligations. Adherence does not require every Justice to endorse the precedent’s original substantive reasoning. This ground does not extend physical presence to every tax or establish a quantitative sales threshold. It preserves the particular rule and the independent constitutional limits that any different measure must satisfy.

**Precedent treatment:**
- National Bellas Hess v. Department of Revenue, 386 U.S.753 — retained for its specific Commerce Clause sales-and-use collection rule; no general immunity for interstate commerce is restored.
- Complete Auto Transit v. Brady, 430 U.S.274 — substantial nexus remains distinct from fair apportionment, nondiscrimination and fair relation to state services; its rejection of Spector’s labeling rule remains.
- National Geographic Society v. California Board of Equalization, 430 U.S.551, and D.H. Holmes Co. v. McNamara, 486 U.S.24 — their express distinctions of Bellas Hess preserve reasonable reliance on the particular collection precedent rather than establish its abandonment.
- Patterson v. McLean Credit Union, 491 U.S.164, and Payne v. Tennessee, 501 U.S.808 — their congressional-correction and reliance principles support the narrower adherence ground at the stated scope.
- Prudential Insurance Co. v. Benjamin, 328 U.S.408 — preserves Congress’s power to authorize otherwise dormant-Commerce burdens; Congress cannot authorize a due-process violation.

### 3. The limited software interests do not establish sufficient collection nexus

**Holding and operative rule:** Quill’s insignificant interests in the licensed ordering software on this record do not supply the local commercial presence required to take its mail-order sales outside the retained Commerce Clause collection rule. This conclusion rejects the State’s asserted alternative nexus for this seller without prescribing a general rule for all software, licenses or in-state property.

**Authority:** Stevens, Blackmun, O’Connor and Souter in Part IV, together with Stone’s expressly matching record-specific software ground in his concurrence in the judgment; five Justices adopt this application at the same scope. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas support reversal but are not counted as joining this substantive software explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the limited ordering-software interests do not establish the local commercial activity missing from this record. Bellas Hess supplies the specific collection boundary that the Court retains, and Tyler Pipe identifies the significance of activity within the State that establishes and maintains the seller’s market. Quill’s licensed ordering materials facilitate communication with its out-of-state business; the insignificant property interest does not turn those materials into the local business operation or representative activity on which the State’s alternative depends. The argument that any retained property must end the inquiry gives the bare ownership label more significance than this record warrants. The Court rejects that alternative on the record before it and does not find an unproved warehouse, employee or agent. Nor does it define a universal property-value threshold or decide that every software license is insufficient. Its holding concerns these limited interests and this challenged collection demand alone.

**Precedent treatment:**
- National Bellas Hess v. Department of Revenue, 386 U.S.753 — applied to this mail-order collection demand after rejection of the asserted distinguishing property interest.

**Fractured authority:** There is an express majority for the particular surviving-precedent rule, not merely eight matching dispositions. Marks v. United States, 430 U.S.188, does not authorize importing the plurality’s broader economic-policy theory through the concurring votes. The narrower adherence ground is a genuine common ground of the reversal writings and a logical subset of Part IV’s precedent-based reason for the same result. The record-specific software application independently has the five expressly identified supporters. Neither conclusion creates a general physical-presence theory for other taxes.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The consumer’s use tax, other taxes and general tax immunity are outside the holding. No numerical transaction threshold, general software-nexus rule or result under a future statute is announced. Potential retroactive effects of overruling the Commerce rule were excluded from the grant and are not reached. The Court makes no §1983 or §1988 entitlement ruling. Stone alone reserves due process; the eight-Justice due-process holding nevertheless controls.

## 8. Law After Decision

Effective May 26, the two constitutional inquiries must be kept separate. Purposeful sustained solicitation on this record satisfies due process without physical presence; Congress cannot dispense with that constitutional limit. Bellas Hess’s specific dormant-Commerce collection rule continues to bar this demand, including the asserted minimal software basis. Congress remains able to change dormant-Commerce constraints through valid legislation. Before this event Bellas Hess supplied both grounds; afterward its due-process physical-presence requirement is displaced while its specific Commerce rule survives. The broad Part IV account of bright-line rules and national economic burdens has four joins; only the narrower expressly shared precedent ground controls across the eight reversal votes. Neither consumer liability nor every other state tax changes.

## 9. Separate Writings

Scalia, joined by Kennedy and Thomas, joins the Court’s due-process analysis on the understanding that analogous reasoning does not equate every adjudicative and legislative jurisdiction standard. On Commerce, they retain the square precedent through stare decisis, justified reliance and Congress’s authority, without revisiting its substantive merits. Stone separately retains the specific collection rule within Complete Auto, concludes that insignificant software is no substitute for local market-maintaining activity, and reserves due process because the Commerce ground resolves the demand. His reliance and congressional-adjustment reasons support adherence without extending a physical-presence condition to all taxation. White joins the due-process ruling but would overrule Bellas Hess’s Commerce rule: sustained economic exploitation and market benefits should meet nexus, and the physical distinction creates unjustified competitive differences. He also questions deciding the software issue against the State without further inquiry. The plurality’s broader defense of the Commerce boundary commands four votes only.
