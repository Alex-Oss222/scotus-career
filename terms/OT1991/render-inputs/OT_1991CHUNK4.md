# INS v. Doherty, No. 90-925

**Render form: full — An equal division on withholding and fractured asylum reasoning; judgment and controlling-authority structure differ materially from the comparator.**

## 1. Event

*INS v. Doherty*, 502 U.S. 314 (1992), No. 90-925. October Term 1991. Argued 1991-10-16; decided 1992-01-15. On certiorari to the Second Circuit, 908 F.2d 1108, which required reopening after sustaining the Attorney General's rejection of Ireland as the designated destination. The granted controversy concerns denial of reopening for asylum and withholding of deportation; the INS seeks reversal. Destination selection and detention are outside the disposition.

## 2. Participation

Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter participated at argument and at decision. Thomas took no part: argument preceded his October 23, 1991 oath and no reargument is established. Quorum: 8 (six required); five votes are required for a majority.

## 3. Public Action

The Second Circuit judgment is reversed as to asylum and affirmed by an equally divided Court as to withholding.

## 4. Judgment & Remedy

**Asylum: reverse the reopening requirement:** Reverse — 8 (Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter).

**Withholding: requested judgment:** Reverse and sustain denial of reopening — 4 (White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Kennedy); Vacate and remand for review of exclusions — 4 (Stone, Stevens, Scalia, Souter).

Asylum reversal is unanimous, 8-0. On withholding, neither proposed disposition obtains five votes: the 4-4 division leaves the Second Circuit's withholding judgment affirmed by operation of equal division. The Second Circuit implements that surviving withholding reopening relief and the asylum reversal. No protection is awarded; the Supreme Court does not adopt the lower court's reasoning or order Stone's proposed intervening exclusions review. The destination-selection and detention judgments are undisturbed.

## 5. Opinion Topology

A per curiam judgment announces the 8-0 asylum reversal and 4-4 withholding affirmance; it supplies no substantive reopening rationale. White writes an opinion concurring in the asylum judgment and dissenting from the withholding disposition, joined by Blackmun and O'Connor, relying on the new-evidence requirement. Kennedy writes separately in the same judgment alignment, relying on tactical withdrawal. Stone writes an opinion concurring in the asylum judgment and explaining his vote to vacate and remand the withholding component, joined in full by Stevens, Scalia and Souter. None of these substantive writings is an Opinion of the Court.

## 6. Holdings

### Disposition and absence of a controlling reopening rationale

**Holding and operative rule:** The asylum portion of the Second Circuit's judgment is reversed; its withholding portion is affirmed by an equally divided Court. The disposition creates no controlling substantive reopening rule on either issue.

**Authority:** The per curiam judgment records eight votes for asylum reversal and the equal division on withholding. No separate rationale receives five of the eight participating Justices; no substantive proposition is treated as controlling.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court disposes of the two forms of relief separately. All participating Justices would set aside the requirement to reopen the asylum application, but they do not unite behind a reason that controls a later case. Three rely on the regulation's new-evidence condition, one on the consequences of tactical withdrawal, and four on lawful discretion to deny asylum as distinguished from mandatory withholding. Those grounds are independent; one is not a logical subset of the others. On withholding, four would sustain the administrative denial and four would return the case for judicial review of the independent exclusion grounds. Under Durant, the equal division leaves the lower judgment in place without adopting its reasoning. Abudu continues to supply the previously established distinctions among reopening grounds. Neither the asylum vote nor the withholding disposition converts any separate opinion into a new rule binding other courts.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *INS v. Abudu*, 485 U.S. 94 — remains governing preexisting law; the separate opinions disagree about its application, and no new extension controls.
- *INS v. Rios-Pineda*, 471 U.S. 444 — procedural reopening discretion remains intact; no majority specifies its application to this changed destination.
- *INS v. Stevic*, 467 U.S. 407, and *INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. 421 — the distinction between asylum and mandatory withholding remains intact.
- *Marks v. United States*, 430 U.S. 188 — no controlling narrowest rationale can be extracted from independent grounds lacking a logical common denominator.
- *Durant v. Essex Co.*, 74 U.S. 107, 110–113 — applied for the binding interparty effect and absence of precedent when equal division prevents the requested appellate disposition.

# White v. Illinois, No. 90-6113

**Render form: compact — Unanimous judgment; the controlling exception rule, component coalitions, remedy and precedent treatment match the comparator. Substitution of Stone as author and for Rehnquist's vote is not itself material.**

## 1. Event

*White v. Illinois*, 502 U.S. 346 (1992), No. 90-6113. October Term 1991. Argued 1991-11-05; decided 1992-01-15. Certiorari to the Illinois Appellate Court, Fourth District, 198 Ill. App. 3d 641, 555 N.E.2d 1241, after the Illinois Supreme Court denied review. Review is limited to whether the Confrontation Clause requires production or an unavailability showing before admission of statements accepted as spontaneous declarations and statements for medical treatment. Petitioner seeks reversal of his convictions on that ground.

## 2. Participation

Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas participated at argument and at decision. No nonparticipation is established. Quorum: 9 (six required); five votes are required for a majority.

## 3. Public Action

The Illinois Appellate Court judgment is affirmed.

## 4. Judgment & Remedy

**Limited Confrontation Clause claim:** Affirm — 9 (Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas).

9-0, all nine Justices. No new trial or evidentiary hearing is required on the unavailability contention. State-law admissibility outside the granted premise and any other claims receive no new adjudication.

## 5. Opinion Topology

Stone delivers the opinion of the Court. White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Souter join in full. Scalia and Thomas join Part I, the exception and unavailability analysis, but not Part II, rejecting the government's proposed limitation of confrontation to formal accusations. Thomas concurs in part and in the judgment, joined by Scalia.

## 6. Holdings

### No universal production or unavailability prerequisite

**Holding and operative rule:** Where the granted review assumes that statements were properly admitted under the firmly rooted spontaneous-declaration and medical-treatment exceptions, the Confrontation Clause does not require the prosecution first to produce the declarant or establish unavailability. The accepted exception foundations remain necessary; the ruling does not validate a disputed foundation or every police interview.

**Authority:** Part I of Stone's opinion, joined by White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; nine Justices endorse this rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that absence alone does not impose a production requirement on these accepted exceptions. Inadi explains why trial testimony cannot necessarily replace a statement whose evidentiary value derives from the circumstances in which it was made. A spontaneous response and a statement made for treatment carry features that later testimony cannot recreate. Roberts does not establish a universal preliminary demand for unavailability; its former-testimony setting is different. Under the received reliability framework, the firmly rooted exceptions at issue supply the requisite assurance when their foundations are satisfied. Wright preserves the separate inquiry applicable outside such exceptions, and an exception label cannot settle disputed facts. Here state-law membership in the exceptions is the premise of the limited review. Coy and Craig concern safeguards when a trial witness is presented through an altered procedure; they do not require the extra evidentiary gateway sought here. No question of harmless error arises on the claim resolved.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *United States v. Inadi*, 475 U.S. 387 — extended from coconspirator declarations to the two accepted exceptions because later testimony does not replace their contextual value.
- *Ohio v. Roberts*, 448 U.S. 56 — preserved for its received confrontation framework; its former-testimony unavailability analysis is not universalized.
- *Idaho v. Wright*, 497 U.S. 805 — preserved for constitutional reliability outside firmly rooted exceptions; no unsupported judicial assurance substitutes for its requirements.
- *Bourjaily v. United States*, 483 U.S. 171 — firmly rooted exceptions retain their established reliability significance.
- *Coy v. Iowa*, 487 U.S. 1012, and *Maryland v. Craig*, 497 U.S. 836 — distinguished as trial-witness presentation cases; their safeguards remain intact.

### The government's categorical definition of witness is rejected

**Holding and operative rule:** The Confrontation Clause is not categorically confined to formal affidavits or equivalent trial substitutes, so that limiting definition does not supply an alternative basis for admitting these statements. The Court resolves the accepted exceptions under existing confrontation law and leaves any broader reconstruction of that law open.

**Authority:** Part II of Stone's opinion, joined by White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Souter; seven Justices reject the proposed categorical narrowing. Scalia and Thomas withhold that join.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court declines to remove this case from confrontation law by defining its protection to cover only formal accusations. The decisions governing hearsay and confrontation address more than affidavits or their formal equivalents. Mattox recognizes the constitutional problem created by absent testimony while permitting an established exception, and the later reliability cases continue to examine the substance and setting of out-of-court statements. Inadi itself supplies an exception-specific answer rather than declaring that informal statements can never implicate confrontation. The government's proposed categorical boundary would discard that framework without a need to do so here. The accepted spontaneous and treatment exceptions resolve the actual production question. This conclusion does not establish that every hearsay statement violates the Constitution, nor does it decide the constitutional treatment of a formal investigative accusation on a disputed record. Those questions require their own factual premises and candid consideration of the governing precedents.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Mattox v. United States*, 156 U.S. 237 — retained as evidence of confrontation's substantive protection and its coexistence with established exceptions.
- *California v. Green*, 399 U.S. 149, and *Dutton v. Evans*, 400 U.S. 74 — preserved; the Court's treatment of out-of-court statements is not displaced by a formal-affidavit-only definition.
- *Inadi* and *Roberts* — applied as exception-sensitive confrontation decisions, not replaced with a new categorical testimonial rule.

# Rufo v. Inmates of Suffolk County Jail; Rapone v. Inmates of Suffolk County Jail, Nos. 90-954 and 90-1004

**Render form: compact — 6-2 judgment; five-Justice controlling coalition, judgment-only concurrence, legal standard and remand are materially unchanged from the comparator. A change of Court author alone is not material.**

## 1. Event

*Rufo v. Inmates of Suffolk County Jail; Rapone v. Inmates of Suffolk County Jail*, 502 U.S. 367 (1992), Nos. 90-954 and 90-1004. October Term 1991. Argued 1991-10-09; decided 1992-01-15. Consolidated certiorari from the First Circuit, 915 F.2d 1557, affirming refusal to modify a jail consent decree, 734 F. Supp. 561. Officials seek relief under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 60(b)(5) from a single-cell requirement to permit proposed double-celling. Both judgments and the standard governing modification are before the Court.

## 2. Participation

Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter participated at argument and at decision. Thomas took no part: argument preceded his October 23, 1991 oath and no reargument is established. Quorum: 8 (six required); five votes are required for a majority.

## 3. Public Action

Both First Circuit judgments are vacated and the cases remanded.

## 4. Judgment & Remedy

**Modification decision in both consolidated cases:** Vacate and remand — 6 (Stone, White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter); Affirm denial — 2 (Blackmun, Stevens).

6-2. Stone, White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter vote to vacate and remand; Blackmun and Stevens would affirm. The district court must determine whether proven changed circumstances make continued enforcement inequitable and whether a proposed modification is suitably responsive. The existing decree remains operative. No number of double cells is authorized.

## 5. Opinion Topology

Stone delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by White, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter. O'Connor concurs in the judgment. Stevens dissents, joined by Blackmun.

## 6. Holdings

### Threshold for modifying an institutional consent decree

**Holding and operative rule:** Under Rule 60(b)(5), the party seeking to modify an institutional consent decree must prove a significant change in fact or law that makes continued enforcement inequitable; an unforeseeable catastrophe is not required. Actual anticipation and acceptance of the relevant risk impose a heavy burden to justify relief, and dissatisfaction, a new administration, or the availability of a cheaper constitutional alternative alone does not suffice.

**Authority:** Stone's opinion, joined by White, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter; five of eight participating Justices. O'Connor supplies a sixth remand vote on her separate ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that prospective institutional relief remains subject to an equitable assessment of changed circumstances. Swift addressed a particular antitrust decree and does not impose its demanding factual formulation on every continuing public institution. System Federation confirms that continuing decrees can respond to altered circumstances; United Shoe ties modification to the decree's actual operation and purposes. Rule 60(b)(5) therefore requires a supported significant change, not proof that officials could never have foreseen it. Anticipation still matters greatly. Officials who accepted a known risk must explain why enforcement has nevertheless become inequitable, rather than merely regret their bargain. Here repeated commitment to single occupancy cannot be ignored. But the district court's alternative reliance on that term's centrality does not show that it assessed the requested accommodation free of the restrictive premise. Remand permits that assessment. The Court does not find that population growth, cost, or any other asserted change actually satisfies the officials' burden.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *United States v. Swift & Co.*, 286 U.S. 106 — its disposition is preserved; its demanding factual formulation is confined to its setting rather than imposed universally.
- *System Federation No. 91 v. Wright*, 364 U.S. 642 — applied to preserve equitable power to adjust continuing obligations to changed conditions.
- *United States v. United Shoe Machinery Corp.*, 391 U.S. 244 — applied to connect modification to the continuing decree's purpose and operation.
- *Board of Education v. Dowell*, 498 U.S. 237 — reinforces contextual treatment of institutional equitable relief; no school-desegregation termination rule is imported.

### Fit and limits of the proposed modification

**Holding and operative rule:** A justified modification must be suitably tailored to the proven change and preserve lawful protection of the decree's remaining purposes; the court may not simply rewrite a consent decree to the constitutional minimum. Bell's clarification of constitutional limits does not by itself eliminate a voluntarily accepted single-cell obligation, and financial pressure cannot authorize unconstitutional confinement.

**Authority:** Stone's opinion, joined by White, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter; the same five-Justice majority controls the remedial standard.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a threshold change does not authorize abandonment of the settlement. A consent decree can require more than a court could have compelled after a constitutional trial, as Local No. 93 explains. Bell rejects a categorical constitutional objection to double-celling, but it does not dissolve an independently accepted single-cell promise. The district court must connect any alteration to the established change and examine whether the revised protection remains faithful to the decree's purposes. It may consider practical administration and cost in choosing lawful relief while preserving its own equitable judgment; those considerations cannot excuse unconstitutional conditions or substitute for the threshold showing. System Federation supports adaptation, not a unilateral withdrawal from binding obligations. Because the proper standard was not applied, this Court selects no replacement design. The need for double cells, the proposed arrangement's constitutionality, and the adequacy of remaining safeguards are left for the district court on the record.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Local No. 93, International Association of Firefighters v. Cleveland*, 478 U.S. 501 — consent obligations may exceed what constitutional liability alone would require.
- *Bell v. Wolfish*, 441 U.S. 520 — its constitutional treatment of double-celling does not erase a consensual single-cell requirement.
- *System Federation No. 91 v. Wright*, 364 U.S. 642 — preserves adjustment for changed circumstances while maintaining judicial control of lawful relief.

## 8. Law After Decision

Rule 60(b)(5) now supplies a two-part institutional-decree inquiry: significant change making continued enforcement inequitable, then suitably tailored relief. Actual anticipation weighs heavily against relief without an absolute unforeseeability requirement. Consent obligations may exceed constitutional minima; fiscal concerns cannot excuse violations. Application to this proposed design remains open.

## 9. Separate Writings

O'Connor concurs only in the judgment. She agrees that reconsideration is necessary because anticipation was treated as dispositive, relevant financial concerns were excluded, and any departure from single occupancy was treated as defeating the decree. She emphasizes the district judge's experience and discretion and does not compel administrative deference on whether modification is warranted. Stevens, joined by Blackmun, accepts flexibility but would affirm: the officials anticipated growth and reaffirmed single occupancy in 1985, and the trial court's alternative explanation adequately protects settlement reliance.

## 10. Procedure After Action

Both cases return through the First Circuit to the district court for the Rule 60(b)(5) inquiry. The decree remains binding pending a lawful modification. Supreme Court merits proceedings close without retained supervision.

# Dewsnup v. Timm, No. 90-741

**Render form: full — 5-3 judgment with a changed controlling coalition: Stone votes to reverse rather than joining the historical affirmance.**

## 1. Event

*Dewsnup v. Timm*, 502 U.S. 410 (1992), No. 90-741. October Term 1991. Argued 1991-10-15; decided 1992-01-15. Certiorari to the Tenth Circuit, 908 F.2d 588, affirming dismissal of a Chapter 7 debtor's action to reduce a real-property lien under 11 U.S.C. §§506(a) and (d). The debtor seeks avoidance of the lien above the property's established value. The bankruptcy court dismissed with prejudice, 87 B.R. 676, and the district court affirmed.

## 2. Participation

Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter participated at argument and at decision. Thomas took no part: argument preceded his October 23, 1991 oath and no reargument is established. Quorum: 8 (six required); five votes are required for a majority.

## 3. Public Action

The Tenth Circuit judgment is affirmed.

## 4. Judgment & Remedy

**Section 506(d) strip-down claim:** Affirm — 5 (White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy); Reverse and remand — 3 (Stone, Scalia, Souter).

5-3. White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Kennedy affirm; Stone, Scalia and Souter would reverse and remand. The allowed lien is not reduced under §506(d) to the judicially determined property value. The dismissal stands. The Court does not adopt the Tenth Circuit's abandonment rationale or make a new finding about administration of the estate.

## 5. Opinion Topology

Blackmun delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by White, Stevens, O'Connor and Kennedy. Stone dissents, joined in full by Scalia and Souter.

## 6. Holdings

### Allowed real-property liens in the presented Chapter 7 setting

**Holding and operative rule:** In the Chapter 7 setting presented, §506(d) does not reduce a lien on real property to its current judicially determined value when the claim is allowed under §502 and secured by that lien. Section 506(a)'s valuation for treatment of a claim does not, on these facts, compel avoidance of the lien's excess under §506(d); other statutory settings and express exceptions remain outside the decision.

**Authority:** Blackmun's opinion, joined by White, Stevens, O'Connor and Kennedy; five of eight participating Justices control the statutory construction and affirmance.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this allowed lien survives the requested Chapter 7 strip-down. Section 506's interaction is sufficiently uncertain in this setting that the established distinction between personal liability and the lien informs its construction. Long preserves a lien despite discharge, and Johnson confirms that extinguishing personal liability does not itself extinguish the creditor's claim against the property. Section 506(a) assigns secured and unsecured amounts for the claim's treatment; the Court does not read that valuation as automatically avoiding the corresponding lien under subsection (d). The debtor's adjacent-text reading has force, but it would alter the ordinary survival of an allowed lien and transfer later appreciation without a sufficiently clear instruction in this setting. Ron Pair's attention to enacted text remains governing; the disagreement concerns what these provisions mean together here. The Court confines its answer to the presented real-property lien and decides neither hypothetical applications of §506 nor reorganization-specific restrictions.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Long v. Bullard*, 117 U.S. 617 — the ordinary survival of liens through personal discharge remains an interpretive premise, not immunity from every express avoidance provision.
- *Johnson v. Home State Bank*, 501 U.S. 78 — applied for the distinction between extinguished personal liability and a surviving claim against property.
- *Farrey v. Sanderfoot*, 500 U.S. 291 — lien survival informs the Code's operation subject to its actual avoidance provisions.
- *United States v. Ron Pair Enterprises, Inc.*, 489 U.S. 235 — preserved; the Court finds contextual uncertainty here rather than displacing the priority of clear statutory text.
- *United Savings Association v. Timbers of Inwood Forest Associates*, 484 U.S. 365 — §506(a)'s valuation function remains operative; it does not decide this §506(d) question.
- *Union Bank v. Wolas*, simulated December 11, 1991 — its §547(c)(2) rule remains intact; no lien-avoidance rule is drawn from its treatment of debt maturity.

## 8. Law After Decision

Allowed, lien-backed real-property debt in the presented Chapter 7 posture is not stripped down through §506(d) merely because §506(a) values the collateral below the allowed debt. Section 506(a) continues to perform its valuation function. Other subsections, statutory exceptions and reorganization questions are reserved. No constitutional taking or future-valuation question is adjudicated.

## 9. Separate Writings

Stone, joined by Scalia and Souter, would read the adjacent provisions consistently: allowance establishes the claim, while §506(a) supplies the amount secured for §506(d). They would preserve the established $39,000 secured portion and avoid only the unsupported excess, subject to preserved exceptions and estate-administration issues on remand. They distinguish Long and Johnson because survival through personal discharge does not defeat an affirmative statutory avoidance provision. Reliance interests cannot supply an unwritten exception to the enacted valuation language.

## 10. Procedure After Action

The affirmance leaves dismissal of the debtor's strip-down action in place and ends Supreme Court review. No valuation rehearing, new abandonment finding, or retained jurisdiction is ordered.

# Wyoming v. Oklahoma, No. 112, Original

**Render form: full — 7-2 disposition with Stone joining the invalidation coalition rather than the historical jurisdictional dissent; an operative injunction issues with retained implementation jurisdiction.**

## 1. Event

*Wyoming v. Oklahoma*, 502 U.S. 437 (1992), No. 112, Original. October Term 1991. Argued 1991-11-04; decided 1992-01-22. Original action between States under Article III and 28 U.S.C. §1251(a), on exceptions to the Special Master's report and motions for summary judgment. Wyoming challenges Oklahoma's requirement that coal-fired electric utilities use at least ten percent Oklahoma coal, measured by heat input, and seeks declaratory and injunctive relief. There is no lower-court judgment to affirm or reverse.

## 2. Participation

Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas participated at argument and at decision. No nonparticipation is established. Quorum: 9 (six required); five votes are required for a majority.

## 3. Public Action

Wyoming's severability exception is sustained, Oklahoma's exceptions are overruled, and summary judgment is entered for Wyoming. The challenged coal-purchasing Act is declared unconstitutional and its enforcement enjoined. Jurisdiction is retained for implementation.

## 4. Judgment & Remedy

**Standing and exercise of original jurisdiction:** Exercise jurisdiction — 7 (Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter); Dismiss — 2 (Scalia, Thomas).

**Disposition of the exceptions and requested relief:** Grant Wyoming summary judgment, invalidate the Act, enjoin enforcement — 7 (Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter); Dismiss without reaching merits — 2 (Scalia, Thomas).

7-2 jurisdictional disposition. Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Souter grant the stated relief. Scalia and Thomas would dismiss and cast no merits vote to uphold the Act. The injunction bars Oklahoma and its officials from enforcing the challenged ten-percent in-state-coal requirement against covered utilities, including its proposed preservation as a GRDA-only application. It operates from this January 22 judgment; no tax damages are awarded. The Court retains jurisdiction over implementation, without deciding a separately enacted state purchasing policy or setting an invented future hearing.

## 5. Opinion Topology

Stone delivers the opinion and operative decree of the Court, joined in full by White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Souter. Scalia dissents, joined by Thomas. Thomas also dissents separately, joined by Scalia, on discretionary original jurisdiction.

## 6. Holdings

### Wyoming's own fiscal injury

**Holding and operative rule:** A documented loss of severance-tax revenue caused by a mandatory substitution away from the State's taxed coal establishes Wyoming's own Article III injury where the record supports causation and an injunction would remove the challenged purchasing mandate. Redressability does not require a guarantee that every displaced purchase will return, but generalized hopes of future tax growth do not suffice.

**Authority:** Stone's opinion, joined by White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Souter; seven Justices decide standing.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Wyoming asserts a concrete fiscal loss of its own. Maryland v. Louisiana recognizes that a State can challenge another State's commercial restriction when the challenged measure injures its legally protected interests. Here the purchasing record, the tax on extraction and the evidence of available production capacity connect the required substitution to reduced Wyoming severance receipts. This is more than a prediction that a generally healthier economy would someday yield more revenue. The dissent disputes the production inference, but the record supports it without an identified material factual contradiction requiring trial. An injunction removes the discriminatory requirement that caused the displacement; Article III does not demand certainty about every subsequent purchaser's choice. The private producers' injury therefore does not exhaust the controversy. The Court applies ordinary injury, causation and redressability requirements and adopts no special exemption for sovereign plaintiffs or unrelated future fiscal effects.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Maryland v. Louisiana*, 451 U.S. 725 — applied to a State's demonstrated injury from another State's commercial restriction.
- *Hunt v. Washington State Apple Advertising Commission*, 432 U.S. 333 — geographic market exclusion is a concrete commercial injury; Wyoming still proves its own fiscal stake.
- *Warth v. Seldin*, 422 U.S. 490, and *Simon v. Eastern Kentucky Welfare Rights Organization*, 426 U.S. 26 — ordinary personal-injury and causation requirements are preserved, not relaxed for States.

### Exercise of original jurisdiction

**Holding and operative rule:** The Court exercises original jurisdiction over this substantial interstate controversy because Wyoming's own fiscal injury and the challenged statewide mandate warrant direct resolution and no equivalent pending private action would fully resolve the State's claim. Exclusive jurisdiction under §1251(a) does not eliminate the Court's established discretion in administering its original docket.

**Authority:** The same seven-Justice Court opinion supplies the jurisdictional exercise; Thomas's contrary discretionary ground is noncontrolling.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this controversy warrants exercise of its original jurisdiction. Massachusetts v. Missouri makes the seriousness of the State's interest and the availability of other avenues relevant to that decision. Texas v. New Mexico preserves the Court's responsibility to administer substantial controversies between States. Wyoming identifies a present fiscal injury, and the challenged requirement regulates an integrated class of public and private utilities across Oklahoma. No pending private case provides an equivalent resolution of Wyoming's own claim. The possibility that a producer or utility could sue does not require the State to surrender a distinct, documented injury to another litigant's choice of case and relief. The earlier acceptance of the bill does not render jurisdiction immune from reconsideration, but the developed record supports retaining it. This decision does not promise an original forum whenever state tax receipts might indirectly be affected by another State's regulation.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Massachusetts v. Missouri*, 308 U.S. 1 — applied for seriousness of the sovereign interest and adequacy of other available relief.
- *Texas v. New Mexico*, 462 U.S. 554 — preserved for administration of substantial interstate original controversies.
- *Illinois v. City of Milwaukee*, 406 U.S. 91 — alternative forums remain relevant; no equivalent proceeding resolves this State's injury here.
- *Arizona v. California*, 460 U.S. 605 — an earlier jurisdictional ruling is not an irrevocable bar to reconsidering jurisdiction on the developed record.

### The discriminatory coal quota and asserted justifications

**Holding and operative rule:** Oklahoma's express in-state-coal quota discriminates against interstate commerce and cannot stand unless the State establishes a legitimate local purpose that cannot adequately be served by reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives; it has not done so here. The Federal Power Act's general preservation of state utility regulation does not clearly authorize this geographic discrimination.

**Authority:** Stone's opinion joined by the same six Associate Justices; seven Justices invalidate the quota. Scalia and Thomas do not reach this merits question.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Act's origin requirement is unlawful discrimination. Philadelphia v. New Jersey rejects barriers favoring local commerce merely because they retain benefits within the State. Hughes requires a discriminatory measure's legitimate purpose to be tested against available nondiscriminatory means, and New Energy confirms that local economic favoritism cannot supply the needed justification. The quota reserves part of the market for Oklahoma coal by excluding competing coal from that portion; its modest percentage does not cure the discrimination. Reliable energy supply is a legitimate concern, but this record does not show that an origin quota is necessary when neutral diversification measures are available. Maintaining the local coal industry is the protected advantage, not a sufficient defense. New England Power also requires clear congressional authorization before discriminatory state regulation is exempted from this limit. The Federal Power Act's general allocation of utility authority supplies no such permission. Neutral energy regulation remains available.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Philadelphia v. New Jersey*, 437 U.S. 617 — applied to invalidate facial geographic discrimination regardless of the favored market share's size.
- *Hughes v. Oklahoma*, 441 U.S. 322 — applied for legitimate purpose and the absence of reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives.
- *New Energy Co. of Indiana v. Limbach*, 486 U.S. 269, and *Bacchus Imports, Ltd. v. Dias*, 468 U.S. 263 — local commercial advantage does not justify discrimination.
- *New England Power Co. v. New Hampshire*, 455 U.S. 331 — general federal permission to regulate does not clearly authorize discriminatory restrictions.
- *Pike v. Bruce Church, Inc.*, 397 U.S. 137 — its evenhanded-regulation balance is not the governing branch for this express origin quota.
- *Maine v. Taylor*, 477 U.S. 131 — a proved need without reasonable neutral alternatives may justify discrimination; that showing is absent here.

### No judicial conversion into a state-only purchasing directive

**Holding and operative rule:** The Act's integrated purchasing mandate cannot be preserved solely for the state-owned Grand River Dam Authority without an adequate state-law basis for severing applications and retaining that different legislative scheme. The Court invalidates the enacted regulatory program while leaving the validity of an independently adopted state procurement policy open.

**Authority:** The seven-Justice Court opinion sustains Wyoming's severability exception and controls the injunction's scope.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that ownership of one regulated utility does not authorize rewriting this Act. Reeves recognizes a State's distinct position when it participates in a market, but the enactment before the Court commands purchases by an integrated class of public and private generators. A potential defense for independent public procurement does not establish that the legislature enacted that separate policy or would retain it alone. Dorchy makes legislative intent central to severability. The statute's severability language concerning parts does not adequately establish that the discriminatory program can be transformed by severing its private applications while retaining a GRDA-only command. The Court therefore sustains Wyoming's exception and enjoins the enacted quota as a whole. This treatment neither abolishes the market-participant doctrine nor decides whether a separately adopted state purchasing preference would qualify for it. Oklahoma remains free to adopt lawful neutral regulation and to present any genuinely distinct procurement policy in an appropriate case.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Reeves, Inc. v. Stake*, 447 U.S. 429, and *Hughes v. Alexandria Scrap Corp.*, 426 U.S. 794 — the market-participant doctrine is preserved; no independent procurement policy is adjudicated.
- *South-Central Timber Development, Inc. v. Wunnicke*, 467 U.S. 82 — distinguishes market participation from regulatory restrictions beyond the relevant participation.
- *Dorchy v. Kansas*, 264 U.S. 286, and *Champlin Refining Co. v. Corporation Commission*, 286 U.S. 210 — severability depends on legislative intent and a workable retained enactment, not judicial preference for a substitute policy.

# INS v. Elias-Zacarias, No. 90-1342

**Render form: full — The judgment to reverse has six votes, but the existing-record disposition and Court rationale have five; Stone requires further review and does not join that disposition. The controlling coalition and remedy alignment differ materially from history.**

## 1. Event

*INS v. Elias-Zacarias*, 502 U.S. 478 (1992), No. 90-1342. October Term 1991. Argued 1991-11-04; decided 1992-01-22. Certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 921 F.2d 844, which displaced the Board of Immigration Appeals' denial of reopening using a categorical view of coercive recruitment as political persecution. The INS seeks reversal. The Court addresses the statutory nexus to political opinion and the evidentiary basis for overturning the Board, preserving the difference between reopening and an initial application.

## 2. Participation

Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas participated at argument and at decision. No nonparticipation is established. Quorum: 9 (six required); five votes are required for a majority.

## 3. Public Action

The Ninth Circuit judgment is reversed. Five Justices conclude that the existing record does not justify overturning the Board's denial; Stone would require further review on remand.

## 4. Judgment & Remedy

**Ninth Circuit's categorical political-persecution disposition:** Reverse — 6 (Stone, White, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas); Affirm — 3 (Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor).

**Operative treatment of the Board's denial:** Leave the Board's denial effective without further nexus review — 5 (White, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas); Remand for record-specific review of the actual reopening decision — 1 (Stone); Affirm the Ninth Circuit's reopening relief — 3 (Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor).

Reversal: 6-3, Stone, White, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas against Blackmun, Stevens and O'Connor. The operative existing-record resolution commands five votes: White, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. The mandate returns to the Ninth Circuit to implement reversal and leave the Board's denial in force; it does not authorize the additional nexus/reopening review Stone requests. No asylum or withholding is awarded.

## 5. Opinion Topology

Scalia delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by White, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. Stone concurs in the judgment insofar as it rejects the Ninth Circuit's categorical premise and dissents from disposing of the agency decision without further review; he joins no part of the Court opinion. Stevens dissents, joined by Blackmun and O'Connor.

## 6. Holdings

### Political purpose of the recruiting group does not itself establish nexus

**Holding and operative rule:** Coercive recruitment by a politically motivated organization does not necessarily constitute persecution on account of the recruit's political opinion; the protected reason for targeting the applicant must be supported by evidence concerning the particular case. Direct evidence of motive is unnecessary, but the group's general agenda and the mere fact of refusal do not establish that connection by a categorical presumption.

**Authority:** Scalia's opinion, joined by White, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; five Justices control this statutory rule. Stone separately endorses rejection of the categorical premise but supplies no join in the Court's full opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the statute asks why the applicant faces persecution, not simply whether the recruiting organization has political goals. The refugee definition protects against persecution for enumerated reasons. A group's desire to advance its political cause by obtaining fighters does not establish that it seeks this individual because of his political opinion. Refusal may rest on fear, family ties, economic concerns or political belief; the record must support the necessary connection. Cardoza-Fonseca distinguishes the chance of persecution required for asylum from withholding's higher threshold, but it does not dispense with the listed-ground requirement. Circumstantial evidence can establish motive, and the Court demands no persecutor's explicit declaration. Nor does it hold that political neutrality can never be protected. Even assuming an attributed opinion could qualify, the record must show persecution because of that attribution. The Ninth Circuit's universal recruitment premise bypassed this inquiry and cannot support its judgment.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. 421 — asylum's well-founded-fear threshold remains distinct from withholding's higher probability requirement; neither removes the protected-ground nexus.
- *INS v. Stevic*, 467 U.S. 407 — withholding's distinct mandatory standard is preserved.
- *INS v. Abudu*, 485 U.S. 94 — distinct reopening grounds remain; the statutory nexus question is not replaced by discretionary denial of mandatory protection.

### The existing record does not warrant overturning the Board

**Holding and operative rule:** Under §1105a(a)(4), a court may overturn the Board's supported factual determination only when the record compels the contrary finding, not merely because that finding could be supported. Here the evidence does not compel a finding of feared persecution because of the applicant's political opinion, so the Ninth Circuit cannot displace the Board's denial on its stated nexus rationale.

**Authority:** Scalia's opinion joined by White, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; five Justices support both the evidentiary conclusion and the operative restoration of the Board's denial. Stone does not join this holding or its remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Ninth Circuit could not replace the agency's permissible assessment with a contrary inference that the evidence did not require. Columbian Enameling explains that substantial-evidence review does not permit a court to overturn an agency merely because another finding would also be possible. The applicant's explanation focused on fear of government retaliation for joining, and the renewed visits showed continued recruitment pressure without compelling a protected-opinion motive for that pressure. The Court neither makes a credibility finding nor requires direct proof of motive. It concludes that this record does not force the particular inference on which the appellate judgment depends. Abudu continues to distinguish reopening grounds; this ruling does not establish one review standard for every prima-facie determination. It resolves the nexus-based displacement of the Board here, while leaving the different substantive thresholds for asylum and withholding intact. Stone would instead require another record-specific review, but that proposed mandate does not command a majority.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *NLRB v. Columbian Enameling & Stamping Co.*, 306 U.S. 292 — applied for the distinction between evidence permitting a finding and evidence requiring displacement of the agency's finding.
- *INS v. Abudu*, 485 U.S. 94 — preserved; the Court does not expand this case's nexus review into a universal rule for all reopening determinations.
- *INS v. Doherty*, simulated January 15, 1992 — its dispositions created no new controlling reopening rationale and supply no contrary rule or merits finding here.

# Presley v. Etowah County Commission; Mack v. Russell County Commission, Nos. 90-711 and 90-712

Direct appeals from a three-judge District Court for the Middle District of Alabama concerning §5 of the Voting Rights Act. ## 2. Participation

Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas participated at argument and at decision. No nonparticipation is established. Quorum: 9 (six required); five votes are required for a majority.

## 3. Public Action

The noncoverage judgments in both appeals are affirmed.

## 4. Judgment & Remedy

**No. 90-711 — Common Fund Resolution:** Affirm noncoverage — 5 (O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas); Reverse and remand for preclearance — 4 (Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens).

## 6. Holdings

### The two resolutions do not change a voting practice

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 5 does not require preclearance of these reallocations of governing authority because they do not alter voting procedures, candidacy, the electorate, or the method of selecting the relevant offices and have no direct relation to voting. This ruling does not resolve whether an extreme transfer could effectively replace an elective office with an appointive one, nor does it exempt a change merely because it occurs after an election.

**Authority:** Kennedy's opinion, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Thomas; five Justices control both noncoverage judgments.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that §5 addresses changes with a direct relation to voting, not every redistribution of power exercised by elected officials. Allen's broad reading reaches changes that alter electoral choice, including replacement of elective selection with appointment. Perkins and Dougherty County likewise concern the electorate or the process by which votes select officials. The Common Fund Resolution changes how an elected commission allocates road money, and the Unit System changes administration while leaving substantial authority with the elected commission. Neither changes the relevant selection mechanism or electorate. The dissent's concern that removing functions can diminish a district's influence is substantial, but influence over a particular governmental function is not itself the statutory voting practice. The familiar categories are illustrative, not exhaustive. The Court therefore leaves open a transfer so complete that an elective office is effectively replaced by an appointed one; the retained powers in this record do not present that question.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Allen v. State Board of Elections*, 393 U.S. 544 — broad coverage of changes affecting electoral choice remains; elective-to-appointive selection is distinguished from these retained elected offices.
- *Perkins v. Matthews*, 400 U.S. 379 — changes to the electorate and voting arrangements remain covered; coverage remains distinct from discrimination merits.
- *Dougherty County Board of Education v. White*, 439 U.S. 32 — candidacy restrictions directly related to electoral choice remain covered.
- *McCain v. Lybrand*, 465 U.S. 236, and *City of Lockhart v. United States*, 460 U.S. 125 — changes in electoral arrangements remain within §5; neither decides ordinary internal governing duties.
- *NAACP v. Hampton County Election Commission*, 470 U.S. 166 — practical changes to election timing or procedure require proper review; postelection timing alone supplies no exemption here.

### Administrative interpretation does not eliminate the voting boundary

**Holding and operative rule:** The Attorney General's interpretation of §5 is entitled to substantial consideration but cannot extend preclearance to governing changes that lack the statutory connection to voting. The proposed significant-transfer/different-constituencies test cannot substitute for that connection in these appeals.

**Authority:** The same five-Justice Kennedy opinion controls this statutory boundary and the rejection of the broader administrative and lower-court formulations.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that administrative expertise cannot remove the limit Congress expressed by linking §5 to voting. Sheffield recognizes the Attorney General's important interpretive role in applying the preclearance scheme. Chevron preserves that role when the agency resolves a statutory gap, but does not authorize an interpretation that exceeds the statute's coverage. Here the proposed focus on substantial transfers between officials serving different constituencies can sweep in decisions about governmental responsibility without identifying a changed voting practice. Section 5's demanding preclearance process does not make all such governing decisions electoral rules. The Court rejects that extension while preserving broad coverage when a measure actually changes election procedures, candidacy, the electorate or office selection. Beer also separates coverage from the later question of discriminatory effect. Accordingly, the Court makes no finding about the resolutions' racial purpose or consequences and leaves the other statutory and constitutional claims to their proper proceedings.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *United States v. Board of Commissioners of Sheffield*, 435 U.S. 110 — administrative interpretation retains substantial weight within the statutory voting boundary.
- *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837 — preserved; deference does not authorize exceeding the statute's scope.
- *Beer v. United States*, 425 U.S. 130 — coverage and discriminatory-effect review remain separate inquiries.
- *South Carolina v. Katzenbach*, 383 U.S. 301 — §5's remedial function is preserved; it does not by itself establish coverage of every governing change.

## 8. Law After Decision

The two challenged allocations of governing power are outside §5 because they lack a direct relation to voting; other claims remain available. The familiar electoral categories are not exhaustive, and effective replacement of an elective office by an appointed one remains open. Administrative interpretation cannot erase the voting boundary. No racial-purpose or effect finding is made.

## 9. Separate Writings

Stevens, joined by White and Blackmun, would require preclearance of both resolutions. Allen's functional protection should reach substantial removal of the authority voters select an official to exercise; Etowah's change following the remedial electoral arrangement supplies an additional reason for coverage, without deciding discriminatory intent. Stone separately would cover substantial structural removal of a core, independently exercisable district-office function into appointment or materially different collective control. He distinguishes these changes from routine appropriations, individual hiring, or losses in ordinary policy votes, and would leave preclearance merits to the designated statutory channels. All four would reverse both appealed noncoverage judgments.

## 10. Procedure After Action

Supreme Court review of both appealed coverage rulings ends. The Common Fund and Unit System are not enjoined under §5 on these grounds. The Road Supervision ruling and remaining district-court claims continue on their own terms; no retained Supreme Court supervision.

# Lechmere, Inc. v. NLRB, No. 90-970

**Render form: compact — 6-3 judgment; nonemployee-access rule, controlling coalition, denial of enforcement and separate grounds match the comparator. Stone's replacement of Rehnquist's vote and the different Court author are not themselves material.**

## 1. Event

*Lechmere, Inc. v. NLRB*, 502 U.S. 527 (1992), No. 90-970. October Term 1991. Argued 1991-11-12; decided 1992-01-27. Certiorari to the First Circuit, 914 F.2d 313, which enforced the National Labor Relations Board's order requiring access for nonemployee union organizers. Lechmere challenges the Board's application of Jean Country, 291 N.L.R.B. 11, to its nondiscriminatory exclusion policy. The questions are the governing statutory access rule and its application to this record.

## 2. Participation

Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas participated at argument and at decision. No nonparticipation is established. Quorum: 9 (six required); five votes are required for a majority.

## 3. Public Action

The First Circuit judgment is reversed and enforcement of the Board's nonemployee-access order is denied.

## 4. Judgment & Remedy

**Enforcement of nonemployee-access order:** Reverse and deny enforcement — 6 (Stone, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas); Affirm enforcement — 3 (White, Blackmun, Stevens).

6-3. Stone, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas reverse; White, Blackmun and Stevens would affirm. The Board's order is set aside insofar as it requires the nonemployee organizers' access on these facts. The judgment permits no interference with employees' distinct organizing rights or lawful activity on public property.

## 5. Opinion Topology

Stone delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. White dissents, joined by Blackmun. Stevens dissents separately, adopting White's first two accommodation-and-precedent reasons but not his criticism of Babcock as wrongly decided.

## 6. Holdings

### Nonemployee access requires demonstrated inaccessibility before balancing

**Holding and operative rule:** For a nondiscriminatory exclusion policy, nonemployee organizers must show that employees are practically beyond reasonable offsite communication before the Board may balance a need for entry against the employer's property rights; greater convenience or organizing success does not satisfy that threshold. This workforce's community residence, public approaches and substantial direct contacts defeat the asserted exception, while discriminatory exclusion, employees' own solicitation and genuinely inaccessible workforces remain governed by their distinct rules.

**Authority:** Stone's opinion, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; six Justices control both the statutory rule and denial of enforcement.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Board cannot replace Babcock's threshold with general balancing. Section 7 protects employee self-organization; access for outside organizers serves that right derivatively and is limited. Babcock distinguishes employees' solicitation from nonemployee entry, and Central Hardware requires the need for entry to be shown before accommodation. Hudgens's flexible language does not erase that distinction, while Sears confirms the demanding burden to establish that reasonable alternatives are unavailable. The Board's expertise remains relevant to actual access obstacles, but an established statutory construction fixes the legal inquiry. These employees live in the surrounding community. Organizers used public approaches and obtained substantial direct contacts; the small number of signed cards does not establish inability to communicate. Jean Country improperly treats alternatives as merely one factor in balancing rather than an antecedent requirement here. The Court demands neither organizational success nor exhaustion of every imaginable technique. It preserves the separate rules for discriminatory exclusion, employee solicitation and workers isolated by their living or working location.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *NLRB v. Babcock & Wilcox Co.*, 351 U.S. 105 — applied and reaffirmed: employee and nonemployee activity differ, and demonstrated inaccessibility precedes balancing under a nondiscriminatory rule.
- *Republic Aviation Corp. v. NLRB*, 324 U.S. 793 — employees' own organizing rights remain distinct and are not narrowed here.
- *Central Hardware Co. v. NLRB*, 407 U.S. 539 — applied for the prior showing of a need for nonemployee access.
- *Hudgens v. NLRB*, 424 U.S. 507 — accommodation language is preserved within Babcock's nonemployee threshold, not treated as authorization for universal balancing.
- *Sears, Roebuck & Co. v. Carpenters*, 436 U.S. 180 — applied for the heavy burden concerning alternative access and the separate discrimination route.
- *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, and *Maislin Industries, U.S., Inc. v. Primary Steel, Inc.*, 497 U.S. 116 — general deference remains; a later agency approach must respect a governing judicial statutory construction.
- *Jean Country*, 291 N.L.R.B. 11 — rejected insofar as its universal balancing replaces the antecedent inaccessibility inquiry for nondiscriminatory exclusion of nonemployee organizers; other contexts are not adjudicated.

## 8. Law After Decision

Babcock's nonemployee threshold controls: demonstrated practical inaccessibility precedes balancing, and a more productive organizing location does not establish necessity. The Board may assess actual obstacles within that rule; it may not replace the rule through Jean Country. Discriminatory exclusion, employee activity, public-property advocacy and genuinely isolated workforces retain distinct treatment. No new general deference or constitutional-entry rule is adopted.

## 9. Separate Writings

White, joined by Blackmun, would uphold the Board's accommodation and its assessment of effective alternatives. He reads Babcock, Central Hardware and Hudgens as leaving broader room to balance and argues that modern administrative deference also supports Jean Country; he criticizes Babcock's original approach. Stevens agrees only with the accommodation and subsequent-precedent reasons. He defends Babcock's employee/nonemployee distinction and declines to join the claim that it was wrongly decided. All three would affirm enforcement.

## 10. Procedure After Action

The access order cannot be enforced on this record; Supreme Court merits proceedings close. No remand for a new balancing exercise or retained supervision is ordered.
