# United States Department of State v. Ray, No. 90-747
**Render form: compact — 8-0 judgment and no material comparator change.**
## 1. Event
*United States Department of State v. Ray*, 502 U.S. 164 (1991), No. 90-747. Argued October 9 and decided December 16, 1991, on certiorari to the Eleventh Circuit, 908 F.2d 1549. The question is whether FOIA Exemption 6 permits withholding identifiers from interviews with Haitian returnees after segregable interview content has been released.
## 2. Participation
Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter participated. Thomas took no part because argument preceded his oath. Quorum present.
## 3. Public Action
The Eleventh Circuit judgment is reversed.
## 4. Judgment & Remedy
8-0. All participating Justices support reversal. The identifiers remain withheld; the segregated substantive reports remain disclosed.
## 5. Opinion Topology
Stevens delivered the opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, White, Blackmun, O'Connor, and Souter in full and by Scalia and Kennedy except as to Part III. Scalia, joined by Kennedy, concurred in part and in the judgment.
## 6. Holdings
**Holding and operative rule:** Under FOIA Exemption 6, identifying information may be withheld when linking names to sensitive personal interviews would cause a substantial privacy invasion and the identifiers add little demonstrated value to public understanding of agency conduct after reasonably segregable substantive material has been released. A confidentiality assurance informs the privacy interest but does not itself create an exemption.

**Authority:** Stevens's Court opinion; all eight participating Justices support the operative rule and judgment, while Scalia and Kennedy do not join the broader discussion of possible derivative public benefits.

**Controlling explanation:** Exemption 6 requires a practical balance between the invasion of personal privacy and disclosure's contribution to public understanding of government operations. *Rose* supplies that balance and demonstrates that useful agency records may be disclosed after identifiers are removed; *Washington Post* confirms that information about particular persons falls within “similar files.” The released reports disclose the questions, answers, dates, sampling, and reported treatment, allowing scrutiny of the Department's monitoring. Adding names would connect intimate family, employment, and living information—and cooperation given under an accepted assurance of confidentiality—to identifiable returnees. On this record, the requesters identify only a general hope that renewed interviews might uncover inaccuracies, not concrete contradictory information or a demonstrated incremental contribution sufficient to outweigh that invasion. The Court does not require proof of government wrongdoing before invoking FOIA, decide that derivative investigation can never matter, or adjudicate Haiti's safety or any asylum claim.
## 7. Precedent Treatment
*Rose*, *Washington Post*, and *Reporters Committee* are applied and preserved; waived Exemptions 1 and 7(C) are not reached.
## 8. Law After Decision
Effective December 16, the Exemption 6 balance measures the incremental public contribution of identifiers against their concrete privacy cost after segregated release; confidentiality is relevant but not dispositive.
## 9. Separate Writings
Scalia, joined by Kennedy, agrees that the identifiers are exempt but would not treat a requester's proposed derivative investigation as a cognizable FOIA public benefit where the disclosed information itself says nothing more about agency performance.
## 10. Procedure After Action
The disclosure order is reversed as to identifiers. Supreme Court proceedings are closed.

Official United States Reports, 502 U.S. 164-182, read in full in the GovInfo volume PDF; complete opinion and record sources identified in the brief; research cutoff September 14, 2026.

# INS v. National Center for Immigrants' Rights, Inc., No. 90-1090
**Render form: compact — 9-0 judgment and no material comparator change.**
## 1. Event
*INS v. National Center for Immigrants' Rights, Inc.*, 502 U.S. 183 (1991), No. 90-1090. Argued November 13 and decided December 16, 1991, on certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 913 F.2d 1350. The question is whether INA §242(a) prohibits the general bond condition against unauthorized employment pending deportability proceedings.
## 2. Participation
All nine Justices participated.
## 3. Public Action
The Ninth Circuit judgment is reversed and the case remanded.
## 4. Judgment & Remedy
9-0. Enforcement is permitted only under the supported unauthorized-work construction and described individualized process; constitutional and as-applied claims remain open.
## 5. Opinion Topology
Stevens delivered the opinion for a unanimous Court. No separate writing.
## 6. Holdings
**Holding and operative rule:** INA §242(a) permits a generally applicable release condition forbidding unauthorized employment when the regulation is construed not to reach authorized workers and retains individual status determinations and discretionary relief. That facial statutory ruling does not resolve constitutional objections or particular misapplications.

**Authority:** Stevens's unanimous opinion for all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 242(a) authorizes release on conditions prescribed by the Attorney General; it does not restrict every condition to securing physical appearance. The regulation's heading, exception, contemporaneous explanation, and operating instructions establish that its target is unauthorized employment. *Witkovich* requires delegated immigration authority to remain connected to the statutory scheme, and Congress's labor-market protections supply that connection. *American Hospital Association* permits general rules to structure delegated discretion so long as legally required individual determinations remain. Here officials must determine existing work authorization, address colorable citizenship or status claims, and consider the regulation's avenue for discretionary relief. The narrower construction therefore defeats the proposition that every application exceeds statutory authority. The Court does not approve interference with lawful employment, presume disputed deportability, decide the complaint's constitutional claims, or foreclose an as-applied challenge where the represented procedures are unavailable or misused.
## 7. Precedent Treatment
*Witkovich*, *Carlson*, *Seminole Rock*, and *American Hospital Association* are applied and preserved. *Ardestani* is confined to EAJA's §554 cross-reference.
## 8. Law After Decision
Effective December 16, §242(a) supports the narrowed unauthorized-work bond rule with individualized status and relief determinations.
## 9. Separate Writings
None.
## 10. Procedure After Action
The facial statutory injunction is reversed; other preserved claims return to the lower courts.

# Hilton v. South Carolina Public Railways Commission, No. 90-848
**Render form: compact — 6-2 judgment and no material comparator change.**
## 1. Event
*Hilton v. South Carolina Public Railways Commission*, 502 U.S. 197 (1991), No. 90-848. Argued October 8 and decided December 16, 1991, on certiorari to the South Carolina Supreme Court. The question is whether FELA's cause of action against a state-owned railroad remains enforceable in state court.
## 2. Participation
Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter participated. Thomas took no part because argument preceded his oath.
## 3. Public Action
The judgment is reversed and the case remanded.
## 4. Judgment & Remedy
6-2. Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy, and Souter support reversal; O'Connor and Scalia dissent. Hilton may litigate the FELA claim in state court but receives no finding of negligence or damages.
## 5. Opinion Topology
Kennedy delivered the opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, White, Stevens, and Souter. Blackmun concurred in the judgment. O'Connor dissented, joined by Scalia.
## 6. Holdings
**Holding and operative rule:** FELA's established inclusion of state-owned interstate railroads remains enforceable in a competent state court. This rule does not revive constructive waiver of Eleventh Amendment immunity in federal court or establish a general rule for every Article I damages action against a State.

**Authority:** Kennedy's opinion joined by five of eight participants supplies the controlling rationale; Blackmun supplies the sixth judgment vote.

**Controlling explanation:** *Parden* separately interpreted FELA's coverage of “every” interstate common carrier and treated operation of a railroad as waiver of federal-court immunity. *Welch* displaced the constructive-waiver rationale but expressly reserved statutory coverage. The remaining construction has governed the specialized railroad relationship for decades, Congress retained the relevant language, and States and employees arranged compensation against that background—including South Carolina's exclusion of FELA-covered employees from workers' compensation. *Will* construed a different statutory term and does not silently overrule the specific FELA holding. Statutory stare decisis thus requires retaining the independently grounded coverage rule unless the Court squarely abandons it. Under *Howlett*, a competent state court may not use state immunity to defeat a defendant's liability under valid federal law. The Court does not reopen Hilton's dismissed federal suit, decide negligence, or announce that entering commerce generally waives immunity.
## 7. Precedent Treatment
*Parden*'s FELA coverage holding is reaffirmed; its constructive federal-forum waiver remains displaced by *Welch*. *Will* is distinguished; *Howlett* is applied.
## 8. Law After Decision
State-owned interstate railroads remain FELA defendants in competent state courts under the specific retained precedent.
## 9. Separate Writings
Blackmun concurs in the judgment on his broader view of congressional power. O'Connor, joined by Scalia, would require a clearer statement before imposing damages liability on a State and would treat *Parden*'s coverage and waiver reasoning as inseparable.
## 10. Procedure After Action
The case returns to state court for the ordinary FELA merits.
## 11. Source Notes
Official United States Reports, 502 U.S. 197-214, read in full in the GovInfo volume PDF; research cutoff September 14, 2026.

# King v. St. Vincent's Hospital, No. 90-889
**Render form: compact — 8-0 judgment and no material comparator change.**
## 1. Event
*King v. St. Vincent's Hospital*, 502 U.S. 215 (1991), No. 90-889. Argued October 16 and decided December 16, 1991, on certiorari to the Eleventh Circuit, 901 F.2d 1068. The question is whether 38 U.S.C. §2024(d) contains an unstated reasonableness limit on protected military leave.
## 2. Participation
Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter participated. Thomas took no part because argument preceded his oath.
## 3. Public Action
The judgment is reversed and remanded.
## 4. Judgment & Remedy
8-0. The per se three-year exclusion and implied durational-reasonableness condition are removed; other statutory obligations remain for further proceedings.
## 5. Opinion Topology
Souter delivered the opinion for the unanimous participating Court. No separate writing.
## 6. Holdings
**Holding and operative rule:** Section 2024(d) contains no implicit reasonableness limit on the duration of otherwise covered military service. An employee must still satisfy the provision's actual coverage, notice, service, and return conditions.

**Authority:** Souter's opinion for all eight participating Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 2024(d) ties protected leave to the period required to perform covered duty, and the Army required the three-year tour associated with King's position. The statutory scheme expressly imposes duration limits on neighboring service categories but omits one here. *Morash* requires provisions to be read as part of the whole enactment; that comparison makes the omission meaningful rather than accidental. *Fishgold* supports protective construction of reemployment rights, although the text and structure—not solicitude alone—control the result. The Hospital's concern about disruption from a lengthy voluntary absence is substantial, but the words “leave,” “employee,” and “return” do not provide an administrable judicial measure for writing a missing cap. The Court rejects both a per se three-year exclusion and a free-ranging reasonableness balance. It does not excuse failure to meet enacted requirements, decide damages, or determine obligations arising after a failure properly to return.
## 7. Precedent Treatment
*Morash* and *Fishgold* are applied; circuit precedent adding a reasonableness condition is rejected. *Wolas* remains statute-specific.
## 8. Law After Decision
Effective December 16, otherwise covered §2024(d) leave has no judicially implied duration limit.
## 9. Separate Writings
None.
## 10. Procedure After Action
The case returns for proceedings on the parties' actual statutory obligations.
## 11. Source Notes
Official United States Reports, 502 U.S. 215-223, read in full in the GovInfo volume PDF; research cutoff September 14, 2026.

# Hunter v. Bryant, No. 90-1440
**Render form: full — 6-2 judgment with a materially different remand from the comparator.**
## 1. Event
*Hunter v. Bryant*, 502 U.S. 224 (1991), No. 90-1440. Submitted on the petition papers and decided December 16, 1991, on review of the Ninth Circuit's denial of qualified immunity. The issue is the agents' entitlement to immunity from the arrest-without-probable-cause claim at summary judgment.
## 2. Participation
Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter participated. Thomas took no part because the matter was submitted before his oath.
## 3. Public Action
The judgment is vacated and the case remanded to apply the correct qualified-immunity summary-judgment standard. The Court does not direct judgment for either side.
## 4. Judgment & Remedy
| Component | Vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy |
|---|---:|---|---|---|
| Vacatur for use of an incorrect legal/factual allocation | 6-2 | Stone, White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter | Scalia, Stevens | Apply the complete contemporaneous record and identify any genuine material predicate dispute. |
| Direction to enter immunity now | Rejected, 6-2 | Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter | White, Scalia | No automatic trial and no directed immunity on the present rationale. |
## 5. Opinion Topology
| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stone | Blackmun, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter | Controls vacatur, legal standard, and remand | 
| Concurring in part and in the judgment | White | — | Agrees the Ninth Circuit's formulation was wrong but would direct immunity | 
| Concurring in part and dissenting in part | Stevens | — | Agrees immunity cannot be directed; would affirm rather than vacate | 
| Dissenting | Scalia | — | Would reverse and direct judgment for the agents | 
## 6. Holdings
**Holding and operative rule:** Qualified immunity from a claim of arrest without probable cause is a legal question for the judge under ordinary summary-judgment constraints. A court must evaluate whether a reasonable officer could believe the arrest lawful on the supported nonmovant version of the complete contemporaneous record; choosing the “more reasonable” interpretation is not the test, but a generalized security concern alone does not establish reasonable belief that the elements of the offense were satisfied.

**Authority:** Stone's opinion joined by Stone, Blackmun, O'Connor, Kennedy, and Souter; five of eight participants and therefore controlling.

**Controlling explanation:** *Harlow*, *Anderson v. Creighton*, and *Malley* protect reasonable mistakes and require early judicial resolution where supported facts permit it. Rule 56 nevertheless requires the court to identify genuine disputes about historical predicates and draw supported inferences for the nonmovant. The agents could consider the assassination references, reported gesture, oral statements, confusing interview, and refusal to elaborate. They also had the letter itself, which repeatedly attributed the supposed plot to an outside group; that feature matters to whether Bryant communicated a serious threat prohibited by §871 or reported an imagined danger. *Watts* supplies the contextual distinction between a true threat and protected expression. The Ninth Circuit erred by asking which interpretation was more reasonable and by suggesting the legal immunity issue belonged generally to a jury. But the opposite error would treat presidential-security concern as dispositive without connecting the accepted facts to the offense. The remand court must decide immunity promptly after specifying any supported material dispute; the Court finds neither guilt, innocence, bad faith, nor a constitutional violation.
## 7. Precedent Treatment
*Harlow*, *Anderson v. Creighton*, *Malley*, *Mitchell*, *Beck*, *Watts*, and *Liberty Lobby* are applied and preserved. No qualified-immunity rule is revised.
## 8. Law After Decision
Qualified immunity remains judge-decided and protects reasonable probable-cause mistakes, subject to Rule 56's supported-fact constraints and offense-specific analysis.
## 9. Separate Writings
White would hold the agents immune now because their complete information permitted a reasonable belief in probable cause. Stevens would leave the denial intact because the agents had not established entitlement on the summary-judgment record. Scalia would summarily reverse and direct immunity, emphasizing early resolution and reasonable error.
## 10. Procedure After Action
The warrant component remains closed. The probable-cause immunity defense returns for prompt judicial decision under the stated standard.
## 11. Source Notes
Official United States Reports, 502 U.S. 224-235, read in full in the GovInfo volume PDF; Ninth Circuit record and authorities identified in the brief; research cutoff September 14, 2026. The changed remand and writings are simulation outputs.

# In re Blodgett, No. 91-716
**Render form: compact — unanimous judgment and no material comparator change.**
## 1. Event
*In re Blodgett*, 502 U.S. 236 (1992), No. 91-716. Submitted as an original mandamus petition and decided January 13, 1992. Washington asks the Court to compel decision of a delayed capital-habeas appeal and disturb an August 7, 1991 abeyance order.
## 2. Participation
All nine Justices participated.
## 3. Public Action
Campbell's motion to proceed in forma pauperis is granted; mandamus is denied without prejudice.
## 4. Judgment & Remedy
9-0. The Ninth Circuit may address a properly presented request to modify or expedite. No habeas result or execution schedule is directed.
## 5. Opinion Topology
Per curiam opinion for Stone, White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas. Stevens, joined by Blackmun, concurred in the judgment.
## 6. Holdings
**Holding and operative rule:** Mandamus is unavailable where the petitioner has not first sought focused relief from the court that entered the operative abeyance order and has not shown that avenue inadequate or futile. Denial may be without prejudice to a renewed petition after the ordinary avenue is pursued.

**Authority:** The per curiam and concurrence together state this ground for all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Rule 20.1, *Kerr*, *Will*, and *Fahey* reserve mandamus for an extraordinary failure to act where no other adequate avenue exists and the entitlement is clear and indisputable. The appeal's extended pendency, unanswered inquiries, and execution stay warrant serious attention. But the August order awaiting related proceedings is the current source of delay, and Washington never asked the panel to modify that specific order. Such a motion would permit the panel to address exhaustion, counsel, the proposed third petition, and a concrete schedule on the current record. Earlier general inquiries and a resubmission request do not establish that focused relief is unavailable or inevitably futile. Denial without prejudice respects both fair habeas review and timely adjudication. The Court does not approve every period of delay, find abuse of the writ, resolve Campbell's sentence, dissolve the stay, or impose an automatic special burden on future capital litigants.
## 7. Precedent Treatment
*Kerr*, *Will*, and *Fahey* are applied; *Barefoot*, *Rose*, and *McCleskey* are not extended.
## 8. Law After Decision
The extraordinary-writ prerequisite remains unchanged; the event creates no habeas-merits rule.
## 9. Separate Writings
Stevens, joined by Blackmun, agrees that failure to seek modification defeats relief but rejects broader criticism of the panel's coordination of related proceedings.
## 10. Procedure After Action
The original proceeding closes without prejudice; the state may seek focused relief below.

# Smith v. Barry, No. 90-7477
**Render form: compact — 9-0 judgment and no material comparator change.**
## 1. Event
*Smith v. Barry*, 502 U.S. 244 (1992), No. 90-7477. Argued December 2, 1991 and decided January 14, 1992, on certiorari to the Fourth Circuit, 919 F.2d 893. The question is whether a timely appellate brief can serve as a notice of appeal when it supplies Rule 3's required notice.
## 2. Participation
All nine Justices participated.
## 3. Public Action
The judgment is reversed and the case remanded.
## 4. Judgment & Remedy
9-0. The Fourth Circuit must examine the informal brief's actual contents under Rule 3(c); underlying claims are not decided.
## 5. Opinion Topology
O'Connor delivered the opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas. Scalia concurred in the judgment.
## 6. Holdings
**Holding and operative rule:** A timely document styled as an appellate brief may serve as a notice of appeal if it objectively identifies the appealing party, the judgment or order appealed, and the appellate court as Rule 3(c) requires. Filing it in the court of appeals is governed by Rule 4's mistaken-filing provision; informal form or title alone cannot require dismissal.

**Authority:** O'Connor's opinion joined by eight Justices supplies the controlling rationale; Scalia agrees with the judgment and the express form-or-title ground.

**Controlling explanation:** *Torres* makes Rule 3 compliance mandatory while recognizing functional compliance with the information the Rule actually requires. *Foman* directs courts to read a notice in context where the intent to seek review is manifest. Rule 3(c) itself prohibits dismissal for informality of form or title, so an administrative expectation that a notice and brief arrive as separate papers cannot add a jurisdictional condition. Smith returned the clerk's informal-brief form within the valid filing period after attempting to appeal; responding to that form does not negate the objective notice his document may convey. Rule 4 also treats a notice mistakenly filed in the appellate court as filed in the district court upon receipt. The first notice remains ineffective under the then-current posttrial-motion rule. The Fourth Circuit must still determine whether the May 4 document actually contains each required item and what judgment or orders it fairly identifies.
## 7. Precedent Treatment
*Torres*, *Foman*, and *Griggs* are applied; *Ibarra* remains confined to timely criminal reconsideration motions.
## 8. Law After Decision
Rule 3 looks to timely objective content, not a document's caption, while preserving every required item.
## 9. Separate Writings
Scalia concurs in the judgment on the Rule's express protection against dismissal for informality of form or title.
## 10. Procedure After Action
The appeal returns to the Fourth Circuit for document-specific jurisdictional review and, if adequate, later merits proceedings.
## 11. Source Notes
Official United States Reports, 502 U.S. 244-250, read in full in the GovInfo volume PDF; research cutoff September 14, 2026.

# County of Yakima v. Confederated Tribes and Bands of the Yakima Indian Nation, Nos. 90-408 and 90-577
**Render form: compact — component margins of 8-1 and 9-0, with no material comparator change.**
## 1. Event
*County of Yakima v. Confederated Tribes and Bands of the Yakima Indian Nation*, 502 U.S. 251 (1992), Nos. 90-408 and 90-577. Argued November 5, 1991 and decided January 14, 1992, on certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 903 F.2d 1207. The questions concern ad valorem and sales-excise taxation of reservation fee land.
## 2. Participation
All nine Justices participated.
## 3. Public Action
The judgment is affirmed and the cause remanded with modified instructions.
## 4. Judgment & Remedy
The ad valorem component is resolved for the County 8-1 (Stone, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas; Blackmun dissenting). The sales excise remains prohibited 9-0. The Brendale impact remand is vacated; parcel patent sources remain for remand.
## 5. Opinion Topology
Scalia delivered the opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas. Blackmun concurred as to the excise and dissented as to ad valorem authority.
## 6. Holdings
**Ad valorem holding and operative rule:** The General Allotment Act's established fee-land rule and the Burke Act proviso authorize state ad valorem taxation of reservation land validly patented in fee under that Act, including qualifying land later held by the Nation. The tax follows the land, but patent source and any independently applicable federal protection must be determined parcel by parcel.

**Authority:** Scalia's opinion joined by eight Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** *Blackfeet* requires unmistakably clear congressional authorization, and here the County relies on more than alienability or policy inference. *Goudy* construed the General Allotment Act to permit taxation following an ordinary fee patent, while the Burke Act proviso expressly addresses removal of restrictions on sale, encumbrance, and taxation. The Indian Reorganization Act halted future allotments and preserved trust periods but also created a distinct mechanism for restoring land to trust; it does not automatically return every existing fee parcel to tax-protected status. *Mancari* requires compatible enactments to operate together. *Moe* rejects general personal taxing jurisdiction over reservation Indians, but a specific tax on qualifying land does not revive that discarded jurisdiction. *Brendale*'s fractured analysis of tribal regulation of nonmember land supplies no governmental-impact test for this statutory tax question. Remand is necessary because the stipulated record does not establish every parcel's patent source.

**Sales-excise holding and operative rule:** Congressional permission to tax qualifying fee land ad valorem does not unmistakably authorize a tax imposed on an Indian owner's sale of that land; securing the transactional tax with a lien does not change its legal incidence.

**Authority:** Scalia's opinion states this proposition for all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** The annual levy is triggered by ownership and burdens the realty. The sales excise is triggered by a transaction. Under *Blackfeet*, authorization must be clear at the level of the tax actually imposed. A collection lien cannot transform an otherwise unauthorized transactional charge into a land tax, because that device would allow the State to enlarge congressional permission through its own collection law. *Capoeman*'s discussion of taxation after fee patent arose in the allotment context and does not provide blanket authorization for every tax related to land. The Court therefore gives the allotment provisions their demonstrated property-tax scope without creating general state dominion over the owner or transaction.
## 7. Precedent Treatment
*Goudy* is reaffirmed for qualifying GAA fee land; *Blackfeet*, *Mancari*, and *Moe* are applied and preserved; *Brendale* is distinguished.
## 8. Law After Decision
Qualifying GAA fee land is subject to ad valorem taxation; sales of that land by Indian owners remain outside the identified authorization.
## 9. Separate Writings
Blackmun agrees that the sales excise is unauthorized but would also reject the ad valorem tax because the asserted authority is not sufficiently clear after later federal Indian legislation.
## 10. Procedure After Action
The case returns for parcel-specific patent-source analysis and relief consistent with both tax holdings; no foreclosure is ordered.
## 11. Source Notes
Official United States Reports, 502 U.S. 251-278, read in full in the GovInfo volume PDF; research cutoff September 14, 2026.

# Norman v. Reed, Nos. 90-1126 and 90-1435
**Render form: compact — 7-1 judgment and no material comparator change.**
## 1. Event
*Norman v. Reed*, 502 U.S. 279 (1992), Nos. 90-1126 and 90-1435. Argued October 7, 1991 and decided January 14, 1992, on review of the Illinois Supreme Court. The questions concern continued justiciability, party-name use, signature consequences, and an unresolved state-law objection.
## 2. Participation
Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter participated. Thomas took no part because argument preceded his oath.
## 3. Public Action
The judgment is affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded.
## 4. Judgment & Remedy
7-1. Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, and Souter support the component disposition; Scalia dissents. Suburban candidates remain excluded for insufficient suburban signatures. Name-based and whole-slate exclusions are reversed. The judicial-candidate objection returns to state court; no new election or automatic established-party declaration is ordered.
## 5. Opinion Topology
Souter delivered the opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, and Kennedy. Scalia dissented.
## 6. Holdings
**Justiciability holding and operative rule:** Completion of an election does not moot a ballot-access dispute where election results carry continuing party-qualification consequences and the short election cycle presents a reasonably recurring controversy that otherwise evades review.

**Authority:** Souter's opinion joined by seven of eight participating Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** *Moore v. Ogilvie* recognizes that election restrictions may retain legal consequences after ballots are counted. Here several candidates exceeded the five-percent threshold that may alter the party's qualification in later elections, and the challenged rules operate on a timetable too short for complete review before voting. The dispute therefore remains concrete. The Court decides the federal grounds actually used by the Illinois Supreme Court but does not supply an answer to the unaddressed judicial-candidate objection. *Bacchus Imports* supports returning that consequential state-law issue for authoritative construction. The Court neither revives the unappealed water-district candidates nor orders a new election.

**Ballot-access holding and operative rule:** Illinois may require meaningful support within each separate electorate, but it may not categorically prevent authorized candidates from expanding an existing party under its name or disqualify otherwise qualified countywide and city candidates solely because candidates in a different district lack sufficient signatures.

**Authority:** Souter's opinion joined by seven of eight participating Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** *Anderson* requires attention to the burden, the State's precise interests, and whether the restriction is necessary; *Socialist Workers Party* rejects a local signature demand more onerous than the statewide route without adequate justification. Protecting a party from unauthorized appropriation is legitimate, but an absolute geographic name barrier prohibits even authorized expansion and is broader than a permission requirement. Likewise, support in each electorate may be demanded for candidates chosen there, which sustains the suburban exclusion on this record. It does not justify invalidating countywide and city candidates who met their own constituencies' requirements merely because a separate slate component failed. That consequence effectively compounds the signature burden without advancing the stated interest in support for each election. The Court leaves other district structures, cumulative burdens, and actual unauthorized name use open.
## 7. Precedent Treatment
*Moore*, *Anderson*, *Socialist Workers Party*, *Jenness*, and *Bacchus Imports* are applied and preserved.
## 8. Law After Decision
Authorized party-name expansion and independently qualified slate components receive constitutional protection; separate-electorate support requirements remain permissible on this record.
## 9. Separate Writings
Scalia would treat the party's county endeavor as requiring support across every election it chose to contest and would sustain the challenged statutory consequences.
## 10. Procedure After Action
The case returns to the Illinois Supreme Court for the judicial-candidate objection and consequent party-status determination consistent with the federal ruling.
## 11. Source Notes
Official United States Reports, 502 U.S. 279-300, read in full in the GovInfo volume PDF; research cutoff September 14, 2026.

# Molzof v. United States, No. 90-838
**Render form: compact — 9-0 judgment and no material comparator change.**
## 1. Event
*Molzof v. United States*, 502 U.S. 301 (1992), No. 90-838. Argued November 4, 1991 and decided January 14, 1992, on certiorari to the Seventh Circuit, 911 F.2d 18. The question is the meaning of “punitive damages” in 28 U.S.C. §2674.
## 2. Participation
All nine Justices participated.
## 3. Public Action
The judgment is reversed and the case remanded.
## 4. Judgment & Remedy
9-0. The federal punitive-damages classification is reversed; Wisconsin-law entitlement, survival, duplication, and offsets remain for remand. No additional sum is ordered.
## 5. Opinion Topology
Thomas delivered the opinion for a unanimous Court. No separate writing.
## 6. Holdings
**Holding and operative rule:** Section 2674 bars damages whose legal nature is punishment for culpable wrongdoing, not every compensatory award alleged to be excessive, duplicative, or difficult for the injured person to perceive. Rejecting the federal bar does not establish entitlement under the governing state law or determine an amount.

**Authority:** Thomas's unanimous opinion for all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** The FTCA generally makes the United States liable like a private person under the law of the place while withholding “punitive damages.” Congress used an established tort term associated with punishment for culpable misconduct. *Morissette* supports reading borrowed legal language against that settled background, and *Massachusetts Bonding* confirms the distinction between punitive recovery and compensation in explaining the Act's special wrongful-death provision. The Government's proposed “punitive effect” test would authorize federal courts to revise state compensation rules whenever an award seems excessive or imperfectly tied to perceived loss, without statutory criteria. Its concession that compensation may be reasonably approximate underscores the absence of a workable boundary. These claims arise from admitted simple negligence and seek categories labeled compensatory under state law; alleged duplication or unconsciousness does not alone change their legal nature. Wisconsin law must still determine whether future care, lost enjoyment, survival, and offsets permit any recovery. The Court neither authorizes punitive payment nor directs an award.
