# Chemical Waste Management, Inc. v. Hunt

## 1. Event

Chemical Waste Management, Inc. v. Hunt, No. 91-471. Argued April 21, 1992; decided June 1, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the Supreme Court of Alabama, reviewing its July 11, 1991 judgment, 584 So.2d 1367. The trial court's February 28, 1991 judgment invalidated the $72-per-ton additional charge on outside-generated waste and sustained the $25.60 base fee and volume cap. Alabama's Supreme Court reversed the additional-fee ruling and otherwise affirmed. The petition challenged the Act more broadly; the grant is limited to whether the additional charge violates the Commerce Clause despite environmental, capacity and fiscal concerns. Petitioner seeks relief from that exaction in its declaratory and injunctive state action.

Render form: full. Basis: a material change in the controlling coalition; the separate concurrence retains distinct authority.

## 2. Participation

At argument and decision the participants are Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. No recusal or nonparticipation is established. Nine participate; six constitute a quorum and five supply a majority judgment or proposition.

## 3. Public Action

The Court unanimously reverses the Alabama Supreme Court's judgment sustaining the additional out-of-state fee and remands for appropriate relief. White announces the Opinion of the Court. Stone concurs separately while joining that opinion.

## 4. Judgment & Remedy

The judgment sustaining the additional fee is reversed. The cause returns to the Alabama Supreme Court for proceedings consistent with the holding, including consideration of appropriate petitioner relief. Independent base-fee, cap and other rulings outside the grant are not invalidated. No replacement fee, operating permit or compulsory acceptance requirement issues.

## 6. Holdings

### Origin-based hazardous-waste surcharge

**Holding and operative rule:** A State may not impose an additional disposal charge on out-of-state hazardous waste that it does not impose on equivalent in-state waste unless it establishes a legitimate local purpose that reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives cannot adequately serve. Alabama's $72-per-ton additional charge fails that requirement because the record supplies no origin-linked difference in the relevant risks or costs and adequate evenhanded means remain available; commercial disposal of hazardous waste receives Commerce Clause protection despite the material's dangers.

**Authority:** White's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. All nine adopt this rule, its application and the stated remedial limits. No fractured-decision analysis is necessary.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Alabama must justify the unequal treatment of equivalent waste. Philadelphia supplies both the protection of interstate waste transactions and the rule that environmental protection does not justify an origin-only barrier. Hunt places on the State the burden of establishing that adequate nondiscriminatory alternatives are unavailable; New Energy applies that requirement to a discriminatory charge even when the State invokes health. Wyoming applies the same discrimination branch to an express geographic preference. The serious disposal and transportation hazards found here are accepted, but they do not become greater merely because an otherwise equivalent ton originates elsewhere. Evenhanded charges, total-volume limits and direct transportation or safety regulation can address the identified mechanisms. These examples expose the missing justification; they do not command a replacement program. Maine sustained a restriction because distinctive biological risks and unavailable inspection alternatives supported it. Alabama has shown no comparable distinction, and allowing the same waste upon payment of an extra fee does not establish a quarantine justification. The independent base charge, capacity provisions and unpresented statutory theories remain outside this decision.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Philadelphia v. New Jersey, 437 U.S. 617 — applied to hazardous-waste disposal and an origin-based surcharge; its nondiscrimination rule remains operative.
- Hunt v. Washington State Apple Advertising Commission, 432 U.S. 333 — applied without changing the State's purpose-and-alternatives burden.
- Maine v. Taylor, 477 U.S. 131 — distinguished because this record lacks the distinctive imported risk and unavailable adequate alternatives that sustained its restriction; its exception remains intact.
- New Energy Co. of Indiana v. Limbach, 486 U.S. 269 — applied to a discriminatory charge defended on environmental grounds; complete exclusion is unnecessary.
- Wyoming v. Oklahoma, simulated January 22, 1992 — applied for express geographic discrimination; its other holdings are neither extended nor displaced.
- Pike v. Bruce Church, Inc., 397 U.S. 137 — its incidental-burden balance does not govern the facial additional fee; the rule for evenhanded regulation is preserved.
- McKesson Corp. v. Division of Alcoholic Beverages and Tobacco, 496 U.S. 18 — its remedial principles remain applicable in state proceedings when their prerequisites are satisfied; no automatic refund entitlement or computation is decided here.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The grant concerns the Commerce challenge to the additional fee. The base fee, independent volume cap, other permitting provisions, state constitutional claims and other federal challenges are not decided. The State did not press its fair-share or compensatory-tax theory here; the Court does not adjudicate it. Amici's congressional-authorization theory was neither decided below nor briefed or argued by the parties and is not reached. Appropriate state remedial proceedings must correct the discriminatory exaction, consistently with McKesson where applicable, without a prescribed refund amount. No unlimited right to dispose of hazardous waste is created.

## 7. Precedent Treatment

The precedent-treatment lines and limits in Holdings supply the complete controlling treatment. The Court applies the existing discrimination framework and does not overrule a precedent or change its proof requirements. Stone's separate observation does not change controlling compensatory-tax doctrine.

## 8. Law After Decision

Effective June 1, 1992, the existing discrimination rule applies to the $72 additional fee and invalidates it. Before this decision Philadelphia, Hunt, Maine, New Energy and Wyoming supplied the governing branch; afterward this hazardous-waste surcharge application is controlling. No proof quantum, requirement of perfect empirical certainty, least-imaginable-alternative rule, neutral-regulation standard, collection-nexus rule or market-participant exception is adopted or altered. Constitutional invalidity does not establish an automatic refund amount or validate every alternative regulatory design.

## 9. Separate Writings

Stone concurs while joining the Court in full. He adds that residents' general contributions to state revenue do not establish a corresponding substantially equivalent intrastate burden that would explain the differential. This is his additional noncontrolling ground. It does not turn the State's unpressed compensatory-tax theory into a holding of the Court.

## 10. Procedure After Action

Supreme Court merits review is complete. The Alabama Supreme Court must give effect to reversal of the additional-fee ruling and consider appropriate relief through state remedial proceedings, consistently with McKesson when applicable. No refund amount, new operating authorization, future filing, hearing date, mandate-issuance date or return to this Court is ordered or established. No retained Supreme Court proceeding is created.

# Fort Gratiot Sanitary Landfill, Inc. v. Michigan Department of Natural Resources

## 1. Event

Fort Gratiot Sanitary Landfill, Inc. v. Michigan Department of Natural Resources, No. 91-636. Argued March 30, 1992; decided June 1, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit, reviewing 931 F.2d 413, decided May 1, 1991, with rehearing denied July 16, 1991. The district court denied the operator's summary-judgment request and dismissed the complaint; the Sixth Circuit affirmed.

The question is whether Michigan may prohibit private landfills from accepting outside-county waste unless a county plan expressly authorizes it, despite treating other Michigan counties like other States. Petitioner seeks declaratory and prospective injunctive relief. The challenged provisions are §§ 299.413a and 299.430(2), enacted December 28, 1988. Its February 13, 1989 request offered to reserve twenty years of county disposal capacity but was denied by the planning committee.

Render form: full. Basis: a material change in the controlling coalition; the dissent requests a distinct remedial course.

## 2. Participation

At argument and decision the participants are Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. No recusal or nonparticipation is established. Nine participate; six constitute a quorum and five supply a majority judgment or proposition.

## 3. Public Action

The Court reverses the Sixth Circuit's judgment, 8–1. Stevens announces the Opinion of the Court. Blackmun dissents and would permit additional factual proceedings before conclusive invalidation.

## 4. Judgment & Remedy

| Component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justice | Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dismissed Commerce challenge to county-origin restrictions at private landfills | Reversed, with proceedings on remand consistent with invalidation, 8–1 | Stone, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Blackmun | Tailored declaratory and prospective relief against the challenged origin exclusion; independent neutral planning and permitting remain. |

The Sixth Circuit's judgment sustaining dismissal is reversed. The cause returns through that court for proceedings consistent with the invalidity of the Waste Import Restrictions in this private-facility setting. The Court does not require unconditional permit issuance, acceptance of particular waste or operation beyond neutral capacity. Blackmun would instead remand for an opportunity to establish a sufficient environmental justification before conclusive invalidation.

## 5. Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Judgment relationship | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stevens | Stone, White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Supports invalidation, reversal and implementing remand | Entire opinion: controlling discrimination rule, private-facility application, preserved boundaries and relief. |
| Dissent | Blackmun | No other Justice | Opposes conclusive invalidation; seeks further factual remand | Comprehensive planning and an opportunity to establish a sufficient environmental justification; does not declare the restriction valid. |

## 6. Holdings

### County-origin exclusions from private disposal facilities

**Holding and operative rule:** A State's prohibition on a private landfill's acceptance of outside-county waste unless a county plan affirmatively authorizes it discriminates against interstate commerce even when it also excludes waste from other counties within that State and some counties choose to admit imports. The restriction cannot stand unless the State establishes a legitimate local purpose that reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives cannot adequately serve; Michigan has not made that showing for these Waste Import Restrictions, and regulation of a private facility does not become market participation because government plans waste services.

**Authority:** Stevens's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone, White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. Eight Justices adopt the same rule, application and limits. Blackmun supports a different remedial course and supplies no controlling join. No fractured-decision analysis is necessary.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a county boundary does not remove a barrier to interstate trade. Philadelphia establishes that waste disposal is commerce and that local capacity may not be reserved by origin alone. Brimmer and Dean Milk establish that excluding other intrastate suppliers does not cure a geographic preference against interstate suppliers; New Energy likewise rejects a requirement of widespread commercial disadvantage. Wyoming preserves the distinction between the existence and the extent of discrimination. Michigan's planning obligations are substantial, but the challenged veto still gives local waste access that equivalent outside waste lacks. Neutral quantity limits, appropriate charges and facility standards can preserve planning without that preference. Hunt and Maine require justification of the discriminatory means, and respondents identify no distinct imported hazard or inadequacy of those alternatives. They have offered no further showing or requested an evidentiary remand that would change that conclusion. Government is regulating a private disposal transaction, not selling its own service. These alternative tools illustrate the failed justification rather than impose a replacement program. The Court leaves public facilities, neutral planning requirements and unconditional operating entitlement undecided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Philadelphia v. New Jersey, 437 U.S. 617 — applied to county-origin restrictions on private disposal facilities; its rule against origin-only exclusion is unchanged.
- Brimmer v. Rebman, 138 U.S. 78 — applied: a substate boundary does not cure a burden on interstate trade merely because some intrastate trade also suffers.
- Dean Milk Co. v. Madison, 340 U.S. 349 — applied to the claimed neutrality of the county line; municipal exclusion remains subject to Commerce scrutiny.
- New Energy Co. of Indiana v. Limbach, 486 U.S. 269 — applied: geographic discrimination need not disadvantage every outside supplier or produce a statewide exclusion.
- Hunt v. Washington State Apple Advertising Commission, 432 U.S. 333 — the State's burden concerning purpose and adequate nondiscriminatory alternatives is preserved.
- Maine v. Taylor, 477 U.S. 131 — distinguished on the absence of a distinctive imported hazard and unavailable adequate alternatives; its environmental exception remains intact.
- Wyoming v. Oklahoma, simulated January 22, 1992 — applied for express geographic preferences; the volume affected does not define whether discrimination exists.
- Hughes v. Alexandria Scrap Corp., 426 U.S. 794, and Reeves, Inc. v. Stake, 447 U.S. 429 — their distinction between government participation in a market and government regulation is preserved; public planning does not transform this regulation of a private facility into proprietary action.
- Pike v. Bruce Church, Inc., 397 U.S. 137 — its incidental-burden branch does not govern the express county-origin exclusion; the separate standard for evenhanded regulation remains.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Only the 1988 Waste Import Restrictions as applied to privately owned and operated landfills are invalidated. The earlier comprehensive planning statute, public-facility practices, neutral health and safety rules, neutral capacity limits, different waste-risk classifications and unconditional facility operation are not adjudicated. No hazardous-waste issue arises. No genuinely applicable congressional authorization is displaced; none supplies an adjudicated defense here. The Court does not reopen the distinct due-process, taking or ownership-transfer licensing matters described below. No showing that every Michigan county bars outside waste is required.

## 7. Precedent Treatment

The precedent-treatment lines and limits in Holdings supply the complete controlling treatment. No precedent is overruled and no new justification burden is adopted. The decision establishes the stated county-origin application of existing law and preserves the public-facility boundary.

## 8. Law After Decision

Effective June 1, 1992, existing Commerce discrimination doctrine applies to the challenged county-origin restrictions at private waste facilities. The controlling application establishes that exclusion of other intrastate counties and admission by some counties do not remove discrimination. Michigan's planning justification fails on this record. Neutral quantity, safety and facility requirements remain governed by their own law. Governmental planning does not enlarge the market-participant exception to cover regulation of private transactions. The State's justification burden, ordinary treatment of empirical findings and Pike's separate branch are unchanged; no novel-scientific-study requirement or universal duty to accept waste is created.

## 9. Separate Writings

Blackmun dissents. He would remand to allow proof that Michigan's comprehensive planning system serves legitimate environmental and safety objectives before invalidating the import restrictions. The system also imposes planning and disposal burdens on Michigan counties, which in his view may distinguish it from a simple local commercial preference. His position seeks an evidentiary opportunity; it neither declares the law valid nor supplies a controlling exception.

## 10. Procedure After Action

Supreme Court merits review is complete. The case returns through the Sixth Circuit for proceedings consistent with the invalidity of the county-origin restrictions at private facilities, including appropriately tailored declaratory and prospective relief. Independent neutral planning, capacity, safety and permitting requirements remain for lawful administration. No unconditional license, required acceptance of particular waste, later hearing date, mandate-issuance date or retained Supreme Court supervision is supplied.

Docket, dates, posture, challenged provisions and private-facility limits are verified by the [official report](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep504/usrep504353/usrep504353.pdf) and [Sixth Circuit opinion](https://static.case.law/f2d/931/html/0413-01.html). No quotation is supplied or authorized. The rule, votes and writings above are the recorded action of this Court in this term. No later administrative decision or remedy has been assumed.

# Morales v. Trans World Airlines, Inc.

## 1. Event

Morales v. Trans World Airlines, Inc., No. 90-1604; October Term 1991; merits decision June 1, 1992. Argued March 3, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the Fifth Circuit, 949 F.2d 141, affirming a permanent injunction based on the earlier preliminary-injunction decision, 897 F.2d 773. Texas challenges preemption of the threatened fare-advertising requirements and the breadth of the decree; the airlines defend protection. The propriety of extending relief to the other 33 Attorneys General is outside this granted matter. June 1's three decisions are uncoordinated and share entering law.

Render form: full. Two independently sufficient statutory grounds, separate equitable components and a distinct judgment concurrence require explicit Judgment and Opinion Topology tables.

## 2. Participation

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas participate at argument and decision. Souter takes no part; no public reason is supplied.

## 3. Public Action

The judgment is affirmed in part, reversed in part and remanded, 6–2. Protection against the specified fare-guideline enforcement is sustained; the excess injunction is vacated.

## 4. Judgment & Remedy

## 6. Holdings

### Prospective relief against the threatened fare enforcement

**Holding and operative rule:** Federal prospective relief may protect a party against imminent state enforcement forbidden by federal law when repeated serious sanctions make awaiting enforcement an inadequate practical remedy. The announced enforcement of these fare guidelines presents that condition; the Court does not decide the separate requirements for abstention, which Texas did not invoke.

**Authority:** Scalia's Opinion of the Court, joined by White, O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas; five of eight participating Justices. Stone states a compatible equitable ground separately but supplies no join to this writing.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the airlines need not expose themselves to repeated substantial liability to obtain a decision on this federal defense. Ex parte Young supplies prospective protection against imminent unlawful enforcement where awaiting suit gives no adequate practical remedy. Texas gave formal notice that it intended to sue, and its law attaches additional consequences to repeated violations. An airline could therefore continue its challenged advertisements and accumulate liability, or suspend them during the test case and any ensuing review. Neither option supplies an adequate substitute for prospective review on this record. The ordinary restraint described in O'Shea remains: equitable relief requires threatened injury, rather than an abstract disagreement about governmental power. The federal statutory holding establishes why this particular enforcement is forbidden; preemption by itself does not excuse the required showing of injury. The Court does not decide Younger abstention, which Texas has not argued, or whether a materially different enforcement threat would justify relief.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 — applied to imminent enforcement with repeated sanctions and no adequate practical test-case alternative.
- O'Shea v. Littleton, 414 U.S. 488 — its adequate-remedy and threatened-injury limits remain operative.
- Younger v. Harris, 401 U.S. 37 — no abstention ruling or exception is adopted; that question is not presented.

### The decree must remain within the threatened controversy

**Holding and operative rule:** A preventive injunction against state enforcement must be confined to the unlawful enforcement actually threatened; repeating a federal preemption clause does not justify protection against hypothetical future applications. The portion of this decree covering unthreatened airline advertising and operations is vacated, and the case is remanded to narrow relief to the specified fare-guideline controversy.

**Authority:** Scalia's Opinion of the Court, joined by White, O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas; five Justices. The other participants' votes to remove the excess do not supply additional joins to that opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that success on one federal defense does not authorize an injunction against every possible state dispute involving an airline. Wycoff requires a concrete threatened controversy before a federal court determines the legality of future regulatory action. Young likewise concerns officers who threaten enforcement, and O'Shea rejects relief founded on conjectural injury. Here the notices and announced policy concern fare advertising. They do not establish impending enforcement against the entire range of airline operations covered by the decree. The lower court therefore went beyond the record when it barred any action touching rates, routes or services. A decree at that breadth would decide hypothetical applications before the State had even asserted that its law reached them. Removing that excess leaves the airlines protected against the unlawful enforcement established here. The Court does not adjudicate frequent-flyer programs, boarding compensation or other practices simply because the NAAG materials discuss them, and it leaves the separate extension-to-other-defendants issue outside this review.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Public Service Commission of Utah v. Wycoff Co., 344 U.S. 237 — applied to exclude hypothetical regulatory disputes from preventive relief.
- Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 — applied at its imminent-enforcement boundary.
- O'Shea v. Littleton, 414 U.S. 488 — applied against relief based on conjectural injury.

### Express relation to rates and the savings clause

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1305(a)(1) preempts state enforcement having a connection with or reference to covered air-carrier rates, routes or services, even through a generally applicable law; the general remedies clause does not preserve enforcement within that specific prohibition. The specified fare-advertising guidelines are preempted because they directly reference fares and impose binding requirements on how tickets at those fares may be marketed; tenuous, remote or peripheral connections remain outside the ruling's resolved boundary.

**Authority:** Scalia's Opinion of the Court, joined by White, O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas; five Justices. This direct-reference ground independently sustains the statutory judgment. Stone's distinct concurrence supports the actual requirements' intrinsic relation but is not a join to the Court's complete construction.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the statute reaches state-enforced fare requirements even when consumer-protection law supplies the enforcement vehicle. Shaw supplies the connection-or-reference meaning of the relational words, together with a reservation for remote effects. Congress used that language to prohibit more than state selection of a numerical fare. These guidelines expressly address fares, require specified disclosure of restrictions and availability, govern surcharges and round-trip prices, and regulate the use of discount descriptions. They bind how an airline may offer tickets at stated prices. General applicability does not erase that relationship. Crawford Fitting supplies the ordinary rule that a specific provision governs a general one, and Ouellette explains why general savings cannot undo a carefully drawn federal limitation. Section 1506 therefore preserves remedies outside the express prohibition without nullifying it. Nader concerned the earlier aviation regime and does not settle the later clause. The Court does not draw the boundary for nonprice advertising, remote effects or materially different state duties.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Shaw v. Delta Air Lines, Inc., 463 U.S. 85 — its connection/reference formulation is adopted by analogy for this clause, with its remote-effects reservation; ERISA's entire statutory scheme is not imported.
- Metropolitan Life Insurance Co. v. Massachusetts, 471 U.S. 724 — applied for congressional intent, relational breadth and construction of provisions together; aviation savings receives its own analysis.
- Crawford Fitting Co. v. J.T.Gibbons, Inc., 482 U.S. 437 — applied: the specific substantive limitation controls over a general savings provision.
- International Paper Co. v. Ouellette, 479 U.S. 481 — applied by analogy against reading general savings to undo the specific federal limitation.
- Nader v. Allegheny Airlines, Inc., 426 U.S. 290 — distinguished as construing the pre-1978 regime; its earlier coexistence conclusion does not override the new express clause.

### Independently sufficient effect of these fare-marketing requirements

**Holding and operative rule:** Independently of their express reference to fares, these compelled disclosures and advertising restrictions fall within § 1305(a)(1) because their substantial effect on the marketing of restricted fares significantly affects the fares themselves. This alternative holding concerns the identified fare-marketing package and does not establish that every state-imposed business cost or indirect effect warrants preemption.

**Authority:** Scalia's Opinion of the Court, joined by White, O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas; five Justices. This is an independently sufficient alternative ground. Stone joins neither this ground nor a freestanding economic-effects threshold.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court also holds that the particular requirements have the forbidden significant effect on fares. Bates explains that price advertising helps consumers find lower-cost sellers and supports price competition. Airlines use restricted lower fares to fill seats while offering different terms to passengers willing to pay more. The challenged package limits their ability to advertise those restricted fares: extensive disclosures burden short advertisements, surcharge rules require market-specific presentations, and restrictions on discount language and availability affect how the lower fares can be offered. Those requirements significantly constrain marketing and consequently fares. Texas's description of the rules as measures against deception does not eliminate their practical operation. The Court does not hold that every consumer law raises costs enough to be displaced, nor does it decide the remote or nonprice applications that Shaw leaves open. Federal unfair-practices authority under § 1381 remains available, but that authority alone is not the reason these state requirements are preempted.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Bates v. State Bar of Arizona, 433 U.S. 350 — applied for the relationship between price information and competitive prices; no constitutional speech issue is decided here.
- Shaw v. Delta Air Lines, Inc., 463 U.S. 85 — its remote-effects reservation remains; no general cost-of-business preemption rule is announced.

## 7. Precedent Treatment

Each Holdings block supplies the exact treatment. No precedent is overruled. Young, Wycoff and O'Shea retain their equitable limits. Shaw supplies the relational formulation by analogy with its remote-effects reservation; aviation savings is construed under its own statute. Nader's pre-1978 holding is distinguished. Crawford Fitting and Ouellette supply the relationship between the specific limitation and general savings. Bates supplies the connection between price information and competition without deciding a speech claim.

## 8. Law After Decision

The four holding blocks state the complete material treatment. No precedent is overruled. Effective June 1, 1992, § 1305(a)(1) has a controlling relational construction and two sufficient applications to these fare guidelines. Before this event no simulated holding construed that clause. The existing Young/Wycoff equitable requirements are applied without a new general test: prospective protection may address this imminent prohibited enforcement, and excess relief must be vacated. No damages right, general airline immunity, complete displacement of ordinary state remedies or new abstention rule results. The federal agency's continuing authority is not a standalone field-preemption holding. Directly covered requirements need not conflict with an existing federal rule to be displaced; mere consistency does not defeat the express prohibition. That qualification belongs to the statutory construction, not a separate generic implied-preemption doctrine.

## 9. Separate Writings

Stone concurs in the judgment. He relies on the intrinsic relation of the binding fare-presentation, availability and mandatory-term requirements to rates, together with the actual enforcement threat. He joins no part of Scalia's opinion and no independent economic-effects ground. His concurrence gives savings a real role outside the displaced category and reserves actual fraud, voluntarily undertaken obligations, unrelated injury and constitutional speech claims for their own records; those reservations do not narrow the five-Justice Court holding.

Stevens, joined by Blackmun, dissents. They would reverse the fare-guideline protection as well because the aviation statute's structure and savings preserve ordinary truthfulness regulation. They also consider the claimed significant effects on fares insufficiently demonstrated. They oppose the excessive decree too, but join none of the Court's reasoning.

## 10. Procedure After Action

Supreme Court merits review ends. The lower courts must retain declaratory and injunctive protection against the specified fare-guideline enforcement and narrow the injunction by removing its coverage of unthreatened practices. No damages, new territorial extension, adjudication of No. 90-1606, retained Supreme Court supervision or later mandate-issuance date is supplied. The Court does not decide Younger abstention, which Texas did not argue, or the constitutional speech claims.

# Burdick v. Takushi — Render Input

## 1. Event

Burdick v. Takushi, No. 91-535; October Term 1991; merits decision June 8, 1992. Argued March 24, 1992. 

Certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, reviewing its replacement opinion filed June 28, 1991, 937 F.2d 415. The March 1, 1991 opinion at 927 F.2d 469 was withdrawn. Hawaii’s Supreme Court construed state law on July 21, 1989 to prohibit write-ins. The District of Hawaii entered summary judgment and an injunction for Burdick on May 10, 1990, 737 F.Supp. 582; that injunction was stayed and the Ninth Circuit reversed.

The questions are whether Hawaii’s complete prohibition violates First and Fourteenth Amendment electoral-choice rights when considered within its access system, and whether the Constitution independently requires an official tally of arbitrary protest votes. Burdick seeks prospective relief from the prohibition; Hawaii seeks affirmance. The Court reaches primary and general-election applications within the challenged system, but no independent challenge to every surrounding access rule. The lower court rejected standing and certification/full-faith-and-credit objections; no supplied defect prevents certiorari review under 28 U.S.C. § 1254(1).

## 6. Holdings

### 1. Review of election regulations follows the actual burden

**Holding and operative rule.** A court reviewing a restriction on voting or candidate access must weigh the character and magnitude of the injury to First and Fourteenth Amendment rights against the State’s precise asserted interests and the extent to which those interests require the burden. Severe restrictions must be narrowly drawn to advance a compelling interest; reasonable, nondiscriminatory restrictions ordinarily are justified by important regulatory interests, so strict scrutiny does not apply to every election rule.

**Authority.** White’s Opinion of the Court, joined by O’Connor, Scalia, Souter, and Thomas, supplies five votes for the complete rule and controls. Kennedy’s dissent, joined by Blackmun and Stevens, and Stone-Zsela’s separate dissent also accept this calibrated framework; their agreement does not make their contrary application controlling.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that constitutional review must account for the regulation and the election system in which it operates. Anderson v. Celebrezze supplies the inquiry into the actual injury, the precise state interests, and the need for the particular burden. Norman v. Reed preserves that inquiry while recognizing that States may require support in the electorate a candidate or party seeks to represent. Storer v. Brown explains why orderly elections require substantial regulation of candidate selection. Treating every restriction as severe would disable the primary procedures and qualification rules those decisions permit. The claimed injury here therefore cannot be measured simply by observing that Hawaii forbids write-ins. The Court must examine the other means of obtaining access and the function the prohibition serves within that system. This approach preserves demanding review when access is severely restricted and meaningful scrutiny when a lesser burden is asserted; it does not make ordinary administration immune from constitutional review.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Anderson v. Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780 (1983): applied as the governing burden, interest, and necessity inquiry; no categorical strict-scrutiny rule replaces it.
- Norman v. Reed (January 14, 1992): applied for actual access review and permissible electorate-specific support requirements; its protection against cross-component exclusion remains effective.
- Storer v. Brown, 415 U.S. 724 (1974): applied for the State’s authority to organize candidate selection; severe barriers remain reviewable.

### 2. Hawaii’s complete write-in prohibition is valid in its reasonably accessible system

**Holding and operative rule.** Where the complete ballot-access system imposes only reasonable burdens, a prohibition on write-in voting is presumptively valid and may be sustained by interests proportionate to that burden. Hawaii’s party and nonpartisan routes provide adequate access, and its write-in prohibition validly supports primary finality, prevention of sore-loser candidacies and party raiding, and its treatment of uncontested primaries; the electoral-choice challenge therefore fails.

**Authority.** White’s Opinion of the Court, joined by O’Connor, Scalia, Souter, and Thomas, controls with five votes. Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, and Kennedy disagree with this application and its resulting judgment. This is a majority rationale; Marks analysis is unnecessary.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that Hawaii’s prohibition imposes a limited burden when assessed with its nomination rules. Small signature requirements and party and nonpartisan routes permit access before the election, although the nonpartisan route requires a primary showing of support and a choice of primary ballot. Jenness v. Fortson permits reasonable demonstrations of support, and Munro v. Socialist Workers Party permits primary winnowing without proof of a particular past disruption. The Constitution does not require the State to accommodate every preference arising after its lawful selection deadlines. Storer v. Brown supports preserving the primary’s role in settling candidacies. The prohibition also prevents candidacies that would evade that selection process, discourages primary raiding, and complements the designation of certain unopposed primary victors. The contrary position underestimates these available access routes and treats the inability to vote for an unlisted choice as decisive. The Court sustains this system without deciding whether the same prohibition would survive materially more restrictive access rules.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Jenness v. Fortson, 403 U.S. 431 (1971): applied to permit reasonable candidate-support requirements; it does not validate every access barrier.
- Munro v. Socialist Workers Party, 479 U.S. 189 (1986): applied to permit primary winnowing and reasonable preventative regulation without an empirical-disruption prerequisite.
- Storer v. Brown, 415 U.S. 724 (1974): applied to primary finality and restrictions preventing circumvention of candidate selection.
- Anderson v. Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780 (1983): applied to the complete Hawaii system; this decision supplies a limited-burden application, not an exception to the inquiry.

### 3. The ballot need not serve as an official tally of arbitrary political messages

**Holding and operative rule.** The First and Fourteenth Amendments do not require the State to count and publicize write-in votes for fictional figures or arbitrary protest messages as an independent form of political expression. Elections select public officials, and a desire to use the ballot for such messages does not establish a constitutional entitlement to a write-in option.

**Authority.** White’s Opinion of the Court, joined by O’Connor, Scalia, Souter, and Thomas, controls. Kennedy’s dissent, joined by Blackmun and Stevens, and Stone-Zsela’s separate dissent expressly agree in rejecting this broader claim. All nine reject this component, although four would grant relief for genuine electoral choice.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the ballot’s purpose limits the claimed expressive entitlement. Voting carries political meaning, but an official election need not become a general forum in which the State records and disseminates any message a voter wishes to send. Storer v. Brown recognizes elections as a means of selecting officeholders through an orderly process, and the access inquiry under Anderson v. Celebrezze protects the ability to participate in that process. Neither principle requires an official tally for fictional figures or arbitrary statements. Burdick’s interest in expressing dissatisfaction therefore does not itself invalidate the prohibition. His distinct interest in supporting an actual alternative candidate requires the contextual analysis stated in the preceding holdings, and the Court has conducted it. Rejecting the broader theory does not diminish political expression outside the ballot or decide every restriction on votes for otherwise eligible candidates under a different access system.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Storer v. Brown, 415 U.S. 724 (1974): applied to the office-selecting function of elections; the ballot is not a general expressive forum.
- Anderson v. Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780 (1983): distinguished from the demand for an arbitrary-message tally; meaningful electoral participation remains protected.

## 6. Holdings

### 1. Distinct parts and service demand and conditional sales present a triable tying claim

**Holding and operative rule.** For a Sherman Act § 1 tying claim, functionally related products are distinct when sufficient separate consumer demand makes separate provision efficient. Evidence that Kodak parts and service meet that test, and that Kodak conditions parts sales on buying its service or forgoing independent service, creates genuine disputes about distinct products and conditioning; respondents may proceed but must prove all remaining tying elements.

**Authority.** Blackmun’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Kennedy, and Souter, controls with six votes, including the author. The commerce volume is not disputed here; no liability judgment follows.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that practical consumer demand, rather than a product’s functional relationship to another product, determines whether the two are distinct for tying purposes. Jefferson Parish Hospital District No. 2 v. Hyde supplies that demand-based inquiry and the requirements of tying power and a substantial amount of tied commerce. The record shows that independent organizations have supplied service separately, that customers have preferred independent service, and that some customers service their own equipment. Those materials permit a finding that separate provision is efficient. Kodak’s restrictions on parts sales to independent organizations and on customers’ use of independent service also permit a finding of conditioning. The fact that parts are installed during service does not conclusively combine both into one product. Respondents still must establish the necessary market power and other elements; the Court holds only that the district court could not end this claim for want of separate products or an arguable tie.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Jefferson Parish Hospital District No. 2 v. Hyde, 466 U.S. 2 (1984): applied for separate demand, conditioning, tying power, and tied commerce; functional integration alone does not merge products.
- Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317 (1986): applied to preserve respondents’ burden of producing evidence on the elements challenged at summary judgment.

### 2. Equipment-market competition is relevant but not a categorical defense

**Holding and operative rule.** Competition in an equipment market does not as a matter of law preclude market power in its parts or service markets. Under Rule 56, a party cannot obtain summary judgment solely through an economic prediction contradicted by evidence permitting a rational inference of power, including material information and switching costs and actual price or exclusion effects; unsupported speculation remains insufficient.

**Authority.** Blackmun’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Kennedy, and Souter, controls with six votes. The holding rejects a conclusive defense, not the relevance of equipment competition.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that Kodak’s proposed equipment-market constraint must be tested against the record. Matsushita Electric Industrial Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp. requires a plausible inference from evidence; it does not authorize a court to replace disputed facts with an economic model. Here the alleged conduct can produce immediate aftermarket gains, unlike the prolonged and implausible loss-producing conspiracy examined there. Evidence of costly equipment replacement and difficult lifetime service-price comparisons permits a finding that some installed customers cannot readily escape unfavorable terms. Evidence of price and exclusion effects supports testing that account at trial. Kodak may show that informed new purchasers and equipment rivalry adequately discipline its policies, but that conclusion is not inevitable as a matter of law. Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc. preserves the factfinder’s role when a genuine dispute could affect the result. The Court neither presumes power from imperfect information nor treats every durable-product seller as a monopolist.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Matsushita Electric Industrial Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp., 475 U.S. 574 (1986): applied as a rational-inference requirement and distinguished on the alleged economic mechanism; it creates no categorical equipment-market defense.
- Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242 (1986): applied to identify genuine material disputes; evidence and reasonable inferences receive the ordinary Rule 56 treatment.
- Jefferson Parish Hospital District No. 2 v. Hyde, 466 U.S. 2 (1984): preserved; respondents must prove actual tying power despite rejection of a conclusive equipment-competition defense.

### 3. A single brand may define a relevant market when actual substitutes do not constrain it

**Holding and operative rule.** A relevant product market may consist of one brand’s parts or service when the actual commercial alternatives and competitive constraints support that definition; brand identity alone neither establishes nor defeats such a market. Evidence of Kodak-specific compatibility, a very high parts share, a high service share, and entry and switching barriers creates a genuine dispute about monopoly power sufficient to resist summary judgment on § 2.

**Authority.** Blackmun’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Kennedy, and Souter, controls with six votes. The record permits a market and power finding; it does not conclusively establish either.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that market boundaries turn on the alternatives buyers can actually use. United States v. Grinnell Corp. requires monopoly power in a relevant market, and United States v. E. I. du Pont de Nemours & Co. directs attention to substitution and competitive restraint. Owners needing compatible parts cannot necessarily substitute another brand’s parts, and changing equipment may cost substantially more than the aftermarket service at issue. Respondents also present evidence of Kodak’s dominant parts position, high service share, and obstacles to independent supply. Together these materials permit an inference of power to control prices or exclude competition. Kodak’s equipment competitors remain relevant because they may constrain lifetime prices; the Court does not exclude that evidence. But their existence does not settle the market question. The result establishes neither a single-brand rule for all manufacturers nor a monopoly presumption from a patent, a proprietary design, or numerical share considered apart from actual constraints.

**Precedent treatment.**

- United States v. Grinnell Corp., 384 U.S. 563 (1966): applied to require monopoly power in a relevant market, distinct from exclusionary conduct.
- United States v. E. I. du Pont de Nemours & Co., 351 U.S. 377 (1956): applied to practical substitution and competitive constraints; no categorical prohibition on a one-brand market follows.

### 4. Exclusionary maintenance and Kodak’s business explanations require factual determination

**Holding and operative rule.** Monopolization under § 2 requires monopoly power plus willful acquisition or maintenance of that power, distinguished from success through a superior product, business acumen, or accident. Evidence of parts-access restrictions that may exclude service rivals creates a genuine issue of unlawful maintenance, and Kodak’s asserted quality, inventory, and free-riding explanations do not conclusively eliminate it on this record; no general duty to supply rivals or final finding of pretext is imposed.

**Authority.** Blackmun’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Kennedy, and Souter, controls with six votes. Attempted monopolization remains a distinct claim with its own intent and dangerous-probability requirements, not a consequence automatically established by this holding.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the reasons and competitive effect of Kodak’s restrictions cannot be settled on this summary-judgment record. Grinnell distinguishes unlawful maintenance of monopoly power from lawful commercial success. Aspen Skiing Co. v. Aspen Highlands Skiing Corp. makes the character of challenged exclusion and its business justification material without imposing a general obligation to help competitors. Respondents’ evidence permits a finding that restricting access to necessary parts protects Kodak’s service position. The asserted quality rationale must be considered with the continued self-service exception and evidence about independent service. Inventory explanations must be tested against evidence that equipment failures and parts needs do not change with the provider, while supplier restrictions may impede alternative sources. Calling independent service free riding because the providers did not enter the equipment and parts businesses does not conclusively justify exclusion. Kodak may prove legitimate reasons and defeat liability. The Court awards no damages, orders no compulsory supply, and leaves ultimate liability and injury for further proceedings.

## 6. Holdings

### The actual dual-use package is outside the making tax

**Holding and operative rule:** The National Firearms Act does not clearly treat the segregated Contender pistol and conversion package, which has genuine lawful pistol and long-rifle uses and has not been assembled as a short-barreled rifle, as the making of that regulated firearm. Because the same definition carries criminal consequences, the remaining statutory ambiguity is resolved in the manufacturer's favor in this civil refund action as well.

**Authority:** Souter and O'Connor's opinion, Scalia's concurrence joined by Thomas, and Stone-Zsela's concurrence expressly support this case-specific statutory construction and uniform application of residual lenity. Those five constitute a majority at this level of generality, although no complete opinion commands five joins. The holding rests on that explicit common proposition, not on a synthetic Marks rationale combining their differing accounts of the ambiguity.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that unresolved criminally consequential ambiguity prevents collection of this making tax. Crandon v. United States supplies the governing principle: the meaning of a statute with criminal applications does not change merely because the present case is civil. The inquiry begins with the enacted language and the related definitions. Section 5845 defines a rifle by its design, making, and intended shoulder firing, while other firearm definitions expressly address combinations of parts. Those provisions do not clearly settle this package, which permits a lawful pistol and a lawful long rifle as well as the regulated short rifle. The Government's concern that packaging must not defeat actual firearms regulation does not itself identify the statutory boundary here. The five Justices differ over how much broader parts coverage the text supports, but agree that the remaining ambiguity concerning this unit must be resolved narrowly. The decision does not decide criminal culpability, impose a storage protocol, or exempt an assembled short-barreled rifle.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Crandon v. United States, 494 U.S. 152 — applied: residual lenity supplies one construction of criminally consequential statutory language in civil litigation; no new criminal proof burden results.
- United States v. R. L. C., simulated March 24, 1992 — preserved: its adult-Guidelines ceiling does not establish a common new lenity method and supplies no different firearms rule.
- Marks v. United States, 430 U.S. 188 — no narrowest-opinion synthesis is necessary or adopted for the five Justices' expressly shared case-specific proposition; their broader incompatible rationales remain distinct.

### Making is not invariably postponed until final assembly

**Holding and operative rule:** The National Firearms Act's references to manufacturing and otherwise producing a firearm are not confined to the final mechanical act of putting its parts together. The Federal Circuit's universal actual-assembly prerequisite is therefore rejected, without deciding tax liability for an unpresented pure-parts or sham arrangement.

**Authority:** Souter's opinion joined by O'Connor expressly adopts this construction, and White's dissent joined by Blackmun, Stevens and Kennedy expressly adopts the same proposition while disagreeing about the dual-use package. Stone-Zsela expressly supports the rejection of a universal final-assembly requirement in his concurrence while reserving liability for pure-parts kits and sham configurations. Seven participating Justices therefore support this exact predicate at the same level of generality. This is a directly shared statutory proposition across the writings, not a Marks combination of nonoverlapping rationales. Stone-Zsela joins no broader categorical ruling about the reserved arrangements. Scalia and Thomas disagree with this construction.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the statutory meaning of making cannot invariably wait for the purchaser to complete the final connection. Section 5845(i) identifies manufacturing and otherwise producing as well as putting together. Reading every term as requiring final assembly would deprive the broader production language of work in this setting. Drasen's analysis of the production terms illustrates why a universal final-assembly rule is too broad; liability for an unpresented pure-parts arrangement remains reserved. The express parts clauses for other regulated weapons do not compel a different answer so as to require a universal assembly prerequisite. The disagreement over this case concerns the additional lawful configurations, not whether the statute invariably waits for the last mechanical connection. This construction does not decide that every miscellaneous collection, incomplete kit, or set with genuine alternative lawful uses is a firearm. Nor does it turn a regulatory purpose into an independent tax: the production must satisfy the enacted definition.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Drasen, 845 F.2d 731 — its construction of manufacturing supports rejecting a universal actual-assembly prerequisite; the Court adopts no new judgment concerning its kits or its broader notice and criminal-responsibility discussion.
- Thompson/Center Arms Co. v. United States, 924 F.2d 1041 — the refund judgment is affirmed, but the universal actual-assembly rationale is rejected at the stated seven-Justice level.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No complete opinion controls the broader division between permissible and regulated combinations. The actual assembly of a short rifle, sham lawful configurations, miscellaneous or incomplete parts, unrelated firearm categories and separate criminal offenses are not adjudicated. No general intent-to-assemble element, criminal proof change, prospective injunction or storage requirement is created. The refund ruling does not exempt all parts kits. The broader utility taxonomy in Souter's opinion, beyond the independently supported rejection of a universal actual-assembly prerequisite, is not additional controlling law.


## 7. Precedent Treatment

Crandon's civil/criminal construction principle is applied. R.L.C.'s juvenile ceiling and unresolved interpretive-method division remain intact. Drasen supports the precise seven-Justice rejection of a universal actual-assembly prerequisite, and the Federal Circuit's universal assembly prerequisite is rejected despite affirmance of its judgment. Marks supplies no synthetic broader rationale.

Residual lenity remains a rule of construction; civil refund and hypothetical criminal proof burdens remain distinct. R.L.C.'s juvenile ceiling and the unresolved choice among general interpretive methods remain unchanged. Scalia and Thomas regard the express treatment of parts in other firearm definitions, and the intended shoulder-firing configuration shown by this record, as the source of statutory uncertainty. They do not accept a usefulness classification or legislative-history expansion of criminal coverage. # Sochor v. Florida

## 6. Holdings

### The jury-heinousness instruction claim is outside review

**Holding and operative rule:** The Court cannot review a federal challenge to a penalty instruction when the state judgment clearly rests on an adequate independent failure-to-object ground, even if the state court also rejects the claim on its merits. Florida's express preservation ruling forecloses this jury-heinousness challenge; no sufficient basis to reject the ground's adequacy or independence has been established here.

**Authority:** Part II-A of Souter's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; seven Justices. Blackmun and Stevens would reach the instructional claim.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an alternative discussion of federal merits does not remove an independent state ground for judgment. Michigan v. Long supplies the requirement to identify clearly the state ground, and Herb v. Pitcairn supplies the jurisdictional consequence when that ground independently sustains the judgment. Florida's penalty-instruction paragraph states that no objection was made and that the claims were not preserved, then separately rejects their merits. Its accompanying note includes the heinousness instruction. That is a clear alternative procedural disposition. The possibility of fundamental-error review does not establish that this particular preservation ruling depended on deciding the federal claim. Nor do later state citations to the merits discussion withdraw the independent ground already given. The contrary reading would treat the state's power to consider an unpreserved error as if it necessarily had displaced the expressly stated procedural basis here. The Court reserves a supported challenge to a different procedural ground and does not decide this instruction's constitutional merits.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Michigan v. Long, 463 U.S. 1032 — applied: an expressly independent state disposition remains sufficient despite alternative federal merits discussion.
- Herb v. Pitcairn, 324 U.S. 117 — applied: an adequate independent state ground limits this Court's review.
- Coleman v. Thompson, 501 U.S. 722 — its pre-divergence distinction between independent state procedure and federal merits remains intact; this direct review does not create a habeas exception.

### The judge's narrowed heinousness application survives

**Holding and operative rule:** A sentencing judge may apply an otherwise vague heinousness aggravator through an authoritative and sufficiently specific state narrowing construction. Florida's consistently recognized conscious-victim strangulation construction supplies adequate guidance on this record; inconsistent applications in other factual settings do not establish that this judge lacked that guidance.

**Authority:** Part II-B of Souter's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; seven Justices. Blackmun and Stevens do not join the Court's disposition of the heinousness challenge, because they would require correction of the separate jury defect.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this judicial application rests on a concrete limiting construction. Godfrey and Maynard require meaningful guidance rather than a bare invitation to label any murder especially evil. Walton permits a judge to apply the state's authoritative narrowing decisions and presumes familiarity with them. Florida's cases consistently recognize the fear and conscious suffering involved when a conscious victim is strangled; the judgment below identifies that specific construction through Tompkins, Johnson and Doyle. The record contains the victim's resistance, fear and the strangulation, so the judge was not left merely to repeat the statutory adjectives. The objection that Florida has used broader language in other cases does not show an absence of a governing boundary in this one. The Court does not approve every Florida heinousness decision, the bare instruction given to this jury, or an application involving a different victim's condition. It rejects only the preserved challenge to the judge's use of the narrowed factor here.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Godfrey v. Georgia, 446 U.S. 420, and Maynard v. Cartwright, 486 U.S. 356 — preserved and applied: the aggravator requires meaningful narrowing.
- Walton v. Arizona, 497 U.S. 639 — applied: the sentencing judge may use an authoritative state narrowing construction and is presumed familiar with it.
- Proffitt v. Florida, 428 U.S. 242 — its approval of a narrowed heinousness category is preserved; no approval of every later Florida application is inferred.

### The general jury recommendation does not establish coldness reliance

**Holding and operative rule:** The submission of a legally available aggravating circumstance that lacks evidentiary support does not, by itself, establish that a Florida advisory jury relied on it when returning a general death recommendation that identifies no aggravator findings. The Court therefore rejects this asserted inference of jury-coldness error without finding that the jury actually rejected the factor or resolving a legally invalid instruction.

**Authority:** Part III-A of Souter's opinion, joined by White, O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas; five Justices. Stone-Zsela reserves the independent jury-coldness question; Blackmun and Stevens would include the submission error in the corrective inquiry; Scalia does not join this independent holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the jury's undisclosed findings cannot be supplied by inference from the instruction alone. Griffin distinguishes a legally forbidden route, which a jury cannot be expected to recognize as forbidden, from a lawful route that the evidence fails to support. Stromberg and Yates retain their different protection against verdicts that may rest on legal invalidity. Cold, calculated and premeditated murder was a legally available aggravator; the state court's objection was the absence of proof of its heightened premeditation requirement. Florida's jury recommended death generally and identified no aggravators. On those circumstances, Griffin's distinction gives no basis to presume that the jury adopted the unsupported option. This conclusion does not declare what the jury found, convert a capital weighing recommendation into a guilt verdict for every purpose, or decide that all instructional errors are harmless. The judge's express coldness finding presents a different, established error that must be examined independently.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Griffin v. United States, simulated December 3, 1991 — its factual-versus-legal distinction is extended narrowly to reject this asserted inference from a general advisory recommendation; broader capital-jury doctrine remains open.
- Stromberg v. California, 283 U.S. 359, and Yates v. United States, 354 U.S. 298 — distinguished and preserved for legally invalid alternatives.
- Estelle v. McGuire, simulated December 4, 1991 — its whole-charge reasonable-likelihood rule remains unchanged; it neither removes the preservation bar nor supplies an automatic inference of factual reliance here.

### The judge's documented coldness reliance requires correction

**Holding and operative rule:** When a Florida capital sentencing judge expressly weighs an aggravator that the state supreme court holds unsupported by the evidence, that invalid factor creates Eighth Amendment error in the weighing decision. The sentence requires lawful reweighing, constitutional harmless-error review, or resentencing; the existence of other valid aggravators does not itself supply the cure.

**Authority:** Part III-B-1 of Souter's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas; eight Justices. Scalia rejects the constitutional weighing-error premise.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the judge's express written finding establishes actual reliance on the unsupported aggravator. Clemons requires a constitutionally adequate correction when a capital weighing decision incorporates an invalid aggravator. Parker applies that principle when Florida has found aggravating circumstances unsupported by the evidence, and Stringer explains why surviving valid factors alone do not remove the invalid factor from the sentencing balance. Here the judge found and weighed coldness, while Florida's supreme court held that heightened premeditation was not proved. The difference between ordinary murder premeditation and this additional aggravating requirement matters: the valid conviction does not establish the invalidated circumstance. The State may show that the error made no difference under Chapman, or use another legally available corrective procedure, but the error cannot be ignored because death eligibility survives. The Court does not invalidate the convictions, reopen the mitigation findings, or prescribe a life sentence upon removal of one aggravator.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Clemons v. Mississippi, 494 U.S. 738 — applied: a weighing error requires adequate appellate correction or resentencing.
- Parker v. Dugger, 498 U.S. 308 — applied to a judge's use of an evidentially unsupported Florida aggravator; the actual sentencing record controls.
- Stringer v. Black, simulated March 9, 1992 — its correction principle is applied to documented reliance on unsupported coldness on direct review; its retroactivity and procedural-default reservations do not bar this case.
- Zant v. Stephens, 462 U.S. 862 — its nonweighing setting is distinguished; surviving eligibility does not automatically cure a weighing error.
- Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18 — preserved as the constitutional harmlessness standard, with the burden on the State.

### The existing appellate explanation does not cure the error

**Holding and operative rule:** A state appellate court affirming this sentence must substantively establish beyond a reasonable doubt that the invalid aggravator did not contribute to the sentence, or provide another authorized constitutional cure. Florida's existing proportionality discussion, count of surviving factors and mixed citations do not establish that inquiry here; no particular verbal formula is either required or sufficient.

**Authority:** Parts III-B-2 and IV of Souter's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Kennedy; six Justices. White and Thomas accept the error framework but find this review sufficient; Scalia would affirm on his separate constitutional ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a strong harmlessness argument is not a substitute for the required appellate determination. Chapman places on the State the burden of showing beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute to the sentence. Clemons and Parker require review of the actual sentencing choice; proportionality asks instead how the sentence compares with other cases. Florida's paragraph principally frames that comparison and observes three valid factors against no found mitigation. Those facts may bear strongly on harmlessness, but they do not explain whether attributing an additional kind of culpability affected this weighing decision. The cited cases do not make the missing inquiry clear. The Court requires no prescribed words and does not suggest that appending a harmless-error label would be enough. The case returns for supported Chapman review, lawful reweighing if Florida law permits it, or resentencing. It remains for the state courts to choose and perform an adequate lawful correction.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18 — applied without changing the State's beyond-a-reasonable-doubt burden.
- Clemons v. Mississippi, 494 U.S. 738, and Parker v. Dugger, 498 U.S. 308 — applied: actual corrective analysis must address the sentence and record rather than rest on a proportionality comparison.
- Stringer v. Black, simulated March 9, 1992 — preserved: factor counting and proportionality alone do not cure constitutional weighing error.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The convictions are not disturbed. The Court decides no automatic life-sentence rule, mandatory new-penalty-jury rule, capital-punishment validity question, new mitigation finding, future execution date, or entitlement to a particular outcome after lawful correction. The merits of the procedurally barred jury-heinousness claim are not reached by the Court. The narrow jury-coldness holding does not decide broader capital-jury error rules or factual jury findings. Stone-Zsela expressly reserves that independent jury-coldness branch and supplies no vote or rationale to its holding.
