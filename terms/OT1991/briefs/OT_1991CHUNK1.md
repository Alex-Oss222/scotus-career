# CASE 1 of 10: UNITED STATES v. IBARRA

# SECTION I: NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

* **Case Name:** United States v. Ibarra
* **Citation or Docket:** 502 U.S. 1 (1991); No. 90-1713
* **October Term:** 1991
* **Entry Stage and Event:** Petition for certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit, presented for disposition on the papers without oral argument. Respondent's motion for leave to proceed in forma pauperis is pending with the petition.
* **Simulated Event Date:** October 15, 1991 (historical decision date)
* **Question Presented:** Whether the 30-day period for a government appeal from a suppression order begins to run on the date of the district court's original order or on the date the district court denies a timely motion for reconsideration of that order, where the sole ground of the motion is a theory the government had earlier abandoned.
* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit, 920 F.2d 702 (1990), dismissing the government's appeal as untimely by a divided panel.
* **Relief Requested:** Grant of certiorari; vacatur of the judgment of dismissal; remand for consideration of the suppression appeal on the merits.
* **Material Record:** Respondent was indicted for possession of cocaine with intent to distribute. Officers stopped his car for a suspected operating violation, obtained consent, and searched briefly without finding cocaine. Neither respondent nor his passenger held a valid operator's license; the officers impounded the car, transported the occupants to a Western Union office, then searched the car a second time at the towing lot and found cocaine in the trunk. Respondent moved to suppress the fruits of the second search. The government initially asserted continuing consent, then expressly abandoned that theory in papers filed before the ruling, citing lack of legal support. The circumstances leading to the indictment are largely uncontested. The suppression ruling itself is not before the Court and its correctness is unresolved.
* **Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** The entire matter is a threshold timing question. Whether the 30-day limit of Fed. R. App. P. 4(b) and 18 U.S.C. § 3731 is jurisdictional in character or a claim-processing rule is treated in the panel opinions as significant to whether a merits-sensitive exception may be engrafted onto it; no party is recorded as contesting that the limit is mandatory. Two related questions are outside the record: whether the abandonment and reassertion of the consent theory was in bad faith, which no one asserts, and what rule governs successive motions for reconsideration, of which only one was filed here.
* **Relevant Dates:** Suppression order entered November 15, 1989 (725 F. Supp. 1195). Motion for reconsideration of the suppression order filed December 13, 1989. Motion denied January 3, 1990 (731 F. Supp. 1037). Notice of appeal filed January 30, 1990, within 30 days of the denial and 76 days after the original order.
* **Companion or Related Matters:** None material.
* **Participation Matter:** Eight-Member Court. Justice Thomas was not yet seated.

## B. LAW ENTERING THE CASE

* **Current Through:** Historical United States law effective immediately before October 15, 1991.
* **Earlier Same-Term Decisions:** None. This is the first matter scheduled in the term.
* **Unresolved Same-Term Dependencies:** None identified.
* **Institutional or Procedural Law:** Disposition of a certiorari petition on the papers, including summary vacatur and remand, is available where the governing rule is settled and the decision below departs from it.

### Issue 1: Date from which the government's 30-day appeal period runs

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** None. First term; no prior simulated holdings.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** None yet operating in the simulation.
* **Other Governing Law:** Fed. R. App. P. 4(b), requiring a government notice of appeal within 30 days after entry of the judgment or order appealed from or a defendant's notice of appeal. 18 U.S.C. § 3731, authorizing the appeal of a suppression order and requiring that it be taken within 30 days after the decision, judgment, or order has been rendered and diligently prosecuted.
* **Material Other Authority:** United States v. Healy, 376 U.S. 75 (1964), holding that a timely petition for rehearing filed within the appeal period renders an otherwise final decision nonfinal until the petition is decided, so that the 30-day period runs from the denial. United States v. Dieter, 429 U.S. 6 (1976) (per curiam), rejecting an exception to Healy for motions asserting mistake or inadvertence rather than error of law, and confirming that the full 30 days runs from the denial. Department of Banking of Neb. v. Pink, 317 U.S. 264, 266 (1942), supplying the working description of a motion for reconsideration as one seeking to reconsider a question decided in the case in order to effect an alteration of the rights adjudicated. Healy, 376 U.S. at 80 n.4, reserving bad faith. United States v. Marsh, 700 F.2d 1322 (10th Cir. 1983), on successive motions. United States v. Ladson, 774 F.2d 436, 438-439 n.3 (11th Cir. 1985), on protective notices of appeal.
* **Open Point:** Whether bad faith in abandoning and reasserting a theory, or the filing of successive motions for reconsideration, can withhold the Healy rule. Neither is presented.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

Issue 1.

* **Controlling reasoning below:** The panel majority read Healy and Dieter as serving judicial efficiency, reasoned that a motion reasserting a previously disavowed theory cannot serve the function of letting a district court correct matters it initially overlooked, and therefore created an exception for such motions. It added that motions to reconsider a conceded position are viewed with disfavor on appeal, citing United States v. Smith, 781 F.2d 184 (10th Cir. 1986). The panel framed the issue as whether the motion "tolled" a period assumed to have begun with the first order. An alternative strand of the opinion treats the motion as not a "true" motion for reconsideration because the ground had not been urged initially.
* **Material alternative or unreached grounds:** The dissenting judge below objected that the majority grafted a merits inquiry onto what should be a bright-line jurisdictional inquiry, 920 F.2d at 710 (Baldock, J., dissenting).
* **Petitioner's strongest supported position:** Healy states a general rule keyed to the filing of a timely motion, not to the motion's quality. Dieter already rejected one attempt to subdivide the category. A merits screen at the timing stage is unadministrable and forces litigants to guess, encouraging protective notices of appeal while a reconsideration motion is pending. The motion here sought reconsideration of a question decided in order to alter the rights adjudicated, which is what Healy and Dieter describe.
* **Respondent's strongest supported position:** The rationale of Healy and Dieter is efficiency, and a motion resting only on a ground the government deliberately abandoned advances no efficiency interest; a rule keyed to that rationale limits itself. Extending the appeal period on such a motion lets the government manufacture time by refiling a theory it discarded. This is the strongest available position; it does not answer the administrability objection, and respondent does not appear to contest that the tolling framing is inapt.
* **Necessary concessions or limiting principles:** The government does not assert that bad faith would be immaterial; the question is reserved. Only one motion was filed, so no position on successive motions is required.
* **Solicitor General or amicus position:** The United States is petitioner. No separate amicus position is material.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

* **Threshold paths:** Deny certiorari, leaving the circuit rule in place. Grant and set for argument. Grant and dispose summarily on the papers.
* **Merits paths:** (i) The timely motion for reconsideration renders the suppression order nonfinal, and the full 30 days runs from the denial, without a merits screen. (ii) The same rule, with an express exception for bad faith or for successive motions. (iii) The Tenth Circuit's exception for motions resting on abandoned theories. (iv) A narrower holding that the tolling framing is wrong and the period restarts, without resolving whether any exception exists.
* **Judgment consequences:** Paths (i), (ii), and (iv) require vacatur or reversal of the dismissal. Path (iii) requires affirmance and ends the government's appeal. Disposition of respondent's in forma pauperis motion accompanies any grant.
* **Remedial or remand consequences:** Vacatur returns the case to the Tenth Circuit to decide the suppression appeal on the merits; the underlying criminal trial remains pending.
* **Uncertainty:** The correctness of the suppression order is undecided and unreviewed. Whether the appeal period is properly characterized as jurisdictional is assumed rather than adjudicated in the papers. Nothing in the record bears on bad faith.

## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Date from which the government's 30-day appeal period runs

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** This is the only question presented, and it disposes of the case. Nothing beyond it is reached.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply the received doctrine unchanged. The governing procedural sources, Rule 4(b) and § 3731, fix timing, decisionmaker, and finality, and under Stone's treatment of procedural questions the form of the rule is supplied by the procedural source rather than by a balance of interests. Healy and Dieter supply a firm rule keyed to the filing of a timely motion. A rule of this kind, marking an appellate boundary and giving notice of when a clock starts, is precisely the occasion for a definite line rather than a standard.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** A timely motion asking the district court to reconsider a question it has decided, in order to alter the rights adjudicated, renders the order nonfinal for appeal purposes until the motion is denied, and the full 30-day period then runs from the denial. The character or apparent strength of the ground asserted in the motion, including that the ground was earlier abandoned, does not affect when the period begins. Applied here, the notice of appeal filed 27 days after the January 3, 1990 denial was timely, and the dismissal cannot stand.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The efficiency rationale recited in Dieter explains why the rule exists; it is not an additional element a movant must satisfy. Converting it into one would require a court of appeals to assess the merits of a reconsideration motion before it could know whether it had an appeal before it, which inverts the order of decision and makes the boundary depend on the answer to the question the boundary exists to reach. Litigants would be driven either to file protective notices while the district court still had the motion or to litigate the sufficiency of their own motion's merit. The tolling vocabulary used below is also inapt: equitable tolling subtracts elapsed time, whereas Healy and Dieter give the appellant the full period from the denial. Stone adopts the restart description because it is what the authorities hold, not as a new rule.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Bad faith abandonment and reassertion of a ground is expressly reserved; no party asserts it and Healy itself left the point open. Successive motions for reconsideration are expressly reserved; only one motion was filed. Nothing is said about the validity of the suppression order or about the lawfulness of the second search.
* **Approved Conditional Position or Fallback:** None exists.

Nothing in this position changes what activates the appeal period, what must be shown, who bears any burden, or what remedy is available. It applies the rule the sources already establish.

# CASE 2 of 10: MIRELES v. WACO

# SECTION I: NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

* **Case Name:** Mireles v. Waco
* **Citation or Docket:** 502 U.S. 9 (1991); No. 91-311
* **October Term:** 1991
* **Entry Stage and Event:** Petition for certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, presented for disposition on the papers. Petitioner seeks summary reversal.
* **Simulated Event Date:** October 21, 1991 (historical decision date)
* **Question Presented:** Whether a state judge who is alleged to have ordered police officers to seize an attorney and bring him into the courtroom forcibly and with excessive force is absolutely immune from a damages action under 42 U.S.C. § 1983, where the allegations are taken as true on a motion to dismiss.
* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** Ninth Circuit, Waco v. Baltad, 934 F.2d 214 (1991), reversing the district court's Rule 54(b) final judgment dismissing the claim against the judge on grounds of complete judicial immunity.
* **Relief Requested:** Reversal of the Ninth Circuit's judgment and reinstatement of the dismissal as to the judge.
* **Material Record:** Respondent Waco, a Los Angeles County public defender, sued a California Superior Court judge and two police officers under § 1983 for damages arising from an incident in November 1989 at the Van Nuys Superior Court building. The complaint alleges that after Waco failed to appear for the initial call of the judge's morning calendar, the judge, angered by the absence of attorneys, ordered the officers to seize Waco forcibly and with excessive force and bring him into the courtroom; that the officers seized him with unreasonable force and violence, removed him backwards from another courtroom where he was waiting to appear, cursed him and called him offensive names, and without necessity slammed him through the doors and gates into the judge's courtroom; and that the judge knowingly and deliberately approved and ratified each of those acts. General and punitive damages are demanded. Because the case arises on a Rule 12(b) motion, no facts are found; the allegations are assumed. Waco was called into the courtroom for purposes of a pending case.
* **Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** The judge moved under Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(b)(1) and 12(b)(6); the district court entered final judgment as to him under Rule 54(b), so the immunity ruling is separately reviewable. The Ninth Circuit decided only the judicial-capacity branch of the immunity inquiry and did not reach the complete-absence-of-jurisdiction branch. Whether the unsettled judicial-capacity boundary warrants summary disposition rather than briefing and argument is a separate procedural question.
* **Relevant Dates:** Incident November 1989. Ninth Circuit decision 1991. Petition disposed of October 21, 1991.
* **Companion or Related Matters:** Claims against the two officer defendants remain pending and are unaffected by the Rule 54(b) judgment.
* **Participation Matter:** Eight-Member Court. Justice Thomas was not yet seated.

## B. LAW ENTERING THE CASE

* **Current Through:** Historical United States law effective immediately before October 21, 1991.
* **Earlier Same-Term Decisions:** None supplied. Ibarra precedes this matter on the calendar; no material dependency is identified.
* **Unresolved Same-Term Dependencies:** None identified.
* **Institutional or Procedural Law:** Summary reversal is an exceptional disposition, conventionally reserved for cases in which the governing law is settled and stable, the facts are not in dispute, and the decision below is clearly in error.

### Issue 1: Whether the alleged conduct was taken in a judicial capacity

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** None.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** None yet operating in the simulation.
* **Other Governing Law:** 42 U.S.C. § 1983 (Rev. Stat. § 1979). Cal. Civ. Proc. Code §§ 128, 177, 187, conferring broad power on state judges over the conduct of proceedings, including power to control the conduct of ministerial officers and all other persons connected with a proceeding before the court; Ligda v. Superior Court, 5 Cal. App. 3d 811, 826 (1970), treating a public defender as such a person who may be ordered to appear.
* **Material Other Authority:** Bradley v. Fisher, 13 Wall. 335 (1872). Pierson v. Ray, 386 U.S. 547, 554 (1967) (immunity applies even where the judge is accused of acting maliciously and corruptly). Stump v. Sparkman, 435 U.S. 349, 356-357, 360, 362 (1978) (two-branch structure; a judge is not deprived of immunity because the action was in error or in excess of authority; whether an act is judicial turns on the nature of the act, whether it is a function normally performed by a judge, and on the parties' expectations). Forrester v. White, 484 U.S. 219, 227-229 (1988) (immunity attaches to judicial acts; a judicial act does not become less judicial by an allegation of malice or corrupt motive; it is the nature of the function performed, not the identity of the actor, that informs the analysis; administrative acts are not protected). Mitchell v. Forsyth, 472 U.S. 511, 520-526 (1985) (immunity from suit, not merely from damages; function-specific analysis). Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800, 815-819 (1982). Burns v. Reed, 500 U.S. 478, 492 (1991) (issuance of a search warrant is a judicial act). Supreme Court of Va. v. Consumers Union, 446 U.S. 719, 736-737 (1980) (no judicial immunity when acting in an enforcement capacity). Dennis v. Sparks, 449 U.S. 24 (1980); Cleavinger v. Saxner, 474 U.S. 193 (1985); Butz v. Economou, 438 U.S. 478 (1978). Limits on the immunity: Ex parte Virginia, 100 U.S. 339, 348-349 (1880) (criminal liability); Pulliam v. Allen, 466 U.S. 522, 536-544 (1984) (prospective injunctive relief and statutory attorney's fees).
* **Open Point:** No decision squarely addresses whether a single communication alleged to contain both a judicial command and a command to commit a battery is treated as one act or two for purposes of the judicial-capacity branch. That is the contested boundary.

### Issue 2: Whether the alleged conduct was taken in the complete absence of all jurisdiction

* **Other Governing Law and Authority:** Stump, 435 U.S. at 356-357; Bradley, 13 Wall. at 351.
* **Open Point:** Unreached below. Whether acting in excess of authority in aid of jurisdiction over a matter actually before the judge can amount to the complete absence of jurisdiction.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

Issue 1.

* **Controlling reasoning below:** The Ninth Circuit held the judge not immune because his alleged actions were not taken in his judicial capacity. It stated that directing officers merely to bring Waco to the courtroom would have been judicial, but that requesting and authorizing the use of excessive force would not.
* **Material alternative or unreached grounds:** The jurisdiction branch was not reached below.
* **Petitioner's strongest supported position:** Immunity is from suit, and allegations of malice or bad faith cannot defeat it, because such allegations can be tested only through discovery and trial, which is the burden the immunity exists to prevent. Stump forecloses stripping immunity for error or excess of authority, and the Ninth Circuit's rule does exactly that: if the particular act alone is examined, every unauthorized act becomes nonjudicial, since no improper act is normally performed by a judge. The relevant function here is directing court officers to bring counsel in a pending case before the court, which is a function normally performed by a judge and is expressly authorized by state law. The officers' role does not convert the direction into executive action, since a judge's direction to an executive officer to produce counsel is no more executive than issuing a search warrant.
* **Respondent's strongest supported position:** Immunity attaches only to judicial acts. Respondent's strongest argument treats the alleged communication as two commands: produce counsel, and inflict unnecessary force. On that reading the first is judicial and the second is operational conduct outside the adjudicative function; communicating them together does not enlarge the immunity. Consumers Union and Forrester make the inquiry function-specific rather than actor-specific. Respondent must therefore establish a functional distinction between the challenged directions, not merely allege that the judge performed a judicial function unlawfully.
* **Necessary concessions or limiting principles:** Petitioner must accept that a genuinely nonjudicial act is unprotected however closely it is connected in time to a judicial one. Respondent must accept that erroneous, malicious, and ultra vires judicial acts remain immune, and that only the command to use force is arguably severable.
* **Solicitor General or amicus position:** None material.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

* **Threshold paths:** Deny certiorari. Grant and set the case for briefing and argument. Grant and dispose summarily.
* **Merits paths:** (i) The relevant unit of analysis is the particular act's relation to a general judicial function, so the entire direction is judicial and immunity applies. (ii) The two commands are analytically separate; immunity covers the direction to produce counsel and not the direction to use excessive force, so the judgment below is affirmed or affirmed on a narrowed rationale. (iii) Remand for the Ninth Circuit to address the jurisdiction branch before any final immunity ruling. (iv) Resolve the jurisdiction branch in the first instance, holding that action in aid of jurisdiction over a pending matter is not action in the complete absence of jurisdiction.
* **Judgment consequences:** Path (i) reverses and restores the dismissal, ending the damages claim against the judge while leaving the officer claims alive. Path (ii) leaves the case pending against the judge on the severed allegation, with qualified immunity, causation, and the merits all unresolved. Path (iii) vacates and remands without resolving immunity.
* **Remedial or remand consequences:** No remedial doctrine is reached on any path. A ruling against immunity decides nothing about liability, damages, or the availability of any other defense.
* **Uncertainty:** There are no findings; everything turns on allegations assumed true. The complaint's phrasing bears more than one reading: it may allege a single instruction to produce counsel by force, or an instruction to produce plus a separate instruction to use excessive force, plus subsequent ratification. Whether summary disposition is appropriate depends on whether this unresolved functional boundary can properly be decided on the present papers.

# SECTION II: STONE POSITION SUPPLEMENT

## A. STATUS

* **Version:** 1.0
* **Approval Status:** Proposed and unapproved. Newly drafted; not represented as previously approved.
* **Current-Through Baseline:** Historical law effective immediately before October 21, 1991.
* **Conditions:** The disposition in B has two components, ordered. The second is reached only if the Court declines the first.

## B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

* **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** First, deny summary reversal. Stone would grant the petition and set the case for briefing and argument on the judicial-capacity boundary, because the governing law is not settled on the question the case actually turns on. Second, if the Court instead disposes of the matter on the present papers, Stone would affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals on a narrowed rationale and remand.
* **Stone's Remedy and Remand Position:** On either component, no remedial ruling issues. Affirmance on the narrowed rationale returns the case to the district court with the claim against the judge surviving only as to the alleged direction to use excessive force and its alleged ratification, with qualified immunity and every element of the claim open. The Rule 54(b) posture means nothing decided here touches the pending claims against the officers.

## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Whether the alleged conduct was taken in a judicial capacity

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the judicial-capacity branch because it is the ground of the decision below and the only branch the Court of Appeals decided.
* **Question Stone Resolves:** Narrowed to this: where a complaint alleges that a judge in one communication both directed officers to produce counsel and directed them to use excessive force in doing so, does absolute judicial immunity extend to the second direction because of its connection to the first.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply the received doctrine, and decide the open boundary it leaves. Stone does not propose changing Stump, Pierson, Forrester, or Mitchell. He identifies what they hold: absolute immunity is function-specific; it runs to the adjudicative function and not to the office; it is not defeated by error, excess of authority, malice, or corrupt motive; and it is immunity from suit. He then answers the question none of them answers.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Absolute judicial immunity attaches to a judge's direction to court officers to bring a person who is in the courthouse before the court, including where that direction is erroneous, unauthorized, or maliciously motivated. It does not attach to a separate direction to inflict unnecessary force on that person. Where a complaint alleges both, the second direction is analyzed on its own; it acquires no absolute immunity from its proximity to, or its stated purpose of executing, the first. Applied here, the Rule 54(b) dismissal of the entire claim against the judge was error, and the judgment reversing it is affirmed to that extent and no further.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Stone keeps legal consequences in the doctrines that produce them. The judicial character of the command to produce counsel is a conclusion about that command. Extending that protection to an alleged direction to inflict unnecessary force requires deciding whether the directions perform one function or two. Stump and Forrester supply the functional principles without resolving that precise boundary. Stone reads them to protect the adjudicative direction without extending absolute immunity to a separately alleged operational command merely because the same judge issued both. The competing approach treats the force direction as part of the general judicial function of producing counsel. Stone considers that approach insufficiently confined because it risks protecting independently nonjudicial conduct whenever the judge describes it as executing an order. That unresolved question about the scope of the immunity warrants briefing before summary reversal.
  The strongest form of the opposing position deserves statement. If courts examine each act in isolation, every ultra vires judicial act becomes nonjudicial, since nothing improper is normally performed by a judge, and the immunity collapses into a merits inquiry, which is what Stump forbids. Stone's answer is that the excess-of-authority principle protects the erroneous or unauthorized performance of an adjudicative function, not conduct falling outside any adjudicative function. Ordering counsel produced under a mistaken view of the court's power is the first. Ordering a battery is the second. The distinction is administrable because it asks what function the challenged direction performed, which is the question Forrester already requires.
  Stone also declines to treat the Ninth Circuit's rationale as correct as stated. That court framed the test as whether the judge requested and authorized excessive force, which risks turning on characterization of a single instruction. The narrowed rationale states the severance expressly and confines it.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** At the dismissal stage, Stone reads the complaint favorably to Waco as sufficiently alleging a direction to inflict unnecessary force distinct from the direction to produce counsel. Whether the judge actually gave such a direction, or whether the officers exceeded a direction confined to producing counsel, remains for further proceedings. He reserves the ratification allegation and the requirements for making that conduct actionable. He decides nothing about qualified immunity, causation, or the merits of the § 1983 claim, and failure of absolute immunity establishes neither a cause of action nor liability. Stone need not decide whether the alleged abuse satisfies Section 14: those commitments supply no exception to a separate absolute immunity, and his disposition rests on the function-specific boundary.

### Issue 2: Complete absence of all jurisdiction

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Not reached. The Court of Appeals did not decide it, no party has briefed it as an independent ground, and it is unnecessary on Stone's disposition, since the case survives on the judicial-capacity branch whether or not jurisdiction was wholly absent. Stone notes only that a direction issued in aid of the court's jurisdiction over a matter actually pending before it is a poor candidate for that branch, and he does not resolve it.
* **Approved Conditional Position or Fallback:** None exists. The ordered components in B are Stone's own positions, each of which he regards as independently lawful, not alternatives offered to attract votes.

# SECTION III: HISTORICAL COMPARATOR SUPPLEMENT

*Comparison material only. Not simulated law; does not control Stone.*

## A. MERITS COMPARATOR

* **Historical Outcome and Vote:** Certiorari granted and judgment of the Ninth Circuit summarily reversed. Per curiam for six Members; Justice Stevens dissenting; Justice Scalia dissenting, joined by Justice Kennedy. Eight Members participating.
* **Historical Opinion Structure:** Per curiam with two footnotes, the first collecting the recognized limits on judicial immunity and the second setting out the California statutory powers. Stevens dissent on the merits of the judicial-capacity question. Scalia dissent on the propriety of summary disposition, stating that the decision below was at a minimum not clearly in error, that he was unsure which disposition was correct, and that he would have denied the petition.
* **Historical Holding:** Judicial immunity is overcome only by nonjudicial actions or by actions taken in the complete absence of all jurisdiction, and not by allegations of bad faith or malice. A judge's direction to court officers to bring a person in the courthouse before him is a function normally performed by a judge. Although a direction to carry out a judicial order with excessive force is not itself such a function, the relevant inquiry is the particular act's relation to a general function normally performed by a judge, here directing officers to bring counsel in a pending case before the court. The officers' executive character does not convert the direction into executive action. Authorizing and ratifying excessive force was action in excess of authority but, taken in aid of jurisdiction over a pending matter, was not taken in the absence of jurisdiction.
* **Historical Authority:** Stump, 435 U.S. 349; Forrester, 484 U.S. 219; Pierson, 386 U.S. 547; Mitchell, 472 U.S. 511; Bradley, 13 Wall. 335; Burns v. Reed, 500 U.S. 478.
* **Historical Sources:** 502 U.S. 9; U.S. Reports volume 502.

### Issue 1: Whether Rule 39.8 authorizes denial of leave on the showing made

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** None.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** None yet operating in the simulation.
* **Other Governing Law:** Rule 39.8, providing that if satisfied that a petition for certiorari, jurisdictional statement, or petition for an extraordinary writ is frivolous or malicious, the Court may deny a motion for leave to proceed in forma pauperis. Rule 38 (fees); Rule 33 (document preparation); 28 U.S.C. § 1915.
* **Material Other Authority:** In re Amendment to Rule 39, 500 U.S. 13 (1991), stating the rule's purpose to discourage frivolous and malicious in forma pauperis filings, particularly from those few persons whose filings are repetitive with the obvious effect of burdening the office of the Clerk and other members of the Court's staff, and stating that it is vital that the right to file in forma pauperis not be encumbered by those who would abuse the integrity of the Court's process. In re Sindram, 498 U.S. 177 (1991) (per curiam), on the compromise of the Court's goal of fairly dispensing justice when limited resources are devoted to repetitious and frivolous requests. In re McDonald, 489 U.S. 180, 184 (1989) (per curiam). Talamini v. Allstate Insurance Co., 470 U.S. 1067, 1070 (1985) (Stevens, J., concurring), on the tradition of leaving the Court's door open to all classes of litigants.
* **Open Point:** Whether the rule's trigger is the character of the particular petition or the pattern of the petitioner's filings, and what if anything follows for petitions not individually assessed.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

* **Position supporting denial:** In forma pauperis petitioners lack the financial disincentives, filing fees and attorney's fees, that deter other litigants from frivolous filings, which is why the rule was adopted. These two petitioners are unique not merely among those seeking to file in forma pauperis but also among those who pay the fees, because they have repeatedly made totally frivolous demands on limited resources. Invoking the rule against them, while continuing to deny in the ordinary manner the many other frivolous in forma pauperis petitions to which the rule might technically apply, advances rather than undermines equal access, because abusive filing is what actually hinders the Court in providing access for all.
* **Position opposing denial:** Since the rule took effect, almost 1,000 in forma pauperis petitions have been denied in the ordinary manner without any frivolousness determination, and well over half could have been characterized as frivolous; the integrity of the process was not compromised in the slightest by declining to spend time enforcing the rule against them. Multiple filings have merely enabled the Court to single out these two petitioners. The practical effect of a Rule 39.8 denial is the same as a simple denial, since none of the petitions would be granted in any event, but the symbolic effect of drawing distinctions among frivolous petitions conveys that the Court lacks an overriding concern about equal access for rich and poor. Branding these petitioners increases the chance that a future petition containing a colorable claim will not receive the attention it deserves.

## B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

* **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** No. 90-913, reversed. No. 90-914, affirmed. The district court lacked jurisdiction to enjoin either administrative proceeding.
* **Stone's Remedy and Remand Position:** Both injunctions fall, together with the procedure the district court prescribed for the Board's future filings, which rests on the same absent jurisdiction. No remedial ruling issues. The regulation's validity and application remain reviewable under § 1818(h)(2) if and when the Board enters a final order, and Stone would say so in terms.

## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Effect of § 1818(i)(1)

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Reached first, because jurisdiction precedes everything.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply the enacted text. Stone does not propose changing anything.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Section 1818(i)(1) withdraws jurisdiction to affect by injunction the issuance or enforcement of a notice or order under the section, and it does so in terms Congress attached to the court's power rather than to claim processing. The district court therefore had no jurisdiction to enjoin the prosecution of either proceeding.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The provision states that no court shall have jurisdiction. That is a clear statement, and clear-statement presumptions, including the presumption against preclusion of review, do not defeat clear language. MCorp moved at the notice-of-charges stage, before any order existed, which is the moment the provision most plainly covers.

### Issue 2: The Bankruptcy Code

* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Section 1818(i)(1) is neither qualified nor superseded by 11 U.S.C. § 362. The Board's planned actions fall within § 362(b)(4), which excepts proceedings to enforce a governmental unit's police or regulatory power. Sections 362(a)(3) and (a)(6) do not apply to ongoing, nonfinal administrative proceedings. Section 1334(b) does not help, because the Board is not another court within the meaning of that provision, and because the prosecution of the proceedings, before any final order and before any enforcement action, is unlikely to impair the exclusive jurisdiction over property of the estate that § 1334(d) protects.
* **Essential Reasoning:** Statutes are read to coexist, and implied repeal requires irreconcilability. Nothing here is irreconcilable: the police and regulatory exception is the provision Congress wrote for precisely this collision, and reading the stay to reach a regulator's pending proceedings would make the exception do no work.

### Issue 3: Leedom v. Kyne

* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** The exception is unavailable. Kyne addressed agency action taken in excess of delegated power where preclusion would leave the affected party no means of protection. Here § 1818(h)(2) expressly supplies review of the regulation's validity and its application if and when the Board finds a violation, and § 1818(i)(1) states the preclusion of interim review clearly and directly. Where both conditions hold, a court has no jurisdiction to review and enjoin the ongoing proceedings.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Stone states the strongest contrary position and answers it. If a statute barred all review of a regulation the agency had no power to adopt, the right to contest it would survive in form while being unavailable in fact, and Stone does not treat a nominally available remedy as adequate merely because a statute names it. That is why the presence of § 1818(h)(2) is doing the work, not the bare existence of the bar. Court of appeals review after a final order is a real route: it reaches validity and application, it is available to this party, and it can set the order aside. The cost of waiting is the ordinary cost of defending an administrative proceeding, which is not the kind of harm that converts a real remedy into a nominal one.
  Stone adds a limit, and labels it as his own. He would not read today's holding to mean that § 1818(i)(1) forecloses review where the statutory route is in fact unavailable to the party, or where the agency's asserted authority is not merely contestable but absent on the face of the enabling text with no order in prospect against which review could run. Those cases are not this one, and he would leave them.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Stone expresses no view on the validity of the source of strength regulation, on whether a holding company's failure to transfer assets to a troubled subsidiary can be an unsafe or unsound practice under § 1818(b), or on the section 23A charge. A ruling that a court cannot act now says nothing about whether the Board is right, and Stone does not let the jurisdictional holding carry an implication about the merits it cannot support.
* **Approved Conditional Position or Fallback:** None exists.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

* **Controlling reasoning below:** The Seventh Circuit affirmed, reasoning that negating a verdict supported by evidence but possibly resting on an erroneous view of law differs from negating one on the remote chance that the jury convicted on a ground lacking adequate evidence when sufficient alternative grounds existed.
* **Material alternative or unreached grounds:** The Sixth Amendment theory and the supervisory-power theory.
* **Petitioner's strongest supported position:** Yates states that a verdict must be set aside where it is supportable on one ground but not another and it is impossible to tell which the jury selected, and that statement covers this case. Turner is distinguishable because assurance that the jury did not use the inadequate ground exists only where the prosecution presented no evidence at all on that theory; where some but insufficient evidence was offered, the impossible-to-tell rationale governs. The distinction between legal error and insufficiency is illusory in any event, since a judgment unsupported by the requisite minimum of proof is invalid as a matter of law and constitutionally required to be set aside.
* **Government's strongest supported position:** The common-law rule was settled in England before the Declaration of Independence and in this country long afterwards, so historical practice does not support the due process claim. Yates is the single decision extending Stromberg to a basis that violated no constitutional provision but was merely legally inadequate, and it was an unexplained extension. Turner, decided later, squarely holds that a general verdict stands if the evidence suffices as to any charged act, and a logical application of Turner requires that alternative facts in conspiracy cases be treated like alternative facts elsewhere. Petitioner's proposed distinction rewards the greater failure of proof and has no foundation in Turner or the presumption underlying it. Jurors are not equipped to detect that a theory is contrary to law, but they are well equipped to weigh evidence.
* **Necessary concessions or limiting principles:** The Government concedes the evidence was insufficient as to the DEA object. Continued adherence to Yates is not at issue; the Government asks only that it not be extended. Petitioner does not ask that Turner be overruled and offers no adequate basis for distinguishing it.
* **Solicitor General or amicus position:** The United States is respondent.

* **Threshold paths:** Decline to reach the Sixth Amendment theory as undeveloped. Reach it.
* **Merits paths:** (i) Affirm on the ground that Turner rather than Yates governs, the line between legal inadequacy and evidentiary insufficiency being the controlling distinction. (ii) Affirm on the narrower ground that the objects of a § 371 conspiracy are alternative means of committing a single offense, so no element went unproved. (iii) Reverse, extending Yates to evidentiary insufficiency. (iv) Exercise supervisory power to require removal of unsupported theories from the jury's consideration in federal trials, without a constitutional holding.
* **Judgment consequences:** Affirmance leaves the conviction and sentence intact. Reversal would vacate the conviction on Count 20 and require a new trial, and would carry consequences well beyond conspiracy counts, reaching for example an indictment charging murder by shooting or drowning where proof of drowning failed.
* **Remedial or remand consequences:** None on affirmance.
* **Uncertainty:** The risk of jury confusion in a 23-count, multiple-defendant, five to six week trial is a matter of judgment on which the record permits differing assessments. What Yates was grounded in is genuinely unclear from its own text.

## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Due process and the general verdict

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Reached. Stone would take the narrowest adequate ground first, which requires identifying what § 371 makes an element.
* **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether the Due Process Clause requires reversal of a general verdict on a single conspiracy count where the evidence sufficed as to one alleged object and not the other.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply the received doctrine unchanged, on a sequence that puts the enacted offense first. Stone proposes no change to Yates, Turner, or Stromberg, and he declines to extend Yates.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Section 371 punishes the unlawful agreement together with the required overt act, and the two objects alleged in this single count are alternative means of committing that conspiracy rather than separate elements each of which must be separately proved. Where the evidence suffices to establish the defendant's knowing participation in the charged conspiracy with at least one alleged unlawful object, and the remaining elements are satisfied, failure of proof as to another alleged object does not invalidate the general verdict. Applied here, the evidence connected petitioner to the IRS object, the sufficiency of the remaining elements is not challenged, and the conviction stands. Where, by contrast, one submitted basis of conviction is legally inadequate, whether because the Constitution forbids conviction on it or because it falls outside the statute, Yates and Stromberg govern when the general verdict does not disclose which basis the jury selected.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Stone begins with the statute because a constitutional claim about an unproved element cannot be assessed until the elements are identified. Section 371 punishes conspiring to commit any offense against the United States or to defraud any agency in any manner or for any purpose. The conjunctive pleading of two objects in one count is the ordinary and long-sanctioned method of charging alternative means, and Rule 7(c)(1) says so. Nothing in the enacted text makes each object an element.
  The constitutional question then answers itself on the received law. The prosecution must prove every element beyond a reasonable doubt, and it did. The distinction between legal inadequacy and evidentiary insufficiency supported by Yates and Turner also holds independently, and Stone adopts it as the further ground: jurors cannot be expected to detect that a submitted theory is contrary to law, because that is not a question their competence reaches, whereas weighing evidence is the whole of what a jury is for. That asymmetry, not a preference for finality, is why Yates and Turner sit where they do.
  Stone declines to make the Government's historical-practice argument dispositive. Historical practice is evidence of what due process meant and of the principle the guarantee protects; it is not a veto, and a long line of citations for an unexamined practice does not by itself establish a constitutional rule. Stone therefore treats the common-law materials as confirming what the statutory analysis and the jury-competence distinction independently establish, rather than as the load-bearing ground. He would also not characterize the dispute between legal error and insufficiency as merely semantic: the two are distinguishable because they call on different institutional capacities, which is a substantive reason and the one worth stating.
  On the record, Stone notes the danger of jury confusion in a 23-count, multiple-defendant trial lasting five to six weeks. It is real, and it is why the better practice is to remove an unsupported theory from the jury's consideration, and why charging the objects in separate counts or agreeing to special interrogatories would have avoided the question entirely. Stone commends those practices without converting any of them into a requirement. He invents no rule that a trial court must give such an instruction, because no source supplies one, and the refusal to give it is not an independent ground for reversing an otherwise valid conviction.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Stone expressly reserves the Sixth Amendment theory. It appears in the petition but was not developed in the briefing or argument, and the alleged defect is that a jury determination was permitted rather than withheld, which does not present the jury-trial question in a form the Court can decide. The due process conclusion does not resolve the reserved Sixth Amendment theory or determine whether jurors must agree on an object. He also reserves whether unanimity as to the object could be required by statute or rule. He declines to exercise supervisory power, because the practice he would commend does not require compulsion to be adopted and a supervisory rule announced here would extend well beyond conspiracy counts without the benefit of argument about those consequences.
* **Approved Conditional Position or Fallback:** None exists.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

* **Controlling reasoning below:** The Ninth Circuit concluded the trial was arbitrary and fundamentally unfair in violation of due process. It held the prior injury evidence erroneously admitted to establish battered child syndrome because no evidence linked respondent to the prior injuries and no claim of accident was made at trial, and held that the prior act instruction allowed a finding of guilt based simply on a judgment that respondent had committed the prior acts.
* **Material alternative or unreached grounds:** Harmlessness was not addressed below because relief was granted outright.
* **Petitioner's strongest supported position:** The battered child evidence was probative on the intent with which the person who injured the child acted, because it showed the death resulted from an intentional act by someone rather than an accident, and the absence of a defense claim of accident did not relieve the prosecution of proving every element beyond a reasonable doubt. A federal habeas court may not reexamine state-law evidentiary determinations, and the Ninth Circuit's reliance on state-law error was itself improper. The instruction's own words left to the jury whether respondent committed the prior acts and permitted use of the evidence only if the jury so believed, and the court expressly forbade use of the evidence to show bad character or disposition. Circumstantial evidence permitted identification of respondent as the prior abuser, even without direct evidence, because he and his wife were the child's only regular caregivers.
* **Respondent's strongest supported position:** The instruction told the jury that the prior injury evidence had been admitted to show similar offenses and that on finding a clear connection it could logically conclude that if respondent committed the other offenses he also committed the charged crime. So framed, it encouraged the jury to assume he inflicted the prior injuries and then directed it to conclude that the prior abuser was the murderer, which may have relieved the State of proving identity beyond a reasonable doubt. The relevance of battered child syndrome to nonaccidental injury does not establish the abuser's identity. Even if circumstantial evidence could support identifying respondent, the jury still had to determine that predicate and decide whether to draw the further inference; the charge could not treat those determinations as already made. His wife's testimony claiming responsibility for the fatal injuries makes the disputed identity question material.
* **Necessary concessions or limiting principles:** Petitioner must accept that a state instruction which relieves the prosecution of an element presents a federal question cognizable on habeas. Respondent must accept that the evidence itself was relevant and properly admitted, that state-law error is not a ground for federal habeas relief, and that the limiting instruction forbidding character and disposition use cuts against the propensity reading.

* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply received doctrine. Under Boyde, McGuire bears the burden of establishing a reasonable likelihood of constitutionally impermissible application of the ambiguous instruction in the context of the charge and trial as a whole. A mere possibility does not suffice; he need not prove that misapplication was more likely than not. Stone proposes no change to Boyde, Winship, Allen, Sandstrom, or Francis, and he does not reach the question whether a state rule authorizing propensity use of prior crimes would itself violate due process.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** An instruction telling the jury that evidence was admitted to show similar offenses committed by the defendant, and that upon finding a clear connection between them it may be logically concluded that if he committed the other offenses he also committed the charged crime, creates a reasonable likelihood in the context of this charge and record that the jury treated his commission of the earlier injuries as already established and the further identity conclusion as required. McGuire has met his burden of showing that reasonable likelihood. So understood, the instruction relieves the State of proving identity beyond a reasonable doubt. The instruction was therefore constitutional error, and the case is remanded for a determination whether the State has proved the error harmless under Chapman and Rose.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Stone's route to this conclusion runs through the element and the burden, not through a general sense of unfairness. The State had to prove that this defendant killed the child. The syndrome evidence supported a finding that the fatal injuries were inflicted intentionally by someone. Circumstantial evidence could also support a finding that respondent inflicted the earlier injuries, including the evidence that he and his wife were the only regular caregivers. A permissible inference may help prove an element; its use is not unconstitutional merely because it supplies part of the prosecution's proof. The defect here concerns who determined the predicate and whether the further inference remained permissive. The instruction grouped respondent's identified rough handling with the unattributed rib fractures and rectal tearing, described the evidence as showing similar acts he committed, and then linked a clear connection to a logical conclusion of guilt. Read together, those directions created a reasonable likelihood that jurors understood the prior-abuser predicate as settled and the identity conclusion as prescribed, rather than making the factual determinations while holding the State to its burden.

The phrase conditioning the inference on whether the defendant committed the other offenses supports a reading that leaves that question to the jury, and the separate instruction forbidding bad-character or disposition use is a genuine safeguard against propensity reasoning. Stone accepts the force of both points. He nonetheless concludes that the conditional phrase did not adequately correct the instruction's opening attribution of the similar acts to respondent or clarify that the proposed identity conclusion remained for the jury to accept or reject. The anti-propensity direction addressed character reasoning without resolving those ambiguities. The wife's testimony claiming responsibility for the fatal injuries and the three days of deliberation provide context for the disputed identity question, without establishing what any juror actually believed. Winship, Sandstrom, Francis, and Allen require the jury to make the necessary factual determinations under instructions preserving the prosecution's burden. Stone concludes that this charge did not preserve that division of responsibility.

* **Case Name:** Wooddell v. International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers, Local 71
* **Citation or Docket:** 502 U.S. 93 (1991); No. 90-967
* **October Term:** 1991
* **Entry Stage and Event:** Certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit, granted to address two questions; argued October 16, 1991; submitted for decision.
* **Simulated Event Date:** December 4, 1991 (historical decision date)
* **Question Presented:** Whether a union member who sues his local union for money damages under Title I of the Labor-Management Reporting and Disclosure Act is entitled to a jury trial; and whether the subject-matter jurisdiction conferred by § 301(a) of the Labor-Management Relations Act extends to a suit by an individual union member alleging violation of a union constitution.
* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** Sixth Circuit, judgment order reported at 907 F.2d 151 (1990), reversing the dismissal of the LMRDA free speech and job discrimination claim but otherwise affirming, including the holding that there was no right to a jury trial on the LMRDA claim, and holding on the basis of prior circuit precedent that § 301 did not authorize a breach-of-contract action by an individual union member for violation of a union constitution.
* **Relief Requested:** By petitioner, reversal on both questions and remand. By respondents, affirmance.
* **Material Record:** Petitioner is a member of Local 71 of the International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers. Following a dispute arising from his opposition to an announced dues increase and to the appointment of a union representative, the local's president, who is petitioner's brother, filed internal disciplinary proceedings against him; no decision was finally rendered on the charges. Petitioner alleges that the union then discriminated against him in job referrals in the operation of a hiring hall provided for in the local's collective-bargaining contracts with electrical contractors. His complaint alleged violation of LMRDA-protected rights through discrimination in referrals because of his opposition to proposed union policy, violation of his LMRDA right to a fair hearing, violations of the IBEW Constitution and the local's bylaws alleged to be breaches of contract redressable under § 301 and state law, breach of the duty of fair representation, and pendent state claims for interference with contractual relations and intentional infliction of emotional distress. He sought injunctive relief, lost wages and benefits, additional compensatory damages, punitive damages, and attorney's fees. The amended complaint alleged that the IBEW Constitution requires all local unions to live up to all collective bargaining agreements, and that the Constitution and the local's bylaws are contracts binding on Local 71; in its amended answer the local admitted those allegations. The record sets out selected provisions of the IBEW Constitution, including rules for local unions requiring locals to honor their contracts and requiring IBEW approval of local bylaws and agreements; the local's charter, constitution if any, and relevant bylaws are not in the record. That the IBEW and Local 71 are labor organizations representing employees in an industry affecting commerce is undisputed.
* **Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** The § 301 question as presented is one of subject-matter jurisdiction; petitioner's personal standing is not disputed before the Court. The case was briefed and argued below before Teamsters v. Terry was decided, and the decision below neither cites nor discusses it. There is a circuit conflict on the § 301 question.
* **Relevant Dates:** Internal disciplinary proceedings and referral discrimination alleged; district court dismissal of all claims against all defendants in the course of acting on two summary judgment motions; Sixth Circuit judgment order 1990; Teamsters v. Terry decided March 20, 1990, before the decision below; certiorari granted at 498 U.S. 1082 (1991); argued October 16, 1991; decided December 4, 1991.
* **Companion or Related Matters:** None material.
* **Participation Matter:** Justice Thomas took no part. Eight Members participating.

### Issue 2: Section 301(a) jurisdiction over an individual member's suit on a union constitution

* **Other Governing Law:** LMRA § 301(a), 29 U.S.C. § 185(a), providing that suits for violation of contracts between an employer and a labor organization representing employees in an industry affecting commerce, or between any such labor organizations, may be brought in any district court of the United States having jurisdiction of the parties, without respect to the amount in controversy or the citizenship of the parties.
* **Material Other Authority:** Smith v. Evening News Assn., 371 U.S. 195, 198, 200-201 (1962) (the word between refers to contracts rather than suits; § 301 suits are not limited to the contracting parties, and an individual employee may sue under § 301 for violation of an employer-union contract; individual claims lie at the heart of the grievance and arbitration machinery, and a contrary holding would allow individual contract terms to bear different meanings under state and federal law, exerting a disruptive influence on the negotiation and administration of collective agreements). Plumbers and Pipefitters v. Plumbers and Pipefitters, Local 334, 452 U.S. 615, 624 (1981) (union constitutions were at the time of enactment, and remain, probably the most commonplace form of contract between labor organizations, so Congress would not likely have used the unqualified term contract without intending to encompass them; § 301 was enacted to supply federal jurisdiction for enforcement of contracts made by labor organizations and to counteract jurisdictional defects in state courts). Teamsters v. Lucas Flour Co., 369 U.S. 95, 103 (1962).
* **Open Point:** Whether the merits of the alleged breaches state a claim, and whether the individual officer defendants are proper defendants, are not presented.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

* **Controlling reasoning below:** On the jury trial question the Sixth Circuit affirmed the denial without citing or discussing Terry. On the § 301 question it relied on prior circuit precedent holding that § 301 does not authorize such an action by an individual union member.
* **Material alternative or unreached grounds:** The merits of the contract claims; the duty of fair representation claim; the pendent state claims.
* **Petitioner's strongest supported position:** On the jury right, although he seeks injunctive relief as well as damages, the injunctive relief is incidental to the damages rather than the reverse, and his claim for lost wages cannot be treated as restitutionary incident to reinstatement, because the damages sought are pay for jobs to which the union failed to refer him rather than pay for a job from which he was terminated; and LMRDA actions are closely analogous to personal injury actions, the prototypical action at law. On § 301, the IBEW Constitution is a contract between labor organizations; his complaint alleges that it requires locals to honor collective bargaining agreements and that it and the bylaws bind the local, allegations the local admitted; and § 301 is not limited to suits by parties to the interunion contract but extends to individual members who are its beneficiaries.
* **Respondents' strongest supported position:** On § 301, jurisdiction reaches only suits by the parties to the interunion contract, and third-party suits to enforce it lie outside the grant; construing the section to reach member suits is an unwarranted intrusion on state contract law, since the federalization of union-member relations should be confined to the provisions Congress enacted in the later LMRDA; and the construction will inundate the federal courts with trivial intraunion litigation. On the jury trial question, respondents now concede that Terry controls.
* **Necessary concessions or limiting principles:** Respondents concede Terry's application. Petitioner must accept that the answer on § 301 is affirmative only where the breach alleged violates a contract between two labor organizations, and that personal standing and the merits are separate questions. Respondents' intrusion and floodgates arguments have no supporting evidence, the construction having been the law in several circuits for years, including a decision a decade earlier involving this same union constitution.
* **Solicitor General or amicus position:** The Association for Union Democracy and others filed as amici urging reversal. No government position.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

* **Threshold paths:** Decide both questions. Decide the jury question and remand the § 301 question. Dismiss the § 301 question as not properly presented.
* **Merits paths:** (i) A jury trial right attaches to the LMRDA damages claim, and § 301(a) jurisdiction extends to an individual member's suit alleging violation of a contract between labor organizations; reverse and remand on both. (ii) A jury right attaches but § 301 reaches only suits by contracting parties. (iii) Neither.
* **Judgment consequences:** Path (i) reverses and remands with the LMRDA claim triable to a jury and the § 301 claim within federal jurisdiction, leaving every merits question open. Path (ii) or (iii) leaves the § 301 claim to state court and state law.
* **Remedial or remand consequences:** No remedy is reached. Nothing decided establishes breach, entitlement, or damages, or that the officer defendants may be held liable.
* **Uncertainty:** The local's charter, constitution if any, and relevant bylaws are not in the record, so the contractual structure is known only in part. The merits of the referral discrimination allegations are untested.

## B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

* **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Reverse the judgment of the Court of Appeals and remand.
* **Stone's Remedy and Remand Position:** The case returns for further proceedings, with the LMRDA damages claim triable to a jury and the § 301 claim within the district court's subject-matter jurisdiction. No remedy is reached and no view is expressed on the merits of any claim.

## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Jury trial right on the LMRDA claim

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Reached; it is the first question presented and is independent of the second.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply Terry unchanged. The controlling decision was available to the court below and was neither cited nor discussed there.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** A union member suing his local for money damages under Title I of the LMRDA is entitled to a jury trial. The remedy sought is legal: the damages are neither analogous to equitable restitution nor incidental to or intertwined with injunctive relief, the claimed lost wages being pay for jobs to which the union failed to refer him rather than a restitutionary incident of reinstatement to a job from which he was terminated, and the injunctive relief sought is incidental to the damages. An LMRDA action is also closely analogous to a personal injury action, the prototypical action at law.
* **Essential Reasoning:** The two-part inquiry asks what the action resembles historically and whether the remedy is legal or equitable, with the second the more important. Both point one way here, and respondents now concede the point.

### Issue 2: Section 301(a) jurisdiction

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Reached. It is jurisdictional and was the ground of dismissal.
* **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether the jurisdiction conferred by § 301(a) extends to a suit by an individual union member alleging violation of a union constitution, where the breach alleged is of a contract between two labor organizations.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply the enacted text as construed in Smith v. Evening News and Plumbers and Pipefitters. Stone proposes no change.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** The subject-matter jurisdiction conferred by § 301(a) extends to a suit on a union constitution brought by an individual union member, where the member alleges violation of a contract between labor organizations and is a beneficiary of that contract. Applied here, petitioner alleged that the IBEW Constitution requires locals to honor collective bargaining agreements and that the Constitution and bylaws bind Local 71, allegations the local admitted, and that the defendants breached those contracts by discriminating against him in hiring-hall referrals. That is an allegation of violation of a contract between two labor organizations, and the courts below erred in holding federal jurisdiction unavailable.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The statute's word between attaches to contracts, not to suits, so the jurisdictional grant turns on the character of the contract and not on the identity of the plaintiff. Union constitutions were the most commonplace form of contract between labor organizations when the section was enacted and remain so, and Congress would not have used the unqualified term contract meaning to exclude them. Smith v. Evening News establishes that § 301 suits are not confined to contracting parties, and the reason it gives applies directly here: if a member must go to state court and state law to enforce an interunion contract that the international itself could enforce in federal court under federal law, the same contract terms would bear different meanings according to who sued, which is the disruptive influence the section was written to prevent.
  Stone takes respondents' two objections in turn. The intrusion argument asks the Court to read a later statute as narrowing an earlier one; but if § 301, fairly construed and apart from the LMRDA, covers this suit, the later statute changes that only if something in it shows Congress meant to narrow the section, and nothing does. Silence is neither repeal nor approval, and a partial implied repeal requires more than the observation that Congress has legislated nearby. The floodgates argument is a prediction, and it is one the record contradicts: several circuits have followed this construction for years, one of them a decade earlier in a case involving this very union constitution, and respondents identify no resulting flood. Stone would not let an interpretation of enacted language turn on a forecast in any event.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Stone reaches no question about the merits of the contract claims. He notes that the record omits the local's charter, constitution if any, and bylaws, which may matter on remand. Personal standing is not disputed and is not decided. Jurisdiction is not a cause of action, and the holding establishes neither that any defendant breached any obligation nor that the individual officers are proper defendants nor that any particular remedy is available. The duty of fair representation claim and the pendent state claims are untouched.
* **Approved Conditional Position or Fallback:** None exists.

## A. MERITS COMPARATOR

* **Historical Outcome and Vote:** Reversed and remanded. Unanimous among the eight participating Members; Justice Thomas took no part.
* **Historical Opinion Structure:** Single opinion of the Court by Justice White, joined by all other participating Members. No concurrence or dissent.
* **Historical Holding:** Petitioner was entitled to a jury trial on the LMRDA cause of action. Although he sought injunctive relief as well as damages, the injunctive relief was assertedly incidental to the damages; his claim for lost wages could not be treated as restitutionary incident to reinstatement because the damages sought were pay for jobs to which the union failed to refer him; and an LMRDA action is closely analogous to a personal injury action, a prototypical action at law, so Teamsters v. Terry controls. The subject-matter jurisdiction conferred by § 301(a) extends to suits on union constitutions brought by individual union members, since union constitutions are an important form of contract between labor organizations and petitioner alleged that the IBEW Constitution requires locals to live up to collective bargaining agreements, that the constitution and bylaws bind the local, and that the defendants breached them by discriminating against him in referrals. Section 301 is not limited to suits brought by a party to an interunion contract but extends to individual members who are its beneficiaries, because otherwise identical contract terms might bear different meanings under state and federal law, exerting a disruptive influence on the negotiation and administration of interunion contracts. The contention that this construction is an unwarranted intrusion on state contract law was rejected for want of any indication in the later LMRDA that Congress meant to narrow § 301, and the prediction that federal courts would be inundated with trivial intraunion suits was rejected as unsupported. The Court expressed no view on the merits of the breach-of-contract claims.
* **Historical Authority:** Teamsters v. Terry, 494 U.S. 558; Reed v. United Transportation Union, 488 U.S. 319; Plumbers and Pipefitters, Local 334, 452 U.S. 615; Smith v. Evening News Assn., 371 U.S. 195.
