## Decisions and Dispositions

### Holmes v. Securities Investor Protection Corp. — Correction to chunk 6, No. 90-727

Merits decision, March 24, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 13, 1991. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, *Securities Investor Protection Corp. v. Vigman*, 908 F.2d 1461 (August 3, 1990). This correction supplies the completed entry omitted from chunk 6. The decision belongs to the March 24 group with R.L.C., Darden and Wilson; no order within that day is established.

The granted question is whether the Securities Investor Protection Corporation (SIPC) may pursue an express civil claim under the Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act (RICO) despite the purchaser-seller restriction applicable to its implied securities claim. Proximate cause—the required legal connection between the violation and injury—is fairly included in that question and was briefed. The Court also addresses SIPC's asserted independent right under §78eee(d) of the Securities Investor Protection Act (SIPA) to recover its own liquidation-administration advances. Responsibility for coconspirators' conduct falls outside the limited grant and is assumed without decision.

SIPC and the liquidation trustees of First State Securities Corporation and Joseph Sebag, Inc. alleged a stock-manipulation conspiracy. Losses on the brokers' proprietary holdings allegedly caused insolvency and inability to satisfy customer claims, leading SIPC to advance nearly $13 million for customers and separately to fund liquidation administration. SIPC asserts subrogation—the right to pursue another person's claim—to customers who did not purchase the manipulated securities, as well as its own right under SIPA. Fraud and defendants' causal contribution remain disputed; no findings establish liability.

The district court entered an immediately appealable judgment under Rule 54(b) for Holmes, relying on purchaser-seller status and an analysis isolating his conduct. The Ninth Circuit reversed, rejecting the categorical restriction for RICO and considering the conspiracy as a whole. Holmes challenges SIPC's permitted recovery; the trustees' standing is not challenged. The separate, unappealed Rule 10b-5 judgment is not reopened. The record and proceedings are documented in the [reported proceedings, 503 U.S. 258](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-503/pdf/USREPORTS-503-258.pdf), the [Ninth Circuit opinion](https://static.case.law/f2d/908/html/1461-01.html), and the [argument transcript](https://www.supremecourt.gov/pdfs/transcripts/1991/90-727_11-13-1991.pdf).

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses the Ninth Circuit's judgment insofar as it permits the two asserted SIPC recovery routes and remands with directions to enter judgment for Holmes on those claims, 9–0.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Civil RICO requires a sufficiently direct causal relation

**Controlling proposition:** A plaintiff seeking damages under 18 U.S.C. §1964(c) must establish both factual causation and proximate cause, including some direct relation between the injury asserted and the alleged §1962 violation. Injury that merely follows harm to another person is insufficient on the causal chain presented here; directness is a contextual requirement informed by attribution, overlapping recovery and the availability of directly injured plaintiffs, not a mechanical rule resolving every third-party injury.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part II, joined by Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas; eight Justices adopt this rule. Scalia agrees that proximate cause is required but joins no part of the Court's opinion; his distinct reasoning is not added to the opinion's support.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that factual connection alone does not establish the right to recover under civil RICO. Congress used the injury and causation language of the Clayton Act. Agency Holding and Sedima identify that statutory relationship; Associated General Contractors explains the established causal limits attached to the borrowed language. Congress's adoption of those words carries their settled judicial meaning. A direct relation matters because distant injury makes damages harder to attribute to the violation, risks overlapping recoveries at different levels, and adds claims when immediately injured persons can enforce the law themselves. These considerations explain the inherited requirement; they are not newly created elements that replace the statute. SIPC's advance obligation and the predictability of customer losses therefore cannot alone establish recovery. Sedima's rejection of a special racketeering injury remains intact, and no antitrust-injury requirement enters RICO. The Court does not announce a formula for every causal chain or decide whether a securities purchaser-seller restriction independently limits this action.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Associated General Contractors of California, Inc. v. Carpenters, 459 U.S. 519 (1983) — its account of common-law causal limits in the borrowed Clayton Act language is extended to §1964(c); its antitrust-specific injury requirement is not imported.
- Agency Holding Corp. v. Malley-Duff & Associates, Inc., 483 U.S. 143 (1987) — relied on for the close statutory relationship between civil RICO and the Clayton Act; its limitations holding remains unchanged.
- Sedima, S.P.R.L. v. Imrex Co., 473 U.S. 479 (1985) — preserved: no special racketeering injury or prior conviction is required; the enacted causal limitation still must be satisfied.
- Cannon v. University of Chicago, 441 U.S. 677 (1979) — applied for Congress's presumed awareness of the settled interpretation of borrowed language; no new implied action is recognized.

##### The nonpurchasing customers' derivative losses are too remote

**Controlling proposition:** Even assuming SIPC may be subrogated to the nonpurchasing customers' rights against Holmes, it cannot recover under §1964(c) on the asserted stock-manipulation theory because their unpaid claims depend on the broker-dealers' prior losses and intervening insolvency. The trustees' distinct claims remain undisturbed, and SIPC may share in any estate recovery according to the priority SIPA lawfully gives its claim.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part III-A, joined by Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas; eight Justices adopt the application and its limits. The validity and source of the asserted common-law subrogation are assumed, not established.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that replacing the customers with their asserted subrogee cannot make the customers' injury more direct. The alleged manipulation first reduced the brokers' assets; those losses then left the brokers unable to satisfy the nonpurchasing customers' claims. Associated General Contractors supplies the reasons this intervening injury matters. Calculating customer damages would require separating the manipulation's effects from other causes of broker failure and apportioning recovery between the brokers and their creditors. Blue Shield v. McCready and Hawaii v. Standard Oil identify the concern about overlapping damages, while Southern Pacific v. Darnell-Taenzer describes the ordinary preference for recovery at the immediate level of injury. The trustees already represent the brokers' direct claims, so denying these derivative claims does not leave that injury without a plaintiff. SIPC's statutory obligations and RICO's remedial purpose do not justify bypassing that causal limit or liquidation priorities. The Court assumes responsibility for coconspirators' conduct without deciding it and leaves separately grounded parking, purchasing-customer and directly injured claims outside this ruling.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Associated General Contractors, 459 U.S. 519 — applied to the derivative creditor-loss chain, attribution difficulties, overlapping recovery and available directly injured plaintiffs.
- Blue Shield of Virginia v. McCready, 457 U.S. 465 (1982) — relied on for causal limits and avoidance of overlapping damages; its recognition of a directly injured claimant's action is not displaced.
- Hawaii v. Standard Oil Co. of California, 405 U.S. 251 (1972) — applied for the concern about duplicate recovery; the Court does not decide a State's right to sue on behalf of its citizens.
- Southern Pacific Co. v. Darnell-Taenzer Lumber Co., 245 U.S. 531 (1918) — applied as a general direct-injury principle, without converting its first-step formulation into an exceptionless rule.

##### SIPA participation rights do not create damages liability

**Controlling proposition:** Section 78eee(d) of SIPA gives SIPC party-in-interest status, a right to be heard and deemed intervention in liquidation matters; it does not itself create an independent right to recover administration advances as damages from Holmes. Neither that participation provision alone nor its combination with §1964(c) supplies a missing substantive recovery right or dispenses with RICO's own conditions.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part III-B, joined by Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas; eight Justices adopt this independently sufficient statutory ground for rejecting SIPC's own §78eee(d) claim. This is distinct from the customer-subrogation causation holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a right to participate in a proceeding does not itself establish a right to collect damages from another party. Section 78eee(d) identifies SIPC's status in liquidation matters and gives it the same opportunity to be heard that an allowed intervention would provide. It says nothing about conduct making Holmes liable or conditions for an affirmative monetary recovery against him. Section 78fff-3(b)(2) requires SIPC to fund administration when the general estate is insufficient, but that obligation does not turn participation language into a reimbursement action. Adding §1964(c) does not cure the problem: that statute retains its own requirements for recovery. The Court rejects only the asserted statutory source of liability and does not decide every claim SIPC might possess under other law. Its statutory participation remains effective even though this particular damages theory fails. The distinct suggestion of subrogation to trustees for expense advances, raised only at argument and not developed in the merits brief, is not decided on its merits.

**Precedent treatment:** No earlier decision is independently construed, extended or overruled by this provision-specific holding. Its participation/recovery distinction is consistent with the existing action/remedy distinction; it does not announce a general rule foreclosing implied or subrogated actions under other law.

#### Limits and questions not reached

The Court does not decide whether all civil RICO plaintiffs alleging securities fraud as a predicate must have purchased or sold securities. Part IV's decision to leave that question open has five joins: Souter, Stone, Blackmun, Kennedy and Thomas. White, Stevens and O'Connor withhold that join; Scalia joins no Court opinion. Their separate rejection of a purchaser-seller restriction does not create a Court holding on that question.

No fraud, conspiracy participation, actual causal contribution or damages amount is found. Responsibility for coconspirators' acts is assumed because that question was outside the limited grant. The Court neither validates the source of customer subrogation nor decides claims of purchasing customers, the parking allegations not pursued as a separate recovery theory in this Court, a claim of directly aimed injury to SIPC, or the merits of the unbriefed expense-advance subrogation theory. The unappealed separate Rule 10b-5 summary judgment is not reopened. No broad prohibition on foreseeable third-party injury, new burden of proof, general zone-of-interests rule, or automatic transfer of trustees' claims is adopted.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Effective March 24, 1992, civil RICO damages require factual and proximate causation, including the direct relation described above. SIPC's asserted nonpurchasing-customer loss chain fails that requirement. Section 78eee(d)'s participation rights do not create an independent damages action for administration advances or remove RICO's requirements. The rule does not determine generic tort causation, injury sufficient for Article III jurisdiction, securities purchaser-seller limits, trustee capacity, all equitable subrogation, or a damages amount. Ordinary proof rules, statutory advance duties and lawful liquidation priorities remain.

The precedent treatment accompanying each holding supplies its effect on earlier law. No precedent is overruled. *Blue Chip Stamps* remains the rule for the implied §10(b)/Rule 10b-5 action; its proposed extension to RICO remains undecided. The SIPA holding does not extend *Barbour*'s distinct customer-versus-SIPC holding or announce a general rule about implied actions. The three eight-Justice holdings control on their own authority. The separate opinions establish no additional Court holding on SIPC's own RICO advances or the reserved purchaser-seller question.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

O'Connor, joined by White and Stevens, agrees with the causal and SIPA grounds but would answer the purchaser-seller question. Section 1964(c) creates an express action for a person injured in business or property by reason of a RICO violation. *Blue Chip Stamps*, which shaped an implied securities action, does not in their view add a purchaser-seller requirement to that enacted right. Their view does not remove the need to prove proximate cause, establish every alleged predicate violation or resolve the fraud merits. It is noncontrolling.

Scalia concurs only in the judgment. He treats proximate cause as an established background limit on civil recovery and agrees that the asserted relationship is insufficient. He also would reject the purchaser-seller requirement here because the policies used to define an implied securities action do not alone justify that categorical restriction under RICO's text. His account of statutory scope is separate from the Court's explanation based on the borrowed antitrust language; it does not resolve the reserved question for the Court.

Stone concurs while joining the Court. He adds that, insofar as SIPC seeks its own advances under §1964(c), its expenditure remains dependent on the broker-dealers' intervening losses and therefore fails the same proximate-cause requirement. This additional ground addresses the alternative characterization of SIPC's own claim. It is not a causal test for the independent SIPA provision and does not bar distinct trustee claims or lawful recovery through liquidation estates. No other Justice joins it, and it creates no additional controlling holding.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court review ends. The Ninth Circuit must give effect to judgment for Holmes on the two SIPC claims and conduct further proceedings consistent with the preserved claims. Trustees' distinct claims continue on their own footing. If the trustees recover, SIPC may share only according to lawful estate priorities; the Court predicts no recovery or distribution.

The remand does not reinstate the unappealed securities theory or adjudicate the unbriefed expense-subrogation theory, the parking allegations not pursued as a separate recovery theory in this Court, or an independently grounded direct-injury claim. The Court orders no payment to SIPC or a trustee, new damages procedure, hearing, retained supervision or further Supreme Court action. No award amount, finding of liability or mandate-issuance date is established.

### United States v. Felix, No. 90-1599

Merits decision, March 25, 1992

Argued January 14, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the Tenth Circuit under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1); the grant preceded argument. The question is whether evidentiary overlap bars prosecution of the distinct Oklahoma substantive offenses or the conspiracy whose acts include conduct involved in the Missouri prosecution. Review concerns the reversal of conspiracy count 1 and substantive counts 2–6.

The Missouri prosecution concerned attempted manufacture during August 26–31, 1987. Evidence of the earlier Oklahoma manufacturing operation was admitted to establish intent; that evidentiary use did not enlarge the charged Missouri offense. The later Oklahoma prosecution concerned the earlier completed acts and a broader agreement. The Tenth Circuit reversed counts 1–6 under Grady v. Corbin, applying its double-jeopardy rule to the overlapping conduct, but affirmed the travel convictions, counts 9–10, with sentencing proceedings. No prior acquittal or issue-preclusion finding controls this record.

The Court reverses the judgment as to substantive counts 2–6 and conspiracy count 1, each by a vote of 9–0. Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas support reversal on each component. The Double Jeopardy Clause supplies no bar to either component. The prosecution’s judgments are restored against that objection, subject to preserved unresolved appellate issues; the separate disposition of counts 9–10 is not disturbed.

White delivers the Opinion of the Court. Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas join Part I, addressing the substantive offenses. Stone, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas join Part II, addressing the distinct conspiracy. Stevens, joined by Blackmun, concurs in part and in the judgment and joins Part I only.

#### Reused evidence and separate substantive offenses

The use of evidence of an uncharged crime in one trial does not itself place the defendant in jeopardy for that crime. The later Oklahoma substantive charges concern different acts from the Missouri attempt and are not barred merely because evidence of those acts helped prove intent in Missouri.

This rule and explanation control in Part I of White’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: all nine Justices support them.

The Court holds that evidence of one crime is not a prosecution for that crime. Dowling permits evidence to perform a legitimate function in a prosecution without treating every described act as an offense adjudicated there. Grady likewise rejects a rule forbidding the reuse of evidence; its inquiry concerns conduct used to establish an essential element of an already prosecuted offense. Missouri charged an attempted manufacture in late August. The Oklahoma manufacturing offenses occurred earlier and elsewhere. Their admission as evidence of intent in Missouri neither changed that indictment nor submitted the Oklahoma offenses for conviction. The contrary argument would make the scope of jeopardy depend on everything mentioned at trial and would erase the distinction Dowling preserves. The Court decides only the double-jeopardy objection. It does not approve every use of other-act evidence or decide an objection to the admission of particular proof.

Dowling v. United States, 493 U.S. 342, is applied: evidentiary use does not itself constitute prosecution for the described offense.

Grady v. Corbin, 495 U.S. 508, is applied and confined to essential-conduct analysis; its express rejection of a same-evidence test remains operative.

#### Agreement remains a distinct offense

A conspiracy and its substantive objects are distinct offenses; prosecution of the substantive crime does not bar prosecution of the agreement merely because the same acts help prove both. The overlapping Missouri conduct therefore does not bar this Oklahoma conspiracy.

This rationale controls in Part II of White’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: seven Justices support it. Stevens and Blackmun agree only with the conspiracy judgment on a narrower ground.

The Court holds that an agreement to commit crimes is not the same offense as the crimes committed under it. Bayer explains that the agreement has elements and dangers independent of its execution; Pinkerton and Iannelli preserve that distinction even when the offenses share proof. The conspiracy charged here embraces an agreement and additional conduct extending beyond the Missouri attempt. The presence of that attempt among the acts used to prove the conspiracy does not transform the agreement into the previously prosecuted attempt. Grady does not silently displace this settled body of conspiracy law. Garrett reinforces the need to examine a continuing or multilayered course of criminal activity with attention to its actual structure. The Court rejects the circuit’s contrary use of evidentiary overlap. It neither overrules Grady nor creates a same-transaction requirement, and it leaves ordinary evidentiary restrictions and materially different successive-prosecution records for their own cases.

United States v. Bayer, 331 U.S. 532, is applied: the agreement is distinct from its execution.

Pinkerton v. United States, 328 U.S. 640, is applied: substantive crime and conspiracy remain separately punishable offenses.

Iannelli v. United States, 420 U.S. 770, is applied: conspiracy’s independent character survives overlapping proof.

Garrett v. United States, 471 U.S. 773, is applied by analogy to the treatment of multilayered criminal conduct.

Grady v. Corbin, 495 U.S. 508, is clarified without overruling: it does not abrogate the established conspiracy/substantive-offense distinction.

Distinct evidentiary use does not itself create prior jeopardy for an uncharged offense. The established agreement/substantive-offense distinction governs this conspiracy. Grady remains in force within its proper scope; ordinary evidentiary objections and a different single-incident successive prosecution remain open.

Stevens, joined by Blackmun, agrees that the separate substantive crimes can be prosecuted and joins Part I. They also agree with the conspiracy judgment because the two overlapping acts did not establish the agreement, but decline a general exception to Grady for conspiracy or other complex crimes. Their narrower ground does not replace the seven-Justice Part II rule.

The Tenth Circuit must give effect to the reversal as to counts 1–6 and resolve any preserved outstanding appellate matters. No new evidentiary ruling or automatic new trial is ordered. The decision resolves Supreme Court review on the stated terms; lower-court proceedings do not imply retained Supreme Court supervision.

**End of entry: United States v. Felix, merits decision, March 25, 1992.**

### Barnhill v. Johnson, No. 91-159

Merits decision, March 25, 1992

Argued January 14, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the Tenth Circuit under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1), reviewing the honor-date ruling in the trustee’s preference action. The question is whether, for the preference period under 11 U.S.C. §547(b), a transfer by ordinary check occurs on delivery or honor—payment by the bank.

The ordinary check was delivered November 18, dated November 19, and honored November 20. The parties’ chronology places honor on the ninetieth day before the bankruptcy petition; delivery preceded the preference window. Before payment, the drawer retained control of the account and could stop payment. The state-law rules distinguish the payee’s instrument and claim against the drawer from any interest in bank funds.

The Court affirms the Tenth Circuit’s honor-date judgment, 7–2. Stone, White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas vote to affirm; Blackmun and Stevens vote to reverse. November 20 places this transfer within §547(b)’s ninety-day period. All other preference elements and properly presented §547(c) defenses retain their separate operation. Stone delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined by White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. Stevens dissents, joined by Blackmun.

#### Honor determines the §547(b) transfer

For §547(b), an ordinary check transfers the debtor’s relevant bank-account interest when the drawee honors it, because delivery gives the payee no assigned interest in that account. The November 20 honor date therefore satisfies the ninety-day timing element here, without deciding other preference elements or defenses.

This rule and explanation control in Stone’s Opinion of the Court, joined by White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: seven Justices support them.

The Court holds that the debtor parts with the bank funds when the bank pays the ordinary check. McKenzie makes transfer timing a federal question while looking to state law for the property transferred; Butner likewise respects state-created property interests. Under the governing commercial law, delivery creates an instrument and a claim against the drawer, but it assigns none of the account. The drawer may stop payment, and intervening events may prevent honor. The statutory word conditional does not turn the payee’s claim against the drawer into bank-account property that state law has not conveyed. Section 547(e) does not relate the transfer back to a time before that interest passed. The competing delivery rule therefore identifies the wrong property event. Wolas preserves the separate ordinary-course defense; it supplies no contrary transfer date. The Court leaves §547(c), nonordinary payment instruments and the remaining preference elements undisturbed.

McKenzie v. Irving Trust Co., 323 U.S. 365, is applied: federal transfer law operates on property interests identified by state law.

Butner v. United States, 440 U.S. 48, is applied: state law defines the relevant property interest absent a contrary federal rule.

Union Bank v. Wolas, decided December 11, 1991, is left intact: ordinary-course eligibility and proof remain distinct from §547(b) timing.

For §547(b), honor fixes transfer of the relevant account interest by ordinary check. No creditor automatically loses every payment honored within ninety days: the trustee must establish the remaining elements, and available defenses remain. Nonordinary instruments and §547(c) timing are not decided.

Stevens, joined by Blackmun, would treat delivery as a conditional transfer if honor follows within ten days, and use honor otherwise. They rely on the breadth of the statutory transfer definition, §547(e), ordinary commercial treatment and consistent language within §547. That view does not control §547(b), and neither writing decides a general §547(c) timing rule.

The honor-date ruling stands. Further administration of the preference claim remains subject to its other elements and available defenses. The decision resolves Supreme Court review on the stated terms; lower-court proceedings do not imply retained Supreme Court supervision.

**End of entry: Barnhill v. Johnson, merits decision, March 25, 1992.**

### National Railroad Passenger Corp. v. Boston & Maine Corp., Nos. 90-1419 and 90-1769

Merits decision, March 25, 1992

Argued January 13, 1992. On consolidated writs of certiorari to the D.C. Circuit under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1), reviewing its decision setting aside the Interstate Commerce Commission’s conveyance order. Both dockets receive the same judgment. The question is whether §562(d), as amended, authorizes this acquisition and reconveyance, and whether the ICC’s actual decision adequately establishes the statutory conditions and a public use.

The Montrealer used Boston & Maine’s 48.8-mile segment. After maintenance problems and service suspension, Amtrak arranged acquisition and immediate reconveyance to Central Vermont, with rehabilitation, twenty-year passenger rights and continued freight access for B&M. Amtrak made a take-it-or-leave-it purchase offer. B&M proposed discussing upgrades while declining the sale proposal. The ICC found inability to agree, allowed Amtrak to elect the acquisition remedy, applied the statutory need presumption, and ordered conveyance with $2,373,286 compensation. The circuit rejected the transaction; Judge Ginsburg would instead remand for adequate findings. A 1990 amendment expressly authorizes service-promoting reconveyance and applies to pending cases. Its applicability here is undisputed. Maintenance allegations are not independent judicial findings. Valuation, competition and other issues left unresolved by the circuit remain open.

The Court reverses the D.C. Circuit’s judgments in both dockets and remands both cases, 6–3. Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter support reversal and remand; White, Blackmun and Thomas would remand to the ICC for explanation. The ICC acted within the acquisition authority and satisfied the challenged statutory prerequisites. The circuit must resolve the compensation challenges and other issues it did not decide. Kennedy delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia and Souter. White dissents, joined by Blackmun and Thomas.

#### Passenger-service acquisition and reconveyance

Section 562(d) permits the ICC’s construction that property required for passenger service includes property usefully and appropriately acquired for Amtrak’s rail operations; it does not require indispensable permanent Amtrak ownership. The statutory need presumption and express service-promoting reconveyance authority therefore permit this transaction, subject to the statute’s independent conditions.

This holding and explanation control in Kennedy’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia and Souter: six Justices support them.

The Court holds that planned reconveyance does not defeat the acquisition authority. Chevron requires a court to examine the statutory text and structure and to accept an administering agency’s permissible resolution of a remaining ambiguity. The word required can bear more than an indispensable-ownership meaning. Reading it that narrowly would drain the presumption of need of its force and frustrate Congress’s express authorization of reconveyance. K mart and Everhart direct attention to the whole enactment, not an isolated dictionary choice. Amtrak will use this track for passenger service, while Central Vermont will maintain it under the arrangement. The statute allows Amtrak a reasonable business judgment within those service-related limits, subject to the ICC’s oversight and the specified rebuttal. The contrary interpretation adds a permanent-ownership condition Congress did not enact. The Court leaves compensation and distinct regulatory objections for the remaining review proceedings.

Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. NRDC, 467 U.S. 837, is applied: permissible agency construction governs the unresolved statutory ambiguity.

K mart Corp. v. Cartier, Inc., 486 U.S. 281, is applied: statutory text and structure constrain deference.

Sullivan v. Everhart, 494 U.S. 83, is applied: the meaning of a disputed term is assessed within the whole statute.

Lechmere, Inc. v. NLRB, decided January 27, 1992, is preserved: no prior binding construction is displaced here.

#### The agency’s discernible ground

A reviewing court may recognize an interpretation necessarily embodied in the agency’s own decision even when the agency did not state that interpretation in the exact words later used by counsel. That rule sustains this ICC order because its application of the need presumption and its disposition of the competing remedies disclose the statutory construction; it does not permit a court to supply a new agency ground.

This holding and explanation control in Kennedy’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia and Souter: six Justices support them.

The Court holds that Chenery requires the agency’s ground, not a prescribed verbal formula. The ICC rejected the contention that Amtrak must seek trackage rights first, treated the passenger-service use as sufficient to invoke the presumption, and examined the two specified means of rebutting need. Its actual analysis necessarily rests on a construction that does not demand indispensable ownership. Recognizing that construction explains the decision the Commission made; it does not replace it with a court’s preferred policy. State Farm’s prohibition against counsel’s later rationalizations remains fully applicable when the agency’s reasoning cannot be found in its decision. The dissent’s objection would be decisive in that situation, but this record discloses the operative premise. The Court therefore does not remand merely to obtain the same interpretation in more explicit words. It supplies no permission to invent omitted findings or uphold action on an unrelated ground.

SEC v. Chenery Corp., 318 U.S. 80, is applied: the agency’s own ground must sustain its action; a necessarily discernible premise suffices here.

Motor Vehicle Manufacturers Assn. v. State Farm, 463 U.S. 29, is distinguished on the record, not weakened: new appellate rationalizations remain insufficient.

#### Inability to agree on sale terms

Section 562(d)’s inability-to-agree prerequisite permits the ICC to find from the parties’ positions that further sale negotiations will not produce agreement; it does not impose an unstated mandatory bargaining ritual. The supported finding here satisfies that condition, without making every unilateral offer sufficient.

This holding and explanation control in Kennedy’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia and Souter: six Justices support them.

The Court holds that the sale prerequisite turns on whether agreement can be reached, not on a bargaining ceremony absent from the statute. Amtrak offered to purchase the segment on fixed terms. B&M declined that proposal and offered instead to discuss upgrading the track while retaining it. The Commission determined from those positions that further efforts would not produce a sale agreement. Under Chevron, that understanding is a permissible reading of the enacted prerequisite, and the record supports its application. B&M’s demand for a separate good-faith negotiation requirement would add a condition beyond inability to agree. The Court does not hold that an unaccepted offer alone invariably establishes inability, or that the statutory presumption of need dispenses with this antecedent finding. The agency must address the parties’ actual positions. Its finding here is sufficient; compensation and the remaining challenges still require their separate consideration.

Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. NRDC, 467 U.S. 837, is applied to the ICC’s interpretation of the independent sale-negotiation prerequisite.

#### Rebuttal: significant impairment

To defeat §562(d)’s presumption of need, the railroad must establish both statutory rebuttal conditions. In assessing significant impairment of its common-carrier obligations, the ICC may consider the transaction as a whole, including preserved trackage rights and compensation; the supported finding of no significant impairment independently prevents rebuttal here.

This holding and explanation control in Kennedy’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia and Souter: six Justices support them.

The Court holds that the Commission need not assess a conveyance as though its protective terms did not exist. B&M retains the right to serve its existing customers, and the conveyance provides compensation. Those terms bear directly on whether the transaction significantly impairs its common-carrier obligations. The statute does not require the ICC to disregard them and examine only the bare transfer of title. The Commission’s finding of no significant impairment is supported by substantial evidence and falls within its permissible construction of the provision. Chevron supports that construction, while the Court’s current Arkansas decision preserves the requirement to review the actual evidentiary record. Because the two rebuttal conditions are conjunctive, failure to prove significant impairment is sufficient to leave the need presumption unrebutted. This holding does not determine whether the compensation amount is correct; the valuation challenges remain for the circuit.

Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. NRDC, 467 U.S. 837, is applied: the statute permits consideration of mitigation within the transaction.

Arkansas v. Oklahoma, decided February 26, 1992, is consistent with, and unchanged by, review of an agency conclusion against the actual record.

#### Rebuttal: alternative property

The ICC permissibly reads the alternative-property condition of §562(d) to ask whether an adequate alternative route is available on the statutory terms, rather than whether a lesser interest in the same segment would suffice. The absence of such an alternative independently prevents rebuttal of the need presumption here.

This holding and explanation control in Kennedy’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia and Souter: six Justices support them.

The Court holds that the alternative-property inquiry does not require the Commission to reintroduce the circuit’s indispensable-ownership rule through the rebuttal provision. The ICC considered whether Amtrak could provide the required passenger service over an adequate alternative route and found none. Its construction gives coherent effect to the acquisition remedy, the need presumption and the separate availability of trackage-rights proceedings. Chevron requires acceptance of that permissible reading, not substitution of a judicial preference for a lesser interest in the very property sought. B&M’s argument would make the rejected ownership limitation do the same work under a different clause. The Court sustains the Commission’s construction and application on this record. Because both rebuttal conditions must be established, this failure independently leaves the presumption in place, even apart from the no-impairment finding. No rule for property unrelated to passenger service is adopted.

Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. NRDC, 467 U.S. 837, is applied: the ICC’s route-based construction is permissible within the statutory scheme.

#### Public use despite private reconveyance

A compensated condemnation serving a rationally supported public passenger-service purpose satisfies the Fifth Amendment’s public-use requirement even when title is reconveyed to another private railroad. The ICC’s determination that this transaction facilitates Amtrak service meets that requirement.

This holding and explanation control in Kennedy’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia and Souter: six Justices support them.

The Court holds that transfer to a private railroad does not by itself negate public use. Midkiff sustains a condemnation rationally related to a conceivable public purpose, including a subsequent private transfer; Berman likewise recognizes that private participation can implement a public project. Here the conveyance supports restoration and maintenance of passenger service over the Montrealer route while securing Amtrak’s continuing use. The ICC’s public-purpose determination is rational, and the record supplies no basis for treating the arrangement as naked private favoritism. Carmack’s concern about limited delegations to private entities does not alter the result: the condemnation power here belongs to the ICC, which must apply the statute and provide compensation. The Court need not predict whether every operational objective will be achieved. It decides the public-use objection, leaving the amount of just compensation and the remaining statutory review issues unresolved.

Hawaii Housing Authority v. Midkiff, 467 U.S. 229, is applied: rational public purpose can support condemnation followed by private transfer.

Berman v. Parker, 348 U.S. 26, is applied: private implementation does not defeat an otherwise public purpose.

United States v. Carmack, 329 U.S. 230, is distinguished: the condemnation power here is exercised by a government commission.

Section 562(d) allows the approved service-connected acquisition and reconveyance. Judicial deference remains tied to an interpretation actually discernible in the agency’s decision. The conjunctive rebuttal, inability-to-agree finding, compensation obligation and passenger-service boundary retain distinct work; no condemnation power for naked private favoritism follows.

White, joined by Blackmun and Thomas, accepts that the statute permits acquisition followed by reconveyance. They would require the ICC to explain and apply the service-need condition itself, treating counsel’s useful-or-appropriate construction as a later rationalization. They rely on State Farm, Chenery and Burlington Truck Lines to require the agency itself to supply the explanation, and do not endorse the circuit’s categorical permanent-ownership rule. Their agreement about possible statutory power is not a join in the Court’s approval of this order.

Both cases return to the D.C. Circuit for the compensation challenges and other outstanding review issues. The Court does not itself fix a new valuation, resolve distinct regulatory claims, or schedule further Supreme Court proceedings. The decision resolves Supreme Court review on the stated terms; lower-court proceedings do not imply retained Supreme Court supervision.

**End of entry: National Railroad Passenger Corp. v. Boston & Maine Corp., merits decision, March 25, 1992.**

### Robertson v. Seattle Audubon Society, No. 90-1596

Merits decision, March 25, 1992

Argued December 2, 1991. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1). The question is whether §318(b)(6)(A) changes applicable law or impermissibly dictates judicial results under unchanged law. Sixteen Forest Service sales offered during fiscal year 1990 remain enjoined and supply a live controversy; the Bureau of Land Management portion appears moot and is not an independent basis for merits relief.

Environmental groups challenged Forest Service and BLM timber activity under MBTA, NEPA, NFMA, FLPMA and OCLA. The fiscal-year 1990 Northwest Timber Compromise both required sales and imposed new protected-area limits. Section 318(b)(6)(A) treated compliance with (b)(3) and (b)(5) as meeting the statutory requirements identified by the pending cases’ captions. Individual-sale compliance remained for judicial determination; some additional BLM areas were to be designated after enactment. The Ninth Circuit held that Congress directed results under unchanged law and could not amend substantive law in an appropriations measure.

The Court reverses the Ninth Circuit’s judgment and remands, 9–0, for application of the new statutory criteria. Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas support that disposition of the Article III challenge to §318(b)(6)(A). No particular timber sale is declared compliant. Thomas delivers the unanimous Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter.

#### Substitution of governing law

Section 318(b)(6)(A) changes the statutory law applicable to the identified timber disputes by making compliance with the new restrictions sufficient to meet the specified old requirements, while leaving actual compliance for courts to decide. That clear substantive amendment is effective although enacted in an appropriations statute and does not violate Article III on the ground decided below.

This holding and explanation control in Thomas’s unanimous Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter.

The Court holds that Congress supplied new legal standards and left their application to the judiciary. Before the Compromise, the challenges depended on the identified environmental requirements; afterward, compliance with the two new provisions could satisfy those obligations. Simpson explains why the specific enactment qualifies the more general laws. No provision finds that a particular timber sale meets the new restrictions, and the contemplated later designation of protected areas confirms that judicial application remains necessary. The case captions identify the requirements affected; they do not themselves decide facts. Will permits Congress to amend substantive law clearly in an appropriations act, while TVA’s caution against implied repeal does not defeat this express substitution. Because the premise of the circuit’s Klein ruling fails, the Court need not define Klein’s full reach. It reserves the unraised challenge to narrowly targeted legislation and leaves actual sale compliance for remand.

United States v. Klein, 13 Wall. 128, is distinguished on the ground decided below; its full constitutional reach is not resolved.

Pennsylvania v. Wheeling & Belmont Bridge Co., 18 How. 421, is consistent with adjudication under changed law; no broad targeted-legislation holding is drawn from it.

Simpson v. United States, 435 U.S. 6, is applied: specific provisions qualify general ones.

United States v. Will, 449 U.S. 200, is applied: clear substantive amendments may appear in appropriations legislation.

TVA v. Hill, 437 U.S. 153, is distinguished: the amendment here is express rather than an inferred repeal.

NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp., 301 U.S. 1, is preserved: a possible constitutionally valid construction reinforces, but does not replace, the enacted meaning.

The specified statutory requirements are modified for the Compromise’s defined geographical, temporal and contract scope. The decision establishes no broad exception to Article III, no constitutional environmental entitlement and no finding that any sale complies. The unraised challenge to legislation narrowly targeting pending litigation, Klein’s full reach and final-judgment reopening remain undecided.

The courts apply the new criteria to the still-live sale disputes. Fiscal-year limits and continued application to covered sales contracts remain. The decision resolves Supreme Court review on the stated terms; lower-court proceedings do not imply retained Supreme Court supervision.

### Freeman v. Pitts, No. 89-1290

Merits decision, March 31, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 7, 1991. On writ of certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1), the Court reviews the Eleventh Circuit’s restriction on partial withdrawal of supervision under a school-desegregation decree. The question is whether supervision may be withdrawn in completed areas while other decree obligations remain, and what findings and limits must govern that decision.

A 1969 decree required dismantling DeKalb County’s dual school system. On the district’s 1986 request for dismissal, the trial court found compliance in student assignment, transportation, facilities, and extracurricular activities. It retained supervision over faculty and administrative assignments and educational resources and quality. The trial court attributed the challenged student imbalance to demographic change and praised the district’s progress. The Eleventh Circuit required simultaneous compliance across all the factors identified in Green for several years. The factual findings, the legal completeness of the causal inquiry, and whether control in one area remains useful to cure another are distinct questions. Neither this Court’s judgment nor praise for progress establishes final systemwide compliance.

Chief Justice Stone and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter participated at argument and decision. Justice Thomas took no part. The October 7 argument preceded his October 23 oath; no reargument is established.

#### Judgment

The Court vacates the categorical restriction in the Eleventh Circuit’s judgment and remands, 8–0, for an integrated assessment of each component of the decree. This Court releases no component and finds no final systemwide compliance. Each party bears its own costs.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|
| Categorical restriction and further proceedings | Vacated and remanded, 8–0 | Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter | Determine the properly open issues and obtain findings on good faith and remedial connections; define any released obligations and remaining supervision. |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Author and joining Justices | Relationship to judgment and scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stone | Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter | All eight participants join the limited Court opinion supporting vacatur and remand. |
| Concurrence | Kennedy | Kennedy, White, Scalia | Concurs while emphasizing the existing demographic findings and the limits on perpetual balancing. |
| Concurrence | Blackmun | Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor | Concurs while urging examination of the district’s contribution to residential movement and racially identifiable schools. |
| Concurrence | Souter | Souter | Concurs while addressing the effects of past segregation and remaining violations on migration and renewed imbalance. |
| Concurrence | Scalia | Scalia | Concurs while urging future reconsideration of the causation presumption. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Partial withdrawal under an unfinished decree

**Controlling proposition:** A court may withdraw supervision from a completed part of a school-desegregation decree while retaining jurisdiction and supervision elsewhere only after the district shows full and satisfactory compliance and elimination of the relevant vestiges to the extent practicable. The court must assess demonstrated good faith toward the whole decree and whether retaining control is necessary or practicable to achieve compliance elsewhere; release may not obstruct the remaining remedy.

**Authority:** Stone, joined by White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter: all eight participating Justices support this limited rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that completion can justify ending supervision of one obligation without ending the case. Green requires removal of the former dual system’s effects throughout school operations; it does not make each remedial obligation indivisible. Swann supplies equitable flexibility directed to the constitutional injury, and Dowell requires good-faith compliance and practicable elimination of vestiges. Those principles permit partial withdrawal when supported by findings. They also require attention to connections among assignments, personnel, resources and other remaining duties. The court must assess whether retaining control is necessary or practicable to complete compliance elsewhere and preserve an effective remaining remedy. The contrary categorical rule would preserve restrictions that no longer perform remedial work. The opposite shortcut would mistake progress in one area for completion of the whole decree. Rufo’s tailored approach to consent modification is consistent with this inquiry but does not displace the school-specific obligations. The Court orders findings, not final release.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Green v. School Board of New Kent County, 391 U.S. 430 — applied: the affirmative duty and operational inquiry continue across the unfinished decree.
- Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg Board of Education, 402 U.S. 1 — applied: equitable flexibility remains tied to effective repair of the violation.
- Board of Education v. Dowell, 498 U.S. 237 — clarified for partial withdrawal: good faith and practicable elimination must be assessed in that setting.
- Milliken v. Bradley, 433 U.S. 267 — applied: restoration of local control remains consistent with completion of the constitutional remedy.
- Rufo v. Inmates of Suffolk County Jail, current-term decision January 15, 1992 — preserved within its consent-modification scope; it does not replace the Green/Dowell release inquiry.

##### Causation and the limits of the remand

**Controlling proposition:** The district bears the burden of showing that imbalance for which it disclaims remedial responsibility is not traceable to the constitutional violation; genuinely independent demographic change does not require perpetual racial balancing. Existing factual findings receive clear-error review, but findings on one question do not decide unexamined causal or remedial connections; the remand must resolve properly open issues and define the obligations released and retained.

**Authority:** Stone, joined by White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter: eight votes; the separate opinions’ disputed factual applications are outside this holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an explanation of population change must answer the question on which release depends. Spangler protects a district from endless readjustment for genuinely independent demographic change after it has performed the required remedial work. It does not establish that every residential or enrollment pattern is independent merely because migration occurred. Swann preserves the district’s responsibility to show why remaining imbalance is not an effect it must remedy. At the same time, ordinary appellate review respects factual findings actually made; a legal challenge to the completeness of an inquiry is different from disagreement with a resolved fact. The present remand requires findings on whole-decree good faith and the relationship between proposed withdrawal and unfinished remedies. It does not require relitigation of every demographic finding or endorse every causal conclusion. Specific withdrawal terms and retained jurisdiction allow the court to end unnecessary supervision while continuing to enforce the remaining decree.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Pasadena Board of Education v. Spangler, 427 U.S. 424 — applied with its independent-cause and completed-remedy limits.
- Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg Board of Education, 402 U.S. 1 — preserved: the district’s causal showing and violation-linked remedy remain required.
- Board of Education v. Dowell, 498 U.S. 237 — applied: good faith requires demonstrated compliance, not an assurance alone.

#### Limits and Current-Law Effect

Partial withdrawal is permissible only upon the required showing for each released area, including good faith toward the whole decree, elimination of the effects of segregation to the extent practicable, and attention to connections among the remaining remedies. The court retains jurisdiction and active authority over outstanding violations. Existing causation burdens remain. The decision creates neither a perpetual racial-balance requirement nor an automatic demographic exemption. Rufo’s rules for modifying consent decrees and Dowell’s rules for ending desegregation supervision retain their distinct operations.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Kennedy, joined by Justices White and Scalia, regards the existing demographic findings as credible and emphasizes Spangler’s limit on perpetual balancing. They accept remand for unresolved connections and good faith toward the whole decree. Justice Blackmun, joined by Justices Stevens and O’Connor, urges examination of the district’s contribution to residential movement and racially identifiable schools. They do not regard initial neighborhood assignments or demographic change alone as proof of completed desegregation.

Justice Souter explains that past segregation can cause migration and that remaining violations can generate renewed imbalance in a released area; retained jurisdiction permits renewed control when justified. Justice Scalia urges future reconsideration of the causation presumption as past violations recede. The Court does not adopt that proposed change. The Justices’ common join in the Court’s limited opinion does not adopt every factual application in the separate opinions.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Eleventh Circuit must identify the issues properly open under the parties’ earlier presentations and order the necessary proceedings, including district-court findings on good faith and remedial connections. Any withdrawal order must define the released obligations and the supervision that remains. Existing factual findings receive ordinary clear-error review; no general retrial of settled facts is ordered. The Court orders no final discharge or new remedy and sets no filing date. Supreme Court review is resolved on these terms; the lower-court proceedings do not remain under Supreme Court supervision.

**End of entry: Freeman v. Pitts, merits decision, March 31, 1992.**

### Yee v. City of Escondido, No. 90-1947

Merits decision, April 1, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 22, 1992. On writ of certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1257 after the California Supreme Court denied review, the Court considers whether restrictions on voluntarily created tenancies impose a permanent physical occupation requiring compensation. The granted question concerns physical taking; the related regulatory-taking theory is not fairly included. All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

The park owners voluntarily rented pads to mobile-home owners. California limits termination and interference with sales of homes in place, but permits withdrawal through a change of land use. Escondido’s 1988 ordinance rolled rents back to 1986 levels and requires approval for increases. The owners alleged that reduced rents and constrained replacement of tenants create premiums in home resale prices. They did not attempt the statutory change-of-use process. The trial court sustained a demurrer, rejecting the claim on the allegations, and the Court of Appeal affirmed. The California Supreme Court denied discretionary review. The due-process claim appeared only in the request for discretionary review; no state court adjudicated it. No denial of withdrawal in a particular application is established.

#### Judgment

The Court affirms the judgment rejecting the physical-taking claim, 9–0. No compensation is awarded on that theory. The Court declines to decide the unpreserved due-process claim and the regulatory-taking question outside the grant; neither is rejected on its substantive merits.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Remedy or effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Physical-taking claim | Affirmed, 9–0 | Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | The California judgment stands on this theory; no compensation is awarded. |
| Due-process merits and regulatory-taking merits | Not reached; all nine Justices agree | Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Any properly available claim must proceed in an appropriate case under its own procedural requirements. |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Author and joining Justices | Relationship to judgment and scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, I and II-D | O’Connor | Stone, White, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Physical taking and Rule 14.1(a); supports affirmance and the refusal to reach the regulatory-taking merits. |
| Opinion of the Court, II-A–C | O’Connor | White, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Preservation, the distinction between claims and arguments, and ripeness; these procedural grounds have six joins. |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Blackmun | Blackmun | Agrees in the judgment without joining the Court’s opinion. |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Souter | Souter | Agrees in the judgment without joining the Court’s opinion. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Voluntarily created tenancies

**Controlling proposition:** Regulating rents and continuation of voluntarily created pad tenancies does not impose a permanent physical occupation where the law permits the owner to change the land’s use and no contrary application is established. A tenant’s resale premium and the owner’s reduced ability to select incoming tenants do not themselves convert that regulation into a compelled occupation.

**Authority:** O’Connor, I, joined by Stone, White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas: seven votes control. Blackmun and Souter separately agree with rejection of the physical-taking claim.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the owners invited the occupation for which they now seek automatic compensation. Florida Power distinguishes that invitation from a government license forcing an unwanted occupant onto property; regulation of rent does not erase the distinction. Loretto requires compensation for a compelled permanent occupation, but preserves ordinary regulation of the landlord-tenant relationship. Here the challenged framework permits a change of use, and the owners have not alleged an actual unsuccessful effort to invoke that process. Their loss of rent and tenants’ ability to capitalize favorable terms describe economic effects, not governmental compulsion to surrender possession. The right to exclude remains important, but it does not turn every limit on an invited tenancy into physical appropriation. Nollan’s compelled easement presents a different premise. The Court leaves a law actually forbidding withdrawal, its particular application, and the separate regulatory-taking merits for appropriate proceedings.

**Precedent treatment:**

- FCC v. Florida Power Corp., 480 U.S. 245 — applied: invitation to occupy remains distinct from compelled occupation despite regulated rent.
- Loretto v. Teleprompter Manhattan CATV Corp., 458 U.S. 419 — applied and distinguished: its permanent-occupation rule requires compulsion absent here.
- Nollan v. California Coastal Commission, 483 U.S. 825 — distinguished: a required easement cannot substitute for proof of forced occupation here.
- Kaiser Aetna v. United States, 444 U.S. 164 — preserved: the right to exclude is important, but the facial scheme has not compelled this occupation.

##### Unpreserved due-process claim

**Controlling proposition:** The Court declines a substantive due-process claim neither properly raised nor decided in the state courts; presentation only in a denied request for discretionary review does not supply the missing state-court decision. The Court need not decide here whether this review limitation is jurisdictional or prudential.

**Authority:** O’Connor, II-A, joined by White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas: six Justices join this procedural rationale, which controls. Blackmun separately agrees that the claim is not properly raised. Stone does not join this rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the due-process claim is not properly presented for this review. The owners did not put that distinct federal claim in their complaint or litigate it in the Court of Appeal. They raised it when seeking discretionary review, which the California Supreme Court denied without deciding the merits. Gates describes the Court’s customary refusal to decide claims not raised or addressed below. A denial of discretionary review does not cure the omission by silently resolving the new claim. The distinction matters because due process is not merely a new supporting argument about an already presented taking. Whether the rule for reviewing state judgments is jurisdictional or prudential need not be settled: the Court would follow it in either event here. This disposition decides no due-process merits and gives no view on the ordinance’s validity under that separate constitutional theory.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Illinois v. Gates, 462 U.S. 213 — applied: an unpresented state-court claim is not decided on review; the jurisdictional/prudential classification remains open.
- People v. Triggs, 8 Cal.3d 884 — applied only to the effect of denied California discretionary review, which expresses no merits determination.

##### Preserved taking claim and facial ripeness

**Controlling proposition:** A party that properly presents a federal taking claim below may offer a different supporting argument on review, subject to the separate limitation imposed by the question presented. Under the then-governing standards, this facial allegation that the ordinance never substantially advances a legitimate state interest is ripe without a rent-increase request because it does not depend on the particular property’s economic loss or compensation.

**Authority:** O’Connor, II-B–C, joined by White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas: six Justices join and control these procedural propositions. Stone does not join these sections.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that preservation of a federal claim and preservation of every supporting argument are different inquiries. Bankers Life and Gates permit a new argument supporting the taking claim actually presented in the state courts. The owners’ physical and regulatory theories invoke that same federal claim, unlike their separate due-process claim. The city’s ripeness objection also does not bar the facial theory asserted here. Williamson County would require the pertinent final determination for a claim dependent on how regulation affects particular property. This theory instead alleges that the ordinance never advances a legitimate state interest, a question independent of any requested rent increase. Agins and Keystone supply that distinction under the law governing this case. These conclusions establish neither a regulatory taking nor permission to disregard the grant’s limits. Rule 14.1(a) still prevents consideration of the regulatory merits in this proceeding.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Bankers Life & Casualty Co. v. Crenshaw, 486 U.S. 71 — applied: new arguments may support a preserved federal claim.
- Illinois v. Gates, 462 U.S. 213 — applied: argument and claim preservation are distinct.
- Williamson County Regional Planning Commission v. Hamilton Bank, 473 U.S. 172 — distinguished: the asserted facial theory does not require a property-specific rent determination.
- Agins v. Tiburon, 447 U.S. 255 — applied only to the then-current facial-ripeness proposition; no merits determination follows.
- Keystone Bituminous Coal Assn. v. DeBenedictis, 480 U.S. 470 — applied to distinguish facial allegations from property-specific application.

##### Question presented limits merits review

**Controlling proposition:** Under Rule 14.1(a), a question asking whether the ordinance causes a physical taking does not fairly include the related but independent question whether it causes a regulatory taking. No exceptional urgency or economy justifies departing from that prudential limit here, so the regulatory merits remain undecided.

**Authority:** O’Connor, II-D, joined by Stone, White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas: seven votes; this section adopts no regulatory merits or ripeness conclusion.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the question chosen in the petition defines the issue for decision. The petition sought resolution of disagreement over physical occupation. A regulatory taking is related to that issue, but neither question is subsidiary to the other. Rule 14.1(a) gives the respondent notice of the proposed grounds for review and permits the Court to select and decide focused questions. Stone v. Powell recognizes departure only in exceptional circumstances, and none calls for expanding this case. The lower courts’ physical-taking disagreement explains review of that issue; it does not supply a developed conflict or record on the regulatory theory. Lytle counsels against deciding a new application without adequate lower-court consideration. The owners’ preservation of a general taking claim therefore does not remove the separate restriction imposed by their petition. The Court leaves regulatory-taking merits to an appropriate proceeding.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Stone v. Powell, 428 U.S. 465 — applied: departure from the question-presented rule remains exceptional.
- Lytle v. Household Manufacturing, Inc., 494 U.S. 545 — applied: developed arguments and lower-court consideration support disciplined review.
- American National Bank & Trust Co. v. Haroco, Inc., 473 U.S. 606 — applied by analogy: related questions are not necessarily fairly included within one another.

#### Limits and Current-Law Effect

On this facial record, regulation of voluntary tenancies does not compel physical occupation. The regulatory-taking merits remain open. Rule 14.1(a), preservation, and the facial-ripeness principles governing this case retain distinct functions. The limited ripeness holding rests on the six Justices joining II-B–C; it is not part of Chief Justice Stone’s partial join or of the judgment-only concurrences. The discussion of possible regulatory significance decides no regulatory liability.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Blackmun concurs in the judgment because there is no physical taking and neither additional merits claim is properly presented. He declines the Court’s ripeness determination and its suggestions about which allegations may bear on regulatory taking. Justice Souter concurs in the judgment and would otherwise join except for the suggestions about regulatory relevance.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The California judgment stands on the physical-taking theory. Any properly available regulatory or as-applied claim must proceed in an appropriate case under its own procedural requirements. The Court orders no new merits remand or compensation and decides no actual denial of withdrawal. ### Jacobson v. United States, No. 90-1124

Merits decision, April 6, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 6, 1991. On writ of certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1), the Court reviews the Eighth Circuit’s judgment sustaining a federal conviction under 18 U.S.C. §2252(a)(2) after a jury rejected entrapment. The question is whether rational jurors could find beyond a reasonable doubt that Jacobson was predisposed to commit the charged crime independently of the Government’s campaign. All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

Jacobson legally bought material in 1984, before the federal amendment. Beginning in January 1985, government agents used fictitious organizations, surveys, a pen pal, and appeals against censorship over twenty-six months. He eventually ordered illegal material twice; the first order was unfilled, and a controlled delivery followed the second. The prosecution relies on prompt orders, requests for more, expressed interests, and precautions about detection. The search found the lawful earlier magazines and government-supplied material, with no independent illicit collection. His testimony about the expected contents of the earlier purchase and the meaning of later correspondence must be assessed within the whole record. Inducement is not disputed for the question before the Court. The contested issue is whether Jacobson was independently disposed to commit the crime before the Government cultivated that disposition.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses the Eighth Circuit’s judgment, 6–3, and returns the case for entry of a judgment of acquittal. The prosecution may not retry the case to supply the missing proof of independent predisposition.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Predisposition independent of official cultivation

**Controlling proposition:** When the Government induces the charged crime and entrapment is at issue, it must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant’s predisposition existed before and independently of its cultivation; evidence obtained after contact may prove that earlier state of mind. Even giving the prosecution its reasonable favorable inferences, the lawful earlier purchase and responses generated during this twenty-six-month operation do not establish that independent predisposition.

**Authority:** White, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, Souter, Thomas: six votes control.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Government cannot establish independent readiness merely by pointing to the offense its efforts eventually produced. Sorrells and Sherman distinguish offering an opportunity from creating the disposition to accept it; Russell preserves that subjective inquiry and permits legitimate undercover work. The earlier lawful purchase shows an interest, but not by itself readiness to commit the later crime. The prompt orders, expressions of interest and precautions deserve consideration, yet all arose after sustained official cultivation and do not close the evidentiary gap on this record. Jackson requires favorable reasonable inferences for the prosecution, not abandonment of proof beyond reasonable doubt. Later evidence can reveal an earlier disposition; the Court imposes no rule excluding it. Nor does the decision require reasonable suspicion before contact or knowledge that the conduct violated law. The defect is the failure to prove this defendant’s independent predisposition.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Sorrells v. United States, 287 U.S. 435 — applied: the Government may offer opportunities but may not manufacture the disposition and prosecute its product.
- Sherman v. United States, 356 U.S. 369 — applied: the record can establish entrapment as a matter of law despite an eventual willing transaction.
- United States v. Russell, 411 U.S. 423 — preserved: subjective predisposition remains the inquiry; no objective misconduct defense is substituted.
- Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 307 — applied: reasonable prosecution-favorable inferences remain subject to proof beyond reasonable doubt.
- Mathews v. United States, 485 U.S. 58 — preserved: a routine offered opportunity does not automatically support an entrapment instruction.

##### Insufficiency requires acquittal

**Controlling proposition:** Because the prosecution’s proof was legally insufficient to overcome entrapment, the proper disposition is acquittal rather than a new trial. Burks bars a second opportunity to supply the missing evidence of independent predisposition.

**Authority:** White, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, Souter, Thomas: six votes support the remedial direction.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this evidentiary failure ends the prosecution on the charge. Burks distinguishes reversal for an error in the trial process from reversal because the prosecution failed to prove what the law requires. A new trial can correct the first kind of defect, but cannot give the Government another chance to supply evidence missing from its completed case. The present judgment does not rest on an incorrect instruction or a ruling that excluded available proof. The Court has considered the prosecution’s evidence and its reasonable favorable inferences and found them legally insufficient to establish independent predisposition beyond reasonable doubt. Sherman confirms that entrapment can have that matter-of-law consequence. The case therefore returns for acquittal. The Court does not authorize another entrapment trial, determine a separate outrageous-conduct claim, or revise the elements of the underlying statutory offense.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Burks v. United States, 437 U.S. 1 — applied: insufficiency requires acquittal and bars retrial to repair the proof.
- Sherman v. United States, 356 U.S. 369 — applied: matter-of-law entrapment supports termination, not a second prosecution opportunity.

#### Limits and Current-Law Effect

The prosecution must prove independently existing predisposition, not merely readiness after successful official cultivation. Evidence collected later may reveal earlier readiness; neither the evidence’s date nor investigative persistence alone decides entrapment. This record is insufficient even when reasonable inferences favor the prosecution, and Burks requires acquittal. The subjective defense, which asks about this defendant’s predisposition, ordinary sting operations, and the statutory elements otherwise remain. The Court decides no separate claim based on outrageous government conduct and imposes no new prerequisite for beginning an investigation.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice O’Connor, joined by Justice Kennedy and by Justice Scalia as to Part I, would defer to the jury’s inference from prompt illegal orders, requests for more, and precautions about detection. They distinguish preliminary contact from inducement and fear that the Court’s reasoning will restrict legitimate undercover investigation. In Part II, joined only by Justice Kennedy, Justice O’Connor objects that the Court appears to require a predisposition knowingly to violate law. The Court expressly adopts no new requirement that the defendant know the conduct is illegal; Justice Scalia does not join that criticism. No theory based on an objective assessment of police misconduct controls.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns through the Eighth Circuit for entry of acquittal. There may be no second trial on this charge to repair the evidentiary insufficiency. The Court neither amends the offense nor decides a separate outrageous-conduct claim. Supreme Court review is resolved on these terms; the proceedings to enter acquittal do not remain under Supreme Court supervision.

### Trevino v. Texas, No. 91-6751

Certiorari grant and summary merits disposition, April 6, 1992

On petition under 28 U.S.C. §1257 from the final state judgment, the Court grants leave to proceed without payment of fees and grants certiorari. By per curiam opinion, an opinion issued in the Court’s name, the Court reverses the Texas judgment and remands for consideration of Trevino’s preserved equal-protection claim. No dissent is noted. The petition is submitted for this action without oral argument; no earlier separate submission date is established. The question is whether Trevino preserved an equal-protection objection to racial peremptory strikes for application of Batson on direct review.

Before his 1984 capital trial, Trevino alleged Texas’s historical racial use of peremptory challenges and sought to prohibit it. He renewed his objection after each of the only three Black prospective jurors was struck and referred to Swain. His December 19, 1985 appellate brief invoked the Fourteenth Amendment while renewing the same issue. Batson issued while direct review remained pending. The State answered with an objection that Trevino and the excluded jurors were of different races. The Texas court treated the issue as a Sixth Amendment claim only and rejected it under Holland; it affirmed the conviction and death sentence on June 12, 1991, and denied rehearing on September 18. Trevino is Hispanic. The present record establishes preservation, not the ultimate purpose of each strike.

The Court holds that a motion made before Batson alleging a historical pattern of racial peremptory strikes, renewed after the challenged exclusions and carried forward on appeal with a Fourteenth Amendment reference, preserves the equal-protection objection despite imperfect labels. Trevino’s objection meets that standard under Ford. This rule is established by the per curiam Opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas, with no noted dissent.

The Court holds that Trevino presented the substance of the equal-protection claim to the state courts. Ford treats a historical-pattern objection as invoking the evidentiary theory then associated with Swain, even when counsel does not name the correct constitutional clause precisely. Trevino alleged that pattern before trial, renewed the motion after each challenged strike, and referred to Swain. On appeal he renewed the same objection under a heading invoking the Fourteenth Amendment. The State understood the claim and disputed its legal basis through the asserted same-race limitation. Holland’s rejection of a different Sixth Amendment theory does not answer that preserved claim. Requiring greater precision here would impose a burden the Court did not impose in Batson itself. The holding concerns these concrete objections and renewals, not an automatic rule that any general assertion of unfairness preserves every constitutional claim.

Ford v. Georgia, 498 U.S. 411, is applied: the substance of a historical-pattern objection can preserve equal protection before Batson. Swain v. Alabama, 380 U.S. 202, identifies the contemporaneous theory counsel invoked; its displaced requirement of proof across case after case is not restored. Batson v. Kentucky, 476 U.S. 79, supplies the preservation comparison; no heightened predictive precision is imposed. Holland v. Illinois, 493 U.S. 474, is distinguished because its Sixth Amendment rule does not dispose of an equal-protection claim.

The same per curiam opinion also holds that, because Trevino’s case remained on direct review when Batson was decided, Griffith requires its application, and Powers removes the objection that Trevino and the excluded jurors are of different races. The state court must decide the preserved claim under Batson’s ordinary burdens. Preservation alone establishes neither purposeful discrimination nor an automatic entitlement to a new trial. The same named coalition establishes this rule as the Court’s holding.

The Court holds that the timing and racial-identity objections do not prevent consideration of the preserved claim. Griffith applies Batson to criminal cases pending on direct review when that decision issued. Trevino’s appeal was pending, so the collateral-review limitation associated with Teague does not govern. Powers rejects the further contention that a defendant can challenge racial exclusion only when the excluded jurors share his race. Those rules require consideration of the claim, but do not decide the disputed purpose of the strikes. Batson supplies the remaining sequence: the required initial showing, a race-neutral explanation when called for, and an ultimate determination of purposeful discrimination. The state court must obtain the explanations and findings appropriate to that inquiry. Relief must follow its lawful completion, rather than an assumption that the preservation ruling itself proves discrimination. The Court leaves all other asserted trial errors undecided.

Griffith v. Kentucky, 479 U.S. 314, is applied: Batson governs this still-pending direct review. Powers v. Ohio, 499 U.S. 400, is applied: different racial identity does not bar the challenge. Batson v. Kentucky, 476 U.S. 79, supplies the ordinary proof sequence and leaves the ultimate discrimination question for remand. Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S. 288, is distinguished because its collateral-review setting does not displace Griffith here. The law after this decision preserves an objection with this historical-pattern substance and appellate renewal despite imperfect labels; ordinary Batson burdens and factfinding remain.

Chief Justice Stone concurs, emphasizing that preservation alone cannot warrant a new trial. He would order one only if the completed inquiry establishes a violation or lawful corrective proceedings cannot resolve it. That additional qualification on possible later relief is his separate position, not a direction adopted by the Court.

The state court must consider the preserved racial-strike claim and obtain the explanations and findings appropriate under Batson. The Court finds no completed Batson violation and orders no automatic new trial. It does not decide the consequences of an inability to complete a future corrective inquiry or any other trial errors. The case stands remanded for that consideration; this action sets no execution date and issues no stay.
