### United States Department of State v. Ray, No. 90-747

Judgment reversed, December 16, 1991

*United States Department of State v. Ray*, 502 U.S. 164 (1991), No. 90-747. Argued October 9 and decided December 16, 1991, on certiorari to the Eleventh Circuit, 908 F.2d 1549. The question is whether Exemption 6 of the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) permits withholding identifiers from interviews with Haitian returnees after segregable interview content has been released.

The Court reverses the Eleventh Circuit's judgment, 8–0. Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter all support reversal. Thomas took no part because argument preceded his oath; a quorum was present. The identifiers remain withheld, and the segregated substantive reports remain disclosed.

Stevens delivered the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone, White, Blackmun, O'Connor, and Souter. Scalia and Kennedy joined except as to Part III. Scalia, joined by Kennedy, concurred in part and in the judgment.

The Court holds that, under FOIA Exemption 6, identifying information may be withheld when linking names to sensitive personal interviews would cause a substantial privacy invasion and the identifiers add little demonstrated value to public understanding of agency conduct after reasonably segregable substantive material has been released. A confidentiality assurance informs the privacy interest but does not itself create an exemption.

Stevens's opinion supplies the Court's operative rule. All eight participating Justices support that rule and the judgment; Scalia and Kennedy do not join the broader discussion of possible public benefits from further investigation.

Exemption 6 requires a practical balance between the invasion of personal privacy and disclosure's contribution to public understanding of government operations. *Rose* supplies that balance and demonstrates that useful agency records may be disclosed after identifiers are removed; *Washington Post* confirms that information about particular persons falls within *similar files*. The released reports disclose the questions, answers, dates, sampling, and reported treatment, allowing scrutiny of the Department's monitoring. Adding names would connect intimate family, employment, and living information—and cooperation given under an accepted assurance of confidentiality—to identifiable returnees. On this record, the requesters identify only a general hope that renewed interviews might uncover inaccuracies, not concrete contradictory information or a demonstrated incremental contribution sufficient to outweigh that invasion. The Court does not require proof of government wrongdoing before invoking FOIA, decide that derivative investigation can never matter, or adjudicate Haiti's safety or any asylum claim.

The Court applies and preserves *Rose*, *Washington Post*, and *Reporters Committee*. It does not reach the waived claims under Exemptions 1 and 7(C). Effective December 16, the Exemption 6 balance measures the additional public contribution of identifiers against their concrete privacy cost after the substantive information has been separately released. Confidentiality is relevant but does not settle the balance.

Scalia, joined by Kennedy, agrees that the identifiers are exempt but would not treat a requester's proposed derivative investigation as a cognizable FOIA public benefit where the disclosed information itself says nothing more about agency performance.

The disclosure order is reversed as to identifiers. Supreme Court proceedings are closed.

**End of entry: United States Department of State v. Ray, Judgment reversed, December 16, 1991.**

### INS v. National Center for Immigrants' Rights, Inc., No. 90-1090

Judgment reversed and case remanded, December 16, 1991

*INS v. National Center for Immigrants' Rights, Inc.*, 502 U.S. 183 (1991), No. 90-1090. Argued November 13 and decided December 16, 1991, on certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 913 F.2d 1350. The question is whether §242(a) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) prohibits the general bond condition against unauthorized employment pending deportability proceedings.

The Court reverses the Ninth Circuit's judgment and remands the case, 9–0. All nine Justices—Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas—participated and support that disposition. Stevens delivered the opinion for a unanimous Court.

The Court holds that INA §242(a) permits a generally applicable release condition forbidding unauthorized employment when the regulation is construed not to reach authorized workers and retains individual status determinations and discretionary relief. That facial statutory ruling does not resolve constitutional objections or particular misapplications.

Stevens's unanimous opinion supplies this holding for all nine Justices.

Section 242(a) authorizes release on conditions prescribed by the Attorney General; it does not restrict every condition to securing physical appearance. The regulation's heading, exception, contemporaneous explanation, and operating instructions establish that its target is unauthorized employment. *Witkovich* requires delegated immigration authority to remain connected to the statutory scheme, and Congress's labor-market protections supply that connection. *American Hospital Association* permits general rules to structure delegated discretion so long as legally required individual determinations remain. Here officials must determine existing work authorization, address colorable citizenship or status claims, and consider the regulation's avenue for discretionary relief. The narrower construction therefore defeats the proposition that every application exceeds statutory authority. The Court does not approve interference with lawful employment, presume disputed deportability, decide the complaint's constitutional claims, or foreclose an as-applied challenge where the represented procedures are unavailable or misused.

The Court applies and preserves *Witkovich*, *Carlson*, *Seminole Rock*, and *American Hospital Association*. *Ardestani* remains confined to the Equal Access to Justice Act's (EAJA) §554 cross-reference. Effective December 16, §242(a) supports the narrowed bond rule against unauthorized work, with individual determinations of status and relief.

Enforcement is permitted only under that construction and the described individualized process. The facial statutory injunction is reversed; constitutional and as-applied claims—challenges to particular applications of the rule—remain open, and other preserved claims return to the lower courts.

**End of entry: INS v. National Center for Immigrants' Rights, Inc., Judgment reversed and case remanded, December 16, 1991.**

### Hilton v. South Carolina Public Railways Commission, No. 90-848

Judgment reversed and case remanded, December 16, 1991

*Hilton v. South Carolina Public Railways Commission*, 502 U.S. 197 (1991), No. 90-848. Argued October 8 and decided December 16, 1991, on certiorari to the South Carolina Supreme Court. The question is whether the Federal Employers' Liability Act's (FELA) cause of action against a state-owned railroad remains enforceable in state court.

The Court reverses the judgment and remands the case, 6–2. Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy, and Souter support reversal; O'Connor and Scalia dissent. Thomas took no part because argument preceded his oath.

Kennedy delivered the opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, White, Stevens, and Souter. Blackmun concurred in the judgment. O'Connor dissented, joined by Scalia.

The Court holds that FELA's established inclusion of state-owned interstate railroads remains enforceable in a competent state court. This rule does not revive constructive waiver of Eleventh Amendment immunity in federal court or establish a general rule for every Article I damages action against a State.

Kennedy's opinion, supported by five of the eight participating Justices, supplies the controlling rationale. Blackmun supplies the sixth judgment vote.

*Parden* separately interpreted FELA's coverage of *every* interstate common carrier and treated operation of a railroad as waiver of federal-court immunity. *Welch* displaced the constructive-waiver rationale but expressly reserved statutory coverage. The remaining construction has governed the specialized railroad relationship for decades, Congress retained the relevant language, and States and employees arranged compensation against that background—including South Carolina's exclusion of FELA-covered employees from workers' compensation. *Will* construed a different statutory term and does not silently overrule the specific FELA holding. Adherence to precedent interpreting a statute thus requires retaining the independently grounded coverage rule unless the Court squarely abandons it. Under *Howlett*, a competent state court may not use state immunity to defeat a defendant's liability under valid federal law. The Court does not reopen Hilton's dismissed federal suit, decide negligence, or announce that entering commerce generally waives immunity.

The Court reaffirms *Parden*'s FELA coverage holding. Its rule treating railroad operation as a waiver of federal-court immunity remains displaced by *Welch*. The Court distinguishes *Will*, which construed a different statutory term, and applies *Howlett*, which requires a competent state court to honor liability under valid federal law. State-owned interstate railroads remain FELA defendants in competent state courts under the specific retained precedent.

Blackmun concurs in the judgment on his broader view of congressional power. O'Connor, joined by Scalia, would require a clearer statement before imposing damages liability on a State and would treat *Parden*'s coverage and waiver reasoning as inseparable.

Hilton may litigate the FELA claim in state court but receives no finding of negligence or damages. The case returns to state court for the ordinary FELA merits.

**End of entry: Hilton v. South Carolina Public Railways Commission, Judgment reversed and case remanded, December 16, 1991.**

### King v. St. Vincent's Hospital, No. 90-889

Judgment reversed and case remanded, December 16, 1991

*King v. St. Vincent's Hospital*, 502 U.S. 215 (1991), No. 90-889. Argued October 16 and decided December 16, 1991, on certiorari to the Eleventh Circuit, 901 F.2d 1068. The question is whether 38 U.S.C. §2024(d) contains an unstated reasonableness limit on protected military leave.

The Court reverses the judgment and remands the case, 8–0. Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter participated and support that disposition. Thomas took no part because argument preceded his oath. Souter delivered the opinion for the unanimous participating Court.

The Court holds that §2024(d) contains no implicit reasonableness limit on the duration of otherwise covered military service. An employee must still satisfy the provision's actual coverage, notice, service, and return conditions.

Souter's opinion supplies this holding for all eight participating Justices.

Section 2024(d) ties protected leave to the period required to perform covered duty, and the Army required the three-year tour associated with King's position. The statutory scheme expressly imposes duration limits on neighboring service categories but omits one here. *Morash* requires provisions to be read as part of the whole enactment; that comparison makes the omission meaningful rather than accidental. *Fishgold* supports protective construction of reemployment rights, although the text and structure—not solicitude alone—control the result. The Hospital's concern about disruption from a lengthy voluntary absence is substantial, but the words *leave*, *employee*, and *return* do not provide an administrable judicial measure for writing a missing cap. The Court rejects both a categorical three-year exclusion and a free-ranging reasonableness balance. It does not excuse failure to meet enacted requirements, decide damages, or determine obligations arising after a failure properly to return.

The Court applies *Morash*, which requires reading the provision within the entire enactment, and *Fishgold*, which supports protective construction of reemployment rights. It rejects circuit precedent adding a reasonableness condition. *Wolas* remains confined to its statute. Effective December 16, otherwise covered §2024(d) leave has no judicially implied duration limit.

The categorical three-year exclusion and implied durational-reasonableness condition are removed. Other statutory obligations remain for further proceedings, and the case returns for consideration of the parties' actual statutory obligations.

**End of entry: King v. St. Vincent's Hospital, Judgment reversed and case remanded, December 16, 1991.**

### Hunter v. Bryant, No. 90-1440

Judgment vacated and case remanded, December 16, 1991

#### Chronology and Posture

*Hunter v. Bryant*, 502 U.S. 224 (1991), No. 90-1440. Submitted on the petition papers and decided December 16, 1991, on review of the Ninth Circuit's denial of qualified immunity. The issue is the agents' entitlement to immunity from the arrest-without-probable-cause claim at summary judgment.

Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter participated. Thomas took no part because the matter was submitted before his oath.

#### Judgment

The judgment is vacated and the case remanded to apply the correct qualified-immunity summary-judgment standard. The Court does not direct judgment for either side.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Vacatur for an incorrect allocation between legal questions and factual disputes | Vacated, 6–2 | Stone, White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter | Scalia, Stevens | Apply the complete contemporaneous record and identify any genuine dispute over material facts on which immunity depends. |
| Direction to enter immunity now | Rejected, 6–2 | Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter | White, Scalia | No automatic trial and no directed immunity on the present rationale. |

The supporting Justices in the second row support rejection of immediate immunity.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment and scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stone | Blackmun, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter | Controls vacatur, the legal standard, and remand. |
| Concurring in part and in the judgment | White | — | Agrees the Ninth Circuit's formulation was wrong but would direct immunity. |
| Concurring in part and dissenting in part | Stevens | — | Agrees immunity cannot be directed; would affirm rather than vacate. |
| Dissenting | Scalia | — | Would reverse and direct judgment for the agents. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**Controlling proposition:** Qualified immunity from a claim of arrest without probable cause is a legal question for the judge under ordinary summary-judgment constraints. A court must evaluate whether a reasonable officer could believe the arrest lawful on the supported version of the complete contemporaneous record offered by the party opposing summary judgment; choosing the more reasonable interpretation is not the test, but a generalized security concern alone does not establish reasonable belief that the elements of the offense were satisfied.

**Authority:** Stone's opinion, supported by Stone, Blackmun, O'Connor, Kennedy, and Souter, controls because those five Justices constitute a majority of the eight participants.

**Controlling explanation:** *Harlow*, *Anderson v. Creighton*, and *Malley* protect reasonable mistakes and require early judicial resolution where supported facts permit it. Rule 56 nevertheless requires the court to identify genuine disputes about the facts on which immunity depends and draw supported inferences for the party opposing summary judgment. The agents could consider the assassination references, reported gesture, oral statements, confusing interview, and refusal to elaborate. They also had the letter itself, which repeatedly attributed the supposed plot to an outside group; that feature matters to whether Bryant communicated a serious threat prohibited by §871 or reported an imagined danger. *Watts* supplies the contextual distinction between a true threat and protected expression. The Ninth Circuit erred by asking which interpretation was more reasonable and by suggesting the legal immunity issue belonged generally to a jury. But the opposite error would treat presidential-security concern as decisive without connecting the accepted facts to the offense. The remand court must decide immunity promptly after specifying any supported material dispute; the Court finds neither guilt, innocence, bad faith, nor a constitutional violation.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

The Court applies and preserves *Harlow*, *Anderson v. Creighton*, and *Malley*, which protect reasonable mistakes and require early judicial resolution where supported facts permit. It also applies and preserves *Mitchell*, *Beck*, *Watts*, and *Liberty Lobby*. No qualified-immunity rule is revised. Qualified immunity remains a question for the judge and protects reasonable probable-cause mistakes, subject to Rule 56's constraints on the treatment of supported facts and analysis tied to the particular offense.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

White would hold the agents immune now because their complete information permitted a reasonable belief in probable cause. Stevens would leave the denial intact because the agents had not established entitlement on the summary-judgment record. Scalia would summarily reverse and direct immunity, emphasizing early resolution and reasonable error.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The judgment is vacated and the case remanded for application of the correct qualified-immunity summary-judgment standard. The Court directs neither judgment for either side nor an automatic trial. The warrant component remains closed. The probable-cause immunity defense returns for prompt judicial decision under the stated standard.

**End of entry: Hunter v. Bryant, Judgment vacated and case remanded, December 16, 1991.**

### In re Blodgett, No. 91-716

Mandamus denied without prejudice, January 13, 1992

*In re Blodgett*, 502 U.S. 236 (1992), No. 91-716. Submitted as an original mandamus petition and decided January 13, 1992. Washington asks the Court to compel decision of a delayed appeal seeking habeas review of a capital sentence and disturb an August 7, 1991 order holding proceedings in abeyance. Mandamus is an order compelling a lower court to act.

The Court grants Campbell's motion to proceed without prepaying fees. Mandamus is denied without prejudice, 9–0. All nine Justices—Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas—participated and support denial.

The unsigned opinion of the Court is for Stone, White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas. Stevens, joined by Blackmun, concurred in the judgment.

The Court holds that mandamus is unavailable where the petitioner has not first sought focused relief from the court that entered the operative abeyance order and has not shown that avenue inadequate or futile. Denial may be without prejudice to a renewed petition after the ordinary avenue is pursued.

The unsigned opinion and Stevens's concurrence together state this ground for all nine Justices.

Rule 20.1, *Kerr*, *Will*, and *Fahey* reserve mandamus for an extraordinary failure to act where no other adequate avenue exists and the entitlement is clear and indisputable. The appeal's extended pendency, unanswered inquiries, and execution stay warrant serious attention. But the August order awaiting related proceedings is the current source of delay, and Washington never asked the panel to modify that specific order. Such a motion would permit the panel to address exhaustion, counsel, the proposed third petition, and a concrete schedule on the current record. Earlier general inquiries and a resubmission request do not establish that focused relief is unavailable or inevitably futile. Denial without prejudice respects both fair habeas review and timely adjudication. The Court does not approve every period of delay, find abuse of the writ, resolve Campbell's sentence, dissolve the stay, or impose an automatic special burden on future capital litigants.

The Court applies *Kerr*, *Will*, and *Fahey*, which reserve mandamus for extraordinary circumstances where the ordinary avenue is inadequate and the right to relief is clear. It does not extend *Barefoot*, *Rose*, or *McCleskey*. The prerequisite for an extraordinary writ remains unchanged; this decision creates no rule on the merits of a habeas claim.

Stevens, joined by Blackmun, agrees that failure to seek modification defeats relief but rejects broader criticism of the panel's coordination of related proceedings.

The Ninth Circuit may address a properly presented request to modify the abeyance order or expedite proceedings. The Court directs no habeas result or execution schedule. This original proceeding closes without prejudice; Washington may seek focused relief below.

**End of entry: In re Blodgett, Mandamus denied without prejudice, January 13, 1992.**

### Smith v. Barry, No. 90-7477

Judgment reversed and case remanded, January 14, 1992

*Smith v. Barry*, 502 U.S. 244 (1992), No. 90-7477. Argued December 2, 1991 and decided January 14, 1992, on certiorari to the Fourth Circuit, 919 F.2d 893. The question is whether a timely appellate brief can serve as a notice of appeal when it supplies Rule 3's required notice.

The Court reverses the judgment and remands the case, 9–0. All nine Justices—Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas—participated and support that disposition. O'Connor delivered the opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas. Scalia concurred in the judgment.

The Court holds that a timely document styled as an appellate brief may serve as a notice of appeal if it objectively identifies the appealing party, the judgment or order appealed, and the appellate court as Rule 3(c) requires. Filing it in the court of appeals is governed by Rule 4's mistaken-filing provision; informal form or title alone cannot require dismissal.

O'Connor's opinion, supported by eight Justices, supplies the controlling rationale. Scalia agrees with the judgment and the express ground concerning a document's form or title.

*Torres* makes Rule 3 compliance mandatory while recognizing functional compliance with the information the Rule actually requires. *Foman* directs courts to read a notice in context where the intent to seek review is manifest. Rule 3(c) itself prohibits dismissal for informality of form or title, so an administrative expectation that a notice and brief arrive as separate papers cannot add a jurisdictional condition. Smith returned the clerk's informal-brief form within the valid filing period after attempting to appeal; responding to that form does not negate the objective notice his document may convey. Rule 4 also treats a notice mistakenly filed in the appellate court as filed in the district court upon receipt. The first notice remains ineffective under the then-current posttrial-motion rule. The Fourth Circuit must still determine whether the May 4 document actually contains each required item and what judgment or orders it fairly identifies.

The Court applies *Torres*, *Foman*, and *Griggs*. *Ibarra* remains confined to timely criminal reconsideration motions. Rule 3 looks to a timely document's objective content, rather than its caption, while preserving every required item.

Scalia concurs in the judgment on the Rule's express protection against dismissal for informality of form or title.

The Fourth Circuit must examine the informal brief's actual contents under Rule 3(c). The underlying claims are not decided. The appeal returns for document-specific jurisdictional review and, if the brief is adequate, later merits proceedings.

**End of entry: Smith v. Barry, Judgment reversed and case remanded, January 14, 1992.**

### County of Yakima v. Confederated Tribes and Bands of the Yakima Indian Nation, Nos. 90-408 and 90-577

Judgment affirmed and cause remanded with modified instructions, January 14, 1992

*County of Yakima v. Confederated Tribes and Bands of the Yakima Indian Nation*, 502 U.S. 251 (1992), Nos. 90-408 and 90-577. Argued November 5, 1991 and decided January 14, 1992, on certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 903 F.2d 1207. The questions concern ad valorem and sales-excise taxation of reservation fee land. An ad valorem tax is a property tax based on value; the sales excise is a tax on the transaction.

The Court affirms the judgment and remands the cause with modified instructions. All nine Justices participated. The ad valorem component is resolved for the County, 8–1: Stone, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas support that disposition; Blackmun dissents. The sales excise remains prohibited, 9–0, with all nine Justices supporting that result.

Scalia delivered the opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas. Blackmun concurred as to the excise and dissented as to ad valorem authority.

The Court holds that the General Allotment Act's established fee-land rule and the Burke Act proviso authorize state ad valorem taxation of reservation land validly patented in fee under that Act, including qualifying land later held by the Nation. The tax follows the land, but patent source and any independently applicable federal protection must be determined parcel by parcel.

Scalia's opinion supplies this holding for the eight Justices supporting it.

*Blackfeet* requires unmistakably clear congressional authorization, and here the County relies on more than alienability or policy inference. *Goudy* construed the General Allotment Act to permit taxation following an ordinary fee patent, while the Burke Act proviso expressly addresses removal of restrictions on sale, encumbrance, and taxation. The Indian Reorganization Act halted future allotments and preserved trust periods but also created a distinct mechanism for restoring land to trust; it does not automatically return every existing fee parcel to tax-protected status. *Mancari* requires compatible enactments to operate together. *Moe* rejects general personal taxing jurisdiction over reservation Indians, but a specific tax on qualifying land does not revive that discarded jurisdiction. *Brendale*'s fractured analysis of tribal regulation of nonmember land supplies no governmental-impact test for this statutory tax question. Remand is necessary because the stipulated record does not establish every parcel's patent source.

The Court holds that congressional permission to tax qualifying fee land ad valorem does not unmistakably authorize a tax imposed on an Indian owner's sale of that land; securing the transactional tax with a lien does not change its legal incidence.

Scalia's opinion states this proposition for all nine Justices.

The annual levy is triggered by ownership and burdens the realty. The sales excise is triggered by a transaction. Under *Blackfeet*, authorization must be clear at the level of the tax actually imposed. A collection lien cannot transform an otherwise unauthorized transactional charge into a land tax, because that device would allow the State to enlarge congressional permission through its own collection law. *Capoeman*'s discussion of taxation after fee patent arose in the allotment context and does not provide blanket authorization for every tax related to land. The Court therefore gives the allotment provisions their demonstrated property-tax scope without creating general state dominion over the owner or transaction.

The Court reaffirms *Goudy* for qualifying General Allotment Act fee land. It applies and preserves *Blackfeet*, *Mancari*, and *Moe*, and distinguishes *Brendale*, whose analysis of tribal regulation supplies no governmental-impact test here. Qualifying General Allotment Act fee land is subject to ad valorem taxation; sales of that land by Indian owners remain outside the identified authorization.

Blackmun agrees that the sales excise is unauthorized but would also reject the ad valorem tax because the asserted authority is not sufficiently clear after later federal Indian legislation.

The *Brendale* impact remand is vacated. The case returns for parcel-specific analysis of the sources of fee patents and relief consistent with both tax holdings. No foreclosure is ordered.

**End of entry: County of Yakima v. Confederated Tribes and Bands of the Yakima Indian Nation, Judgment affirmed and cause remanded with modified instructions, January 14, 1992.**

### Norman v. Reed, Nos. 90-1126 and 90-1435

Judgment affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded, January 14, 1992

*Norman v. Reed*, 502 U.S. 279 (1992), Nos. 90-1126 and 90-1435. Argued October 7, 1991 and decided January 14, 1992, on review of the Illinois Supreme Court. The questions concern continued justiciability, party-name use, signature consequences, and an unresolved state-law objection.

The Court affirms in part, reverses in part, and remands, 7–1. Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, and Souter support the component disposition; Scalia dissents. Thomas took no part because argument preceded his oath.

Souter delivered the opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, and Kennedy. Scalia dissented.

The Court holds that completion of an election does not moot a ballot-access dispute where election results carry continuing party-qualification consequences and the short election cycle presents a reasonably recurring controversy that otherwise evades review.

Souter's opinion supplies this holding for seven of the eight participating Justices.

*Moore v. Ogilvie* recognizes that election restrictions may retain legal consequences after ballots are counted. Here several candidates exceeded the five-percent threshold that may alter the party's qualification in later elections, and the challenged rules operate on a timetable too short for complete review before voting. The dispute therefore remains concrete. The Court decides the federal grounds actually used by the Illinois Supreme Court but does not supply an answer to the unaddressed judicial-candidate objection. *Bacchus Imports* supports returning that consequential state-law issue for authoritative construction. The Court neither revives the unappealed water-district candidates nor orders a new election.

The Court holds that Illinois may require meaningful support within each separate electorate, but it may not categorically prevent authorized candidates from expanding an existing party under its name or disqualify otherwise qualified countywide and city candidates solely because candidates in a different district lack sufficient signatures.

Souter's opinion supplies this holding for the same seven of the eight participating Justices.

*Anderson* requires attention to the burden, the State's precise interests, and whether the restriction is necessary; *Socialist Workers Party* rejects a local signature demand more onerous than the statewide route without adequate justification. Protecting a party from unauthorized appropriation is legitimate, but an absolute geographic name barrier prohibits even authorized expansion and is broader than a permission requirement. Likewise, support in each electorate may be demanded for candidates chosen there, which sustains the suburban exclusion on this record. It does not justify invalidating countywide and city candidates who met their own constituencies' requirements merely because a separate slate component failed. That consequence effectively compounds the signature burden without advancing the stated interest in support for each election. The Court leaves other district structures, cumulative burdens, and actual unauthorized name use open.

The Court applies and preserves *Moore*, *Anderson*, *Socialist Workers Party*, *Jenness*, and *Bacchus Imports*. Authorized party-name expansion and independently qualified slate components receive constitutional protection. Requirements of support within each separate electorate remain permissible on this record.

Scalia would treat the party's county endeavor as requiring support across every election it chose to contest and would sustain the challenged statutory consequences.

Suburban candidates remain excluded for insufficient suburban signatures. Name-based and whole-slate exclusions are reversed. The case returns to the Illinois Supreme Court for the judicial-candidate objection and the consequent determination of party status consistent with the federal ruling. No new election or automatic declaration of established-party status is ordered.

**End of entry: Norman v. Reed, Judgment affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded, January 14, 1992.**

### Molzof v. United States, No. 90-838

Judgment reversed and case remanded, January 14, 1992

*Molzof v. United States*, 502 U.S. 301 (1992), No. 90-838. Argued November 4, 1991 and decided January 14, 1992, on certiorari to the Seventh Circuit, 911 F.2d 18. The question is the meaning of punitive damages in 28 U.S.C. §2674. The provision is part of the Federal Tort Claims Act (FTCA).

The Court reverses the judgment and remands the case, 9–0. All nine Justices—Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas—participated and support that disposition. Thomas delivered the opinion for a unanimous Court.

The Court holds that §2674 bars damages whose legal nature is punishment for culpable wrongdoing, not every compensatory award alleged to be excessive, duplicative, or difficult for the injured person to perceive. Rejecting the federal bar does not establish entitlement under the governing state law or determine an amount.

Thomas's unanimous opinion supplies this holding for all nine Justices.

The FTCA generally makes the United States liable like a private person under the law of the place while withholding *punitive damages*. Congress used an established tort term associated with punishment for culpable misconduct. *Morissette* supports reading borrowed legal language against that settled background, and *Massachusetts Bonding* confirms the distinction between punitive recovery and compensation in explaining the Act's special wrongful-death provision. The Government's proposed *punitive effect* test would authorize federal courts to revise state compensation rules whenever an award seems excessive or imperfectly tied to perceived loss, without statutory criteria. Its concession that compensation may be reasonably approximate underscores the absence of a workable boundary. These claims arise from admitted simple negligence and seek categories labeled compensatory under state law; alleged duplication or unconsciousness does not alone change their legal nature. Wisconsin law must still determine whether future care, lost enjoyment, survival, and offsets permit any recovery. The Court neither authorizes punitive payment nor directs an award.

The Court applies and preserves *Massachusetts Bonding*, *Morissette*, *Muniz*, *Richards*, and *Sheridan*. The FTCA's punitive-damages exclusion uses the traditional category of damages imposed to punish wrongdoing. State-law entitlement and federal classification remain distinct.

The federal punitive-damages classification is reversed. Wisconsin-law entitlement, survival of claims, duplication, and offsets remain for remand; no additional sum is ordered. The case returns for those unresolved state-law and remedial questions, with admitted liability undisturbed.

**End of entry: Molzof v. United States, Judgment reversed and case remanded, January 14, 1992.**
