### United States v. Ibarra, No. 90-1713

Summary merits disposition, October 15, 1991

The Court reviews the Tenth Circuit's dismissal, at 920 F.2d 702, of the Government's appeal from a suppression order. The issue is whether the Government's single, timely motion for reconsideration postponed appellate finality even though it renewed a consent theory the Government had earlier abandoned. Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter participate; the Marshall line remains vacant.

Without recorded dissent, the Court grants certiorari and Ibarra's motion to proceed in forma pauperis, vacates the judgment, and remands. Its per curiam opinion holds that a timely substantive motion asking a criminal court to reconsider an adjudicated question suspends finality under *United States v. Healy* and *United States v. Dieter*, including when the motion renews an abandoned ground. The full appeal period begins when reconsideration is decided. The December 13 motion therefore kept the suppression order nonfinal through January 3, and the January 30 notice was timely.

The Court does not decide successive motions, bad faith, the effect of abandonment on the consent theory, the search, or suppression. On remand, the Tenth Circuit may consider the Government's appeal without any direction concerning its merits.

**End of entry: United States v. Ibarra, summary merits disposition, October 15, 1991.**

### Mireles v. Waco, No. 91-311

Summary merits disposition, October 21, 1991

#### Chronology and Posture

On review of *Waco v. Baltad*, 934 F.2d 214, the Court considers a pleading-stage claim that Judge Mireles ordered court officers to bring a public defender to his courtroom using excessive force. Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter participate; the Marshall line remains vacant.

#### Judgment

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ninth Circuit judgment rejecting dismissal | Affirmed by an equally divided Court, 4–4 | Stone, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy | White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Souter | The judgment stands without precedential effect; ordinary proceedings resume below |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Per curiam order | Court | — | Announces equal division and affirmance | Judgment only; no rationale |
| Opinion concurring in affirmance | Stone | Stevens | Supports affirmance on functional-immunity grounds | Separable operational direction alleged in the complaint |
| Opinion concurring in affirmance | Scalia | Kennedy | Supports affirmance because summary reversal is unwarranted | Vehicle and summary-disposition ground only |

No opinion commands a majority, and the affirmance establishes no controlling rationale. Stone's concurrence preserves immunity for a judicial attendance order but treats a plausibly separable direction to inflict gratuitous physical violence as operational rather than judicial conduct. It decides no constitutional violation, proof, causation, damages, or defense. Scalia's concurrence would not expend the Court's summary-review authority on the case and does not adopt Stone's functional classification. Four Justices would instead classify the challenged command as judicial and reverse.

Existing judicial-immunity doctrine remains unchanged. The Ninth Circuit judgment governs the parties, and the case returns for ordinary proceedings on the surviving claim.

**End of entry: Mireles v. Waco, summary merits disposition, October 21, 1991.**

### Campos v. City of Houston, application A-301

Referred full-Court application order, October 29, 1991

#### Chronology and Posture

The application seeks an injunction stopping Houston's November 5 election or, alternatively, a stay of the Southern District of Texas's October 12 order directing use of the City's 9-5-1 Plan 2. The Attorney General had objected to Plan 2 under §5 of the Voting Rights Act; Plan 3 later received a nonobjection. The application is presented to Fifth Circuit Justice O'Connor and referred by her to the participating full Court. All nine Justices participate.

#### Judgment

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justice | Operative effect |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Free-standing injunction stopping all City elections | Denied, 9–0 | Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | None | Unrelated City contests are not halted |
| Alternative stay of the direction to use Plan 2 | Granted, 8–1 | Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Scalia | Plan 2 implementation is stayed pending further order |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | O'Connor | Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Controls both components | Authority for limited relief and §5 treatment of Plan 2 |
| Dissent in part | Scalia | None | Agrees that the broad injunction should be denied; opposes the stay | Delay, uncertainty, and public disruption |

#### Controlling Proposition and Authority

A legislative plan does not become an independently fashioned judicial plan merely because a court embodies it in an interim order. Scheduling, turnout, and expense do not authorize implementation of a legislative plan subject to an outstanding §5 objection when a nonobjected alternative exists. O'Connor's opinion for eight Justices controls. Applying *McDaniel v. Sanchez*, *Upham v. Seamon*, and *Clark v. Roemer*, the Court concludes that the district court's nonretrogression assessment could not substitute for statutory preclearance or answer the outstanding purpose issue.

The Court reaches only entitlement to limited relief in aid of appellate jurisdiction. It does not decide the §2 claim, discriminatory purpose, permanent districting, Plan 3's ultimate validity, or a categorical rule for every imperfect judicial interim plan.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

“The application presented to Justice O'Connor and by her referred to the Court is granted in part. The portion of the District Court's October 12, 1991 order directing use of 9-5-1 Plan 2 is stayed pending further order of this Court. The application is otherwise denied.” The prohibition on malapportioned Plan 1 remains. The lower court may consider a lawful interim plan and necessary schedule changes; this Court prescribes neither a map nor a sixteen-district structure.

**End of entry: Campos v. City of Houston, referred full-Court application order, October 29, 1991.**

### Zatko v. California, lead No. 91-5052 and 16 grouped dockets

Rule 39.8 filing disposition, November 4, 1991

Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter participate; Thomas does not participate. The matter concerns 17 pending fee motions by Vladimir Zatko and James L. Martin, including the companion caption *Martin v. Mrvos*. It does not place the merits of their underlying petitions before the Court.

By 5–3, White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter deny the motions to proceed in forma pauperis under Rule 39.8. The per curiam disposition finds the current submissions frivolous and the repeated filing histories an extreme abuse warranting discretionary fee denial. Stone dissents, joined by Blackmun and Stevens. Assuming present frivolousness, they would use ordinary fee-waived filing and screening rather than add a financial barrier; they neither invalidate Rule 39.8 nor recognize an unrestricted right to free filing.

The applicants may submit the papers with the required docketing fees and petitions compliant with the Court's rules by November 25, 1991. The disposition decides no underlying petition and imposes no additional prospective filing ban.

**End of entry: Zatko v. California, Rule 39.8 filing disposition, November 4, 1991.**

### Hafer v. Melo, No. 90-681

Merits decision, November 5, 1991

Argued October 15, 1991, on certiorari to the Third Circuit and decided November 5, the case asks whether a state officer sued individually is a “person” under 42 U.S.C. §1983 when the challenged conduct used official authority. Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter participate; Thomas does not participate.

The Court affirms 8–0. O'Connor writes for every participating Justice. A state officer sued in an individual capacity is a §1983 person even when acting through official authority. *Will v. Michigan Department of State Police* governs States and officers sued in their official capacities; it does not convert a personal-capacity action into one against the State. *Scheuer v. Rhodes* and *Kentucky v. Graham* preserve the individual-capacity route, and state immunity does not independently bar it.

The Third Circuit's judgment is affirmed, and the employees' personal-capacity claims may proceed. The Court decides neither whether the dismissals violated federal law nor qualified immunity, other defenses, liability, damages, or reinstatement.

**End of entry: Hafer v. Melo, merits decision, November 5, 1991.**

### Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System v. MCorp Financial, Inc., Nos. 90-913 and 90-914

Merits decision, December 3, 1991

Argued October 7, 1991, on cross-petitions from the Fifth Circuit and decided December 3, the case asks whether bankruptcy jurisdiction or *Leedom v. Kyne* permits a district court to halt ongoing Board proceedings despite 12 U.S.C. §1818(i)(1). Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter participate; Thomas does not participate.

The Court rules 8–0, reversing in No. 90-913 and affirming in No. 90-914. Stevens writes for every participating Justice. Section 1818(i)(1) deprives the district court of jurisdiction to enjoin the two pending administrative proceedings. Bankruptcy jurisdiction does not supersede that express review channel: the Board is not another court for §1334(b), regulatory continuation falls within §362(b)(4), and movement toward a nonfinal order does not itself seize estate property. *Kyne* supplies no premature review where Congress expressly precludes interference and meaningful final-order review remains available.

The injunction against the source-of-strength proceeding is removed, and both Board proceedings may continue through their statutory channels. The Court does not decide the source-of-strength regulation's validity, any final demand, or an actual later conflict with control of estate assets.

**End of entry: Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System v. MCorp Financial, Inc., merits decision, December 3, 1991.**

### Griffin v. United States, No. 90-6352

Merits decision, December 3, 1991

Argued October 7, 1991, on certiorari to the Seventh Circuit and decided December 3, the case asks whether a general verdict on a multiple-object conspiracy must be reversed when one lawful object lacks evidence connecting the defendant and another is sufficiently proved. This uncoordinated decision uses the same start-of-day entering-law baseline as *MCorp*. Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter participate; Thomas does not participate.

The judgment is affirmed 8–0. Scalia writes the Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, and Souter. Blackmun concurs in the judgment, agreeing that *Turner v. United States* governs without joining the opinion's broader historical account.

The Court holds that a general verdict does not require reversal merely because proof is insufficient as to one lawful conspiracy object when another object sufficiently supports conviction under the unchanged reasonable-doubt requirement. *Stromberg v. California* and *Yates v. United States* concern legally unavailable theories; *Turner* governs factual insufficiency. The Court does not decide a legally invalid alternative, failure of proof on every route, an instruction diluting an element, an independent unanimity claim, or harmless error.

The Seventh Circuit's judgment and Griffin's conviction are affirmed. No retrial or acquittal follows on the presented ground.

**End of entry: Griffin v. United States, merits decision, December 3, 1991.**
