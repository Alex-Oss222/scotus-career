### *United States v. Ibarra*, No. 90-1713

Summary disposition, October 15, 1991

*United States v. Ibarra*, 502 U.S. 1 (1991), came to the Court on a petition for certiorari to the Tenth Circuit, 920 F.2d 702. Without argument, the Court considered whether a timely Government motion to reconsider suppression postponed appellate finality even though the motion renewed an abandoned ground. Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter participated. The Marshall line was vacant, and Thomas had not taken the oath.

The Court granted certiorari, vacated the judgment, and remanded by an 8-0 vote. Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter supported the disposition. A per curiam opinion was joined by every participating Justice; there was no separate writing.

The Court holds that a single timely motion asking a district court to alter a suppression ruling renders that ruling nonfinal for appeal purposes until the motion is decided, even when the motion renews a ground the Government earlier abandoned. The full appeal period begins upon disposition. The unanimous per curiam opinion supplies the controlling authority.

The Court applies *United States v. Healy*, which supplies the rule that a timely criminal rehearing request suspends finality, and *United States v. Dieter*, which rejects classifications based on the kind or strength of asserted error. The Government filed one motion within thirty days and appealed within thirty days after denial. Earlier abandonment may matter to the merits, but the motion still sought alteration of adjudicated rights. Making time depend on assessing strength, preservation, or likely success would leave litigants guessing about the jurisdictional date. *Department of Banking v. Pink* is distinct because its filing did not seek substantive alteration.

*Healy* and *Dieter* are applied, and *Pink* is distinguished. No appeal period or preservation rule changes. The timely-reconsideration rule enters law on October 15, 1991. The decision addresses only finality: it leaves bad faith, successive motions, waiver, the renewed ground's possible waiver on the merits, suppression, consent, and the search undecided.

The Tenth Circuit must consider the appeal. The Supreme Court proceeding is closed, and the case returns to the Tenth Circuit for further appellate proceedings.

### *Mireles v. Waco*, No. 91-311

Summary disposition by an equally divided Court, October 21, 1991

#### Chronology and Posture

*Mireles v. Waco*, 502 U.S. 9 (1991), came to the Court without argument on a petition for certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 934 F.2d 214. The question was whether absolute judicial immunity covers an alleged separable command to use excessive force. Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter participated. The Marshall line was vacant, and Thomas had not taken the oath.

#### Judgment

The Court granted certiorari and affirmed the judgment by an equally divided Court.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Judicial-immunity dismissal | Affirmed, 4-4 | Stone, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy | White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Souter | Ninth Circuit judgment remains in place; ordinary proceedings resume. |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Equal-division disposition | Court | — | Affirms without precedent | Judgment only |
| Statement concurring in disposition | Stone | Stevens | Supports affirmance | Separable operational-command ground |
| Statement concurring in disposition | Scalia | Kennedy | Supports affirmance | Summary-reversal objection only |
| Dissent from disposition | White | Blackmun, O'Connor, Souter | Would reverse | General judicial-attendance-function ground |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**Judgment without controlling rationale:** The Ninth Circuit judgment is affirmed by an equally divided participating Court. The disposition establishes no Supreme Court precedent and no controlling rationale.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

There is no controlling treatment of precedent. The separate writings discuss *Stump* and *Forrester* without altering them, and the entering judicial-immunity doctrine remains unchanged.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stone, joined by Stevens, distinguishes an immune attendance order from a plausibly separate command of gratuitous violence, which they classify as operational. Scalia, joined by Kennedy, concurs only because summary reversal is unwarranted. White, joined by Blackmun, O'Connor, and Souter, would classify the conduct at the general level of bringing counsel before the court and extend immunity.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Supreme Court proceeding is closed. The Ninth Circuit judgment remains in place, and the pleaded claim returns for ordinary proceedings. Liability, causation, defenses, and damages remain unresolved.

### *Zatko v. California* and grouped matters, lead No. 91-5052 and sixteen grouped dockets

Fee-waiver motions denied, November 4, 1991

In *Zatko v. California*, 502 U.S. 16 (1991), the Court considered seventeen motions for leave to proceed in forma pauperis under Rule 39.8. No underlying petition merits were before the Court. Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter participated. Thomas took no part; no public reason was stated.

The Court denied all seventeen fee-waiver motions, with leave to pay fees and submit compliant petitions by November 25, 1991. White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter formed the 5-3 majority denying fee status; Stone, Blackmun, and Stevens would allow it. The per curiam opinion is the opinion of the Court. Stevens dissented, joined by Stone and Blackmun.

The Court holds that, when it is satisfied that pending submissions are frivolous and repeated frivolous filings constitute extreme abuse, Rule 39.8 permits denial of in-forma-pauperis status. The Court may require fees and compliant form by a deadline without deciding the merits or imposing a broader ban. The per curiam majority of White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter supplies the controlling authority.

Rule 39.8 makes fee denial discretionary when a present filing is frivolous or malicious. The Court finds that predicate in these submissions and treats the applicants' extraordinary histories as evidence of continuing abuse. *McDonald* and *Sindram* permit targeted measures preserving resources for substantial claims. The Court adopts no numerical threshold and does not make old denials independently conclusive. It denies only the pending fee motions and permits paid filing in ordinary form by November 25. The Court leaves ordinary screening available upon compliance and reaches no underlying claim, conviction, petition merits, or constitutional right to unrestricted free filing.

*McDonald* and *Sindram* are applied as authority for tailored process protection, not extended to a categorical ban. Rule 39.8 remains discretionary; extreme repetitive frivolous use may justify fee denial for identified submissions. Stevens, joined by Stone and Blackmun, argues that filing history should not replace careful review of the present paper and that a financial barrier may exclude a later colorable indigent claim.

No underlying petition was decided. Each applicant may pay and comply by November 25; absent compliance, the submissions do not enter the ordinary paid docket.

### *Hafer v. Melo*, No. 90-681

Merits decision, November 5, 1991

*Hafer v. Melo*, 502 U.S. 21 (1991), was argued October 15, 1991, and came to the Court on certiorari to the Third Circuit, 912 F.2d 628. The question was whether an officer acting through official authority is a “person” when sued in an individual capacity under §1983. Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter participated. Thomas took no part because argument preceded his oath.

The Court affirmed the Third Circuit by an 8-0 vote. Every participating Justice supported affirmance. O'Connor delivered the opinion of the Court, joined by all participating Justices; there was no separate writing.

The Court holds that a state officer sued individually is a §1983 “person” even for acts undertaken through official authority, and state immunity does not independently bar recovery from that officer personally. Plaintiffs must prove the officer's own violation under color of law, and personal immunities remain available. O'Connor's unanimous participating-Court opinion supplies the controlling authority.

Section 1983 reaches a person who, under color of state law, causes a federal deprivation. *Graham* distinguishes an official-capacity claim representing government from an individual-capacity claim seeking the officer's liability. *Will* excluded States and official-capacity damages defendants; its language described litigation capacity, not every act performed with authority. *Scheuer* confirms possible personal liability, and *Monroe* confirms that misuse of official power remains under color of law. The Court of Appeals construed these damages claims as personal. Hafer's firing authority supplies the state-law nexus but does not make her Pennsylvania for every remedy. Legitimate-administration concerns belong to personal immunity doctrine, not statutory personhood.

*Will* is clarified and preserved; *Graham*, *Scheuer*, and *Monroe* are applied; *Ex parte Young* remains unchanged. The individual-capacity coverage rule enters law on November 5, 1991, and immunity doctrine does not change. The constitutional violations, qualified immunity, damages, and reinstatement remain unresolved.

Individual claims may proceed, but no damages or reinstatement are awarded now. The Supreme Court proceeding is closed, and the claims return for ordinary proceedings on elements and defenses.

### *Board of Governors v. MCorp Financial*, Nos. 90-913 and 90-914

Merits decision, December 3, 1991

*Board of Governors v. MCorp Financial*, 502 U.S. 32 (1991), was argued October 7, 1991, and came to the Court on certiorari to the Fifth Circuit, 900 F.2d 852. The question was whether a court had jurisdiction to interrupt two ongoing Board proceedings. Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter participated. Thomas took no part because argument preceded his oath.

By an 8-0 vote on each docket, the Court reversed in No. 90-913 and affirmed in No. 90-914. The source-of-strength injunction is removed, the refusal to enjoin the §23A proceeding stands, and both proceedings may continue. Stevens delivered the opinion of the Court, joined by all participating Justices; there was no separate writing.

The Court holds that §1818(i)(1) bars a district court from enjoining an ongoing Board notice-or-order proceeding except through §1818's channels. Neither bankruptcy jurisdiction nor *Kyne* supplies an alternative where regulatory proceedings are excepted from the stay and meaningful final-order review remains. Stevens's unanimous participating-Court opinion supplies the controlling authority.

Section 1818(i)(1) withdraws jurisdiction to affect Board notices or orders except as provided. Congress separately authorized challenges to temporary orders, review of final orders, and enforcement; this action fits none. Section 362(b)(4) lets governmental regulatory proceedings continue, while §§362(a)(3) and (a)(6) do not stop these nonfinal proceedings before seizure of property or collection. Section 1334 does not alter the result because the Board is not another court and continuation does not displace control of estate property. *Kyne* supplied exceptional review when a statutory right otherwise would be lost. Section 1818(h)(2) preserves meaningful later review here, while §1818(i)(1) directly precludes intervention now. Regulation validity and an actual enforcement conflict remain open.

*Kyne* is confined to the absence of meaningful later review, and *Boire*'s narrow understanding is applied. The statutory-channel rule enters law on December 3, 1991. No agency-deference rule changes.

The Supreme Court proceeding is closed. Both regulatory proceedings may continue through statutory channels; their validity and enforcement remain open.

### *Griffin v. United States*, No. 90-6352

Merits decision, December 3, 1991

*Griffin v. United States*, 502 U.S. 46 (1991), was argued October 7, 1991, and came to the Court on certiorari to the Seventh Circuit, 913 F.2d 337. The question was what effect a factually unsupported lawful object has on a general conspiracy verdict. Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter participated. Thomas took no part because argument preceded his oath.

The Court affirmed the Seventh Circuit by an 8-0 judgment. Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter supported affirmance. Scalia delivered the opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, and Souter. Blackmun concurred in the judgment.

The Court holds that a federal general verdict on a multiple-object conspiracy need not be set aside merely because evidence is inadequate for one legally valid object when another is supported beyond a reasonable doubt. The rule does not govern legally invalid objects, failure on every route, element-diluting instructions, or a preserved independent unanimity defect. Scalia's opinion, joined on this proposition by Stone, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, and Souter—seven of the eight participating Justices—supplies the controlling authority.

*Stromberg* and *Yates* invalidate a verdict potentially resting on a legally forbidden basis because jurors cannot be expected to correct legal error. *Turner* supplies the different rule for factual inadequacy because jurors ordinarily can reject a lawful theory lacking proof. Both conspiracy objects here were legally permissible. The Government conceded no evidence connected Griffin to the DEA object, but sufficient IRS-object evidence satisfied *Jackson*'s rational-factfinder inquiry and *Winship*'s unchanged proof requirement. A trial court should ordinarily withdraw an unsupported route when asked, but preferred management does not itself create constitutional reversal. Legal invalidity, failure on all routes, element dilution, and independent unanimity questions remain open.

*Turner* is applied; *Yates* and *Stromberg* are distinguished and preserved; *Winship* and *Jackson* remain unchanged. The factual-versus-legal inadequacy distinction enters law on December 3, 1991. Blackmun concurs in the judgment, declining broad historical or jury-competence reasoning while agreeing that governing precedent sustains the verdict.

No retrial or acquittal is ordered. The conviction remains affirmed, and Supreme Court proceedings are closed.

Source note: Official United States Reports, 502 U.S. 46–61, read in full; research cutoff September 14, 2026.

**End of entry: *Griffin v. United States*, merits decision, December 3, 1991.**
