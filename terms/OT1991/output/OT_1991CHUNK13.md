### Reynolds v. International Amateur Athletic Federation, No. A-954

Application for a stay, June 20, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Submitted and decided June 20, 1992. Reynolds presented the application to Justice Stevens, the allotted Sixth Circuit Justice, who referred it to the Court under its standing practice. No plenary argument occurred. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at submission and decision.

On June 19, the Sixth Circuit stayed the Southern District of Ohio's preliminary injunction, issued that day after a June 17 hearing. Reynolds seeks suspension of the appellate stay to protect his participation in the United States Olympic Trials, which begin June 20. His underlying action alleges private contract and tort claims following conflicting sports adjudications of a testing dispute. The district injunction extends beyond protection of trials participation. The procedural background is reported at [505 U.S. 1301](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep505/usrep5051301/usrep5051301.pdf) and in the [district opinion, 841 F. Supp. 1444](https://static.case.law/f-supp/841/cases/1444-01.json).

#### Public Action and Judgment

The application for a stay presented to Justice Stevens and by him referred to the Court is granted to the extent necessary to restore the District Court's preliminary protection of applicant's participation in the United States Olympic Trials, pending further order. The order issues without an explanatory opinion. Justices White, O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas would deny the application.

#### Limits and Current-Law Effect

The unexplained interim order supplies specific relief and creates no controlling substantive holding or reusable rule concerning stays, jurisdiction, exhaustion, sports access or harm to third parties. Existing stay and personal-jurisdiction law, the ordinary appellate channel and existing equitable principles retain their force. ### Sawyer v. Whitley, No. 91-6382

Merits decision, June 22, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued February 25, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the Fifth Circuit, [945 F.2d 812 (1991)](https://static.case.law/f2d/945/cases/0812-01.json), which affirmed dismissal of Sawyer's second federal habeas petition. The question is what showing of innocence of a death sentence permits consideration of otherwise barred habeas claims and whether Sawyer makes that showing. He seeks further review of his constitutional claims and appropriate habeas proceedings. Those claims concern alleged suppression of exculpatory evidence and sentencing counsel's failure to present psychiatric records. The prior lower-court competency claim is not separately before the Court. The reported procedural background appears at [505 U.S. 333–376](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep505/usrep505333/usrep505333.pdf).

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Fifth Circuit's judgment is affirmed. All nine Justices support affirmance; five join the Court's rule governing access to review of the barred claims.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Refusal to reopen the abusive Brady claim and successive sentencing-ineffectiveness claim | Affirmed, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | No opposing Justice | The denial of further habeas review remains in place; no new sentencing proceeding, merits hearing or release is ordered. |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Kennedy | White, Scalia, Souter, Thomas | Affirmance | Entire opinion: eligibility gateway, clear-and-convincing burden and record application |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Stevens | Blackmun, O'Connor | Affirmance on broader grounds | Entire concurrence: more-likely-than-not clearly-erroneous-sentence inquiry, including exceptional mitigation |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Blackmun | No other Justice | Affirmance; also joins Stevens | Fundamental fairness is not exhausted by factual innocence; no new categorical death-penalty holding |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Affirmance on a distinct broader gateway | More likely than not that no reasonable sentencer would select death on the corrected record; eligibility and constitutionally relevant mitigation |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Capital innocence as a gateway to otherwise barred claims

**Controlling proposition:** A prisoner seeking review of an otherwise successive, abusive or procedurally defaulted capital habeas claim without the ordinarily required cause and prejudice must show by clear and convincing evidence that, absent constitutional error, no reasonable juror would find the prisoner eligible for death under applicable state law. The inquiry includes the offense, required aggravating facts and other conditions of death eligibility; additional mitigation bearing only on the discretionary choice of punishment does not satisfy this gateway.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by White, Scalia, Souter and Thomas; all five adopt the precise eligibility rule and clear-and-convincing quantum. The four judgment concurrences do not join that rule. Five of nine participating Justices make it controlling without Marks.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an exceptional gateway must remain distinct from the constitutional claim it permits a court to hear. Murray v. Carrier preserves review when constitutional error probably convicted an actually innocent person; Smith v. Murray recognizes a sentencing application while distinguishing factual from merely legal innocence. McCleskey v. Zant preserves the narrow exception alongside cause and prejudice. Zant v. Stephens and Lowenfield v. Phelps distinguish the facts authorizing death from the subsequent individualized choice. The gateway therefore reaches all necessary eligibility conditions, rather than only elements of the capital crime. It does not extend to additional mitigation affecting only that discretionary choice. Lockett and Eddings continue to require constitutionally adequate consideration of mitigation on an available claim; they do not remove the separate obstacle to reopening a barred claim. Treating ordinary sentencing prejudice as innocence would eliminate the cause requirement. The clear-and-convincing showing protects the exception's extraordinary character. The Court leaves independently applicable exhaustion, retroactivity and constitutional merits requirements intact and decides no freestanding innocence claim.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Murray v. Carrier, 477 U.S. 478 (1986): preserved for the ordinary cause-and-prejudice requirement and miscarriage exception; this decision specifies the capital-eligibility branch without changing the crime-innocence standard.
- Smith v. Murray, 477 U.S. 527 (1986): its open capital-sentencing application is specified through eligibility, not discretionary mitigation reweighing.
- McCleskey v. Zant, 499 U.S. 467 (1991): applied; abuse of the writ remains a separate obstacle unless cause and prejudice or the applicable exceptional gateway is established.
- Kuhlmann v. Wilson, 477 U.S. 436 (1986): its plurality's factual-innocence discussion informs the successive-claim setting; no plurality statement is retrospectively converted into a majority holding.
- Dugger v. Adams, 489 U.S. 401 (1989): applied; an error capable of affecting sentencing accuracy does not itself demonstrate the exceptional innocence showing.
- Zant v. Stephens, 462 U.S. 862 (1983), and Lowenfield v. Phelps, 484 U.S. 231 (1988): relied on for the distinction between narrowing eligibility and selecting punishment; their distinct capital holdings are unchanged.
- Lockett v. Ohio, 438 U.S. 586 (1978), and Eddings v. Oklahoma, 455 U.S. 104 (1982): preserved for mitigation on the merits; their protection does not independently reopen a barred claim.
- Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668 (1984), and United States v. Bagley, 473 U.S. 667 (1985): distinguished; their reasonable-probability merits standards are not the clear-and-convincing gateway.

##### Sawyer's additional evidence does not satisfy the gateway

**Controlling proposition:** On the whole record, Sawyer's offered psychiatric and family evidence concerns mitigation, and his impeachment affidavits and caregiver account do not clearly and convincingly establish that no reasonable juror would find the surviving grounds of death eligibility. The Brady and successive sentencing-ineffectiveness claims therefore remain barred; denial of further merits review supplies no new finding that every allegation is false or that death is mandatory.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Opinion of the Court, joined by White, Scalia, Souter and Thomas; five adopt this application of the controlling gateway. Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens and O'Connor independently support affirmance on their broader grounds and do not supply joins in the Court's application rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that these submissions leave a reasonable factual basis for death eligibility. Louisiana requires a valid aggravating circumstance, and its law of principals reaches participation in aggravated arson without requiring that Sawyer personally ignite the victim. The caregiver's account therefore does not erase the significance of the other evidence, including Sawyer's participation and the lighter-fluid evidence. The preceding beating and scalding also support the surviving cruelty aggravator independently of who applied the flame. The affidavits attacking Shano's credibility do not establish that every reasonable juror would reject the core account. This is an assessment of these submissions, not a categorical rule excluding later impeachment evidence. The psychiatric records and childhood evidence may support mitigation but do not negate the necessary eligibility facts presented here. Brady and Strickland supply the underlying constitutional inquiries; the Court does not decide those merits after the gateway fails. Nor does it reopen the accepted procedural classifications and cause determination or direct a new factual hearing, sentence or release.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Brady v. Maryland, 373 U.S. 83 (1963): the suppressed-evidence claim remains subject to the independent procedural gateway; no Brady merits adjudication occurs.
- Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668 (1984): the previously rejected sentencing-assistance claim is not reopened; the added evidence does not replace the gateway with ordinary prejudice.
- Sawyer v. Smith, 497 U.S. 227 (1990): its distinct Caldwell retroactivity disposition remains intact; this event does not revive that earlier claim.

##### Scope and questions not reached

The Court does not determine the merits of the two barred constitutional claims, find the new evidence false in every respect, hold death mandatory, decide a freestanding innocence claim, revisit the prior Caldwell ruling, or resolve a new challenge to the wording of a Louisiana aggravator. The distinct lower-court competency claim was not among the two claims presented in this Supreme Court petition. Its omission creates no new competency holding. The gateway does not itself grant relief even when satisfied.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Effective June 22, 1992, a prisoner seeking the capital miscarriage-of-justice exception to a bar on review must show, by clear and convincing evidence, that no reasonable juror would find any lawful basis of death eligibility. Carrier, Smith and McCleskey had left the precise capital formulation open; this decision specifies its proof requirement and eligibility-only scope. Ordinary merits prejudice, cause, exhaustion, retroactivity and the rules for developing facts on a properly available claim remain distinct. The broader sentencing-selection tests in the concurrences do not control. Five Justices directly join the Court's rule, so no synthesis of separate opinions under Marks is necessary.

Keeney v. Tamayo-Reyes retains Townsend for inadequate factual development of a properly available claim; it does not eliminate barriers to successive or abusive claims. Stringer v. Black and Sochor v. Florida require lawful correction of available weighing errors while leaving independent review barriers intact. Wright v. West changes no general standard of habeas review. Those distinct rules remain unchanged.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stevens, joined by Blackmun and O'Connor, would consider all relevant evidence and require a showing that the death sentence was more likely than not clearly erroneous. Their inquiry includes decisive mitigation and sentencing choices no reasonable sentencer would make even when eligibility remains. They reject both categorical exclusion of mitigation and the heightened proof requirement. Sawyer's proof nevertheless fails their test: the challenged affidavits do not displace the brutal conduct, and the medical evidence does not make this sentence clearly erroneous.

Stone-Zsela also considers constitutionally relevant mitigation, but requires the corrected record to make it more likely than not that no reasonable sentencer would choose death. Evidence supporting another permissible sentence is insufficient. The affidavit does not negate Sawyer's participation or preceding violence, the impeachment does not probably displace the core account, and the medical mitigation does not meet this exceptional showing. He concurs in the judgment alone and rejects categorical exclusion of mitigation.

Blackmun's additional concurrence rejects treating factual innocence as the exclusive concern of fundamental fairness. It changes neither his vote to affirm nor the Court's controlling rule and announces no new categorical death-penalty holding.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court merits review is complete. The ordinary mandate leaves the Fifth Circuit's refusal of further habeas review in force. No new trial, sentencing proceeding, release, stay, execution date or retained Supreme Court proceeding is ordered or established. No later filing or execution is established.

**End of entry: Sawyer v. Whitley, merits decision, June 22, 1992.**

### R. A. V. v. City of St. Paul, No. 90-7675

Merits decision, June 22, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 4, 1991. On writ of certiorari to the Minnesota Supreme Court, [464 N.W.2d 507 (January 18, 1991)](https://static.case.law/nw2d/464/cases/0507-01.json), which reversed dismissal and reinstated count two of a juvenile petition under St. Paul Legislative Code § 292.02. The granted questions concern whether the content-based ordinance violates First Amendment overbreadth and vagueness principles and whether a construction limited to fighting words or imminent lawless action saves it. R. A. V. allegedly helped burn a cross in a Black family's fenced yard; this pretrial review makes no finding of guilt. He seeks reversal of the reinstated ordinance charge. The reported procedural background appears at [505 U.S. 377](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep505/usrep505377/usrep505377.pdf).

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Minnesota Supreme Court's judgment is reversed and the case remanded, 9–0 in judgment. A five-Justice opinion of the Court holds the ordinance facially invalid for substantial overbreadth: its coverage of protected expression is substantial in relation to its legitimate applications.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reinstated ordinance charge, count two | Reversed and remanded, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | No opposing Justice | Dismiss the charge under the facially invalid ordinance; preserve adjudication of independently valid charges. |

Count one, under the separate racially motivated assault provision, was not challenged in this review and is not adjudicated. The remand creates no new offense through construction or severance of the ordinance.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stone-Zsela | White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor | Reversal and remand | Narrow substantial-overbreadth rule, application, limits and remedy |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Scalia | Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Same judgment on a different ground | Content and viewpoint discrimination within assumed fighting words; qualifications for genuine category-related injury, secondary effects, conduct and absence of censorship |
| Additional concurrence | White | Blackmun and O'Connor throughout; Stevens except the categorical account | Joins the Court and disputes Scalia's broader theory | Categorical account, selective injury-based regulation and strict-scrutiny objections; Stevens does not join the categorical account |
| Additional concurrence | Blackmun | No other Justice | Joins the Court | Distinct harms and preservation of different constitutional treatment of different speech categories |
| Additional concurrence | Stevens | White and Blackmun as to criticism of Scalia's selection theory only | Joins the Court | The unjoined remainder rejects categorical absolutes and explains the importance of content, context and the nature of the restriction |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Substantial overbreadth despite the state-law construction

**Controlling proposition:** A criminal prohibition is facially invalid when, even under its authoritative state-law construction, it reaches substantial protected expression relative to its legitimate sweep and that defect cannot be cured by a permissible limiting construction or partial invalidation. St. Paul's ordinance fails that rule because its injury and resentment coverage extends beyond personally abusive provocation of an immediate breach of the peace; the ordinance charge must be dismissed, while independently valid charges remain available.

**Authority:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's opinion of the Court, joined by White, Blackmun, Stevens and O'Connor, supplies five of nine votes for this exact overbreadth rule and application. Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas concur in the judgment on a distinct content-discrimination ground. No Marks synthesis is needed or permitted to enlarge the five-Justice holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that causing resentment does not by itself turn expression into fighting words. Chaplinsky permits a narrowly confined prohibition, while Cohen, Gooding and Lewis establish that offensive ideas and generalized verbal injury do not satisfy its constitutional boundary. Broadrick requires substantial protected coverage before a court invalidates a law on its face. That requirement is met here: religious criticism and hostile debate over race or gender may cause deep anger without personal provocation of immediate violence. The Minnesota Supreme Court's construction binds the Court as a matter of state law, but its repeated injury formulation leaves that protected coverage in place. Calling the coverage fighting words cannot settle the federal question. Nor does its additional reference to Brandenburg's incitement category eliminate the broader branch. The grave alleged invasion of this family's yard does not cure the ordinance's ordinary unconstitutional applications. The Court preserves prosecution under independently valid laws and reserves whether a properly confined prohibition may select particularly harmful instances of genuinely unprotected expression.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire, 315 U.S. 568 (1942): applied within its narrow constitutional scope; generalized emotional injury is not a freestanding fighting-words category.
- Cohen v. California, 403 U.S. 15 (1971): applied; offensive expression is distinct from personally directed fighting words.
- Gooding v. Wilson, 405 U.S. 518 (1972): applied; an authoritative construction must actually confine a criminal prohibition to unprotected expression.
- Lewis v. City of New Orleans, 415 U.S. 130 (1974): applied; a state-law narrowing label does not cure remaining protected coverage.
- Broadrick v. Oklahoma, 413 U.S. 601 (1973): applied; overbreadth must be real and substantial relative to legitimate sweep, with permissible narrowing considered first.
- Brandenburg v. Ohio, 395 U.S. 444 (1969): retained; advocacy must be directed to and likely to produce imminent lawless action to fall within its incitement rule, and that category does not cure the ordinance's separate broad injury coverage.
- New York v. Ferber, 458 U.S. 747 (1982), and Brockett v. Spokane Arcades, Inc., 472 U.S. 491 (1985): applied for authoritative narrowing, facial challenge and available partial invalidation; no judicial rewriting of this offense is ordered.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court does not decide the broader content-discrimination question concerning distinctions entirely within unprotected categories, the separate vagueness theory, guilt on count one, a new threat mental-state standard, sentencing enhancements, or the constitutionality of antidiscrimination laws not before it. Reversal removes count two under this ordinance; it does not immunize independently punishable conduct.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Effective June 22, 1992, St. Paul's ordinance cannot support the reinstated charge because it remains substantially overbroad under its authoritative state-law construction. The Court applies the existing narrow fighting-words, incitement and substantial-overbreadth doctrines. Emotional injury alone creates no new category of unprotected expression. The Court adopts no general system governing distinctions within unprotected speech, and no precedent is overruled. The content and context theories advanced in separate writings remain noncontrolling.

Simon & Schuster preserves scrutiny of content-based burdens on protected speech. Forsyth County preserves its licensing and audience-hostility rules. Burson retains its seven-Justice strict-scrutiny classification and fractured application. None decides the reserved question of selecting a harmful subset entirely within unprotected expression, and none is overruled or enlarged here. The four-Justice content-discrimination concurrence supplies no Court rule or exceptions on that question.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia, joined by Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, would accept the fighting-words construction for purposes of decision but invalidate the ordinance's selection of disfavored subjects and its viewpoint operation. Their proposed rule would permit distinctions resting on the very reason a category may be prohibited, genuine secondary effects, incidental application of conduct laws, or selectivity presenting no realistic censorship risk. They conclude that this ordinance satisfies none of those qualifications. Their ground has four votes and does not control.

White, joined by Blackmun and O'Connor, and by Stevens except on the categorical account, disputes Scalia's extension. He regards the particular harms of racial and similar intimidation as a permissible reason for focused regulation of a genuinely unprotected subset. That position does not save an ordinance covering protected speech. His concurrence also raises strict-scrutiny objections to Scalia's theory.

Blackmun separately warns that treating every speech category alike can weaken protection for core expression while leaving insufficient room to regulate distinct harms. Stevens, with White and Blackmun joining his criticism of the selection theory, rejects categorical absolutes in his unjoined discussion and treats content, context and the kind of restriction as relevant. These positions leave overbreadth as the Court's shared ground and supply no broader controlling rule.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court merits review is complete. The case returns to the Minnesota Supreme Court to implement reversal and dismissal of the ordinance charge. Independently valid charges may proceed under their own elements and procedures. No further Supreme Court act, mandate-issuance date, trial result or replacement ordinance is established.

**End of entry: R. A. V. v. City of St. Paul, merits decision, June 22, 1992.**

### Medina v. California, No. 90-8370

Merits decision, June 22, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued February 25, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the California Supreme Court, [51 Cal.3d 870, 799 P.2d 1282 (1990)](https://static.case.law/cal-3d/51/cases/0870-01.json), which affirmed Medina's convictions and death sentences. Procedural-background source locator: [505 U.S. 437](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep505/usrep505437/usrep505437.pdf). The questions are whether California may require a defendant to prove incompetence by a preponderance at a competency hearing and retain the associated presumption of competence. Medina seeks relief from the judgment based on that allocation. A six-day hearing included conflicting psychiatric and lay evidence and the defendant's serious outbursts; its jury found him competent, followed by a separate criminal trial. No finding establishes actual incompetence or equally balanced evidence.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The California judgment is affirmed, 6–3. Kennedy's principal opinion has four supporters; O'Connor and Souter concur in the judgment and expressly support the narrow burden and presumption conclusions.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Preponderance allocation at competency hearing | Affirmed, 6–3 | White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens | No new competency inquiry or trial is ordered. |
| Presumption of competence as a restatement of that allocation | Affirmed, 6–3 | White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens | The challenged statutory presumption remains available within the stated limits. |

#### Opinion Topology

No complete opinion commands a majority. The two narrow operative propositions below have six express supporters.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Principal opinion; plurality as to its exclusive analytical framework | Kennedy | White, Scalia, Thomas | Affirmance | Entire principal opinion; four supporters of Patterson methodology and its broader rejection of Mathews. |
| Concurrence in the judgment | O'Connor | Souter | Affirmance; express support for both narrow operative rules | Entire concurrence; practical fairness assessment and retained usefulness of Mathews. |
| Dissent | Blackmun | Stevens | Would reverse | Entire dissent; Pate/Drope require the State to bear the risk of an inconclusive competence inquiry. |
| Dissent | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice joins | Would vacate the affected judgment and remand | State persuasion by the preponderance standard at issue after sufficient doubt requires a hearing; retrospective feasibility and conditional retrial remedy. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Defendant's preponderance burden at a competency hearing

**Controlling proposition:** Due process permits California to require a defendant claiming incompetence to prove it by a preponderance of the evidence at an adequate competency hearing with counsel and access to psychiatric evidence. The allocation decides a case in which the evidence is evenly balanced; it does not authorize trial of a defendant established to be incompetent or excuse the hearing when constitutionally sufficient doubt arises.

**Authority:** Kennedy, White, Scalia and Thomas expressly adopt this proposition in the principal opinion; O'Connor and Souter expressly adopt the same California-specific burden rule and adequacy conclusion in O'Connor's concurrence in the judgment. These six Justices expressly support the rule, which controls. No whole opinion has five joins; no categorical exclusion of Mathews from criminal procedure is controlling, and no broader rationale is synthesized under Marks.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that California's full competency hearing provides constitutionally adequate protection despite assigning the defendant the preponderance burden. Pate v. Robinson and Drope v. Missouri require a hearing when sufficient doubt arises and forbid trial while incompetent; they do not themselves settle the hearing's burden. The defendant has counsel, and psychiatric evidence bears directly on present capacity. An inability to assist counsel is itself relevant evidence, while requiring the defense to produce available information can aid an informed determination. The competing concern is serious: the challenged capacity may impair assistance in proving incapacity. The six Justices supporting this rule nevertheless conclude that the existing hearing safeguards make this allocation permissible. A State need not select the procedure most favorable to the accused merely because that alternative is possible. This decision preserves Dusky's functional definition and does not treat competence as an offense element or equate present competence with sanity when the crimes occurred. It decides no higher proof quantum and makes no finding that Medina's jury actually faced equally balanced evidence.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375 (1966): preserved; a possibly incompetent defendant cannot waive the necessary competency inquiry, but the hearing burden is a distinct question.
- Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162 (1975): applied to require adequate protection of the prohibition against trial while incompetent; that prohibition and the hearing threshold remain intact.
- Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960): unchanged; the functional standard requires rational consultation with counsel and rational and factual understanding of the proceedings.
- Patterson v. New York, 432 U.S. 197 (1977): its allowance for state procedural choice is consistent with the narrow result; the principal opinion's exclusive-framework extension commands only four votes.
- Ake v. Oklahoma, 470 U.S. 68 (1985): its protection of necessary psychiatric assistance remains in force; the Court adopts no general restriction of its method.
- Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790 (1952): distinguished; allocating an insanity defense does not itself settle allocation of present trial competence.
- In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358 (1970): unchanged; the prosecution retains proof of every offense element beyond reasonable doubt.

##### The associated presumption states the same permitted burden

**Controlling proposition:** California may retain its statutory presumption of competence at the competency hearing insofar as the presumption means that the party asserting incompetence must prove it by a preponderance. The existence of sufficient doubt to require inquiry does not itself establish incompetence or make that burden-allocating presumption unconstitutional.

**Authority:** Kennedy, White, Scalia and Thomas expressly adopt this interpretation and conclusion in the principal opinion; O'Connor and Souter adopt the same operative allocation and affirmance of the associated presumption in their concurrence. Six support the exact burden-allocating proposition. No broader factual presumption or evidentiary weight is inferred from the judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the presumption adds no independent evidentiary command to the permitted allocation. California's supreme court construed section 1369(f) to place persuasion on the party contesting competence; it did not treat the statutory presumption as psychiatric proof. Pate's threshold protects the right to an inquiry when the court has sufficient doubt. The hearing then resolves a different question under Dusky's functional definition, with counsel and expert evidence available. Requiring that inquiry therefore does not necessarily abolish every presumption governing its outcome. Medina argues that doubt and restrictions on his ordinary decisionmaking make a continuing presumption irrational. Those precautions instead protect the inquiry while capacity remains unresolved; they do not establish its factual answer. Because the six supporting Justices permit the underlying preponderance allocation, they also permit its restatement as this presumption. The Court decides no irrebuttable presumption, separate evidentiary weight, increased burden of proof or authority to proceed despite an actual finding of incompetence. Ordinary constitutional hearing and trial-capacity protections remain operative.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375 (1966): its inquiry requirement is preserved and distinguished from the burden governing the resulting hearing.
- Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960): the presumption does not amend or replace the functional competence standard.

#### Limits and Questions Not Reached

The judgment is an affirmance supported by six Justices, but no whole opinion speaks for the Court. The broad proposition that Mathews is categorically the wrong framework for state criminal procedures receives only Kennedy, White, Scalia and Thomas; it is not controlling. O'Connor and Souter retain Mathews as useful guidance. Their method and the principal opinion's historical approach are not logical subsets from which a broader Marks rule can be extracted. The two narrow operative propositions above have six express supporters and need no such synthesis. No determination of actual incompetence, actual equipoise, harmlessness of an invalid burden, retrospective feasibility, insanity burden, higher proof quantum or other trial error is made.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S. 319 (1976), and Ake remain available on their existing terms; no majority adopts their categorical exclusion from criminal process. Foucha v. Louisiana concerns the grounds and safeguards of psychiatric custody and does not allocate the competency-hearing burden. Riggins v. Nevada protects against unjustified compelled medication and its trial risks; it does not find every mental-health issue governed by the same burden. Both retain their exact force. Wright v. West concerns federal habeas sufficiency review and does not replace the direct-review inquiry here.

Effective June 22, 1992, California’s defendant-borne preponderance allocation and its equivalent competence presumption are constitutionally permissible when the required adequate hearing and protections exist. The previously open allocation question is resolved at that scope. Dusky’s definition, the threshold duty to inquire, the prohibition against trial while incompetent, offense-element proof and separate insanity questions retain their existing operation. No exclusive criminal-procedure due-process method receives five votes; no broader Marks rationale is controlling.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

O'Connor, joined by Souter, retains Mathews as useful guidance and assesses practical fairness. Counsel and psychiatric inquiry supply substantial protection; incentives to cooperate and produce information can favor a fuller competence record, while government persuasion would not necessarily improve overall reliability. They agree with both narrow burden and presumption conclusions, but do not join the principal opinion's broad methodological exclusion.

Blackmun, joined by Stevens, would place persuasion on the State because Pate and Drope demand procedures adequate to protect the foundational ability to participate in a criminal trial. They would reverse without finding actual incompetence.

Once sufficient doubt requires a hearing, he would require the State to establish competence by the preponderance standard at issue and would reject using the presumption to assign equipoise against the defendant. He preserves the existing hearing threshold and Dusky definition and proposes no higher quantum or different insanity burden. The state court would first determine whether reliable retrospective evaluation remains possible; otherwise the convictions could not rest on this process and retrial could occur upon competence. He orders neither acquittal nor indefinite commitment.

### Estate of Cowart v. Nicklos Drilling Co., No. 91-17

Merits decision, June 22, 1992

Argued March 25, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the Fifth Circuit, whose en banc judgment, [927 F.2d 828 (1991)](https://static.case.law/f2d/927/cases/0828-01.json), sustained reversal of the Benefits Review Board's compensation award. Procedural-background source locator: [505 U.S. 469](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep505/usrep505469/usrep505469.pdf). The question is whether section 33(g) reaches a worker whose employer is neither currently paying compensation nor subject to an award when the worker settles a third-party claim. The estate seeks restoration of compensation eligibility on the theory that entitlement requires payment or an award. Cowart was injured on July 20, 1983, and settled the Transco negligence action on July 1, 1985. Nicklos funded the settlement under an indemnity agreement and knew its amount, but prescribed prior written approval from employer and carrier was not obtained. The informal Labor Department notice was not an award.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision. The Fifth Circuit judgment is affirmed, 6–3, on the statutory entitlement question. Stone-Zsela, White, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas support affirmance; Blackmun, Stevens and O'Connor dissent. Kennedy delivered the Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, White, Scalia, Souter and Thomas. Blackmun's dissent, joined in full by Stevens and O'Connor, would reverse.

A worker whose substantive compensation right has vested is a person entitled to compensation under LHWCA section 33(g), even if the employer disputes liability, makes no current payments and is not subject to an award. When the covered third-party settlement is less than the statutory compensation entitlement, section 33(g)(1) requires prescribed written approval from employer and carrier before execution and filing within thirty days; failure to obtain and file required approval terminates compensation and medical benefits under subsection (g)(2), while judgments and settlements at least equal to compensation entitlement require notification rather than that prior approval.

Kennedy's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, White, Scalia, Souter and Thomas, controls. These six Justices adopt the entitlement interpretation, the necessary relationship between the approval and notification subsections, the application and the express reservations. No independently sufficient alternative ground is adopted, and no fractured-decision inquiry is needed.

The Court holds that entitlement concerns an existing legal right, rather than its later acknowledgment or adjudication. Demarest v. Manspeaker requires enforcement of clear statutory language; Congress's addition of subsection (g)(2) expressly makes prior payment or acknowledgment immaterial. Retention of one phrase does not adopt every earlier administrative limitation when the same amendment supplies contrary context. The uses of entitlement in sections 14(h) and 33(f) reinforce that reading: disputed claims can prompt investigation, and third-party recovery can reduce liability before payments begin. Sullivan v. Stroop and Sorenson v. Secretary of Treasury support consistent meaning in that setting. The estate's reading would separate those linked protections. Banks v. Chicago Grain Trimmers Association explains why approval guards against a settlement leaving excessive liability with the employer. Notification still serves independent purposes for judgments and sufficiently large settlements, so that requirement is not redundant. Cowart's vested right brings him within the condition despite the missing award and current payments. The Court does not decide whether employer participation independently satisfies or excuses approval, the gross-versus-net comparison, or the effect on final awards to other workers.

Demarest v. Manspeaker, 498 U.S. 184 (1991): applied; contrary administrative practice and later retention of words cannot overcome clear enacted meaning.

Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc., 467 U.S. 837 (1984), and K mart Corp. v. Cartier, Inc., 486 U.S. 281 (1988): preserved; no deference may override unambiguous statutory text. No new rule of deference to a changed litigation position is announced.

Sullivan v. Stroop, 496 U.S. 478 (1990), and Sorenson v. Secretary of Treasury, 475 U.S. 851 (1986): applied; the recurring statutory phrase is read consistently where surrounding provisions reinforce that meaning.

Banks v. Chicago Grain Trimmers Association, Inc., 390 U.S. 459 (1968): relied on for approval's protection against an inadequate third-party settlement; the statutory function remains operative before acknowledgment of liability.

Northeast Marine Terminal Co. v. Caputo, 432 U.S. 249 (1977), and Voris v. Eikel, 346 U.S. 328 (1953): their remedial-construction principles remain available but do not authorize an exception contrary to this provision's text and structure.

Potomac Electric Power Co. v. Director, OWCP, 449 U.S. 268 (1980): preserved concerning the Board's adjudicative rather than policymaking role; no special deference to the Board supplies a contrary construction.

The estate cannot recover on its asserted payment-or-award prerequisite, and the Fifth Circuit judgment stands. The holding does not mean that every injured person has a vested compensation right or that all settlements require prior written approval. It decides no independently preserved participation, alternative approval, waiver or gross-versus-net calculation issue; employer involvement is not held incapable of mattering in a properly presented case. The Court neither fixes new liability nor decides retroactive effect, reopening of other final awards or a constitutional challenge. Difficult questions concerning deference to a newly formulated litigation position are unnecessary because the statutory meaning resolves the question.

The Board’s O’Leary and Dorsey payment-based interpretations are rejected on this statutory question; those administrative rulings are not Supreme Court precedents. National Railroad Passenger Corp. v. Boston & Maine Corp. preserves Chevron’s requirement that text and structure constrain agency interpretation; its permissive construction of a different statute does not command deference to the Board here. No contrary precedent is overruled.

Effective June 22, 1992, section 33(g)’s entitlement condition attaches when the substantive compensation right vests, before acknowledgment, payment or award. The statutory conditions for low settlements, prescribed approval and filing, and notification for judgments or sufficiently large settlements remain distinct. The previously disputed payment-or-award limitation is rejected. No equitable exception, new compensation category, new deference method or ruling on employer participation is created.

Blackmun, joined by Stevens and O'Connor, would reverse. They read the Act's text in light of its compensation structure, administrative practice and the 1984 amendment's retention of the entitlement phrase while adding the broader notification provision. They would reserve prior approval for workers whose compensation liability was acknowledged or adjudicated and require notice for workers in Cowart's situation. An employer denying liability has different incentives and should not receive a veto that can strip a worker's statutory protection. They regard this construction as consistent with ordinary language and the Act's remedial purpose. Their treatment of the relationship among subsections (b), (f) and (g) challenges a selective literal reading; it does not demand a double recovery. The contrary construction is noncontrolling.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The administrative award remains overturned. No additional settlement, waiver or approval question is adjudicated. Supreme Court review of this statutory event is complete. Ordinary mandate leaves the Fifth Circuit judgment in place. No benefits are restored on the rejected theory, and no new administrative award, damages amount, remand hearing, settlement calculation, later filing, special mandate date or retained Supreme Court proceeding is ordered or established.

**End of entry: Estate of Cowart v. Nicklos Drilling Co., merits decision, June 22, 1992.**

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The 1965 Act does not displace these common-law damages claims

**Controlling proposition:** Section 5 of the 1965 Federal Cigarette Labeling and Advertising Act bars positive enactments requiring particular cautionary statements on cigarette packages or in advertising; it does not preempt the common-law damages claims presented here. The required federal package warning does not itself establish immunity from those claims.

**Authority:** Stevens's opinion on the 1965 Act, joined by White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Kennedy and Souter, and by Stone-Zsela as to this statutory construction and its application: seven of nine. Scalia and Thomas oppose restoration of warning claims under this Act but agree that liability for voluntarily made warranty and fraudulent statements is not barred by its statement requirement.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress's original prohibition concerned required cautionary statements, not every damages obligation connected with cigarettes. Rice supplies the presumption that traditional state police authority survives unless Congress clearly displaces it. The repeated reference to statements in sections 4 and 5 links the prohibition to prescribed warnings. The regulatory setting likewise concerned competing legislative and administrative warnings. English requires attention to the particular federal operation, and Gade rejects automatic displacement from regulatory overlap alone. A federal warning and a state damages remedy therefore do not inherently conflict. The manufacturers answer that damages can influence conduct. Garmon establishes that possibility, but it does not decide what this narrower cigarette provision covers. Congress could distinguish a compulsory statement from compensation for a private wrong, and this text does so. The Court rejects the asserted uniformity defense to these claims. It decides neither design-defect liability nor the operation of later amendments, and adopts no universal immunity from independently established actual conflict.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp., 331 U.S. 218 — applied to retain traditional state remedies absent clear statutory displacement.
- English v. General Electric Co., 496 U.S. 72 — applied; additional tort consequences do not establish displacement without the necessary statutory or conflict basis.
- San Diego Building Trades Council v. Garmon, 359 U.S. 236 — its recognition that damages may regulate is preserved; the labor statute does not determine these cigarette clauses.
- Gade v. National Solid Wastes Management Association (June 18, 1992) — applied only for its shared negative overlap rule; its noncontrolling broader theories are not adopted.

##### The successor clause reaches covered warning and warning-neutralization duties

**Controlling proposition:** For cigarettes whose packages comply with the federal labeling requirements, amended section 5(b) reaches common-law duties and preempts liability requiring additional or clearer smoking-health warnings in advertising or promotion, including a duty forbidding advertising because it neutralizes the mandated warning. Preemption turns on the duty necessary to liability, so a warning claim cannot avoid the clause merely by being pleaded as fraud.

**Authority:** Stevens's amended-Act analysis, joined by Stone-Zsela, White and O'Connor, and Scalia's separate express-preemption analysis, joined by Thomas, expressly support these duties' inclusion and displacement: six of nine at this operative scope. The shared statutory proposition controls directly; it does not depend on treating Scalia's broader preemption position as a join to Stevens's whole opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the amended words include duties enforced through damages. Garmon explains why a legal obligation may regulate conduct even when a court enforces it by compensation. Congress replaced the original reference to statements with requirements or prohibitions imposed under state law and extended the activity description to advertising or promotion. Those words cover the asserted obligation to add health warnings to promotional material. A prohibition on material because it neutralizes the required warning imposes the same kind of obligation from the other direction. Calling that claim intentional fraud does not change the showing on which liability depends. Morales confirms that an express clause must receive its enacted reach, but its airline language does not supply a cigarette-wide immunity. The Court decides only the covered duty and preserves the federal-label compliance condition. State-law liability, causation and damages remain for the lower courts, and genuinely independent duties are addressed separately.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Garmon, 359 U.S. 236 — applied to common-law duties as legal requirements; no labor-specific exclusivity rule is imported.
- Morales v. Trans World Airlines, Inc. (June 1, 1992) — distinguished by statutory language; its recognition of enforceable express boundaries remains consistent with this construction.

##### Voluntarily undertaken express warranties survive

**Controlling proposition:** Amended section 5(b) does not preempt an otherwise valid claim enforcing a manufacturer's voluntarily undertaken express warranty, because the substantive promise is supplied by the manufacturer rather than imposed by a state smoking-health command. The presence of the promise in an advertisement does not itself change that result.

**Authority:** Stevens's analysis, joined by Stone-Zsela, White and O'Connor, supplies the narrow controlling ground under Marks for this component. Blackmun, Kennedy and Souter also vote to restore the warranty claim on their broader ground that the amended Act reaches no common-law damages claims. The voluntary-warranty category is a true subset of that broader nonpreemption rule, with the same result whenever it applies; the two judgment-supporting groups total seven. The broader exemption is not adopted.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that enforcing a promise does not prescribe its substantive content. The warranty claim requires proof that the manufacturer made an assurance that became binding under state law and then failed to honor it. Its terms measure the obligation. State law supplies enforceability, but that does not transform every freely assumed promise into a smoking-health requirement imposed by the State. Norfolk & Western explains that contracts depend on law for their force; its broader exemption from all other law does not erase the narrower words Congress chose here. An assurance printed in advertising is therefore treated like the same assurance made elsewhere. This conclusion neither finds a warranty nor supplies reliance, breach, causation or damages. Those questions remain open under New Jersey law and the existing appellate posture. Marks permits the claim-specific ground shared by the judgment's narrower and broader nonpreemption positions to control without converting the broader position into law.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Norfolk & Western Railway Co. v. American Train Dispatchers Association, 499 U.S. 117 — distinguished; an exemption from all other law does not define this prohibition of state-imposed substantive requirements.
- Marks v. United States, 430 U.S. 188 — applied component by component to the narrow warranty ground; no broader common-law exemption follows.

##### Independently actionable nonpromotional conduct remains outside the clause

**Controlling proposition:** Amended section 5(b) does not preempt claims resting solely on testing, research or other conduct unrelated to advertising or promotion, or concealment claims resting on an independently actionable duty to disclose through nonpromotional channels. This limit does not preserve a disguised demand for additional advertising warnings or establish that any asserted state-law duty actually exists.

**Authority:** Stevens's analysis, joined by Stone-Zsela, White and O'Connor, supplies the narrow component ground under Marks, together with Blackmun, Kennedy and Souter's broader nonpreemption position: seven judgment supporters. The rule concerning claims independently outside the activity boundary is a logical subset of their broader common-law exemption. Scalia and Thomas disagree with restoring the presented consumer-warning theories merely by assigning communication to other channels, while reserving duties genuinely unrelated to advertising.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the clause's activity boundary must remain effective. The relevant inquiry is what the plaintiff must establish as the defendant's legal duty. A claim that rests solely on research or testing practices unrelated to advertising does not impose an advertising requirement. Likewise, an independently existing obligation to disclose facts through a nonpromotional channel does not become promotional because the facts concern smoking. English requires examination of the actual state duty rather than its general association with a regulated industry. Morales does not replace this statute's particular activity words with a boundless effects inquiry. The manufacturers' practical-compulsion objection remains relevant to identifying a disguised advertising duty, but it cannot erase every independent nonpromotional obligation. The Court finds no particular duty to report to an agency and no breach of one. The lower courts must determine the viable allegations and state-law elements, preserving the distinction between the duty asserted and advertising offered as evidence of another wrong.

**Precedent treatment:**

- English, 496 U.S. 72 — applied to examine the particular duty and its actual relationship to the federally regulated activity.
- Morales (June 1, 1992) — distinguished; its airline relation clause and specific fare effects do not abolish this statute's activity limitation.
- Marks, 430 U.S. 188 — applied to the surviving nonpromotional category only; no complete nonpreemption theory is extracted.

##### General intentional falsehood and surviving concealment conspiracies are not barred

**Controlling proposition:** Amended section 5(b) does not preempt liability for intentional material falsehood under the general duty not to deceive, even when the false statement appears in advertising, if liability does not depend on prescribing additional warning content or punishing warning neutralization. Conspiracy to misrepresent or conceal material smoking-health facts survives only to the extent its predicate deceptive duty survives; a conspiracy label creates no exemption of its own.

**Authority:** Stevens's claim-specific analysis, joined by Stone-Zsela, White and O'Connor, is the narrow controlling ground under Marks for these components, with Blackmun, Kennedy and Souter supporting the same restoration on the broader ground that no common-law damages claim is preempted. The protected falsehood and surviving-concealment categories are subsets of that broader rule, giving seven judgment supporters; Scalia and Thomas dissent on the presented amended-Act claims.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the subject of a falsehood does not invariably identify the legal basis of the duty it violates. A claim requiring proof of a knowingly false material representation enforces the general obligation not to deceive. It does not necessarily ask a jury to choose different warning content. The retained federal authority over deceptive advertising supports reading the statute as a warning boundary rather than permission to make false statements. Garmon establishes that damages can regulate, but it does not eliminate the statutory distinction between a smoking-health warning command and ordinary intentional deception. The contrary approach would convert the clause into protection for wrongdoing Congress did not identify. At the same time, relabeling the warning-neutralization theory as fraud cannot revive it. A conspiracy claim follows its predicate: surviving intentional deception or independently actionable concealment remains available, while a prohibited warning duty does not. The Court leaves every state-law element and the merits of the allegations to further proceedings.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Garmon, 359 U.S. 236 — its regulatory insight is preserved without making every damages claim a covered smoking-health requirement.
- Marks, 430 U.S. 188 — applied separately to these surviving claim categories; Blackmun's complete exclusion of common law remains noncontrolling.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

No earlier Supreme Court decision is overruled. Marks operates only at the three specified surviving-claim components; the six-Justice express warning proposition controls without Marks.

Effective June 24, 1992, the original Act leaves these common-law damages claims available, while the amended Act bars covered promotional warning/neutralization duties and preserves the specified warranty, falsehood and independent nonpromotional categories. Each preserved category remains subject to state-law elements. The amended-Act holdings have the component authority stated above; no complete amended-Act opinion commands five formal joins. The claim-specific Marks rules do not make Blackmun's exemption of all common law controlling, and Scalia's wider displacement does not become law. Gade's negative overlap holding and Morales's distinct airline construction remain in force. No generic prohibition of independently established impossibility preemption, field immunity for cigarettes, constitutional advertising rule, design-defect ruling or construction of later amendments is adopted. Before this decision there was no cigarette-specific construction; the Acts' separate boundaries now govern the presented defense.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Blackmun, joined by Kennedy and Souter, reads both clauses as confined to positive regulatory commands and would preserve all the common-law claims. He objects to treating fraud and warnings at different levels of generality. His broader exemption supplies judgment support for the surviving claims but does not become the Court's general rule. Scalia, joined by Thomas on the express statutory analysis, treats warning requirements under the original Act and the presented health-related advertising duties under the amended Act as displaced; he rejects the warranty and general-falsehood distinctions and would include consumer-warning duties that practically compel advertising. They reserve duties genuinely unrelated to advertising. Thomas's partial join does not adopt Scalia's broader defense of implied field or obstacle preemption.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court merits review is complete. On the ordinary mandate the Third Circuit must implement the partial affirmance and partial reversal and provide further proceedings applying the separate statutory periods and predicate-duty boundaries, together with preserved state-law trial issues. The Court awards no damages, reinstates no particular verdict, finds no fraud or causation and decides no design-defect claim. No retained Supreme Court proceeding, new filing, hearing date, mandate-issuance date or later outcome is established.

### City of Burlington v. Dague, No. 91-810

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Contingency does not justify an enhanced statutory fee

**Controlling proposition:** A reasonable attorney's fee under 42 U.S.C. §6972(e) and 33 U.S.C. §1365(d) may not be enhanced above a properly determined lodestar to compensate for the risk of nonpayment when the lawyer's compensation depends on success. The 25% contingency addition is removed; the unreviewed lodestar, fee eligibility and underlying environmental relief remain intact.

**Authority:** Scalia's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by White, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: five of nine. The fee-statute rule is an ordinary majority holding; the dissenters' alternative market approach supplies no controlling component.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that these statutes compensate reasonable legal services in successful litigation without adding insurance against losing. Hensley supplies the calculation of reasonable hours multiplied by a reasonable rate and keeps fee disputes from becoming a second major litigation. Blum and Delaware Valley I establish the strong presumption that this lodestar is sufficient and forbid counting difficulty and skill twice. White's opinion in Delaware Valley II identified the incompatibility between risk enhancement and the statutes' allocation of fees to prevailing parties; the Court now adopts that position. Dague answers that contingent payment differs from guaranteed payment. The statutory fee need not reproduce every feature of private compensation. Blanchard makes the lodestar independent of the private contingent bargain. A market-access exception would still require uncertain risk comparisons and would not reliably isolate an uncompensated service. The prohibition concerns contingency, not adjustment for delay or correction of an unreasonable underlying rate. Those questions and the environmental judgment remain outside this review.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Hensley v. Eckerhart, 461 U.S. 424 — applied for the lodestar, compensation for successful litigation and avoidance of extensive collateral fee litigation.
- Blum v. Stenson, 465 U.S. 886 — applied for the strong lodestar presumption, applicant's burden and prohibition on double counting; its reserved contingency question is answered here.
- Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens' Council for Clean Air, 478 U.S. 546 (Delaware Valley I) — applied for lodestar primacy; no new rule concerning delay or deficient rates follows.
- Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens' Council for Clean Air, 483 U.S. 711 (Delaware Valley II) — White's previously noncontrolling categorical position is adopted for these statutes; O'Connor's limited permission and Blackmun's broader permission remain noncontrolling.
- Blanchard v. Bergeron, 489 U.S. 87 — applied; the private contingent agreement is not the governing measure of a statutory fee.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Effective June 24, 1992, contingency risk cannot support an enhancement under the two reviewed fee provisions. The previously unresolved permission question is resolved categorically; the lodestar method, reasonable-hours/rates inquiry, applicant's ordinary burden and prohibition of double counting remain. The Court applies the same reasonable-fee principles used for similarly worded prevailing-party statutes but adjudicates this award under §6972(e) and §1365(d). It does not rule on a differently worded compensation scheme, delay, an incorrectly calculated lodestar or common-fund fees. No broader rule against all fee adjustments is announced.

Delaware Valley II's categorical position is adopted as a majority holding for these statutes; the earlier judgment is not rewritten and its other components remain. Ardestani's administrative-eligibility rule remains distinct, and Department of Energy v. Ohio supplies no federal-immunity premise for this municipal award.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Blackmun, joined by Stevens, would affirm: contingent payment differs from guaranteed hourly payment, and an otherwise supported adjustment can reflect that economic difference without awarding a losing party fees. O'Connor would permit a narrowly demonstrated class-wide market adjustment but vacate because this record does not support the 25% figure with market evidence. Stone-Zsela also would vacate and remand: the applicant must prove both the relevant market differential and substantial difficulty obtaining competent counsel without adjustment, account for mitigation of nonpayment risk, and avoid case-specific speculation or factors already counted in the lodestar. Neither market-based dissent fixes an amount or requires an enhancement.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court review of the enhancement is complete. Ordinary mandate implementation removes the $49,506.87 addition from the fee judgment and preserves the $198,027.50 lodestar. The environmental relief, fee eligibility and other unreviewed portions remain outside this action. No market-evidence remand is ordered by the Court, and no replacement multiplier is authorized. No retained Supreme Court proceeding, later filing or mandate-issuance date is established.

**End of entry: City of Burlington v. Dague, merits decision, June 24, 1992.**

### Lee v. Weisman, No. 90-1014

Merits decision, June 24, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 6, 1991. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the First Circuit, reviewing [908 F.2d 1090](https://static.case.law/f2d/908/cases/1090-01.json) (July 23, 1990), which affirmed the District of Rhode Island's prospective injunction, [728 F.Supp. 68](https://static.case.law/f-supp/728/cases/0068-01.json) (January 9, 1990). The question is whether school officials may select clergy and include clergy-led invocations and benedictions in official public secondary-school graduation ceremonies. Officials seek reversal; the Weismans seek preservation of the injunction. No companion or consolidation is involved. Procedural background source locator: [505 U.S. 577](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep505/usrep505577/usrep505577.pdf).

Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

Affirmed, 6–3.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Constitutional challenge to official clergy-led graduation prayers and prospective injunction | Affirmed, 6–3 | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter | White, Scalia, Thomas | The First Circuit's judgment is affirmed. The injunction against school officials' authorizing or encouraging the challenged clergy-led graduation prayer practice remains in force. It reaches the official program and does not prohibit private prayer or genuinely private forum speech. |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Parts I–II | Blackmun | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter in full; Kennedy in Part I only | Affirmance | Part I: continued prospective exposure and justiciability. Part II: official sponsorship as an independently sufficient merits ground; Kennedy does not join Part II. |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Kennedy | Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter | Affirmance; independent majority-supported ground | Entire writing: school-directed graduation prayer creates impermissible pressure on objecting adolescents. Does not join the sponsorship-only ground. |
| Concurrence | Souter | Stevens, O'Connor | Affirmance | Entire writing: broader historical and neutrality account; all-religion endorsement, coercion not a universally necessary element, and accommodation must lift a discernible burden. |
| Dissent | Scalia | White, Thomas | Would reverse | Entire writing: historical ceremonial accommodation; religious coercion requires law-backed compulsion; sectarian endorsement remains distinct. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Continued exposure supplies a live controversy

**Controlling proposition:** A student who remains in the same public-school system and faces the challenged official prayer policy at her forthcoming high-school graduation has a live claim for prospective protection even though her earlier graduation is over. Deborah Weisman's continued exposure permits review and preservation of the injunction without deciding her father's taxpayer standing.

**Authority:** Part I of Blackmun's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Souter; six Justices expressly agree at this threshold level.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the claim remains live because the challenged policy continues to bear directly on Deborah's own education. She attended the middle-school ceremony after temporary relief was denied and now attends Classical High School within the same system. The stipulated record makes a clergy-led invocation and benediction at her later graduation likely. Her request therefore concerns an identified future application of the policy, not merely disagreement with a completed ceremony. Engel and Schempp supply the school-prayer interest for which prospective protection is sought; deciding that interest requires no conclusion about an unrelated taxpayer's right to sue. The officials' merits argument that attendance is optional does not erase the event's expected occurrence or Deborah's concrete relation to it. The Court preserves review of this ongoing practice and leaves taxpayer standing unresolved. It announces no exception permitting advisory decisions whenever an expired school dispute might recur.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Engel v. Vitale, 370 U.S. 421, and School District of Abington Township v. Schempp, 374 U.S. 203 — supply the substantive school-prayer interest; neither is expanded into a new taxpayer-standing rule.

##### Officially organized graduation worship is forbidden

**Controlling proposition:** Public secondary-school officials violate the Establishment Clause when they select clergy, request invocations and benedictions, guide the prayers' religious content, and incorporate that worship into the official graduation program. Neither nonsectarian wording nor formally optional attendance cures that official sponsorship; no separate showing of punishment or quantified social pressure is required to invalidate this practice.

**Authority:** Part II of Blackmun's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter; five Justices adopt this independently sufficient ground. Kennedy does not join Part II.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the school may not organize worship as part of its own graduation ceremony. Engel forbids governmental composition and sponsorship of school prayer even when the words are denominationally neutral and observance is voluntary. Schempp likewise rejects excusal from school devotional exercises as a cure. Lemon's prohibition on a primary effect advancing religion applies directly: the principal selected a rabbi, requested prayer, supplied civic-prayer guidance and placed the exercise in the official program. Asking that the prayer be broadly acceptable still makes religious presentation an undertaking of the State. The officials' interest in solemnity does not authorize that undertaking; a secular ceremony can mark the achievement without selecting an approved devotional expression. Marsh concerns legislative prayer in a distinct institutional and historical setting. Mergens protects private religious activity under equal access, not school-directed worship. The Court preserves those distinctions, leaves general accommodation doctrine intact, and decides no question about funding, denominational aid or genuinely private religious speech.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Engel v. Vitale, 370 U.S. 421 — applied to official school-organized worship at graduation; denominational neutrality and voluntary observance do not cure sponsorship.
- School District of Abington Township v. Schempp, 374 U.S. 203 — applied: permission to abstain does not validate the official devotional exercise.
- Lemon v. Kurtzman, 403 U.S. 602 — applied through its prohibition on a primary effect advancing religion; no replacement test and no separate decision under its purpose or entanglement prongs.
- Marsh v. Chambers, 463 U.S. 783 — distinguished: legislative prayer does not authorize this school-directed graduation exercise.
- Board of Education of Westside Community Schools v. Mergens, 496 U.S. 226 — distinguished: private religious activity under equal access is materially different from official sponsorship.

##### The school-directed ceremony also imposes impermissible religious pressure

**Controlling proposition:** The Establishment Clause independently forbids school-directed clergy prayer at a public secondary-school graduation when the school's control and the ceremony's importance place objecting students under substantial pressure to participate, appear to participate, or forgo the occasion. On this record, formally optional attendance and an opportunity to stand or remain silent do not remove that unconstitutional pressure; mere offense alone is insufficient.

**Authority:** Kennedy's concurrence in part and in the judgment, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter; five Justices expressly adopt this independently sufficient rationale. It controls by direct majority support, without a Marks inference. Stone-Zsela does not join this writing.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court also holds that the school may not use the importance and discipline of graduation to press students into a religious exercise. Engel and Schempp recognize the distinctive constitutional dangers of prayer organized for schoolchildren. Here officials selected the religious participant and controlled the ceremony in which classmates stood together. An objecting adolescent could reasonably understand standing or silence during the collective prayer as her participation or approval. Requiring her instead to protest publicly or miss a singular educational occasion does not preserve a meaningful choice. The absence of a disciplinary penalty therefore does not answer the constitutional injury. Marsh rests on an adult legislative setting where attendees can enter and leave without the same educational and social consequences. The Court does not equate every disagreeable message or ordinary demand for courtesy with religious coercion. It leaves the treatment of mature adult audiences and other religious accommodations open; the holding concerns this State-directed exercise and the pressures attending public secondary-school graduation.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Engel v. Vitale, 370 U.S. 421, and School District of Abington Township v. Schempp, 374 U.S. 203 — applied to indirect religious pressure in the secondary-school graduation setting; formal excusal is not dispositive.
- Marsh v. Chambers, 463 U.S. 783 — distinguished by the adult legislative setting and freedom to leave without the same consequences.
- Lynch v. Donnelly, 465 U.S. 668 — its minimum prohibition on coerced religious support or participation is applied, without extending its display holding.
- County of Allegheny v. ACLU, 492 U.S. 573 — the present decision does not reopen the display judgments or settle every dispute about accommodation.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Effective June 24, 1992, official organization of the identified secondary-school graduation worship violates the Establishment Clause independently of a separate showing of coercion, and the school's indirect pressure supplies a second independently sufficient ground. Before this decision Engel, Schempp and Lemon governed school worship, but their precise application to this graduation practice was unresolved. Both stated applications now control, each with five Justices. Each ground has express majority support, so no inference under Marks is necessary to identify controlling law.

No general replacement establishment test, rule against mere offense, quantitative pressure scale, funding rule or universal accommodation rule is adopted. Lemon remains in force; its purpose and entanglement prongs are not independently adjudicated. Adult legislative prayer and genuinely private religious speech remain governed by their own authorities.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Souter, joined by Stevens and O'Connor, adds a broader explanation that the Establishment Clause reaches nonpreferential religious endorsement and that coercion is not universally necessary; his further accommodation account requires a discernible burden on religious exercise. Those broader propositions are not additional holdings of this decision.

Scalia, joined by White and Thomas, would reverse: the historical tradition of nonsectarian ceremonial prayer and absence of law-backed compulsion distinguish graduation from mandatory classroom worship. The dissent treats respectful silence as civility, not compelled observance, while preserving a separate objection to sectarian governmental endorsement.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The First Circuit's judgment is affirmed. The existing prospective injunction against school officials' authorizing or encouraging the challenged clergy-led graduation prayer practice remains in force. It reaches the official program and does not prohibit private prayer or genuinely private forum speech.

Supreme Court merits review is complete. No further Supreme Court hearing, remand program, altered institutional practice or retained enforcement proceeding is ordered. Future disputes require actual filings; no such filing is established.

**End of entry: Lee v. Weisman, merits decision, June 24, 1992.**

### Doggett v. United States, No. 90-857

Merits decision, June 24, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 9, 1991; reargued February 24, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit, reviewing [906 F.2d 573](https://static.case.law/f2d/906/cases/0573-01.json) (July 19, 1990), which affirmed the conviction following denial of the speedy-trial motion and a conditional guilty plea. The question is whether extraordinary negligent post-indictment delay violates the Sixth Amendment without particularized proof of trial prejudice. The reargument also raised an independent asserted right to repose; the Court does not resolve that separate theory. Doggett seeks reversal and dismissal. Procedural background source locator: [505 U.S. 647](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep505/usrep505647/usrep505647.pdf).

At the October 9, 1991 argument, Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter were seated and participated; Thomas had not yet taken his October 23 oath. The February 24, 1992 reargument brought the case before all nine Justices, including Thomas. All nine participated in the June 24 decision.

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded, 6–3.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Preserved Sixth Amendment challenge to post-indictment delay and resulting conditional conviction | Reversed and remanded, 6–3 | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter | O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas | Reverse the Eleventh Circuit's judgment and remand through that court for dismissal of the indictment and consequent vacatur of the conditional conviction and sentence. The constitutional violation is established; no additional trial-prejudice finding or sentence-only adjustment is authorized. |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Parts I–III | Souter | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy | Reversal and remand | Entire opinion: conditional plea, Sixth Amendment scope, Barker application and dismissal remedy. |
| Dissent | O'Connor | Writing alone | Would affirm | Full Barker balance; no proved defense harm, custody or anxiety, and possible impairment alone insufficient under Loud Hawk. |
| Dissent | Thomas | Scalia | Would affirm | Entire writing: speedy-trial protection requires accusation-related liberty impairment; defense erosion or repose alone insufficient. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### An outstanding indictment protects fair adjudication before arrest

**Controlling proposition:** Once an indictment or other official accusation triggers the Sixth Amendment, the speedy-trial inquiry must consider impairment of the defense even if the accused remains free and unaware of the charge. Absence of incarceration or anxiety eliminates those possible injuries on this record, but it does not exclude defense impairment from the Barker balance.

**Authority:** Part II of Souter's opinion for the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens and Kennedy; six Justices adopt the scope rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an outstanding indictment brings the fairness of the eventual trial within the speedy-trial guarantee. Barker identifies defense impairment as a protected interest distinct from detention and anxiety, and Moore rejects a rigid insistence upon affirmative proof of trial prejudice. The Government's narrower account would remove that protection whenever an accused remained unaware and unrestricted, although the pending prosecution could still erode his ability to defend himself. Marion addresses delay before formal accusation; MacDonald and Loud Hawk address periods outside an operative prosecution and the consequences of justified proceedings. Those decisions do not erase Barker's protection after accusation. Doggett's indictment remained pending throughout the delay, so his defense interest must enter the balance even though two other kinds of injury are absent. The Court does not constitutionalize a limitations period running from the offense, decide an independent right to repose, or withdraw due process protection against unfair delay before or after accusation.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Barker v. Wingo, 407 U.S. 514 — applied: fair adjudication is an independent protected interest in the existing four-factor inquiry.
- Moore v. Arizona, 414 U.S. 25 — applied: affirmative proof of trial prejudice is not indispensable in every claim.
- United States v. Marion, 404 U.S. 307 — distinguished as to preaccusation delay; its accusation boundary remains effective.
- United States v. MacDonald, 456 U.S. 1 — distinguished as to periods after dismissal; its separate due process protection is preserved.
- United States v. Loud Hawk, 474 U.S. 302 — distinguished as to absence of an operative prosecution and justified interlocutory delay; no categorical restriction to custody or anxiety is adopted.

##### Extraordinary negligent delay and unrebutted impairment require dismissal here

**Controlling proposition:** Under Barker, extraordinary post-indictment delay caused by sustained governmental negligence can violate the Sixth Amendment without identified trial prejudice when the accused did not knowingly acquiesce and the resulting inference of defense impairment is not persuasively rebutted. The eight-and-one-half-year delay here, including approximately six avoidable years while Doggett lived openly, establishes that violation; Strunk requires dismissal of the indictment and vacatur of the conditional conviction, not a sentence adjustment.

**Authority:** Part III of Souter's opinion for the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens and Kennedy; six Justices adopt the rule, application and dismissal consequence.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Government's prolonged neglect cannot be excused merely because Doggett cannot identify everything time has destroyed. Barker first requires delay sufficient to prompt examination and then weighs its duration, reasons, assertion of the right and prejudice together. Its warning that forgotten evidence can rarely be identified explains why presumptive impairment matters beyond the initial threshold. Negligence weighs less heavily than deliberate obstruction, but its weight grows when it persists for years without adequate justification. The accepted findings establish that ordinary investigation could have located Doggett while he lived openly, and that he did not know of the indictment before arrest. His prompt assertion therefore supplies no knowing acquiescence. Moore forecloses treating particularized prejudice as an extra mandatory element. The Government's answers to specific allegations of harm do not persuasively rebut the accumulated inference here. Diligent pursuit, justified obstacles, evasion or materially shorter neglect may yield another result. Strunk supplies dismissal once this constitutional violation is established; no fixed deadline or general transfer of the ultimate constitutional burden is announced.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Barker v. Wingo, 407 U.S. 514 — applied and specified for extraordinary negligent delay; the threshold inquiry remains distinct from the ultimate balance and knowing acquiescence remains relevant.
- Moore v. Arizona, 414 U.S. 25 — applied against a categorical actual-prejudice prerequisite.
- United States v. Loud Hawk, 474 U.S. 302 — preserved for the significance of justified delay and the insufficiency of possible prejudice considered alone.
- Strunk v. United States, 412 U.S. 434 — applied: dismissal and vacatur, rather than sentence credit or a new trial, implement an established speedy-trial violation.

##### The express conditional plea preserves effective review

**Controlling proposition:** A guilty plea entered under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 11(a)(2), with the Government's express consent to reservation of the adverse speedy-trial ruling, preserves that claim and its defense-impairment argument for appellate review. Doggett's plea does not waive the claimed consequence of unconstitutional delay merely because he ultimately admitted guilt.

**Authority:** Part I of Souter's opinion for the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens and Kennedy; six Justices adopt this threshold application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Government must honor the appellate reservation it accepted. Rule 11(a)(2) permits a conditional plea preserving review of an adverse pretrial ruling. Doggett's agreement expressly reserved the denial of his motion to dismiss for post-indictment constitutional delay, and the Government consented to that reservation. The impairment claim was part of that motion. Broce addresses the consequences of an ordinary guilty plea; it does not transform an expressly conditional plea into an unconditional surrender of the very issue reserved. Accepting the Government's position would permit an appeal in name while disabling the principal argument supporting it. The Court therefore reviews the preserved speedy-trial issue as effectively as it could have been reviewed after trial. This application does not excuse an unreserved objection or reopen the factual admissions establishing guilt. Relief follows because the preserved constitutional claim succeeds and Strunk requires the conviction to be set aside with dismissal of the prosecution.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 11(a)(2) — applied according to the express reservation and prosecutorial consent; no new exception to plea waiver.
- United States v. Broce, 488 U.S. 563 — distinguished: an unconditional plea's consequences do not defeat this expressly preserved issue.
- Strunk v. United States, 412 U.S. 434 — applied to the consequence of success on the preserved constitutional claim.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Effective June 24, 1992, Barker's existing four-factor balance expressly reaches extraordinary negligent post-indictment delay with unrebutted presumptive defense impairment despite an accused's freedom and ignorance. The threshold delay needed to trigger inquiry is distinct from the growing weight of presumed impairment in the ultimate balance. Negligence remains less culpable than deliberate obstruction but weighs increasingly against the Government as unjustified delay persists. No fixed time limit, automatic presumption of constitutional violation, universal prosecutorial burden transfer, rule governing preaccusation delay or independent right of repose is created. Strunk's dismissal consequence and Rule 11(a)(2)'s express reservation operate without change. Six Justices support the Court's entire opinion; no fractured-decision or Marks issue arises.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

O'Connor dissents separately. Applying Barker to this record, she finds no demonstrated defense impairment, no detention and no anxiety about an unknown charge. Loud Hawk's concern that delay can weaken the prosecution as well as the defense prevents speculative harm from deciding her balance. Thomas, joined by Scalia, dissents on scope: the speedy-trial guarantee protects accusation-related liberty harms, and an entirely unaware, unrestricted accused has not suffered the kind of injury that calls Barker into operation. They would not treat defense erosion or disruption of later settled life as independent speedy-trial injuries; limitations statutes and due process perform distinct work. Both dissents would affirm and neither controls.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Eleventh Circuit's judgment is reversed. The case is remanded through that court for dismissal of the indictment and consequent vacatur of the conditional conviction and sentence. The constitutional violation is established; no additional trial-prejudice finding or sentence-only adjustment is authorized.

Supreme Court merits review is complete. The implementing lower-court acts remain to be performed. No retrial, new factual hearing, sentencing adjustment, future Supreme Court date or retained proceeding is ordered.

**End of entry: Doggett v. United States, merits decision, June 24, 1992.**
