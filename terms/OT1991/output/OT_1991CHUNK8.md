## Decisions and Dispositions

### Barker v. Kansas, No. 91-611

Merits decision, April 21, 1992

The Court reverses the judgment of the Supreme Court of Kansas and remands, 9–0. Kansas may not tax the military retirement benefits at issue while exempting comparable state and local retirement benefits.

Argued March 3, 1992, the case comes on certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1257 from the Kansas Supreme Court, 249 Kan. 186, 815 P.2d 46 (July 12, 1991), which affirmed summary judgment for the State. Certiorari was granted before argument. The question is whether Kansas may tax federal military retirement benefits while exempting comparable state and local retirement benefits. Military retirees and, where applicable, their spouses challenged taxes paid for 1984–1989 in two class actions seeking declaratory and injunctive relief and refunds. Kansas exempted state and local pensions and certain federal retirement benefits but taxed military retired pay. The State relied chiefly on retained service status, discipline, employment restrictions, and possible recall to characterize that pay as current compensation for reduced current services. Benefits were calculated by rank and completed years of service. Because the Kansas courts upheld the tax, they did not decide the remaining retrospective-relief questions. The record establishes no present individual refund amount. The [Kansas Supreme Court opinion](https://static.case.law/kan/249/html/0186-01.html) sets out the judgment below and the unresolved relief issues.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision. Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas vote both to reverse the retirement-tax discrimination ruling and to remand for proceedings consistent with the nondiscrimination holding, without a fixed refund award. Justice White delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas. Justice Stevens files a concurrence, joined by Thomas; both also join the Court’s opinion in full.

For 4 U.S.C. §111, the military retirement benefits at issue are deferred compensation for past service, comparable to exempt Kansas state and local retirement benefits. Continued military status, restrictions and possible recall do not justify taxing the entire military retirement stream while exempting those comparable benefits; the asserted difference must directly relate to and justify the unequal tax treatment. White’s Opinion of the Court supplies this controlling rule with the votes of all nine Justices. The Stevens concurrence does not limit either Stevens’s or Thomas’s join.

The Court holds that Kansas has not identified a significant difference that justifies its unequal taxation. Davis applies section 111 to retirement compensation and requires the unequal treatment to relate directly to a meaningful difference between the compared classes. Phillips Chemical supplies that justification requirement. Military retired pay principally depends on completed years of active service and the rank attained, rather than duties actually performed after retirement. Continued status and possible recall do not explain why that entire pension should be taxed when comparable state pensions are exempt. Tyler addressed statutory eligibility for a pay increase; it did not classify retirement benefits for every legal purpose. McCarty expressly left the current-pay characterization unresolved, and Congress subsequently permitted state treatment of retirement pay as marital property. Federal and Kansas treatment of retirement pay for retirement-account contributions further undermines the asserted current-service distinction. The Court decides the section 111 comparison, leaving compensation specifically tied to new service and preserved retrospective-remedy questions for appropriate proceedings.

Davis v. Michigan Department of Treasury, 489 U.S. 803, is applied: retirement income receives §111 protection, and a tax-relevant significant difference must justify unequal treatment.

Phillips Chemical Co. v. Dumas Independent School District, 361 U.S. 376, is applied through Davis: the difference must directly relate to and justify the disparity.

United States v. Tyler, 105 U.S. 244, retains its statutory pay-increase result; continuing military status does not establish current compensation for all purposes.

McCarty v. McCarty, 453 U.S. 210, is distinguished because it reserved the characterization question. The Uniformed Services Former Spouses’ Protection Act displaced its community-property prohibition, and no surviving rule authorizes this tax disparity.

Davis therefore governs the military retirement payments at issue: past-service rank and tenure determine the §111 comparison, and formal continuing status or recall does not justify Kansas’s source-based disparity. The decision creates no automatic refund sum or rule resolving every consequence of military retirement. Other pay actually tied to new service, other consequences of military status, alternative materially different tax classifications, and preserved retroactivity and refund questions remain undecided. Other constitutional and state-law claims supply no independent controlling holding. McKesson and Beam are not modified.

Stevens, joined by Thomas, accepts Davis as controlling but maintains that its comparison improperly treats a benefit for state employees as discrimination against federal retirees who bear the ordinary tax. He identifies congressional correction as available. His concurrence does not limit the unanimous §111 holding.

Merits review is complete. The case returns to the Kansas courts for proceedings consistent with the nondiscrimination holding, including properly preserved retrospective-relief questions. Kansas must comply with §111’s nondiscrimination rule. The Court awards no refund amount.

**End of entry: Barker v. Kansas, Merits decision, April 21, 1992.**

### United States Department of Energy v. Ohio, Nos. 90-1341 and 90-1517

Merits decision, April 21, 1992

The Court reverses the judgment and remands the consolidated action. None of the four provisions invoked by Ohio clearly permits the punitive fines sought from the Department of Energy for completed pollution violations. The Court’s rejection of the Resource Conservation and Recovery Act’s federal-facilities route is unanimous; its rejection of the other three routes and the consolidated reversal and remand are 6–3.

Argued December 3, 1991, the consolidated cases come on certiorari and cross-certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) from the Sixth Circuit’s interlocutory review under §1292(b), 904 F.2d 1058 (1990), of the district court’s ruling, 689 F. Supp. 760 (S.D. Ohio 1988). The question is whether the invoked citizen-suit or federal-facilities provisions of the Clean Water Act (CWA) and the Resource Conservation and Recovery Act (RCRA) waive federal immunity from punitive fines for completed pollution violations. A waiver is Congress’s consent to subject the United States to the asserted liability.

Ohio sued DOE over pollution at the Fernald uranium-processing facility. Settlement resolved most substantive claims but left disputed punitive civil penalties, with the stipulated amounts contingent on lawful liability. One technical-study claim was stayed. DOE conceded enacted permitting obligations and coercive fines enforcing prospective orders. The Sixth Circuit found a waiver under the CWA federal-facilities and RCRA citizen-suit provisions, rejected the RCRA federal-facilities route, and did not reach the CWA citizen-suit route. DOE’s No. 90-1341 challenges the waivers the Sixth Circuit found. Ohio’s No. 90-1517 seeks relief under the RCRA federal-facilities provision and preserves the CWA citizen-suit alternative. The Court decides waiver, without deciding the settled allegations or a free-standing amount of damages. The [Sixth Circuit opinion](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F2/904/904.F2d.1058.89-3329.html) sets out the judgment below and the four statutory routes.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision. In No. 90-1341, Stone, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas vote to reverse the CWA federal-facilities and RCRA citizen-suit punitive-waiver rulings; White, Blackmun, and Stevens oppose that disposition, making the vote 6–3. In No. 90-1517, Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas unanimously sustain rejection of the RCRA federal-facilities waiver. Stone, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas reject the alternative CWA citizen-suit waiver, over the opposition of White, Blackmun, and Stevens, 6–3. The consolidated reversal and remand likewise has the votes of Stone, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas; White, Blackmun, and Stevens would retain the three broader waiver routes.

Justice Souter delivers the Opinion of the Court. Stone, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas join Parts I, II-A, II-B, and III. Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas join Part II-C, addressing RCRA federal facilities. Justice White files an opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by Blackmun and Stevens. They join Part II-C but reject the remaining no-waiver holdings.

The CWA and RCRA citizen-suit provisions authorize proceedings against the United States but do not unequivocally expose it to the punitive civil penalties incorporated from their separate enforcement provisions. Those incorporations include the limits of the penalty provisions, whose generally defined liable persons exclude the United States. Part II-A of Souter’s opinion supplies this controlling rule, joined by Stone, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas. Its six votes control; White, Blackmun, and Stevens dissent on this issue.

The Court holds that permission to sue the United States does not by itself answer which monetary remedies Congress authorized. Mitchell requires unequivocal consent, and Nordic Village applies that requirement to the particular monetary liability sought. The citizen-suit clauses expressly include the United States among possible defendants, but their civil-penalty remedies arise through references to separate provisions. Engel explains that incorporation carries the incorporated provision’s limits as well as its affirmative authority. Here the relevant general definitions include several governmental bodies but omit the United States. Elsewhere Congress expressly extended special definitions throughout whole sections or subchapters; the citizen-suit language does not make that broader extension. Ohio’s competing reading would transfer a local defendant description into a separate remedial limitation. The enacted text does not clearly require that transfer. This construction leaves both clauses useful: punitive penalties remain available against covered defendants, and proceedings against federal agencies retain their conceded compliance function. The Court leaves other express waivers and later enactments outside this decision.

United States v. Mitchell, 445 U.S. 535, is applied to require unequivocal consent to the asserted federal liability.

United States v. Nordic Village, Inc., decided February 25, 1992, is applied: a plausible narrower construction defeats asserted consent to the particular affirmative monetary remedy.

Engel v. Davenport, 271 U.S. 33, is applied because statutory incorporation includes the referenced provision’s limitations.

Ruckelshaus v. Sierra Club, 463 U.S. 680, is applied to keep the waiver within what the enacted language requires.

McMahon v. United States, 342 U.S. 25, is likewise applied to prevent enlargement of a waiver beyond that language.

The CWA federal-facilities provision’s submission to requirements, process and sanctions does not unequivocally waive immunity from the punitive fines sought for completed violations. In its statutory context, sanctions directed to enforcing process support coercive compliance relief without clearly adding retrospective punishment. Part II-B.1 of Souter’s opinion supplies this controlling rule, joined by Stone, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas. Its six votes control; White, Blackmun, and Stevens dissent.

The Court holds that the word sanctions must be read in the enforcement setting Congress supplied. Penfield distinguishes measures compelling obedience from punishment for completed conduct; Hicks likewise recognizes that a conditional sanction can secure compliance. The CWA twice links sanctions with process, while separately listing substantive and procedural requirements. That arrangement points toward measures giving effect to orders and proceedings rather than every penalty for violating substantive law. Ohio correctly observes that sanctions can include punishment in other settings. But a term capable of both meanings does not establish unequivocal consent to the broader one. Shell Oil requires attention to context, and the repeated process pairing supplies a reasonable narrower reading here. Mitchell’s waiver rule and Nordic Village’s remedy-specific application therefore preclude the asserted punitive liability on this language. The Court does not excuse federal facilities from their enacted pollution duties. Prospective orders and conceded coercive enforcement remain distinct, and no particular coercive sanction or settled pollution allegation is adjudicated.

Penfield Co. of California v. SEC, 330 U.S. 585, is applied to distinguish coercive compliance sanctions from punishment.

Hicks v. Feiock, 485 U.S. 624, is applied to explain the conditional character of coercive sanctions; the Court does not decide a contempt case here.

Shell Oil Co. v. Iowa Department of Revenue, 488 U.S. 19, is applied because statutory context determines the relevant meaning.

Mitchell’s requirement of unequivocal consent and Nordic Village’s application to the particular monetary remedy defeat the claimed waiver where punitive liability remains ambiguous.

State-law punitive penalties do not arise under federal law for the CWA federal-facilities waiver merely because EPA approved the state pollution program. The provision’s qualified reference to civil penalties, read with its coercive-enforcement language and the separate penalty definitions, does not unequivocally authorize the requested punitive fines under either asserted source. Part II-B.2 of Souter’s opinion supplies this controlling rule, joined by Stone, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas. Its six votes control; White, Blackmun, and Stevens dissent.

The Court holds that federal approval does not convert Ohio’s penalty statute into federal law for this waiver. Gully explains that a state-law claim can retain its state source even when federal law permits the State to act. International Bridge likewise distinguishes congressional approval from creation of the underlying right. Congress’s use of familiar statutory language supports that distinction here. Arkansas v. Oklahoma recognizes the CWA’s federal and state partnership in pollution control, but it does not decide consent to state punitive fines. Ohio’s broader reading cannot remove the provision’s separate limitation to penalties arising under federal law. Nor does that phrase independently create a federal punitive remedy: the incorporated federal penalty provision still excludes the United States from its liable persons. The express reference to civil penalties creates tension with the narrower coercive language, but does not resolve it unequivocally. Under Mitchell and Nordic Village, that unresolved tension cannot enlarge the waiver. The Court decides only the invoked provisions and requested penalties.

Gully v. First National Bank in Meridian, 299 U.S. 109, is applied by statutory analogy: federal permission does not make a state-law claim arise under federal law.

International Bridge Co. v. New York, 254 U.S. 126, is applied because congressional approval does not itself create the state-law right.

ICC v. Brotherhood of Locomotive Engineers, 482 U.S. 270, is applied for the established construction that familiar statutory language may carry into another statute.

Arkansas v. Oklahoma, decided February 26, 1992, is distinguished: the federal character of permit administration does not determine the scope of the punitive waiver or convert every state penalty into federal law.

Mitchell’s consent rule and Nordic Village’s treatment of the particular monetary remedy prevent uncertain implications from expanding clearer, narrower consent.

RCRA’s federal-facilities provision does not unequivocally waive federal immunity from punitive fines for past violations. Its submission to substantive and procedural requirements, including permits, reporting, injunctive relief and sanctions enforcing that relief, reaches compliance mechanisms without clearly authorizing retrospective penalties. Part II-C of Souter’s opinion supplies this controlling rule, joined by Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas. All nine Justices join the holding.

The Court holds that the RCRA facilities text does not authorize the punitive recovery Ohio seeks. The specific examples concern permits and reports used to assess compliance, or injunctions and sanctions used to secure future obedience. The final sentence again links process and sanctions to enforcement of injunctive relief. None identifies a mechanism punishing completed violations. Mitzelfelt’s reading gives effect to all requirements while recognizing that substantive obligations and the means of punishing their breach are different questions. Ohio invokes Hancock’s discussion of language needed to subject federal facilities to state permitting procedures. But Hancock did not equate full substantive and procedural compliance with consent to every monetary enforcement device. The amended text expressly reaches injunctions without comparably identifying punitive fines. Mitchell requires that additional consent to be clear. This construction leaves the enacted compliance duties and conceded coercive remedies effective. It does not resolve other express waivers, later amendments, or the merits of the settled pollution claims.

Mitzelfelt v. Department of Air Force, 903 F.2d 1293, is adopted on this point: requirements can encompass standards and their implementation without punitive measures.

Hancock v. Train, 426 U.S. 167, is distinguished because subjection to permitting procedures does not determine the scope of punitive remedies. The amended statutory language receives its enacted effect.

EPA v. California, 426 U.S. 200, remains relevant as pre-amendment statutory context and does not authorize disregard of the later express compliance duties.

Mitchell’s requirement of unequivocal consent is applied because that consent to punitive liability does not appear in the specified language governing enforcement of injunctive relief.

These holdings leave enacted compliance duties and conceded coercive remedies effective. Citizen-suit authorization to name the United States as a defendant does not expand incorporated penalty definitions. The CWA’s process-and-sanctions language and qualified civil-penalty clause do not supply the requested consent, and EPA approval alone does not make a state penalty federal law. RCRA’s federal-facilities language reaches compliance and enforcement of injunctions without punitive liability. Nordic Village is applied to the remedies sought here; it is not broadened into immunity from every remedy. Each holding has an express majority coalition. The Court decides no later amendment, other express waiver, particular coercive contempt order, settled pollution merits, independent cause of action, or fine amount. It does not hold generally that all aspects of state permitting lack federal character.

White, joined by Blackmun and Stevens, agrees only with Part II-C. He reads the express civil-penalty language and the citizen-suit inclusion of the United States together as clear consent under the other three routes. He regards approved state CWA enforcement as sufficiently federal for the qualified waiver and disputes that generic definitions can defeat the specific grant. He agrees that the RCRA federal-facilities provision’s express focus on injunctive enforcement does not clearly include punitive fines.

The consolidated action returns through the Sixth Circuit for proceedings consistent with rejection of the disputed punitive penalties under all four asserted routes. The contingent amount stipulation creates no independent liability; any remaining lawful basis must be identified before it operates. The settled compliance obligations and stayed technical-study claim retain their existing procedural status. The Court makes no new findings on the settled pollution allegations.

**End of entry: United States Department of Energy v. Ohio, Merits decision, April 21, 1992.**

### Taylor v. Freeland & Kronz, No. 91-571

Merits decision, April 21, 1992

Argued March 2, 1992; decided April 21, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the Third Circuit, [938 F.2d 420](https://static.case.law/f2d/938/html/0420-01.html), which reversed the district court's affirmance of a bankruptcy order requiring turnover of $23,483.75 plus interest. Davis claimed her discrimination-lawsuit proceeds exempt with unknown value. Taylor knew of the claim but sought neither a timely objection nor an extension. The suit settled for $110,000, after which Taylor sought turnover. The question is whether a trustee may contest a claimed exemption lacking a colorable statutory basis after the objection period expires. The Court reviews that deadline issue and excludes the new §105(a) question. The decision is reported at [503 U.S. 638](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep503/usrep503638/usrep503638.pdf).

All nine Justices participate. The Court affirms the Third Circuit, 8–1. Stone, White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas support affirmance; Stevens dissents and would reverse and remand for the exemption inquiry. Thomas delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone, White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter, including its rule, application and express reservations. Stevens files a dissent, joined by no other Justice.

The Court holds that an exemption claim becomes effective when the time to object expires without an objection or a timely extension. Under 11 U.S.C. §522(l) and the applicable Bankruptcy Rule 4003(b), property claimed as exempt becomes exempt when no party in interest objects within 30 days after the creditors' meeting concludes or a relevant amendment to the exemption list is filed, whichever is later, and the court grants no further time within that period. The trustee cannot later contest the claimed exemption merely because it lacked a colorable statutory basis. This holding concerns the scheduled proceeds claimed here and does not decide a different claim's scope, an unscheduled asset, or an unpreserved fraud or §105(a) theory. Thomas's opinion controls because Stone, White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter join its rule and explanation in full, forming an eight-Justice majority at this level of generality.

The Court holds that a trustee must use the objection procedure while it remains available. Section 522(b) identifies substantive exemptions, while §522(l) states the consequence of a claim left unchallenged. Rule 4003(b) supplies the time to object or obtain an extension, and Rule 9006(b)(3) confines enlargement to that rule's conditions. Ron Pair directs courts to enforce a coherent statutory scheme according to its terms. Taylor knew about the lawsuit and its possible substantial recovery. Uncertainty about its value permitted timely inquiry and an objection; it did not require waiting for collection. A colorability exception would reopen the very merits dispute that the objection period closes. The possibility of an undeserved exemption does not authorize that revision. Misconduct remedies remain available only under their own requirements, and the Court imposes none. The §105(a) argument was not presented below or in the petition and is not decided. Nor does this record require a decision about fraud, unscheduled property, or differently limited exemption language.

United States v. Ron Pair Enterprises, Inc., 489 U.S. 235, is applied to enforce a coherent Bankruptcy Code provision according to its terms; bankruptcy administration supplies no general power to replace explicit procedure with an equitable exception. Connecticut National Bank v. Germain, decided March 9, 1992, supplies a consistent rule that statutory text governs and provisions doing different work may coexist: substantive exemption eligibility and the procedure for contesting a claim perform different work here. Germain's separate bankruptcy appellate-jurisdiction holding remains unchanged. In re Dembs, 757 F.2d 777, and In re Peterson, 920 F.2d 1389, are rejected insofar as their colorability requirement permits a late objection of this kind; that requirement cannot govern the §522(l) consequence decided here. No additional precedent is overruled or limited.

The Court makes no §105(a) construction or retroactivity decision, makes no fraud ruling, imposes no sanction, and does not rule on unscheduled assets or the scope of differently limited claims. The decision rests on no finding of subjective bad faith. Rule 4003(c)'s burden on the objector remains applicable in a properly presented objection proceeding. The Court does not adjudicate respondents' alternative valuation, lien or good-faith arguments. Effective April 21, §522(l) and Rule 4003(b) foreclose this late exemption objection despite the lack of a colorable statutory basis. Other bankruptcy rules and properly supported misconduct remedies retain their own operation.

Stevens's dissent reads §522(l)'s reference to subsection (b) to deny the deadline's protection to a claim with no arguable statutory basis. He would reverse and remand for the appropriate inquiry, protecting creditors without finding fraud or automatically reinstating every turnover theory. His account of the statutory context and equitable administration does not control.

Supreme Court merits review ends in affirmance. The Third Circuit's reversal of the $23,483.75-plus-interest turnover order stands, and the scheduled proceeds remain exempt against this untimely theory. The Court neither reinstates the bankruptcy payment order nor resolves an alternative recovery or sanction. It orders no further remand, award, sanction or hearing and retains no proceeding.

**End of entry: Taylor v. Freeland & Kronz, Merits decision, April 21, 1992.**

### Gomez v. United States District Court for the Northern District of California, No. A-767

Application to vacate execution stays, April 21, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Decided April 21, 1992, without oral argument. California's application was presented to Circuit Justice Kennedy and referred to the full Court. California seeks vacatur of the Ninth Circuit's April 20 stays in No. 92-70237 pending review of Harris's cyanide-gas challenge. All nine Justices participate: Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. The application and target stays are identified in [503 U.S. 653](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep503/usrep503653/usrep503653.pdf).

The district court issued a temporary restraining order on April 18 under §1983. A Ninth Circuit panel directed its vacation on mandamus, an order requiring the lower court to act. The panel majority applied Younger abstention—the rule governing when a federal court must refrain from intervening in state proceedings—and available state review; Noonan disputed that analysis and urged protection for the supported claim. The later appellate stays preserved further review. The lower proceedings are reported at [Fierro, 790 F. Supp. 966](https://static.case.law/f-supp/790/html/0966-01.html), and [966 F.2d 460](https://static.case.law/f2d/966/html/0460-01.html).

Medical declarations and the recent Harding execution account support the asserted unnecessary-pain claim. They are submissions for interim assessment. California invokes procedural abuse and independently unjustified delay through four prior federal habeas proceedings, in which Harris sought federal review of his custody.

#### Judgment

The Court grants application A-767 and vacates the April 20, 1992 orders staying Harris's execution in Ninth Circuit No. 92-70237, 6–3. The only controlling ground is the independent equitable ground concerning delay stated below.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Equitable basis for vacating the execution stay

**Controlling proposition:** A court considering an execution stay may weigh the State's interest in enforcing its judgment and an applicant's unjustified delay in presenting a long-available claim independently of any habeas procedural bar. On this record, the unexplained postponement of the cyanide-gas challenge through four prior federal habeas proceedings leaves the April 20 stays without sufficient equitable support; those stays must be vacated even assuming the claim is cognizable under §1983 and not barred by abuse-of-writ doctrine.

**Authority:** The per curiam opinion is joined by White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. Six of nine participating Justices adopt this independent equitable ground. Stone, Blackmun and Stevens dissent. No other ground is necessary to the judgment, and no fractured-opinion rule is invoked.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the supported seriousness of a constitutional claim does not alone establish an entitlement to last-minute equitable relief. Delo supplies the Court's authority to vacate an execution stay that abuses equitable discretion; Barefoot rejects an automatic stay for successive litigation. California has a substantial interest in carrying out its judgment after the opportunities already provided for review. Harris could have challenged this established method years earlier. The recent Harding account adds support for the claim, but it does not explain withholding the underlying challenge through four federal proceedings. The medical submissions and irreversible consequence of execution deserve weight; they do not, on this record, overcome the unexplained delay. This conclusion assumes the procedural premise most favorable to Harris. The Court therefore need not settle the §1983 boundary, apply every McCleskey element, or decide Younger abstention. It also does not decide whether cyanide gas violates the Eighth Amendment. The ruling vacates the identified stays and creates no general prohibition on later federal orders.

#### Limits and Questions Not Reached

The Court decides only the equitable support for the identified April 20 stays. It does not determine the final method claim, establish a universal alternative-method requirement, adjudicate every habeas-default element, issue a permanent injunction, or direct that no federal court may enter another stay. It decides no categorical rule about whether the §1983 procedure is available for this claim.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Delo v. Stokes, 495 U.S. 320, is applied for full-Court review and vacatur of an unsupported execution stay; its successive-habeas holding remains intact. The equitable assessment here does not treat Kennedy's separate writing as prior Court law.

Barefoot v. Estelle, 463 U.S. 880, is applied because successive litigation does not itself require a stay, and substantial grounds for relief matter to interim protection.

In re Blodgett, decided January 13, 1992, is distinguished: its requirement to seek focused relief below before mandamus neither resolves this application nor supplies a broader rule about capital-litigation delay.

McCleskey v. Zant, 499 U.S. 467, remains governing within its proper habeas domain through its cause-and-prejudice and miscarriage-of-justice rules. Its application to this §1983 action is reserved.

Preiser v. Rodriguez, 411 U.S. 475; Younger v. Harris, 401 U.S. 37; Huffman v. Pursue, Ltd., 420 U.S. 592; and Pennzoil Co. v. Texaco Inc., 481 U.S. 1, retain their respective rules governing the available procedural route and federal abstention from state proceedings. The Court decides no extension of those rules to this execution-method claim. No additional precedent is overruled or limited.

Effective April 21, equitable delay supplies an independent ground to vacate the identified execution stays even assuming an open §1983 channel. The Court adopts no final Eighth Amendment holding, universal alternative-method requirement or ban on further federal stays. Delo and Barefoot retain their stated domains, as does McCleskey.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stevens, joined by Blackmun, dissents. The medical and contemporary-legislative evidence persuades them that cyanide-gas execution inflicts unnecessary cruelty. They would preserve the stay for lower-court adjudication and regard timing as insufficient to permit an unconstitutional punishment.

Stone separately dissents. He finds a supported possibility of relief and an insufficiently established bar to using the asserted procedural route. He concludes that the medical submissions, recent concrete account, irreversibility and focused duration justify the existing stay despite independently serious delay. Recent evidence is no automatic excuse, and irreversibility creates no automatic capital stay. He makes no final determination about the execution method. Neither dissent's propositions control.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

A-767 is resolved by grant of vacatur. The April 20, 1992 orders staying Harris's execution in Ninth Circuit No. 92-70237 cease to operate. The Court imposes no additional injunction, retains no supervision, and places no general restriction on later federal stays. The underlying litigation remains subject to its own lawful proceedings.

**End of entry: Gomez v. United States District Court for the Northern District of California, Application to vacate execution stays, April 21, 1992.**

### Keeney v. Tamayo-Reyes, No. 90-1859

Merits decision, May 4, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 15, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, reviewing 926 F.2d 1492 (March 4, 1991). The question is whether ordinary negligence in failing to develop material facts in state postconviction proceedings requires a showing of cause and prejudice before a federal habeas court must hold a hearing. The State seeks replacement of Townsend’s deliberate-bypass rule; Tamayo-Reyes seeks the limited hearing ordered below. His claim concerns an allegedly inadequate explanation of manslaughter’s mental-state element before his no-contest plea, not the other translations upheld below. All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Court affirms the Ninth Circuit’s requirement of a limited federal evidentiary hearing and remands for proceedings carrying out that requirement.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Hearing on inadequately developed material facts

**Controlling proposition:** When a federal habeas claim properly presented to the state courts turns on material facts inadequately developed in state proceedings, Townsend’s fifth mandatory-hearing circumstance remains governed by its deliberate-bypass qualification: ordinary counsel negligence, without deliberate bypass, does not bar the necessary federal evidentiary hearing. Tamayo-Reyes is entitled to a hearing confined to whether the manslaughter mental-state element was adequately explained; the supported findings and denial of a new hearing concerning translation of the plea form and plea proceeding remain undisturbed.

**Authority:** O’Connor’s Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, and Kennedy. These five Justices form a majority and adopt the rule, its application, and the limited hearing remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Townsend v. Sain governs the missing factual development here. Townsend requires a hearing when facts material to an available constitutional claim were inadequately developed, unless the prisoner deliberately bypassed the state process. Coleman v. Thompson addresses a different obstacle: an independent and adequate state procedural ground preventing consideration of the claim. Tamayo-Reyes presented his claim, and the State identifies no such procedural bar. Vasquez v. Hillery confirms that federal clarification of facts supporting an exhausted claim does not necessarily create a new claim. The State’s concern about duplicative proceedings is substantial, but uniform treatment of different procedural questions does not itself justify replacing the directly applicable hearing rule. Section 2254(d)(3), which removes the presumption of correctness when material facts were inadequately developed, remains consistent with that rule without itself supplying every condition for a hearing. Counsel’s omission here was negligent, not deliberate. The hearing concerns only the explanation of manslaughter’s mental state; it neither reopens supported findings about other translations nor establishes that the plea was unconstitutional.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

- [Townsend v. Sain, 372 U.S. 293 (1963)](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep372/usrep372293/usrep372293.pdf), supplies the fifth mandatory-hearing circumstance and its deliberate-bypass qualification. The Court retains and applies that rule to inadequate factual development; Townsend’s other hearing grounds are not displaced.
- Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. 391 (1963), supplies the deliberate-bypass discussion that remains operative through Townsend for this hearing question. The Court does not revive Fay’s displaced claim-default rule.
- [Coleman v. Thompson, 501 U.S. 722 (1991)](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep501/usrep501722/usrep501722.pdf), governs independent and adequate state procedural defaults. The Court distinguishes that obstacle; Coleman’s cause-and-prejudice rule remains in force and is not extended to this hearing entitlement.
- [Vasquez v. Hillery, 474 U.S. 254 (1986)](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep474/usrep474254/usrep474254.pdf), supplies the distinction between supplementing facts and fundamentally altering an exhausted claim. The Court relies on that distinction and leaves Vasquez’s deliberate-withholding reservation open.

The Court retains and applies existing law, effective in this case May 4, 1992. Ordinary counsel negligence alone does not defeat the necessary hearing on materially incomplete state factual development. Section 2254(d)’s rules for state factual findings remain distinct and operative. The State’s proposed cause-and-prejudice extension is rejected; no new general hearing restriction is created. Other Townsend circumstances, adequately supported findings, and the constitutional merits remain governed by their existing rules.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

White, joined by Scalia, Souter, and Thomas, would replace the deliberate-bypass rule for inadequate state factual development with cause and actual prejudice, preserving a fundamental-miscarriage exception. They regard the State’s opportunity to develop the record, finality, and the policies reflected in Coleman as sufficient to justify that express change, and would reverse and remand to allow Tamayo-Reyes to meet the new test.

Kennedy’s concurrence emphasizes that material inadequacy means a realistic possibility that a hearing could affect the outcome. Where a claim has passed the independently applicable barriers to habeas review, resolving it on an accurate factual record protects the judicial task. His separate explanation adds no general cause-or-prejudice prerequisite.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court merits review is complete. The mandate carries out the affirmed limited hearing order through the Court of Appeals. The matter returns through the Ninth Circuit to the district court for a hearing on the manslaughter mental-state explanation and decision of that branch of the constitutional claim. The separate plea-form and plea-proceeding determinations remain intact.

The Court retains no proceeding and issues no writ or unconditional release on the present allegations. If the mental-state claim is proved and establishes an unconstitutional plea, relief is a conditional writ allowing the State a reasonable opportunity for a lawful plea or trial process. The present record establishes neither that condition nor entitlement to immediate release.

### Denton v. Hernandez, No. 90-1846

Merits decision, May 4, 1992

Argued February 24, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, reviewing its judgment reported at 929 F.2d 1374, reaffirming its earlier decision after remand for consideration of Neitzke and amended April 10, 1991. The questions concern the boundary of factual frivolousness under 28 U.S.C. §1915(d) and the proper appellate standard. Officials challenge the restriction on screening; Hernandez seeks fair consideration and reinstatement of his complaints. The Court of Appeals’ lead opinion relied on the absence of judicially noticeable contradictory facts, while the concurrence relied on notice and amendment. Those were distinct grounds. The review concerns only three appealed actions. All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

The Court vacates the judgment under review and remands, 7–2, for application of the proper factual-frivolity and abuse-of-discretion standards. Stone-Zsela, White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas support that disposition; Blackmun and Stevens oppose it. O’Connor delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, White, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas. All seven adopt both controlling propositions and the remand instructions. Stevens dissents, joined in full by Blackmun.

The Court holds that a claim filed without paying filing fees first may be dismissed as factually frivolous under 28 U.S.C. §1915(d) when its allegations are clearly baseless: irrational or wholly incredible, even without judicially noticeable facts contradicting them. The initial assessment must favor the plaintiff; mere improbability, extraordinary circumstances, or ordinary disbelief does not suffice, and screening cannot resolve genuine factual disputes. This rule and its judgment-supporting explanation control through O’Connor’s Opinion of the Court, adopted by the seven Justices named above. Stevens and Blackmun also agree with this factual-screening standard, although they disagree with the additional appellate remand; their agreement is not needed to establish the rule’s controlling authority.

The Court holds that factual frivolousness requires more than a judge’s conclusion that an allegation is unlikely. Neitzke v. Williams distinguishes the statutory power to screen clearly baseless facts from dismissal for failure to state a legal claim. That power reaches irrational or wholly incredible allegations even when no judicially noticeable fact disproves them; otherwise much of the special screening authority would disappear. But Neitzke also preserves arguable claims, and Haines v. Kerner requires fair treatment of a prisoner’s pleading without counsel. Screening therefore cannot become a trial of ordinary credibility before the plaintiff has had an opportunity to prove a claim. Here the magistrate relied on the combined pattern of complaints while acknowledging that each complaint was not necessarily frivolous. The Esquer affidavit and proposed Pierce affidavit bear on particular alleged events and must be considered as submitted, without deciding their truth from that pattern alone. The Court corrects the restrictive appellate premise and leaves application to the Court of Appeals; it decides neither whether these allegations satisfy the standard nor whether any assault occurred.

[Neitzke v. Williams, 490 U.S. 319 (1989)](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep490/usrep490319/usrep490319.pdf), supplies the distinct screening function and clearly-baseless category. The Court applies and clarifies it: the category includes irrational or wholly incredible allegations without a judicially noticeable contradiction, while preserving the distinction from Rule 12(b)(6), which governs dismissal for failure to state a legal claim. [Haines v. Kerner, 404 U.S. 519 (1972)](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep404/usrep404519/usrep404519.pdf), requires liberal treatment of pleadings filed without counsel. The Court applies that protection to screening without adjudicating the allegations’ truth.

The Court also holds that a dismissal under section 1915(d) is reviewed for abuse of discretion; an erroneous legal premise remains independently correctable as law. Review must address relevant circumstances, including lack of counsel, resolution of genuine factual disputes, legal errors, the adequacy of the stated reasons, prejudice, and whether more specific pleading could cure the defect. The dismissal is not a merits adjudication barring a paid complaint, although its possible effect on later frivolousness determinations on applications to proceed without prepaying fees makes prejudice and curability material. O’Connor’s Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, White, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas, supplies the majority authority for this rule and its remand instructions. No separate proposition establishes a categorical preclusion rule for every later fee application or an absolute right to amend.

The Court holds that section 1915(d) entrusts the frivolousness determination to the court considering the complaint, subject to review for abuse of discretion. Boag v. MacDougall recognizes that discretion while requiring a lawful ground and an explanation sufficient for appellate review. Deference to a screening judgment therefore does not insulate an incorrect legal rule. Haines v. Kerner makes the litigant’s lack of counsel relevant, and Neitzke v. Williams preserves the difference between screening baseless proceedings and resolving their merits. The Court of Appeals must apply that framework to the three appealed actions, including the affidavits, proposed amendment, and aggregate-pattern rationale. Because a statutory screening dismissal does not determine the merits, it does not bar filing a paid complaint with the same allegations. Its possible effect on later fee-screening decisions nevertheless requires attention to prejudice and whether greater specificity could cure the stated defect. The Court leaves the circuit’s categorical notice-and-amendment rule undecided and does not direct affirmance of the district court’s dismissals; it requires a new appellate assessment under the proper standard.

[Boag v. MacDougall, 454 U.S. 364 (1982)](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep454/usrep454364/usrep454364.pdf), supplies the principle that statutory discretion does not preserve a dismissal resting on legal error and that reasons must permit intelligent appellate review. The Court applies that principle here. Haines governs review of pleadings filed without counsel and the significance of potentially curable deficiencies; Neitzke keeps section 1915(d)’s screening function distinct from disposition of the merits under ordinary pleading rules. The Court applies both. The decision clarifies Neitzke’s factual boundary and settles the appellate review rule. It adopts no comprehensive rule governing preclusion in later fee applications and decides neither a categorical notice-and-amendment entitlement nor the frivolousness of these particular complaints.

Stevens, joined by Blackmun, would affirm because they conclude that the Court of Appeals’ judgment already accords with the Court’s factual-frivolousness standard. They dissent from vacatur and the additional remand. Their position does not endorse an exclusive judicial-notice rule or make a merits finding that any alleged assault occurred.

Supreme Court merits review is complete. The judgment under review is vacated, and the matter returns to the Ninth Circuit for abuse-of-discretion review of Nos. CIV S-83-0645, CIV S-83-1348, and CIV S-85-0084 under the corrected factual-frivolity standard and remand instructions. Review must consider the Esquer affidavit, the proposed Pierce affidavit and amendment, Hernandez’s status as a litigant without counsel, and the stated aggregate-pattern rationale. The remand does not reopen the two unappealed dismissals or independently revive the due-process and mattress claims already left dismissed below and outside the granted factual-screening issue. The Court does not affirm or reinstate any particular district dismissal, find the allegations true or false, determine damages, adjudicate liability, or make a merits award. The Court retains no proceeding and orders no reargument.

**End of entry: Denton v. Hernandez, Merits decision, May 4, 1992.**

### United States v. Williams, No. 90-1972

Merits decision, May 4, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 22, 1992; decided May 4, 1992. [504 U.S. 36 (1992)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-504/pdf/USREPORTS-504-36.pdf). On writ of certiorari to the Tenth Circuit, [899 F.2d 898](https://static.case.law/f2d/899/html/0898-01.html), which affirmed dismissal of the seven-count §1014 indictment without prejudice and rejected Williams’s cross-appeal for dismissal with prejudice. The question is whether a federal indictment may be dismissed because the prosecutor failed to present exculpatory evidence—evidence favorable to the accused—to the grand jury. Review requires deciding whether the court below adequately passed upon that question. The Government seeks reversal; Williams seeks to preserve dismissal without prejudice. This decision issues on May 4 with Keeney and Denton.

All nine Justices—Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas—participated at argument and decision. Thomas’s January 22 argument participation followed his October 23 oath.

#### Judgment

The Court reaches the granted question and affirms the dismissal without prejudice. The two rulings have different coalitions.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Review of the granted question | Reach the question, 5–4 | Stone, White, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter | Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Thomas would dismiss the writ as improvidently granted | The Court decides the granted question. |
| Merits | Affirm the Tenth Circuit’s judgment, 5–4 | Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Thomas | White, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter would reverse and permit further proceedings on the indictment | Dismissal remains without prejudice; a lawful new grand-jury presentation remains possible. |

The Court makes no new factual finding about guilt, knowing fabrication or intentional deception. The judgment is neither an acquittal nor a dismissal with prejudice and does not prohibit renewed prosecution.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Review of the question passed upon below

**Controlling proposition:** The Court may review an important federal question expressly decided by the court of appeals even though a party did not renew its challenge to binding circuit precedent in this case, where adherence to that precedent did not concede its correctness. This is a permissible exercise of review, not an obligation to decide every unpressed question or a rule excusing an affirmative waiver.

**Authority:** Stone’s reviewability opinion, joined by White, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter: five Justices adopt the rule and explanation. The four Justices who prefer dismissal of the writ do not join this rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the question is properly before it because the court of appeals actually decided the legal duty now challenged. Stevens v. Department of Treasury permits review of a question passed upon below even when the party did not properly press it. The Tenth Circuit expressly applied Page’s disclosure obligation and used it to sustain dismissal. The Government’s recognition that Page bound the panel did not concede that Page correctly stated federal law; the Government had opposed that rule in the proceeding establishing it. Requiring a renewed demand that the panel disregard its recent controlling precedent would not improve the presentation of this already decided legal issue. The contrary vehicle argument treats compliance with binding precedent as abandonment of a legal objection, although no affirmative abandonment is established. The Court exercises its discretion on this federal appellate record. It does not displace a governing trial-objection requirement, decide the effect of an express concession, or prescribe a broader rule for review of state judgments.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Stevens v. Department of Treasury, 500 U.S. 1 — applied: actual decision below can satisfy the pressed-or-passed-upon practice.
- United States v. Page, 808 F.2d 723 — its binding force within the Tenth Circuit explains the litigation posture; adherence does not establish agreement with its correctness.

##### Disclosure of known evidence directly negating guilt

**Controlling proposition:** Before seeking an indictment, a federal prosecutor must present or otherwise disclose to the grand jury personally known, substantial evidence directly negating the charged offense when its omission would materially distort the grand-jury presentation. This duty requires neither an investigative search for undiscovered favorable evidence nor presentation of every possible defense; a violation alone does not establish entitlement to dismissal.

**Authority:** Stevens’s Parts II and III, joined by Blackmun, O’Connor and Thomas, adopt the personally-known/direct-negation duty. Stone’s separate merits opinion adopts that duty where omission materially distorts the presentation. Under Marks, these five judgment supporters establish the stated common requirement; the four-Justice formulation does not remove Stone’s material-distortion qualification from the controlling rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a prosecutor may not use the grand jury’s independent role to justify the materially misleading omission described here. Calandra recognizes both the grand jury’s protection against unfounded prosecution and enforceable limits on its process. Bank of Nova Scotia recognizes judicial remedies for prejudicial misconduct while separating an actual violation from its effect on an indictment. The obligation here regulates the prosecutor’s presentation of substantial evidence already personally known; it does not require the prosecutor to investigate the defense. Costello prevents challenges to the competence or sufficiency of evidence in an otherwise lawful presentation, and that protection remains. The contrary institutional argument correctly rejects a preliminary trial of every defense, but does not dispose of this confined misconduct question. The Government’s categorical objection to authority therefore fails. The character of the particular accounting documents is outside the question accepted for review. Dismissal requires a separate remedial showing, and constitutional duties and negligent discovery remain open.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Calandra, 414 U.S. 338 — its protective function and enforceable process limits support the confined duty; ordinary evidentiary freedom remains.
- Bank of Nova Scotia v. United States, 487 U.S. 250 — its misconduct and prejudice inquiries remain separate; violation does not automatically warrant dismissal.
- Costello v. United States, 350 U.S. 359 — preserved for competence and sufficiency challenges to an otherwise lawful presentation; distinguished from the specified prosecutorial omission.
- Marks v. United States, 430 U.S. 188 — applied to the judgment supporters’ shared direct-negation duty with the material-distortion qualification retained.

##### Confined supervisory power over a materially misleading presentation

**Controlling proposition:** Federal supervisory authority permits dismissal without prejudice when a prosecutor withholds personally known, substantial evidence directly negating the charged offense, the omission materially distorts the presentation, the evidence would plainly preclude a finding of probable cause, and the violation substantially influenced the indictment or leaves grave doubt that the indictment was free from that influence. This establishes the stated sufficient ground for dismissal; it requires neither a search for undiscovered favorable evidence nor presentation of every potential defense.

**Authority:** Stevens’s merits Parts II and III, joined by Blackmun, O’Connor and Thomas, supply the narrower judgment-supporting rule. Stone concurs in affirmance under the broader material-distortion formulation, covering known evidence substantially undermining an essential element with established Bank of Nova Scotia prejudice. Under Marks v. United States, 430 U.S. 188, the four-Justice rule is the controlling logical subset: every known, directly negating omission that plainly defeats probable cause, materially distorts the presentation and causes the stated prejudice also satisfies Stone’s broader rule. The five judgment supporters together establish its force. Stone is not represented as formally joining the narrower qualification. The four-Justice and broader one-Justice applications are not combined to remove that qualification from the sufficient ground recognized here. Neither the plurality nor the five judgment supporters adopt an exclusive rule forbidding relief in every other circumstance.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court recognizes a confined power to prevent a prosecutor from materially misleading the grand jury while preserving its independent charging function. Bank of Nova Scotia requires actual charging prejudice before dismissal for nonconstitutional misconduct; that safeguard remains necessary here. Calandra recognizes the grand jury’s protective function and legally enforceable limits on its process. The disclosure duty concerns personally known, substantial evidence directly negating guilt. Evidence plainly defeating probable cause supplies a sufficient case for dismissal when the separate prejudice condition is met. Costello protects an otherwise lawful presentation from judicial reassessment of the quality or quantity of the evidence, and Calandra preserves the grand jury’s freedom from ordinary trial restrictions. Neither requires acceptance of the materially distorted presentation covered by this narrow rule. The institutional objection limits supervision but does not eliminate it. Here the accepted question concerns authority; the specific evidentiary application is not reviewed. A lawful new presentation remains possible. Applications outside this sufficient dismissal class, constitutional duties, state grand juries and negligent discovery remain unresolved.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Bank of Nova Scotia v. United States, 487 U.S. 250 — its prejudice requirement is retained; limited supervisory power is extended to the specified known-evidence omission. Its evidence-reliability prohibition survives for lawful presentations.
- Costello v. United States, 350 U.S. 359 — distinguished: the confined misconduct inquiry is not a challenge to the competence or sufficiency of evidence in an otherwise lawful presentation. Its hearsay and ordinary adequacy rules remain.
- United States v. Calandra, 414 U.S. 338 — preserved for grand-jury independence and freedom from trial evidence rules; no exclusionary-rule extension occurs.
- United States v. Mechanik, 475 U.S. 66 — its completed-trial harmlessness holding is not extended to erase a prejudicial violation challenged and resolved before trial; the charging-prejudice formulation adopted in Bank remains operative.
- Brady v. Maryland, 373 U.S. 83 — not transplanted into grand-jury proceedings; its trial disclosure duty remains unchanged.
- Marks v. United States, 430 U.S. 188 — applied to the genuine logical subset of the five judgment supporters’ rules; the shared authority supports the sufficient dismissal ground, without creating a single majority merits opinion or an exclusive boundary on other relief.
- United States v. Page, 808 F.2d 723 — the judgment applying it in Williams is sustained against the categorical no-authority challenge; Page’s full disclosure formulation and any rule requiring every fact relevant to a possible defense are not adopted.

#### Limits and Questions Not Reached

The affirmed dismissal binds this case. The Government presented the existence of supervisory authority, rather than a renewed challenge to the nature of the omitted evidence. The four-Justice writing does not independently find that these accounting documents plainly preclude probable cause; it preserves its doubt about the lower courts’ reasonable-doubt formulation while rejecting the categorical no-power answer on the question granted. Stone separately finds that the supported hearing findings satisfy his broader material-distortion and prejudice rule. Accordingly, affirmance does not establish that any evidence bearing on intent necessarily meets the narrower controlling test, nor does it impose a new finding that the prosecutor knowingly fabricated evidence. No determination of guilt or innocence follows. A distinct negligent-discovery claim, state-grand-jury duty, constitutional disclosure rule and immunity or fabrication claim remain open.

#### Current-Law Effect

Effective May 4, federal supervisory authority supports the limited disclosure duty and prejudice-conditioned dismissal stated above. The sufficient dismissal ground includes the plainly-precluding-probable-cause qualification; applications outside that class remain unresolved. That qualification does not define the disclosure duty itself. Stone’s broader material-distortion application is not a general holding. Review of the passed-upon question is permitted on the specified federal appellate posture. Trial disclosure, ordinary probable-cause charging, grand-jury evidentiary latitude and the right to seek a lawful new indictment remain.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stevens, joined by Blackmun and O’Connor, would dismiss the writ because the Government did not contest the circuit rule in this litigation. Thomas joins the dismissal preference and objection to favored treatment of the Government in Part III, without joining Part I’s fuller analysis. On the reached merits, those four recognize a duty to disclose personally known, substantial evidence directly negating guilt and identify evidence plainly precluding probable cause as a sufficient case for dismissal; they question whether the accounting evidence satisfies that formulation and do not resolve an evidentiary question outside the grant. Stone concurs in affirmance on a broader rule covering known evidence substantially undermining an essential element where omission materially distorts the presentation, and finds the established hearing findings and Bank of Nova Scotia prejudice sufficient. His broader application remains noncontrolling outside the narrow Marks rule. Scalia, joined by White, Kennedy and Souter, would reverse because supervisory remedies enforce independently established law and do not authorize courts to create this presentation duty for a separate accusatory institution.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court review ends in affirmance of dismissal without prejudice. The Government may make a new grand-jury presentation satisfying the controlling rule if it elects to proceed; the Court orders neither reindictment nor acquittal and sets no new filing or trial date. The Court retains no proceeding.

**End of entry: United States v. Williams, Merits decision, May 4, 1992.**
