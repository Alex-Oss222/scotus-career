### *Estelle v. McGuire*, No. 90-1074

Merits decision, December 4, 1991

#### Chronology and Posture

*Estelle v. McGuire*, 502 U.S. 62 (1991), was argued October 9, 1991, on certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 902 F.2d 749. The questions were whether evidence of prior injuries, or the charge governing its use, made McGuire's murder trial fundamentally unfair or relieved California of proving his identity as the killer. Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter participated. Thomas took no part because argument preceded his oath.

#### Judgment

The Court reversed the Ninth Circuit.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Federal habeas judgment | Reversed, 5-3 | White, Blackmun, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter | Stone, Stevens, O'Connor | The habeas writ remains denied; no acquittal, retrial, or release is ordered. Stone, Stevens, and O'Connor would vacate and remand for harmless-error review. |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | White | Blackmun, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter throughout; Stone, Stevens, O'Connor as to the prior-injury-evidence proposition | Controls reversal and both holdings | Stone, Stevens, and O'Connor do not join the instruction or final-disposition portions |
| Opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part | O'Connor | Stone, Stevens | Agrees on evidence; would vacate rather than reverse | Instructional error and harmless-review remedy |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Prior-injury evidence

**Controlling proposition:** On federal habeas review, evidence of a child's prior nonaccidental injuries may be relevant to prove that the charged death was intentional even when the person who caused the earlier injuries is unknown. Admission for that nonidentity purpose does not itself violate due process, and a defendant's choice not to contest accident does not remove the State's burden to prove intentional killing.

**Authority:** White's opinion, joined on this proposition by all eight participating Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Habeas relief does not lie for an error of state evidence law alone. The prosecution retained the burden to prove an intentional killing, and the repeated injuries supplied evidence that the fatal injury was not accidental. That inference did not itself identify McGuire as the abuser, but relevance to one required proposition does not disappear because the defense concentrates on another. *Winship* supplies the State's continuing elemental burden; *Lisenba* supplies the narrow fundamental-unfairness boundary. The Court does not create a general constitutional rule governing propensity evidence and does not decide whether California correctly applied its own evidence law. The Ninth Circuit therefore could not grant relief on the evidence-alone theory.

##### Instructional use

**Controlling proposition:** An ambiguous instruction violates due process only when, read with the charge and trial as a whole, there is a reasonable likelihood the jury applied it in a way that relieved the State of proving an element. This instruction did not create that likelihood because it conditioned any inference on a finding that McGuire committed the prior acts and expressly prohibited a bad-character inference.

**Authority:** White's opinion, joined at this level by White, Blackmun, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter; five of the eight participating Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** *Cupp* requires examination of the complete charge rather than isolated words, and *Boyde* requires a reasonable likelihood of unconstitutional application rather than a conceivable misunderstanding. The charge used conditional language and told jurors not to infer guilt from bad character. It did not direct them to find that McGuire caused the earlier injuries or shift the prosecution's burden to prove identity and every element. *Francis* and *Sandstrom* remain controlling where a charge creates a mandatory presumption or shifts elemental persuasion, but this charge did neither. Because there was no constitutional error, harmless review was unnecessary. The Court does not hold every prior-act instruction valid or decide a general constitutional propensity prohibition.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

*Lewis*, *Pulley*, *Lisenba*, and *Dowling* are applied. *Winship* is applied and unchanged. *Cupp* and *Boyde* govern whole-charge and reasonable-likelihood review, while *Francis* and *Sandstrom* are distinguished and preserved. The majority does not reach *Teague*, *Rose*, or *Chapman*. The evidence and instruction rules enter current law on December 4, 1991. Relevance to nonaccidental injury does not establish identity, and a general constitutional propensity-evidence rule remains open.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

O'Connor, joined by Stone and Stevens, agrees that the evidence had a lawful nonidentity use. They would hold, however, that grouping unidentified grave injuries as acts of “the Defendant,” followed by an invited inference to the charged killing, created a reasonable likelihood of bypassing the identity finding. They would vacate and remand for harmless-error review beyond a reasonable doubt rather than order automatic reversal.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The conviction and denial of federal habeas relief stand. No acquittal, retrial, or release is ordered. The Supreme Court and federal habeas proceedings are closed.

### *Southwest Marine, Inc. v. Gizoni*, No. 90-584

Merits decision, December 4, 1991

*Southwest Marine, Inc. v. Gizoni*, 502 U.S. 81 (1991), was argued October 15, 1991, on certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 909 F.2d 385. The Court considered whether employment in an occupation listed by the Longshore and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act, or receipt of voluntary benefits under that Act, forecloses a Jones Act claim by a worker who may be a vessel crew member. Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter participated. Thomas took no part because argument preceded his oath.

The Court affirmed the Ninth Circuit by an 8-0 vote. All eight participating Justices supported affirmance. White delivered the opinion of the Court, joined by all participating Justices; there was no separate writing.

The Court holds that employment in an occupation listed by the LHWCA does not foreclose Jones Act seaman status because the LHWCA expressly excludes a vessel's master or crew. Receipt of voluntary LHWCA benefits without a final status adjudication does not preclude a Jones Act suit; overlapping recovery remains subject to statutory credit. White's unanimous participating-Court opinion supplies the controlling authority.

The occupational definition and crew exclusion must be read together. A ship-repair worker may fall outside LHWCA coverage by proving crew status under *Wilander*, which requires an employment-related connection to a vessel in navigation and contribution to its work without traditional navigational duties. Gizoni's claimed platform assignment, work aboard the platforms, and towing role create genuine disputes that occupational labels, lack of propulsion, and absence of living quarters do not conclusively resolve. *Tipton* establishes that voluntary benefits do not decide seaman status. Congress supplied mechanisms for moving between remedies and crediting payments, not an election rule forcing refusal of immediate compensation. A final award, settlement, conclusive record, ultimate status, negligence, unseaworthiness, maintenance and cure, and damages remain open.

*Wilander*, *Swanson*, and *Tipton* are applied and unchanged. *MCorp* remains confined to its express bank-regulatory review statute. Effective December 4, listed occupational work and voluntary, unadjudicated LHWCA benefits are not categorical bars to a Jones Act status claim.

Summary judgment remains reversed. No damages or status finding is made. The Supreme Court proceeding is closed, and the case returns for factfinding on status and any viable injury claims.

### *Wooddell v. International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers, Local 71*, No. 90-967

Merits decision, December 4, 1991

*Wooddell v. International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers, Local 71*, 502 U.S. 93 (1991), was argued October 16, 1991, on certiorari to the Sixth Circuit, 907 F.2d 151. The questions were whether Wooddell was entitled to a jury on his LMRDA damages claim and whether §301 permitted his claim as a member-beneficiary under an interunion constitution. Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter participated. Thomas took no part because argument preceded his oath.

The Court reversed and remanded by an 8-0 vote on both reviewed questions. All participating Justices supported reversal. White delivered the opinion of the Court, joined by all participating Justices; there was no separate writing.

The Court first holds that a claim for wages and benefits lost because hiring-hall referrals allegedly were withheld seeks compensatory legal relief and carries a Seventh Amendment jury right. Accompanying equitable requests do not eliminate that right on legal issues. White's unanimous participating-Court opinion supplies the controlling authority. *Terry* supplies the historical-analogue inquiry and makes the remedy's character more important. Wooddell seeks replacement for earnings that retaliation allegedly prevented him from obtaining, not return of identified funds held by the union or backpay incidental to reinstatement. That compensation has the character of legal damages. Combining it with an injunction does not allow the court to withhold a jury; legal and equitable issues instead receive proper sequencing. The holding decides the factfinder, not whether retaliation occurred or what amount is recoverable.

The Court also holds that §301 permits an individual beneficiary of a qualifying contract between labor organizations to sue for its violation. An international constitution binding the international and its local retains its interunion-contract character when a member invokes it. White's unanimous opinion also controls this proposition. Section 301 identifies covered contracts, not an exclusive class of organizational plaintiffs. *Local 334* classifies an international constitution as a contract between labor organizations, and *Evening News* permits individual beneficiaries to enforce agreements within §301. Uniform federal interpretation serves the same function regardless of whether a union or beneficiary brings the claim. Ordinary contract predicates remain necessary. The Court does not federalize every internal dispute, classify standalone local bylaws, or decide breach, defenses, or recoverable loss.

*Terry*, *Local 334*, and *Evening News* are applied and preserved. Effective December 4, compensatory lost-referral damages carry a jury right, and member-beneficiaries may enforce qualifying interunion constitutions through §301. Liability and equitable-remedy questions remain distinct.

The union-constitution claim is restored for §301 proceedings, and a jury is permitted on the LMRDA legal damages claim. No liability or amount is awarded. The Supreme Court proceeding is closed, and both claims return for further proceedings consistent with the judgment.

### *Gibson v. Florida Bar*, No. 90-1102

Merits decision, December 4, 1991

#### Chronology and Posture

*Gibson v. Florida Bar*, 502 U.S. 104 (1991), was argued November 6, 1991, on certiorari to the Eleventh Circuit, 906 F.2d 624. The Court considered whether Florida Bar rule 2-9.3 adequately protects compulsory-dues payers who object to nongermane political lobbying. The prospective dispute remains live; an unpreserved retroactive-refund request is outside review. All nine Justices participated: Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas.

#### Judgment

The judgment is affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Protection before use | Reversed, 8-1 | Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Scalia | Prospective lawful reduction or escrow required on remand. |
| General rather than position-by-position objection | Reversed, 8-1 | Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Scalia | A continuing general objection suffices. |
| Facial challenge to Bar-member arbitrators | Affirmed, 9-0 | All nine Justices | None | Membership alone does not invalidate the balanced panel. |

The lower court's correction requiring interest from collection remains intact. No retroactive damages are awarded.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stone | White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Controls every component | Timing, general objection, panel membership, remedy |
| Opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part | Scalia | — | Joins panel component and preserved interest correction; opposes reversal on timing and repeated objections | Would affirm position-specific objection and post-objection escrow |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Timing and scope of protection

**Controlling proposition:** Once a compulsory-bar member makes a continuing objection to financing nongermane ideological activity, the bar may not demand repeated objections to each announced position or leave the reasonably disputed share available for use until each new objection. It must explain anticipated chargeable and nonchargeable activity and protect disputed funds before use through a reasonable reduction or adequate interest-bearing escrow; the bar bears the chargeability burden.

**Authority:** Stone's opinion, joined by Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas; eight of nine participants.

**Controlling explanation:** *Keller* applies compelled-dues safeguards to an integrated bar while preserving compulsory support for germane activities. *Hudson* requires explanation, prompt review, and protection of disputed funds, and *Ellis* establishes that reimbursement and interest do not undo temporary ideological use. Florida collects annual dues before its twice-monthly announcements and begins escrow only after a member objects to a particular position. Budget estimates and protection of a reasonably sufficient amount can address uncertainty without exact advance prediction. Under *Abood*, the member must make opposition known, but the organization then bears the chargeability burden. Repeated 45-day waivers improperly restore a monitoring burden to a known dissenter. Accounting detail and prospective injunction terms remain for remand; the Court adopts no universal opt-in system.

##### Impartial review

**Controlling proposition:** Bar membership alone does not establish that every arbitrator chosen through a procedure giving the bar and objector one selection each and requiring a mutually selected or judicially appointed chair is constitutionally biased. Actual bias, direct stake, or obstructive delay remains reviewable.

**Authority:** Stone's opinion, joined on this proposition by all nine participating Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** *Hudson* requires reasonably prompt impartial review. The Bar cannot select the panel unilaterally: each side chooses one member, neither controls the chair, and judicial appointment is available when agreement fails. A shared and indirect incidence of bar dues is not by itself a direct stake in a particular award. The Court does not approve an actually biased panel, indefinite delay in constituting it, or a system that fails in practice.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

*Keller*, *Hudson*, *Ellis*, and *Abood* are applied and preserved. Effective December 4, an integrated bar receiving a general objection must protect reasonably disputed nongermane amounts before use and may not require recurring position-specific objections. Balanced selection of Bar-member arbitrators is not facially invalid on membership alone.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia would permit objection after each disclosed lobbying position followed by escrow and arbitration, reasoning that uncertain future activity need not be estimated in advance. He agrees that Bar membership alone does not disqualify arbitrators and that the lower court's interest correction remains.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Supreme Court proceeding is closed. The case returns for prospective relief permitting a lawful reduction or escrow system, preserving interest from collection, and excluding unpreserved retroactive damages.

### *Simon & Schuster, Inc. v. Members of the New York State Crime Victims Board*, No. 90-1059

Merits decision, December 10, 1991

*Simon & Schuster, Inc. v. Members of the New York State Crime Victims Board*, 502 U.S. 105 (1991), was argued October 15, 1991, on certiorari to the Second Circuit, 916 F.2d 777. The question was whether New York Executive Law §632-a unconstitutionally burdens speech by escrowing income from a person's account of crime. Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter participated. Thomas took no part because argument preceded his oath.

The Court reversed the Second Circuit by an 8-0 vote. All participating Justices supported reversal. O'Connor delivered the opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, White, Stevens, Scalia, and Souter. Blackmun and Kennedy each concurred in the judgment.

The Court holds that a law selecting crime-related expression for a special financial escrow is content based and must be necessary and narrowly drawn to serve a compelling interest. Section 632-a fails because it burdens an entire covered contract, including incidental discussion and unprosecuted admissions, while leaving other assets outside its special reach; it may not be enforced against this publishing contract. O'Connor's opinion, joined by Stone, White, Stevens, Scalia, and Souter—six of eight participants—supplies the controlling authority.

The obligation arises from what the work says and restrains payment to its source, affecting a publisher's access to protected expression without requiring suppressive purpose. *Arkansas Writers' Project* supplies scrutiny for content-based financial burdens, and *Leathers* distinguishes general economic regulation from selective burdens keyed to expression. Victim compensation and recovery of crime proceeds are compelling at their strongest, but §632-a is not fitted to them. It captures all income under a contract when crime appears incidentally and reaches admissions never prosecuted. Excluding victimless crimes and releasing limited expenses do not cure that breadth. Neutral restitution, forfeiture, attachment, and collection can pursue compensation without this classification. The Court decides only this statute and leaves materially different laws, stolen property, general judgments, and victim claims open.

*Arkansas Writers' Project* and *Leathers* are applied; *Caplin & Drysdale* is distinguished. *Gibson* remains confined to compelled bar financing. Effective December 10, §632-a's content-based proceeds escrow is unconstitutional; neutral asset-recovery and creditor rules remain available.

Blackmun generally agrees but emphasizes that the statute is underinclusive as well as overinclusive. Kennedy would invalidate the direct content-based burden on fully protected speech without using the Court's compelling-interest balancing formulation.

The case returns for appropriate declaratory and injunctive relief against applying §632-a to the contract. No award to Hill or discharge of victim or creditor claims is ordered. The Supreme Court proceeding is closed.

### *Ardestani v. INS*, No. 90-1141

Merits decision, December 10, 1991

*Ardestani v. INS*, 502 U.S. 129 (1991), was argued October 8, 1991, on certiorari to the Eleventh Circuit, 904 F.2d 1505. The question was whether an administrative deportation hearing is an adjudication under 5 U.S.C. §554 for purposes of EAJA §504. Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter participated. Thomas took no part because argument preceded his oath.

The Court affirmed the Eleventh Circuit by a 6-2 vote. Stone, White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter supported affirmance; Blackmun and Stevens dissented. O'Connor delivered the opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, White, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter. Blackmun dissented, joined by Stevens.

The Court holds that an administrative deportation hearing governed by the INA's exclusive procedure rather than by 5 U.S.C. §554 is not an adjudication “under section 554” and is outside EAJA §504's administrative fee authorization, even if it resembles a formal APA adjudication. O'Connor's opinion, joined by Stone, White, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter—six of eight participants—supplies the controlling authority.

EAJA did not refer generally to formal or trial-type agency hearings; it chose adjudications “under section 554.” *Marcello* establishes that the INA deportation procedure supersedes APA hearing provisions and supplies the exclusive source for this proceeding. Functional similarity and agency regulations cannot enlarge Congress's fee authorization. EAJA's remedial purpose supports protection through enacted language but cannot erase the selected cross-reference. *Sullivan v. Hudson* involved administrative work integral to a qualifying court action and supplies no predicate for this standalone request. Because coverage fails, the Court does not decide whether the agency's position was substantially justified under *Pierce*. The asylum award, covered judicial EAJA claims, and any separately presented constitutional claim remain open.

*Marcello* is applied; *Sullivan v. Hudson* is distinguished; *Shaw* and *Pierce* remain unchanged. Effective December 10, INA deportation hearings outside §554 governance do not qualify for administrative EAJA §504 awards.

Blackmun, joined by Stevens, would treat “under section 554” as a functional category encompassing statutorily required, on-the-record adversary adjudications at which the United States is represented.

No administrative fee award is available under §504. The Supreme Court and administrative fee proceedings are closed. Ardestani's asylum grant and its factual basis remain undisturbed.

### *Union Bank v. Wolas*, No. 90-1491

Merits decision, December 11, 1991

*Union Bank v. Wolas*, 502 U.S. 151 (1991), was argued November 5, 1991, on certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 921 F.2d 968. The question was whether payments on long-term debt may qualify for the then-current ordinary-course exception in 11 U.S.C. §547(c)(2). All nine Justices participated: Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas.

The Court reversed and remanded by a 9-0 vote. All nine Justices supported the judgment. Stevens delivered the opinion for a unanimous Court, joined by Stone, White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas. Scalia also concurred separately, resting the result directly on the enacted text without extended legislative-history or policy analysis.

The Court holds that payments on long-term debt may qualify for the ordinary-course exception in the then-current §547(c)(2). Debt maturity is not an additional categorical exclusion, but the creditor must prove every enacted ordinary-course element for the particular payments. Stevens's opinion states the proposition for all nine Justices; Scalia's concurrence agrees with the statutory holding and judgment.

Section 547(c)(2) contains no distinction between short- and long-term debt. Congress removed the former 45-day restriction and retained three express ordinary-course requirements, so a court may not recreate a maturity bar from legislative purpose or perceived motivating examples. *Ron Pair* supplies the obligation to apply operative Code text when the result is not demonstrably contrary to congressional design. Equality of distribution and discouragement of destructive collection races coexist within the preference rule and its exceptions; neither permits omission of the exception's words. Eligibility does not establish that these interest and commitment-fee payments were incurred and paid in both parties' ordinary course and according to ordinary business terms. Those questions remain for remand with the Bank bearing §547(g)'s burden.

*Ron Pair* is applied. The Ninth Circuit's categorical *CHG International* construction is rejected; no Supreme Court precedent is overruled. Effective December 11, long-term debt payments are eligible for §547(c)(2), subject to all three contemporaneous elements and the creditor's burden. Scalia agrees with the holding and judgment but would decide directly from the unambiguous text without relying on legislative history or policy balancing.

The Ninth Circuit must review any remaining challenges to application of all statutory elements; the Court does not itself restore judgment for the Bank. The Supreme Court proceeding is closed, and the preference litigation returns to the Ninth Circuit for review of the remaining statutory issues.
