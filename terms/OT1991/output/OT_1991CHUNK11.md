## Decisions and Dispositions

### Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, No. 90-1424

Merits decision, June 12, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 3, 1991; decided June 12, 1992. On writ of certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) to the Eighth Circuit, [911 F.2d 117](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F2/911/911.F2d.117.89-5386.89-5192.html), which affirmed the District of Minnesota's summary judgment, declaratory and mandatory rulemaking relief, and dependent attorney-fee award after an earlier remand on standing.

The questions are whether the conservation organizations have Article III standing to challenge the geographic limit on consultation under the Endangered Species Act (ESA) and, if so, whether §7(a)(2) reaches agency actions abroad. The Secretary seeks reversal; respondents seek to preserve the declaration and revised-regulation order. The Court resolves standing and does not reach the statute's geographic scope.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

| Basis and dismissal direction | Lack of standing, six votes | Stone-Zsela, White, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Stevens finds standing and supports reversal on statutory grounds; Blackmun and O'Connor would allow the standing challenge to proceed through factfinding | Dismissal is jurisdictional and does not adjudicate foreign-project consultation or bar a properly supported later action. |
| Dependent §1540(g)(4) attorney-fee award sustained below | Set aside with the reversed entitlement judgment, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Blackmun, O'Connor would not set it aside on the Court's standing ground | No substitute fee amount or independent fee-entitlement rule is adopted. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. Article III elements must be supported at the litigation stage

**Controlling proposition:** A plaintiff invoking federal jurisdiction must establish a concrete and particularized, actual or imminent injury, a causal connection fairly traceable to the defendant rather than independent third parties, and a likelihood that the requested relief will redress the injury. At summary judgment, the plaintiff must supply specific facts by affidavit or other evidence on the challenged elements; favorable reasonable inferences remain available, but allegations cannot substitute for the missing evidence.

**Authority:** Part II of Scalia's Opinion of the Court, joined by White, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, controls with five votes. Stone-Zsela concurs separately in the judgment on the personal-injury ground and does not join this section. The general redressability element is controlling; its application to these absent agencies is not.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that standing is part of the plaintiff's case and must be supported as the litigation progresses. Warth v. Seldin and Allen v. Wright require a personal injury connected to the challenged conduct; Simon v. Eastern Kentucky Welfare Rights Organization requires likely redress through the requested judgment. Lujan v. National Wildlife Federation supplies the distinction between allegations sufficient at pleading and evidence required at summary judgment. Respondents therefore cannot rely on the earlier decision allowing their allegations to survive dismissal. They must produce specific facts supporting their own threatened injury. Rule 56 preserves favorable reasonable inferences from evidence; it does not authorize a court to presume facts necessary to establish jurisdiction merely because the claim otherwise fails. Where independent third parties determine whether the plaintiff will be harmed or helped, respondents must support the necessary causal connection. The Court resolves this action for want of personal injury and does not adopt the separate opinion's application of redressability to the absent financing agencies.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Warth v. Seldin, 422 U.S. 490 (1975): applied for the plaintiff's personal stake and burden to establish standing; its statutory-rights discussion remains subject to concrete injury.
- Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737 (1984): applied for the constitutional elements and the limits on generalized judicial supervision.
- Simon v. Eastern Kentucky Welfare Rights Organization, 426 U.S. 26 (1976): applied for fair traceability and likely redress; no new majority application concerning these absent agencies follows.
- Lujan v. National Wildlife Federation, 497 U.S. 871 (1990): applied for the evidentiary distinction between pleading and summary judgment; its separate APA programmatic-review holding is not enlarged.
- Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317 (1986): applied to require evidence of challenged essential elements while preserving genuine material disputes and reasonable inferences.
- Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242 (1986): applied to require evidence of challenged essential elements while preserving genuine material disputes and reasonable inferences.

##### 2. Indefinite return intentions and general species interests do not establish threatened personal injury here

**Controlling proposition:** Aesthetic, recreational and professional interests in observing wildlife can support standing, but these plaintiffs must show threatened interference with their own concrete use. Prior visits and an indefinite intention to return, without concrete plans or another supported basis for imminent renewed exposure, do not establish standing for these prospective claims; an ecosystem, animal or professional connection that supplies only general interest in distant species does not cure that defect.

**Authority:** Part III-A of Scalia's Opinion of the Court, joined by White, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, controls with five votes. Stone-Zsela's separate concurrence supplies a sixth vote for this record-specific personal-injury conclusion without joining the Court's writing. No purchased-ticket or particular-document prerequisite is adopted.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a recognized interest must be threatened in the plaintiff's own life. Sierra Club v. Morton recognizes aesthetic and recreational injury while requiring a member of the plaintiff organization to be among those harmed. The affidavits identify actual visits to the Egyptian and Sri Lankan areas and an interest in observing their wildlife. They provide no sufficiently definite return undertaking or continuing activity showing imminent exposure to the threatened loss. Los Angeles v. Lyons and Whitmore v. Arkansas require more than past exposure followed by a conjectural future injury. The Court accepts reasonable inferences from the supplied facts, but an unspecified intention cannot itself establish when personal use will recur. The proposed ecosystem and professional theories fail for the same evidentiary reason: they broaden the class of interested persons without showing a concrete effect on these members. National Wildlife Federation requires an affected personal use, not a general environmental concern. The Court does not decide a case involving actual use of an affected ecosystem or demonstrated injury transmitted across geographic distance.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Sierra Club v. Morton, 405 U.S. 727 (1972): applied; aesthetic injury is cognizable, but an organization's special interest cannot replace injury to a member.
- Hunt v. Washington State Apple Advertising Commission, 432 U.S. 333 (1977): applied to require an injured member for this associational claim; no other associational requirement is changed.
- Los Angeles v. Lyons, 461 U.S. 95 (1983): applied for the distinction between past exposure and a present or imminent injury supporting prospective relief.
- Whitmore v. Arkansas, 495 U.S. 149 (1990): applied for actual or imminent rather than conjectural injury.
- Lujan v. National Wildlife Federation, 497 U.S. 871 (1990): applied to specific affected use; no categorical bar to geographically transmitted injury is established.
- Japan Whaling Association v. American Cetacean Society, 478 U.S. 221 (1986): distinguished because its whale-watching and studying members alleged an adverse effect on their own activities; concrete professional or ecological injury remains possible.

##### 3. Citizen-suit authorization does not dispense with a threatened concrete interest

**Controlling proposition:** ESA §11(g)'s authorization for any person to sue does not give Article III standing to a plaintiff asserting only the public's common interest in lawful administration. A procedural right may protect a plaintiff's threatened concrete interest without requiring proof that the procedure will certainly change the ultimate agency decision, but the procedural label cannot replace that personal interest; these respondents have not shown the required connection.

**Authority:** Part IV of Scalia's Opinion of the Court, joined by White, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, controls with five votes. Stone-Zsela does not join this section or its broader congressional-power analysis; he separately concludes only that the claimed consultation deprivation fails to connect to sufficiently imminent interference with these members' concrete use.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that statutory permission to bring suit and constitutional injury remain distinct. Fairchild v. Hughes and the generalized-grievance decisions refuse federal jurisdiction based solely on every citizen's interest in lawful government. Section 11(g) authorizes enforcement but does not itself demonstrate that every violation injures every person. Respondents cannot establish the missing personal connection by treating consultation as an abstract entitlement available to the public at large. This conclusion does not exclude procedural injuries as a class. A person whose concrete interest a required procedure protects need not prove with certainty that performing the procedure will change the final agency choice. Sierra Club preserves the requirement of personal injury while Warth permits Congress to recognize legally protected injuries. Congress may recognize concrete factual injuries previously inadequate in law; the Court does not settle the outer reach of that power. Here, however, the citizen-suit provision does not identify a concrete injury to these members independently of the unsupported future use already examined. The Court leaves the statute's geographic reach undecided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Fairchild v. Hughes, 258 U.S. 126 (1922): applied to reject a general right to compel lawful administration as a sufficient personal injury.
- Massachusetts v. Mellon, 262 U.S. 447 (1923): applied for the distinction between direct injury and an undifferentiated public grievance.
- Ex parte Levitt, 302 U.S. 633 (1937): applied for the distinction between direct injury and an undifferentiated public grievance.
- United States v. Richardson, 418 U.S. 166 (1974): applied to generally shared governmental grievances without distinctive concrete harm.
- Schlesinger v. Reservists Committee to Stop the War, 418 U.S. 208 (1974): applied to generally shared governmental grievances without distinctive concrete harm.
- Valley Forge Christian College v. Americans United for Separation of Church and State, Inc., 454 U.S. 464 (1982): applied; a claimed right to lawful government conduct alone does not satisfy Article III.
- Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737 (1984): applied; a claimed right to lawful government conduct alone does not satisfy Article III.
- Warth v. Seldin, 422 U.S. 490 (1975): preserved for Congress's power to recognize injuries and broaden protected interests while retaining the plaintiff's own concrete injury.
- Sierra Club v. Morton, 405 U.S. 727 (1972): preserved for Congress's power to recognize injuries and broaden protected interests while retaining the plaintiff's own concrete injury.
- Trafficante v. Metropolitan Life Insurance Co., 409 U.S. 205 (1972): distinguished as statutory recognition of concrete personal interests, not authorization of a wholly abstract grievance.
- Hardin v. Kentucky Utilities Co., 390 U.S. 1 (1968): distinguished as statutory recognition of concrete personal interests, not authorization of a wholly abstract grievance.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Effective June 12, 1992, respondents' standing fails for insufficient threatened personal injury. Ordinary evidentiary burdens apply at each litigation stage. The ESA citizen-suit provision does not turn an undifferentiated demand for consultation into concrete injury. Procedural protections tied to a personal interest remain cognizable without certainty that the final agency choice will change. The Court applies and clarifies existing Article III doctrine on this foreign-project record and imposes no documentary prerequisite.

The Court applies Sierra Club's personal-injury requirement, National Wildlife Federation's evidentiary requirements, and the generalized-grievance authorities described above. No earlier precedent is overruled. The Court does not decide whether the Secretary's regulation binds absent agencies, whether particular funding changes would alter the projects, or whether §7(a)(2) reaches actions abroad. Each Court holding has five supporters independently of the judgment-only agreements. Part III-B's redressability application has three votes and Stevens's statutory ground has one; neither controls or changes precedent.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia's Part III-B, joined by White and Thomas, would independently reject redressability because the action agencies are absent and their response, and the foreign projects' response, to a revised rule is not adequately established. Three Justices adopt that application; it does not control.

Kennedy, joined by Souter, reserves that redressability question. Their concurrence leaves room for factually demonstrated ecosystem or professional injuries and for Congress to define injuries and causal chains, while requiring Congress at least to identify the injury and relate it to the persons entitled to sue.

Stone-Zsela concurs in the judgment because the past visits and indefinite return intentions do not show imminent renewed personal use. A definite undertaking or an established return practice could supply the necessary evidence; purchased tickets or a particular travel document are unnecessary. He reserves disputed redressability, the outer constitutional scope of congressionally created procedural rights, and ESA geographic coverage. Actual use of an affected ecosystem presents a materially different record; multiple contributors alone would not defeat such a claim.

Stevens concurs in reversal while finding standing. In his view, the personal aesthetic and professional interest suffers when the threatened species and habitat are destroyed, and consultation can provide meaningful relief. He reads §7(a)(2)'s domestic consultation and exemption structure, alongside the Act's express international provisions, to exclude foreign actions. That construction receives no majority and is not the Court's law.

Blackmun, joined by O'Connor, dissents from ending the action on summary judgment. The prior visits, professional interests and intended return permit favorable inferences about future exposure, and the agencies' involvement and potential project influence create a factual issue about redress. Their procedural-injury discussion concerns Congress's capacity to protect concrete interests through required processes; it does not supply a controlling exception to Article III.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Court reverses and remands. Supreme Court review is complete. The case returns through the Eighth Circuit for vacatur of the merits declaration, mandatory rulemaking relief and dependent fee award, and dismissal for want of Article III jurisdiction. No substitute fee amount or independent fee-entitlement rule is adopted.

The Court requires no cancellation of a foreign project, new regulation, consultation, factfinding remand or particular later filing. The jurisdictional dismissal does not resolve the statute's geographic reach or preclude a later action supported by sufficient standing facts.

**End of entry: Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, merits decision, June 12, 1992.**

### Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc., No. 91-763

Merits decision, June 12, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 1, 1992; decided June 12, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the Second Circuit, [941 F.2d 145](https://static.case.law/f2d/941/html/0145-01.html), which affirmed the Southern District of New York's denial of dismissal, [753 F. Supp. 1201](https://static.case.law/f-supp/753/html/1201-01.html).

The questions are whether issuing and rescheduling the Bonods is commercial activity and whether the missing contractually required New York payment has a direct United States effect under the third commercial-activity clause of the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA).

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

| Component | Judgment and vote | Supporting Justices | Remedy and limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Presented FSIA grounds for dismissal | Affirmed, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | The denial of dismissal stands. The contract action returns for ordinary proceedings; no damages, attachment or execution is ordered. |

The constitutional contention is rejected only as an argument for narrowing the statute. No independent constitutional jurisdiction claim or second-clause domestic-act theory is decided.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment and scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Scalia | White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Affirmance; commercial nature, direct domestic effect and the bounded rejection of constitutional avoidance. |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Affirmance on the distinction between nature and purpose, missed agreed New York performance, rejection of added substantiality, and assumed-personhood avoidance analysis; preserves the stated qualifications and joins no other writing. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. Ordinary negotiable debt remains commercial despite a sovereign monetary purpose

**Controlling proposition:** Under 28 U.S.C. §§1603(d) and 1605(a)(2), an activity is commercial when its nature is the kind of conduct a private participant in a market can undertake; the sovereign's public purpose does not control that characterization. Argentina and Banco Central acted commercially in issuing and rescheduling these negotiable, dollar-denominated debt instruments, even though the instruments served a foreign-exchange program and refinanced existing obligations.

**Authority:** Scalia's Opinion of the Court, joined by White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, controls with eight votes. Stone-Zsela agrees with the statutory conclusion on his separately stated ground but joins no other writing.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress made the character of the activity decisive. Verlinden B. V. v. Central Bank of Nigeria explains the Act's adoption of restrictive sovereign immunity and its exception-dependent jurisdictional structure. Section 1603(d) directs courts to examine nature rather than purpose. Argentina's currency difficulties explain why it acted, but they do not change the means it selected. The Bonods are negotiable promises to repay dollars, with interest tied to a market rate and payment available at specified financial centers. Private borrowers may issue and restructure such obligations. Argentina argues that no private person could administer its exchange program and that the instruments obtained no new money or goods. Those points concern the program's sovereign objective and the origin of the debt. Neither makes refinancing through ordinary negotiable promises an exclusively sovereign power. The Court decides the commercial character of these instruments and this challenged rescheduling. It does not classify every public borrowing, currency regulation or sovereign act affecting money as commercial.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Verlinden B. V. v. Central Bank of Nigeria, 461 U.S. 480 (1983): applied for the restrictive-immunity framework and statutory exception channel; no additional citizenship or commercial-purpose restriction is derived from it.

##### 2. Missing the contractually required New York payment has a direct domestic effect

**Controlling proposition:** For the third clause of §1605(a)(2), an effect is direct when it follows as an immediate consequence of the defendant's activity; the statute adds no separate substantiality or foreseeability requirement, although wholly trivial effects remain outside the ordinary reach of the exception. Failure to make payment in New York, after the holders validly exercised the instruments' contractual payment option, produces the required direct effect in the United States even though the holders are foreign and their ultimate economic loss is abroad.

**Authority:** Scalia's Opinion of the Court, joined by White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, controls with eight votes. Stone-Zsela separately agrees that the missed contractually required New York payment is direct and that no substantiality condition may be added; he is not counted for a separate general proposition concerning foreseeability.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the promised place of payment supplies the direct domestic consequence. The bond terms allowed the holders to choose New York, and they did so. Earlier payments credited their New York accounts. Rescheduling then prevented the dollars from arriving where the contract required performance. This consequence does not depend on treating foreign plaintiffs as residents or on locating all their financial injury here. Verlinden allows qualifying actions by foreign plaintiffs and supplies no contrary limitation. Argentina asks the Court to import substantiality and foreseeability conditions from sources that did not define this enacted clause. The statutory text does not contain those conditions. Ordinary limits exclude wholly trivial effects, but the promised payment here is neither remote nor trivial. New York's reputation as a financial center, relied upon below, is too remote to supply the necessary effect and is unnecessary to the judgment. The Court decides contractually required domestic performance, leaving other payment arrangements and merely incidental domestic financial connections for their own records.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Verlinden B. V. v. Central Bank of Nigeria, 461 U.S. 480 (1983): applied to permit jurisdiction over qualifying foreign-plaintiff actions; its general jurisdictional discussion is not a substantiality or foreseeability test for this clause.
- The Second Circuit's immediate-consequence formulation in the judgment below is sustained; its additional substantiality/foreseeability formulation is rejected, and its financial-center-reputation rationale supplies no controlling ground.

##### 3. The assumed constitutional objection supplies no reason to narrow this exception

**Controlling proposition:** Assuming solely for Argentina's interpretive objection that a foreign state receives the relevant constitutional protection, Argentina's purposeful dollar debt and offered, selected and previously used New York payment arrangements provide sufficient transaction-related contacts to defeat the asserted constitutional doubt. Constitutional avoidance therefore does not justify narrowing the statutory direct-effect clause in this case; the Court decides neither foreign-sovereign constitutional personhood nor an independent constitutional personal-jurisdiction claim.

**Authority:** Scalia's Opinion of the Court, joined by White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, controls with eight votes on this bounded rejection of a preserved interpretive contention. Stone-Zsela independently states the same assumed-personhood limitation in his concurrence. This is not an alternative basis for subject-matter jurisdiction.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the asserted constitutional concern does not require a different reading of the Act. International Shoe Co. v. Washington evaluates the quality and nature of a defendant's contacts and their connection to the suit when constitutional protection applies. Argentina did more than foresee that a foreign creditor might route money through an American bank. It issued dollar obligations offering New York as an agreed place of payment, honored that selection in earlier payments, and then failed to perform the disputed obligation there. Those purposeful arrangements connect this contract dispute to the United States. Assuming the constitutional premise therefore leaves no persuasive doubt that would support Argentina's proposed statutory restriction on these facts. Argentina expressly presents the constitutional point only as an interpretive argument, not an independent personal-jurisdiction claim. The Court respects that boundary. It neither decides whether a foreign state is a constitutional person nor supplies a general test for jurisdiction over unrelated sovereign conduct. The holding answers the avoidance objection and leaves the ordinary merits and asset-immunity questions untouched.

**Precedent treatment:**

- International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310 (1945): applied on an express assumption of constitutional protection, for purposeful contacts related to this dispute; no holding extends constitutional personhood to foreign states.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Effective June 12, 1992, the FSIA commercial-activity exception covers this ordinary negotiable-debt activity and its missed contractually required New York payment. The eight-Justice Court opinion supports each controlling proposition. The immediate-consequence formulation governs; the lower court's additional conditions and financial-reputation explanation do not. No precedent is overruled. Dunhill's background discussion supplies no new act-of-state holding.

Foreign-sovereign constitutional personhood, contractual defenses, liability, attachment and execution remain undecided. No categorical rule makes all sovereign financial activity commercial or every dollar payment a direct domestic effect.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stone-Zsela concurs in the judgment. A private borrower can promise dollar repayment at a creditor-selected financial center; monetary policy explains but does not replace the nature inquiry. New York was validly selected, and the payment was missing at the agreed place of performance. Adding substantiality would rewrite the exception. Assuming personhood solely to answer Argentina's interpretive objection, the purposeful New York payment and financial arrangements satisfy minimum contacts; he makes no independent constitutional personal-jurisdiction ruling. He reserves personhood, ultimate breach defenses and attachment immunity, and emphasizes that ordinary regulatory or expropriatory acts are not commercial simply because they affect money. No other separate writing issues.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Court affirms the judgment sustaining jurisdiction under the commercial-activity exception to foreign sovereign immunity. Supreme Court merits review is complete. The Second Circuit's judgment stands, and the district contract action proceeds subject to undecided defenses and ordinary procedure. The Court orders no damages, attachment, execution or other relief against assets.

**End of entry: Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc., merits decision, June 12, 1992.**

### FTC v. Ticor Title Insurance Co., No. 91-72

Merits decision, June 12, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 13, 1992; decided June 12, 1992. On writ of certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) to the Third Circuit, [922 F.2d 1122](https://static.case.law/f2d/922/html/1122-01.html), which set aside the Federal Trade Commission's order. The agency order was reviewed under §5(c) of the FTC Act, 15 U.S.C. §45(c).

The questions concern the active-supervision standard and its application to Wisconsin and Montana, and the appellate treatment of Commission findings concerning Connecticut and Arizona. The FTC seeks reversal and application of the correct immunity standard; the insurers seek affirmance. Independent review of the legal rule and deference to supported Commission findings govern the two questions.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

| Connecticut/Arizona review | Reverse and remand for reconsideration, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter | O'Connor, Thomas would affirm the existing judgment | Reconsider under the correct legal standard and §45(c); no final immunity determination by this Court. |
| Remaining scope | Not reached by the Court | The seven-Justice reversal coalition limits its judgment to the granted issues | The dissent would sustain the judgment on immunity | New Jersey and Pennsylvania remain outside review; other preserved defenses remain below; no damages, prescribed rate or automatic enforcement judgment. |

| Concurrence in the judgment | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Reverse and remand on the same state-specific terms | Substantive-examination ground, with express procedural flexibility and reservations; no formal join. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. Private rate fixing requires actual substantive state supervision

**Controlling proposition:** Private parties claiming state-action antitrust immunity for collectively fixed rates must establish both a clearly articulated state policy and actual state supervision of the particular restraint. When private rates take effect unless vetoed, the claimant must show that state officials exercised sufficient independent judgment and control to make the rate scheme a product of deliberate state intervention; an available veto, operating regulatory machinery or nominal approval alone is insufficient.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Opinion of the Court, Parts II and III, joined by White, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia and Souter, controls with six votes. Stone-Zsela concurs separately in the seven-Justice reversal on his substantive-examination ground and joins no writing. O'Connor and Thomas dissent.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that immunity for private restraints depends on the State's actual responsibility for them. Parker v. Brown protects sovereign regulatory policy. Midcal requires both authorization and supervision when private parties fix prices, and Patrick requires officials to exercise substantive control over the challenged conduct. A clear policy permitting collective rates cannot answer whether these particular rates implement state judgment. The insurers' proposed reliance on staffed agencies, available vetoes and enforceable duties ends the inquiry too soon. Those features establish capacity; they do not establish the required exercise. This inquiry concerns whose economic decision governs, not whether federal judges consider state regulation efficient or wise. Southern Motor Carriers decided that state compulsion is unnecessary for clear articulation; active supervision was conceded there, so it does not make every negative-option system immune. The Court requires no prescribed hearing, paperwork or number of rejected rates. It leaves materially different comprehensive supervision, infrequent lapses and unilateral governmental decisions outside this private price-fixing record unresolved.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Parker v. Brown, 317 U.S. 341 (1943): applied for the sovereign-policy basis of state-action immunity; mere state permission for private restraints is not enough.
- California Retail Liquor Dealers Assn. v. Midcal Aluminum, Inc., 445 U.S. 97 (1980): applied and specified; its clear-articulation and active-supervision requirements remain independently necessary for this private rate fixing.
- Patrick v. Burget, 486 U.S. 94 (1988): applied; officials must possess and exercise substantive control, and limited judicial review does not substitute for it.
- Southern Motor Carriers Rate Conference, Inc. v. United States, 471 U.S. 48 (1985): distinguished; its rejection of a compulsion requirement concerns clear articulation, and its conceded supervision establishes no automatic rule for negative-option systems.
- Lafayette v. Louisiana Power & Light Co., 435 U.S. 389 (1978): applied for refusing to infer immunity merely from regulation; limited state ends do not silently authorize all associated restraints.
- Cantor v. Detroit Edison Co., 428 U.S. 579 (1976): applied for refusing to infer immunity merely from regulation; limited state ends do not silently authorize all associated restraints.
- Columbia v. Omni Outdoor Advertising, Inc., 499 U.S. 365 (1991): left intact; local zoning, unilateral governmental decisions and different methods of comprehensive rate supervision are not resolved by this record.
- Fisher v. Berkeley, 475 U.S. 260 (1986): left intact; local zoning, unilateral governmental decisions and different methods of comprehensive rate supervision are not resolved by this record.
- 324 Liquor Corp. v. Duffy, 479 U.S. 335 (1987): left intact; local zoning, unilateral governmental decisions and different methods of comprehensive rate supervision are not resolved by this record.

##### 2. Wisconsin and Montana did not actually supervise these private rates

**Controlling proposition:** The supported findings that Wisconsin and Montana performed at most arithmetic checks, left some filings unchecked and allowed rates to remain effective despite missing requested information establish no actual substantive state supervision of these challenged private rate agreements. The agreements therefore receive no state-action antitrust immunity in those two States, notwithstanding the legal effect that state law assigns to official inaction.

**Authority:** Part III of Kennedy's Opinion of the Court, joined by White, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia and Souter, controls with six votes. Stone-Zsela separately agrees with the no-immunity disposition on these supported findings. O'Connor and Thomas would sustain immunity.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the actual findings defeat the immunity claim in Wisconsin and Montana. The Commission adopted findings that review extended at most to mathematical accuracy and sometimes did not occur. Montana allowed a filing to become effective although requested supporting information was never shown to have arrived. In Wisconsin, requested information followed seven years after a filing became effective. These are failures to exercise substantive control, not findings that the States chose economically mistaken rates. Patrick requires actual review of the private decision, and Midcal does not permit a State to confer immunity simply by leaving private prices in place. The insurers' argument that inaction legally signifies approval cannot overcome the established absence of the supervision federal immunity requires. Limited state judicial review cannot supply the missing agency role. The Court decides the claimed immunity, not the ultimate validity of every title-insurance charge. Other preserved statutory and petitioning defenses remain for the proceedings below, and no rate or monetary award is set here.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Patrick v. Burget, 486 U.S. 94 (1988): applied to the supported record; limited judicial review cannot cure the absence of substantive agency supervision.
- California Retail Liquor Dealers Assn. v. Midcal Aluminum, Inc., 445 U.S. 97 (1980): applied to defeat immunity where particular private prices lack actual state control.
- FTC v. Superior Court Trial Lawyers Assn., 493 U.S. 411 (1990): applied for the gravity of horizontal price fixing; this case does not expand the category of challenged conduct beyond the private rate agreements before it.

##### 3. Connecticut and Arizona require renewed review under the correct standard

**Controlling proposition:** The Third Circuit must reconsider its Connecticut and Arizona active-supervision determinations under the substantive-supervision rule stated here and the statutory requirement that supported Commission fact findings are conclusive. The Court does not now resolve the disputed appellate treatment of those findings or finally decide immunity in either State.

**Authority:** Part IV of Kennedy's Opinion of the Court, joined by White, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia and Souter, supplies six votes for this remand. Stone-Zsela separately concurs in the same remedial direction; O'Connor and Thomas would affirm the existing judgment. Section 5(c), 15 U.S.C. §45(c), supplies the existing fact-review obligation; the Court establishes no new general administrative-review test.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court returns the Connecticut and Arizona questions for appellate reconsideration because the governing legal standard has changed the inquiry the Third Circuit must perform. Patrick and Midcal require actual substantive supervision of the private restraint, rather than the regulatory capacity that the court below treated as sufficient. The Commission also challenges that court's treatment of the findings concerning these two States. Section 5(c) makes supported Commission findings conclusive, while leaving the immunity rule for independent legal review. A correction of law therefore neither authorizes replacement of supported agency facts nor proves that every factual disagreement has already been resolved against the insurers. The appellate court should address those matters first under the proper rule. The Commission's request for review does not warrant a new Supreme Court finding about each regulatory act. The Court leaves the ultimate Connecticut and Arizona immunity applications, and the insurers' other preserved defenses, for the remand; Wisconsin and Montana remain governed by the no-immunity determination already made.

**Precedent treatment:**

- California Retail Liquor Dealers Assn. v. Midcal Aluminum, Inc., 445 U.S. 97 (1980): applied as the governing legal framework on remand; no appellate freedom to replace supported agency findings is derived from them.
- Patrick v. Burget, 486 U.S. 94 (1988): applied as the governing legal framework on remand; no appellate freedom to replace supported agency findings is derived from them.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Effective June 12, 1992, actual independent substantive state judgment is necessary for state-action immunity from the challenged private rate fixing. Regulatory capacity and the legal effect of nonobjection do not suffice by themselves. The supported findings defeat immunity in Wisconsin and Montana; Connecticut and Arizona return for proper legal and factual review. Clear articulation of state policy remains independently necessary but is conceded for these four States.

Midcal and Patrick, as explained above, govern privately initiated rates that take effect unless the State objects. Southern Motor Carriers supplies no automatic supervision rule. Parker is neither overruled nor extended to unexamined private conduct; Scalia's separate skepticism changes no law. The Court imposes no mandatory regulatory form and does not decide distinct sovereign action, materially different comprehensive supervision, infrequent lapses or other insurance-law exemptions. It decides no difference between FTC Act and Sherman Act immunity or authority to displace state law because the Commission asserts none here. The six-Justice Court opinion supports each holding.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stone-Zsela concurs in the judgment on substantive examination of the challenged private rate setting. Arithmetic checks and unanswered information requests cannot show that the State actually took responsibility for the rates. Written substantive review can suffice; no oral hearing, uniform form or rejection quota is required. He reserves materially different supervision records, distinct sovereign pricing and unpresented insurance exemptions.

Scalia joins the Court and concurs separately. He accepts that the active-supervision inquiry will create uncertainty for private participants, including outside negative-option systems, but regards that consequence as required by the unchallenged doctrine. His skepticism about exempting state-programmed private collusion does not overrule Parker or alter the Court's holding.

O'Connor, joined by Thomas, dissents. They distinguish the absence of power to review private conduct in prior cases from staffed, funded regulatory schemes with authority and actual agency responses here. They would treat required filings and nonobjection in an operating scheme as adequate state supervision, without assessing regulatory vigor. O'Connor emphasizes that regulated parties cannot control how thoroughly officials examine a filing, and retrospective uncertainty may discourage participation and burden regulators. The discussion of possible private treble-damages suits identifies consequences of their competing rule; no such award is before this Court.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Court reverses and remands. Supreme Court merits review ends. The Third Circuit must implement the Wisconsin and Montana no-immunity determination and reconsider Connecticut and Arizona under the proper standard and §45(c). It may address the other preserved defenses left undecided below, including McCarran-Ferguson and Noerr-Pennington. The unreviewed New Jersey and Pennsylvania disposition remains intact.

This Court does not automatically reinstate the entire Commission order, set rates, award damages, direct a new regulatory form or decide the underdeveloped separation-of-powers claim.

### Burlington Northern Railroad Co. v. Ford, No. 91-779

Merits decision, June 12, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Johnson venue appeals and affirmed both denials of transfer. The question is whether Montana's distinction between domestic and foreign corporations in selecting the county of trial denies equal protection to Burlington, which is incorporated in Delaware and headquartered in Texas. Burlington seeks transfer from Yellowstone County to Hill County; the employees seek to retain their chosen venue.

Although the employees' Federal Employers' Liability Act (FELA) suits remain at an interlocutory merits stage, the state venue determination is final for this review. American Motorists Ins. Co. v. The state court's construction of Montana venue law controls; the Court independently reviews its federal constitutional validity.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. Montana may preserve a resident corporation's home-county preference without extending it to Burlington

**Controlling proposition:** Montana's venue distinction satisfies equal protection as applied to Burlington, which is incorporated outside Montana and has its actual headquarters in Texas: the State may give greater weight to a resident corporation's home-county convenience while allowing plaintiffs to choose a county against this nonresident corporation. Ordinary rational-basis review permits incorporation to approximate headquarters location when the challenger has not shown that the resulting overinclusion or underinclusion makes the venue rule irrational.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, joined by White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, controls with eight votes. Stone-Zsela separately concurs in the unanimous judgment on his as-applied convenience ground, with no formal join. No suspect classification or fundamental right to a preferred county is involved.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Montana may balance plaintiff choice against a defendant's convenience in defending at its actual home. Fritz permits reasonable legislative line drawing, and Bain Peanut recognizes latitude in arranging venue. A corporation's headquarters can matter more to litigation convenience than one local office of a corporation headquartered elsewhere. Montana could therefore protect a resident corporation's home county while leaving this nonresident subject to the plaintiff's county choice. Burlington's substantial local business does not erase that distinction. Incorporation is an imperfect proxy, but Dukes permits approximation, and an easily verified incorporation rule can avoid collateral litigation over headquarters. Burlington has not shown an irrational mismatch on this record. Power Manufacturing invalidated Arkansas's different classification between counties of business activity and unrestricted venue for authorized foreign corporations; it does not decide this distinction based on actual headquarters. The Court does not rely on FELA policy or hypothetical federal venue, and leaves a foreign corporation actually headquartered in Montana for another case.

**Precedent treatment:**

- G. D. Searle & Co. v. Cohn, 455 U.S. 404 (1982): applied for reasonable corporate-location assumptions and the challenger's failure to show an irrational classification on this record.

##### 2. Burlington cannot obtain relief for the hypothetical mismatch that would not affect its own venue

**Controlling proposition:** Independently, Burlington cannot obtain relief on the objection that incorporation imperfectly substitutes for actual corporate headquarters, because a rule using actual headquarters would still treat this Texas-headquartered defendant as a nonresident. The Court does not decide the equal-protection claim of a foreign corporation whose actual headquarters is in Montana.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, joined by White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, adopts this independently sufficient alternative with eight votes. Stone-Zsela reserves the hypothetical corporation but adds no independent claim-limitation ground; his concurrence supports only the as-applied venue rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the suggested defect would not alter Burlington's treatment. Roberts & Schaefer requires a corporate challenger to connect the challenged distinction to its own claimed disadvantage, and Raines preserves the usual rule against invalidating an application by invoking hypothetical injury to others. Burlington's headquarters is in Fort Worth. Replacing incorporation with actual headquarters as the venue criterion would therefore leave it outside Montana's resident-corporation preference. Its Hill County office does not become the corporation's actual home merely because Burlington identifies that office as its principal Montana location. The contention about a foreign corporation genuinely headquartered in Montana concerns a materially different application and supplies Burlington no entitlement to transfer. This alternative does not insulate all incorporation classifications from review; it follows from the precise defect Burlington alleges and the fact that correcting that defect would not help it. The Court leaves that hypothetical application, separate commerce objections and the employees' FELA merits undecided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Roberts & Schaefer Co. v. Emmerson, 271 U.S. 50 (1926): applied to bar relief based on a classification defect whose correction would not benefit this challenger.
- United States v. Raines, 362 U.S. 17 (1960): applied by analogy for the usual limit on invoking unconstitutional applications to others; no categorical exception or new facial-challenge rule is announced.

#### Current-Law Effect and Limits

Effective June 12, 1992, Montana's corporate-venue rule is constitutional as applied to Burlington's established out-of-state headquarters. A State may weigh an actual resident home county more heavily than a nonresident corporation's local office, and rational approximation need not be exact. Independently, changing incorporation to actual headquarters would not aid Burlington. Both holdings rest on the eight-Justice Opinion of the Court. Power Manufacturing's distinct equal-protection holding remains intact. The Court creates no fundamental right to a preferred county, general permission to discriminate against foreign corporations, FELA merits rule, personal-jurisdiction rule, or Commerce Clause holding. Its alternative holding creates no categorical rule governing all challenges to a law based on its possible applications to others.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stone-Zsela concurs in the judgment on the as-applied venue distinction. The legitimate interest is the parties' competing convenience, not local incorporation as a reward. An actual local headquarters may reasonably receive a protection that a Texas-headquartered corporation's Montana office does not. Ordinary approximation is permissible, and this record does not show an irrational mismatch. He reserves the corporation incorporated elsewhere but actually headquartered in Montana, separately presented discriminatory commerce burdens, and all FELA merits. He adds no independent alternative based on another corporation's hypothetical injury.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court venue review ends. Ordinary mandate implementation leaves both Yellowstone County actions in place for their remaining proceedings. The Court orders no Hill County transfer, new venue factfinding, damages, negligence determination, or special federal-forum election. Other preserved trial defenses remain available on their own terms.

### United States v. Alvarez-Machain, No. 91-712

Merits decision, June 15, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 1, 1992. On writ of certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) to the Ninth Circuit, 946 F.2d 1466 (October 18, 1991). The question is whether the United States–Mexico Extradition Treaty bars this prosecution following a Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA)-sponsored forcible abduction of a Mexican national from Mexico over Mexico's formal objection.

Alvarez-Machain, a Mexican citizen and resident, was indicted for alleged participation in the kidnapping and murder of a DEA agent and the agent's pilot. The district court found that persons paid to act at DEA behest abducted him from his medical office in Guadalajara and brought him to the United States. Official responsibility does not mean that regular DEA personnel physically performed the seizure. The Government did not contest those findings on appeal. Mexico formally protested and consistently demanded his return. No extradition proceeding under the Treaty produced this transfer. The allegations of participation in the charged crimes remain unadjudicated.

The district court denied dismissal on outrageous-governmental-conduct grounds. It found that the Treaty violation barred trial for lack of personal jurisdiction, discharged Alvarez-Machain, and ordered immediate repatriation. Its published order did not separately dismiss the indictment. The Ninth Circuit characterized its action as affirming dismissal and repatriation under Verdugo-Urquidez and stated that dismissal was required. The Government disputed that dismissal characterization. The Court reviews the appellate treaty-based trial bar and return disposition.

The United States seeks reversal and restoration of its prosecution. Alvarez-Machain seeks preservation of dismissal and repatriation on the Treaty ground. The granted question seeks no acquittal, damages award, or adjudication of a freestanding customary-international-law claim.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses the Ninth Circuit judgment, 5–4, and remands for further proceedings consistent with its opinion.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Treaty-based trial bar and appellate dismissal direction | Reversed and remanded, 5–4 | White, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor would affirm | The prosecution may proceed. |
| Repatriation order | Reversed, 5–4 | White, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor would require return | No judicial return obligation under this Treaty. |

The judgment determines no guilt and neither orders a diplomatic return nor decides the capture's independent legality. The dissenting Justices would preserve return and dismissal on the Treaty ground, without acquittal or immunity from a later prosecution following lawful transfer.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | White | Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Reverses and remands | Joined in full; the Treaty supplies no prosecution restriction. Five votes support the controlling rule. |
| Dissent | Stevens | Blackmun, O'Connor | Would affirm dismissal and return | The Treaty's comprehensive surrender framework. |
| Dissent | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Would affirm dismissal and return | A narrow official-abduction-and-protest boundary, with express reservations; no formal join in the Stevens dissent. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### This Treaty does not bar prosecution after the abduction

**Controlling proposition:** The United States–Mexico Extradition Treaty does not prohibit this abduction or make extradition its exclusive means of obtaining a defendant’s presence. Because the Treaty supplies no restriction barring this prosecution, the Ker–Frisbie rule permits trial notwithstanding the DEA-sponsored forcible transfer and Mexico’s protest; the treaty-based dismissal and repatriation order must be reversed.

**Authority:** White’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas; five votes support the treaty construction, its application, and resulting remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this Treaty does not withdraw the court’s power to try respondent. Its text creates obligations to surrender persons in specified circumstances and prescribes procedures when extradition is invoked. Article 9 reserves a decision within that process; it does not state that every acquisition of custody must occur through it. Rauscher enforces limits attached to an actual treaty surrender, and Cook enforces an established territorial seizure restriction. Neither requires courts to infer this different prohibition from an agreement that supplies none. Ker and Frisbie therefore govern the consequence for trial authority. Official sponsorship and Mexico’s protest make the conduct different from a private kidnapping, but do not themselves add a term to the Treaty. General international-law objections likewise cannot establish a particular extradition obligation without support in the compact or relevant extradition practice. The Court’s conclusion permits prosecution; it does not authorize abduction, adjudicate guilt, or resolve the conduct’s legality under other law. Diplomatic return outside this Treaty remains an Executive matter.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Ker v. Illinois, 119 U.S. 436 (1886): applied to trial authority after this official international abduction because the Treaty supplies no contrary limitation; not treated as affirmative authorization to seize.
- Frisbie v. Collins, 342 U.S. 519 (1952): retained and applied for the distinction between unlawful capture and the power to conduct a lawful trial.
- United States v. Rauscher, 119 U.S. 407 (1886): retained; its enforceable treaty limitation follows actual extradition on specified terms and does not make this different Treaty exclusive.
- Cook v. United States, 288 U.S. 102 (1933): distinguished; an established treaty restriction on governmental seizure controls there, and no comparable restriction is found here.
- Air France v. Saks, 470 U.S. 392 (1985): applied to begin with the Treaty’s terms and determine the adopted obligation rather than assume it.
- Valentine v. United States ex rel. Neidecker, 299 U.S. 5 (1936): applied to begin with the Treaty’s terms and determine the adopted obligation rather than assume it.

#### Current-Law Effect and Limits

The Treaty supplies no prosecution bar on these facts, and ordinary criminal proceedings may resume. Rauscher and Cook remain enforceable where a treaty supplies the relevant restriction; Ker and Frisbie remain the default rule for trial authority after unlawful capture. The holding does not grant authority to abduct, settle general international legality, foreclose every other remedy, or impose a universal rule against individual treaty enforcement. Neither dissent changes controlling law.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stevens, joined by Blackmun and O'Connor, would affirm. The Treaty's comprehensive surrender framework and reserved national choice would lose effect if a contracting government could bypass them by force. Rauscher permits enforcement of necessary structural implications, and Cook distinguishes a government's treaty-limited power from wrongful private acquisition; the DEA's involvement and Mexico's protest therefore matter.

Stone-Zsela separately would affirm under a narrower proposed extension of Rauscher: the United States may not use its agents to seize a Mexican national from Mexico over formal objection and thereby defeat Article 9's retained surrender decision. He acknowledges textual silence and confines the inference to that governmental bypass. He preserves the Ker–Frisbie trial-authority rule outside this boundary and would openly narrow Ker if it otherwise covered it. Return restores the pre-abduction position; dismissal is no acquittal or permanent bar after lawful transfer. He reserves private captures without attribution, consensual transfers, future extradition, and damages, and reaches no independent constitutional or international-law capture exception.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court merits review of the Treaty ground is complete. The Ninth Circuit judgment is reversed, and the case is remanded for further proceedings with the treaty-based dismissal and repatriation barrier removed. No ruling follows on guilt, a future extradition, damages, or the independently rejected outrageous-conduct theory. No separate Verdugo-Urquidez docket event is adjudicated by this decision.

### Ankenbrandt v. Richards, No. 91-367

Merits decision, June 15, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 31, 1992. On writ of certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) to the Fifth Circuit. Its May 31, 1991 unsigned judgment, reported at 934 F.2d 1262 (table), adopted the Eastern District of Louisiana's December 10, 1990 dismissal without prejudice. The questions are whether a domestic-relations exception to diversity jurisdiction exists, whether it excludes this damages action for alleged abuse of children, and whether abstention—declining to exercise federal jurisdiction on the grounds asserted here—independently sustains dismissal.

A mother brought this diversity action on behalf of her children against their father and his companion, seeking damages for alleged physical and sexual abuse. The allegations are disputed and are not findings. The parties are diverse and the pleaded damages exceed the jurisdictional amount. The action asks for compensation, not a divorce, alimony, custody decree, or review of a state decree. The lower courts nevertheless dismissed on domestic-relations and abstention grounds. The available record identifies no pending state proceeding. Existing family status does not have to be redetermined to adjudicate the tort claims. The Court need not resolve the accuracy or legal effect of the petition's description of the father's parental-rights termination to decide this jurisdictional question.

Petitioner seeks reinstatement and ordinary adjudication of the damages complaint; respondents seek preservation of dismissal on the domestic-relations exception or abstention grounds.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Court unanimously reverses the Fifth Circuit's judgment and remands for further proceedings on the damages action.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dismissal of the damages action on the asserted jurisdiction and abstention grounds | Reversed and remanded, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Reinstate ordinary proceedings and defenses; no damages award or domestic decree. |

The domestic-relations and abstention grounds cannot sustain this dismissal. The Court adjudicates no alleged abuse and issues or modifies no domestic decree.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | White | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter | Reverses and remands | Joined in full; the statutory exception and both abstention grounds. Five Justices support every holding below. |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Reverses and remands | Assumes the limited exception and reserves its foundation; agreement with the ordinary-damages and abstention propositions appears in his own writing, without a formal join in the Court's opinion. |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Blackmun | No other Justice | Reverses and remands | Rejects the statutory exception and uses a limited abstention framework. |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Stevens | Thomas | Reverses and remands | Reserves the unnecessary foundational question. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Diversity jurisdiction and the limited domestic-relations exception

**Controlling proposition:** Article III does not exclude domestic-relations disputes from the federal judicial power. Section 1332 carries a settled domestic-relations exception limited to issuance of divorce, alimony, and child-custody decrees; that exception does not exclude a diverse parties’ tort action seeking damages for alleged child abuse without such a decree.

**Authority:** White’s Opinion of the Court, joined by O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter; five votes support both the statutory exception and its limited scope.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress’s diversity grant reaches this damages action. Article III contains no domestic-relations exclusion, although Congress need not confer every part of the judicial power on the lower federal courts. Barber recognized federal authority to enforce an existing alimony award while reserving issuance of domestic decrees. De la Rama repeated the traditional limitation in distinguishing ordinary federal jurisdiction from territorial authority. Congress retained the jurisdictional language against that settled understanding, and the 1948 revision supplies no reason to treat its change in wording as a substantive abolition of the exception. That history supports a confined exception, not exclusion of every controversy involving relatives. State courts’ experience with domestic decrees likewise does not remove ordinary tort actions from federal jurisdiction. This complaint seeks compensation for alleged misconduct; resolving it does not require a federal decree establishing or changing family status. Its familial setting cannot expand the exception. The Court decides jurisdiction, leaving the truth of the allegations and ordinary defenses for further proceedings.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Barber v. Barber, 62 U.S. (21 How.) 582 (1859): retained as the basis for the limited decree exception and distinguished from a prohibition of ordinary damages actions.
- De la Rama v. De la Rama, 201 U.S. 303 (1906): its distinction between territorial authority and ordinary decree jurisdiction remains; it does not exclude this tort action.
- In re Burrus, 136 U.S. 586 (1890): broad domestic-relations language does not enlarge the exception to this diverse damages suit.
- Patterson v. McLean Credit Union, 491 U.S. 164 (1989): respect for settled statutory interpretation supports preservation of the limited construction; no separate civil-rights issue is decided.
- Fourco Glass Co. v. Transmirra Products Corp., 353 U.S. 222 (1957): a recodification is not presumed to alter substantive law absent a clear indication.
- Lorillard v. Pons, 434 U.S. 575 (1978): congressional retention of language against an established judicial construction supports the retained limited exception.

##### Younger supplies no ground for dismissal

**Controlling proposition:** Younger abstention cannot sustain this dismissal because there is no ongoing state proceeding with which the federal damages action would interfere.

**Authority:** White’s Opinion of the Court, joined by O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter; five votes independently reject this asserted dismissal ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Younger provides no basis for dismissing this action. Younger protects the proper conduct of an ongoing state proceeding against the interference described in that line of cases. Its concern does not create a general power to decline jurisdiction whenever a complaint concerns family relationships. New Orleans Public Service confirms that abstention remains an exception to the federal courts’ duty to exercise the jurisdiction Congress grants and requires attention to the actual proceeding and threatened interference. Here, no state proceeding is alleged to be pending. The federal court is asked to decide damages for past misconduct, not to halt, supervise, or displace an ongoing state adjudication. Respondents’ reliance on the domestic setting supplies no missing proceeding. Because this necessary premise is absent, the Court need not define the outer range of Younger in a different domestic-relations controversy. The dismissal therefore cannot stand on that ground.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Younger v. Harris, 401 U.S. 37 (1971): retained; its ongoing-proceeding premise is absent here.
- New Orleans Public Service, Inc. v. Council of New Orleans, 491 U.S. 350 (1989): applied to require a concrete abstention basis rather than general reluctance to exercise federal jurisdiction.

##### Burford supplies no ground for dismissal on this record

**Controlling proposition:** Burford abstention does not authorize dismissal of this ordinary diversity tort action where adjudication neither requires resolution of an unsettled domestic status nor threatens the state-policy interference that supports that form of abstention.

**Authority:** White’s Opinion of the Court, joined by O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter; five votes reject the other asserted abstention ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the record supplies no Burford ground for dismissal. Burford protects the State’s effort to administer a coherent policy in matters of substantial public concern in the circumstances identified by that decision. New Orleans Public Service confines that doctrine; the existence of an important state interest alone does not displace federal jurisdiction. A domestic dispute might present a different question if an unresolved status determination were necessary to adjudicate the federal action and the suit threatened the relevant state process. This case presents neither condition. The family status has already been determined, and its present legal character does not decide whether the defendants committed the alleged torts or owe compensation. The damages action therefore need not intrude into issuance or alteration of a domestic decree. Respondents’ invocation of state expertise cannot supply the missing interference. The Court leaves materially different future circumstances open and directs ordinary proceedings on the complaint before it.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Burford v. Sun Oil Co., 319 U.S. 315 (1943): retained within its state-policy and administrative-coherence rationale; no basis for abstention is shown here.
- New Orleans Public Service, Inc. v. Council of New Orleans, 491 U.S. 350 (1989): applied to confine Burford abstention to concrete interference, not the mere importance of state interests.

#### Current-Law Effect and Limits

The limited decree exception is confirmed as a construction of §1332, without an Article III bar. A family relationship alone cannot exclude an otherwise proper diversity tort damages action. Younger retains its ongoing-state-proceeding premise, and Burford retains its limits addressing interference with state policy; neither supports dismissal here. The holding creates no federal tort and decides no liability, parental immunity, damages, custody or support decree, or future abstention on materially changed facts.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stone-Zsela concurs in the judgment because §1332 reaches this independent tort action even assuming that a traditional decree-based exception exists. He reserves whether that exception should ultimately survive and its full foundation and reach. The requested remedy controls: ordinary tort adjudication and compensation neither create a federal substantive claim nor modify family status. He rejects Younger abstention because no proceeding is ongoing and Burford abstention because no necessary status question or demonstrated interference appears; he reserves materially changed facts and the truth of the allegations.

Blackmun concurs in the judgment, rejects a categorical statutory exception, and would address the institutional concerns through appropriately confined abstention. Stevens, joined by Thomas, agrees that the case is plainly within diversity jurisdiction and that dismissal is unjustified, while reserving the unnecessary foundational question whether the exception exists or what principle supports it. These separate reservations do not qualify the five-Justice Court opinion's controlling limited statutory rule.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court merits review is complete. The case returns through the Fifth Circuit for ordinary proceedings and defenses on the reinstated complaint. No damages, decree, or finding of liability is entered. No reargument, rehearing, or related docket action is ordered in this event.

**End of entry: Ankenbrandt v. Richards, merits decision, June 15, 1992.**

### Morgan v. Illinois, No. 91-5118

Merits decision, June 15, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 21, 1992. On writ of certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1257(a) to the Supreme Court of Illinois, 142 Ill.2d 410, 568 N.E.2d 755, which affirmed Morgan's conviction and death sentence on February 22, 1991; rehearing was denied April 1, 1991. The question is whether a capital defendant who requests inquiry is entitled to questioning that identifies prospective jurors who would automatically impose death upon conviction, and whether the inquiry here was constitutionally sufficient.

Illinois used the same jury to determine guilt and capital sentence. The judge conducted voir dire, the questioning of prospective jurors, and asked about automatic opposition to death. The judge denied the defense request to ask whether conviction would lead prospective jurors automatically to impose death regardless of the facts. Every seated juror answered general fairness questions; nine were specifically asked about following legal instructions. A prospective juror volunteered a disqualifying death-favoring view and was removed. No finding establishes that an actually seated juror held an automatic-death view. The jury convicted Morgan and imposed death. Illinois's statute and instructions required consideration of aggravating and mitigating circumstances. The requested inquiry was preserved, and the state supreme court addressed it on the merits.

Morgan seeks relief from the death sentence and a lawful sentencing proceeding. Illinois seeks affirmance based on the overall inquiry and trial-court discretion. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses the Illinois judgment insofar as it affirms the death sentence and remands for further proceedings consistent with the opinion.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Contrary positions and remedy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Death-sentence affirmance | Reversed and remanded, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter | Scalia and Thomas would affirm. The death sentence is set aside for lawful further proceedings. |
| Guilt conviction | Unaffected by the decision | Outside the decided question | No new guilt trial or finding of error at guilt. |
| Mandatory jury for any renewed capital sentencing | Separate proposed remand qualification; one vote | Stone-Zsela | The additional condition is noncontrolling; the Court does not require jury sentencing. |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | White | Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter | Reverses the death-sentence affirmance and remands | Entire opinion; six Justices throughout |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Reverses the death-sentence affirmance and remands; adds a separate condition requiring a jury for renewed capital sentencing | Separate inquiry ground and remedial condition; no formal join in another writing |
| Dissent | Scalia | Thomas | Would affirm | Challenges categorical disqualification and independently finds the overall inquiry sufficient |

The six-member Court opinion controls both holdings below. Stone-Zsela's support for the common narrow impartiality proposition is not a formal join, and his additional jury requirement is noncontrolling. No *Marks* issue concerning the controlling rationale of a divided Court arises.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### An automatic-death juror is disqualified from capital sentencing

**Controlling proposition:** When a State provides a jury to decide a capital sentence, due process requires an impartial jury. A juror who would automatically impose death upon conviction regardless of the facts and required consideration of mitigation is subject to challenge for cause; if such a juror serves and death is imposed, that sentence cannot be executed.

**Authority:** White’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, and Souter; six Justices adopt this rule. Stone separately supports the defined inquiry into views preventing or substantially impairing lawful mitigation consideration, without a formal join.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that each capital sentencing juror must be capable of deciding punishment under the governing law. The State need not provide jury sentencing, but once it does, the Fourteenth Amendment requires an impartial tribunal. Witt and Adams identify impairment of duties under instructions and oath as the relevant disability. Ross confirms that an automatic-death juror cannot remain on the jury that imposes death. Illinois requires its jury to consider aggravating and mitigating circumstances before deciding whether mitigation is sufficient to preclude death. A juror who has already fixed death as the inevitable outcome makes that consideration meaningless. The contrary distinction between refusing to consider evidence and invariably finding it insufficient cannot protect a decision made regardless of what the evidence shows. The rule does not disqualify jurors merely for supporting capital punishment or compel any particular weight for mitigation. It concerns an advance refusal to perform the individualized sentencing task, not the judgment a qualified juror reaches after performing it.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Wainwright v. Witt, 469 U.S. 412 (1985): applied to views disabling lawful performance; their governing substantial-impairment rule is not replaced.
- Adams v. Texas, 448 U.S. 38 (1980): applied to views disabling lawful performance; their governing substantial-impairment rule is not replaced.
- Witherspoon v. Illinois, 391 U.S. 510 (1968): its impartiality principle applies to the opposite categorical disability; ordinary death-penalty views remain permissible.
- Ross v. Oklahoma, 487 U.S. 81 (1988): its automatic-death-juror principle is expressly adopted; its result rested on removal of the identified juror.
- Lockett v. Ohio, 438 U.S. 586 (1978), and the received mitigation line: retained as requiring a real opportunity for individualized consideration, not a prescribed result.

##### Requested inquiry must expose the disqualifying view

**Controlling proposition:** On a capital defendant’s request, voir dire must adequately inquire into whether prospective sentencing jurors would automatically impose death regardless of lawful consideration of the facts and mitigation. General promises of fairness and obedience to instructions did not cover that subject here; the denied inquiry requires reversal of the death-sentence affirmance and remand, without disturbing the conviction.

**Authority:** White’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, and Souter; six votes support the requested inquiry, its application, and sentence-only remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the right to challenge a biased juror must include a meaningful opportunity to identify that juror. Witt requires the party seeking exclusion to demonstrate the disability through questioning; that requirement cannot operate if the court bars the necessary inquiry. Mu’Min preserves broad control over wording and detail, but does not permit omission of a subject essential to an impartial jury. A prospective juror may sincerely describe automatic death as fair and may promise to follow the law without understanding that this fixed view defeats lawful consideration of mitigation. General assurances therefore leave this disability unexamined. The State’s successful identification of one juror who volunteered such a view does not establish that the remaining inquiry adequately exposed it in others. Nor can the defendant be required to prove the answers to a question he was forbidden to ask. The resulting risk invalidates this death sentence. The Court finds no actual bias in a particular seated juror, prescribes no fixed questionnaire, and leaves the conviction intact.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Wainwright v. Witt, 469 U.S. 412 (1985): its requirement of demonstrating bias through questioning supports the necessary opportunity to inquire.
- Mu’Min v. Virginia, 500 U.S. 415 (1991): distinguished; discretion over the detail of an inquiry does not authorize complete omission of this necessary subject.
- Lockhart v. McCree, 476 U.S. 162 (1986): its recognition of the opportunity to identify disqualified jurors is applied to the defendant’s corresponding challenge.
- Turner v. Murray, 476 U.S. 28 (1986): its protection against unacceptable capital-sentencing risk supports required subject coverage and sentence-only relief.

#### Limits and Current-Law Effect

Requested capital voir dire must identify the automatic-death disability; generic assurances that leave it unexamined are insufficient. The rule rests on impartiality and lawful consideration. It does not require jurors favorable to either side, prescribe the weight of mitigation, a questionnaire, or a new standard of proof. The Court announces no constitutional right to capital jury sentencing, no general rule for unrelated voir dire omissions, and no collateral-review or retroactivity holding. Stringer and Sochor retain their different scopes concerning the weighing of capital-sentencing factors.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stone-Zsela concurs in the judgment. On request, inquiry must adequately identify automatic-death views that prevent or substantially impair lawful consideration of mitigation. General assurances here did not do so, and neither presumed favorable answers nor the strength of the guilt evidence supplies a lawful sentencing tribunal. He preserves Witt's substantial-impairment rule, trial-court control of wording and follow-up, and juror eligibility despite general support for death or a view that some proved circumstances strongly favor it. He finds no actual juror bias and reserves unrelated omissions and collateral-review questions. His remand position requires a properly selected jury for any renewed capital sentencing, while allowing Illinois to impose a lawful noncapital sentence.

Scalia, joined by Thomas, dissents on two sufficient grounds. A juror may consider mitigation yet regard it as insufficient without violating the actual instructions. Independently, even assuming categorical disqualification, the extensive general inquiry satisfies Mu'Min's allowance for trial-court discretion and warrants deference. They distinguish a refusal to obey the law from a strict but legally permissible assessment of punishment and would affirm the sentence.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court merits review is complete. The death sentence is set aside, and the case returns to Illinois for lawful further sentencing proceedings without this jury-selection defect. The conviction remains intact. The Court orders no new guilt trial or automatic life sentence and decides no collateral-review question. Its controlling remand does not require jury sentencing; if a future capital jury is used, it must be selected consistently with the Court's inquiry rule.

**End of entry: Morgan v. Illinois, merits decision, June 15, 1992.**

### Patterson v. Shumate, No. 91-913

Merits decision, June 15, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 20, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit, 943 F.2d 362. The question is whether an antialienation restriction enforceable under ERISA excludes this beneficial trust interest from the bankruptcy estate under 11 U.S.C. §541(c)(2). The restriction limits transfer of the interest. The Fourth Circuit reversed the district court's judgment including the interest in the estate.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas participated at argument and decision. Thomas was seated before argument.

#### Judgment

The Court affirms the Fourth Circuit judgment excluding Shumate's qualifying pension interest from his bankruptcy estate.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Remedy or nonreach |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exclusion and requested turnover | Affirmed, 9–0 | Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | The interest is excluded and is not subject to turnover as estate property. |
| Alternative §522(b)(2)(A) exemption | Not reached; all nine agree it is unnecessary | Blackmun, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas in the Court opinion; Stone separately | Exclusion makes an exemption determination unnecessary. |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Blackmun | White, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Affirms exclusion; eight-Justice controlling opinion | All sections |
| Concurrence | Scalia | No other Justice | Joins the Court in full and adds noncontrolling observations on consistent statutory usage | Separate observations do not qualify his full join |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Stone | No other Justice | Affirms on the federal-law-inclusive text and the trust and enforceability requirements, with his express reservations | Separate ground; no formal join in another writing |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Holding 1: An enforceable federal pension restriction excludes this beneficial trust interest

**Controlling proposition:** Section 541(c)(2) preserves a restriction on transfer of a debtor's beneficial interest in a trust when that restriction is enforceable under applicable nonbankruptcy law, including federal law. This established ERISA-qualified trust contains an enforceable antialienation restriction, so Shumate's interest is excluded from the bankruptcy estate and cannot be turned over to its trustee as estate property.

**Authority:** Blackmun's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by White, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas; these eight Justices make the proposition controlling. Scalia also concurs separately without limiting his join. Stone's separate concurrence supports the same statutory exclusion subject to his stated qualifications, but is not a formal join in the Court opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that federal law can supply the enforceable transfer restriction that Congress preserved in §541(c)(2). Congress used express references to state law elsewhere in the Code but omitted that restriction here. The required pension clause restricts transfer of Shumate's beneficial trust interest, and ERISA authorizes enforcement of that clause. Guidry v. Sheet Metal Workers National Pension Fund enforces pension antialienation despite strong creditor equities; bankruptcy does not itself create an exception. Union Bank v. Wolas supplies the same-term application of enacted bankruptcy language despite an asserted policy limitation. General references to spendthrift trusts in legislative materials show that those trusts are included, not that federal restrictions are excluded. Section 522(d)(10)(E) still protects qualifying retirement interests that lack an enforceable restriction meeting §541(c)(2); overlap therefore creates no surplusage. The established trust and enforceability predicates exclude this interest without deciding an exemption. The Court leaves other plan qualifications, statutory exceptions, post-distribution treatment, and the exemption provision's coverage of presently payable benefits or undistributed corpus unresolved.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Guidry v. Sheet Metal Workers National Pension Fund, 493 U.S. 365 (1990): applied for enforceability and the refusal to create an equitable exception; its statutory exceptions and reserved plan-fiduciary issue remain intact.
- Union Bank v. Wolas, December 11, 1991: applied for giving effect to an enacted bankruptcy provision despite a proposed policy limitation; its ordinary-course preference holding remains unchanged.
- Dewsnup v. Timm, January 15, 1992: distinguished as a context-specific construction preserving an allowed real-property lien, not a restriction of §541(c)(2) to state law; no holding is overruled.

#### Limits and Current-Law Effect

Section 541(c)(2)'s inclusion of federal law resolves a question left open by the prior framework and this Term's decisions. An established beneficial trust interest with this ERISA-enforceable restriction is excluded. The holding does not change exemption deadlines, ERISA employee classification, preference defenses, or Dewsnup's protection of an allowed real-property lien. Policy observations confirm the textual rule and do not create an independently sufficient alternative holding.

The Court does not determine other plans, nontrust interests, statutory exceptions, distributed benefits, the alternative exemption under §522(b)(2)(A), or whether §522(d)(10)(E) covers presently payable benefits or undistributed corpus. Statutory avoidance remedies are neither adjudicated nor displaced.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia joins the entire Court opinion and emphasizes consistent usage within the Bankruptcy Code. His criticism of the interpretive approach in Dewsnup, which preserved an allowed real-property lien, does not qualify the present holding or overrule that decision.

Stone concurs in the judgment on the enacted phrase's inclusion of federal law and the established trust and enforceability requirements. In his view, §522 independently protects interests not already excluded, and Guidry's enforcement of pension antialienation prevents equitable disapproval of the beneficiary from overriding the enacted protection. He reserves plans outside ERISA, nontrust interests, statutory exceptions, and distributed benefits; his separate writing supplies no general pension immunity or constitutional pension entitlement.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Court affirms the Fourth Circuit's judgment, 943 F.2d 362. Shumate's qualifying trust interest is excluded from the bankruptcy estate, so the trustee is not entitled to turnover as estate property. The alternative §522(b)(2)(A) exemption remains unreached. The Court fixes no payment amount, orders no new factual inquiry, and gives no special mandate timing.

Merits review in this Court is complete. Ordinary issuance of the mandate leaves the Fourth Circuit's reversal of the district court's inclusion judgment in effect. No continuing Supreme Court docket, special stay, retained jurisdiction, or new institutional practice is created.

**End of entry: Patterson v. Shumate, merits decision, June 15, 1992.**

### Allied-Signal, Inc., as successor-in-interest to Bendix Corp. v. Director, Division of Taxation, No. 91-615

Merits decision, June 15, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 4, 1992; reargued April 22, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the Supreme Court of New Jersey, 125 N.J. 20, 592 A.2d 536. The questions are whether to retain the unitary-business limitation and whether New Jersey may include the ASARCO capital gain in Bendix's apportionable tax base. Reargument additionally posed retroactivity and replacement-rule questions only if ASARCO and Woolworth, which preserve the unitary-business limitation, were overruled.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas participated at argument and decision. Thomas was seated before each argument.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses the inclusion of the ASARCO gain and remands for further state tax proceedings consistent with exclusion of that gain.

| Judgment component | Disposition | Vote and supporting Justices | Contrary positions or limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| ASARCO-gain inclusion | Reverse and remand as to this gain | 6–3; Stone, White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter | Blackmun, O'Connor, and Thomas would affirm. |
| Retention of unitary-business limitation | ASARCO and Woolworth are not overruled | Kennedy's Court opinion has five: White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter. Stone separately supports retention. | Blackmun, O'Connor, and Thomas also expressly agree with retention and reject ownership alone. |
| Conditional retroactivity and replacement-rule questions | Conditions absent; questions not reached | The five-Justice Court opinion leaves the untriggered questions unanswered | Stone's and the dissenters' retention positions likewise supply no occasion to reach them. |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Kennedy | White, Stevens, Scalia, Souter | Reverses ASARCO inclusion and remands; five-Justice controlling opinion | All sections |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Stone | No other Justice | Reverses on the retained territorial and unitary-business limit and the absence of an identifiable operational function, with his stated reservations | Separate ground; no formal join in another writing |
| Dissent | O'Connor | Blackmun, Thomas | Would affirm ASARCO inclusion | Agrees common ownership alone is insufficient and an operational route remains available |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Holding 1: The unitary-business limitation and operational-asset route remain governing law

**Controlling proposition:** A State taxing a nondomiciliary corporation may apportion income connected to its unitary business, but may not include discrete, unrelated investment value merely because the corporation does business in the State or owns the asset. A unitary relationship between income payor and recipient is sufficient but not necessary: an income-producing asset or capital transaction serving an operational function in the taxpayer's unitary business may qualify without that relationship, subject to the required territorial connection and fair apportionment.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by White, Stevens, Scalia, and Souter; these five Justices support both the territorial limit and the operational qualification. Stone separately supports those principles subject to his stated reservations, and the dissent agrees with retention and an operational route; neither separate writing is needed to establish the Court opinion's controlling authority.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the power to tax a corporation does not establish power to tax every item it owns. Mobil Oil v. Commissioner of Taxes requires a connection with the State and a rational relationship between the attributed income and values there. ASARCO v. Idaho Tax Commission prevents a general corporate profit purpose from replacing that relationship; F. W. Woolworth v. Taxation and Revenue Department requires actual integration beyond investor oversight. Container Corporation v. Franchise Tax Board permits concrete transfers of operational value even without exchanges of goods. Those decisions allow States practical apportionment methods while preserving a territorial boundary. An asset serving the taxpayer's operations can satisfy that boundary even when its issuer belongs to a separate enterprise. Common ownership alone cannot. The established rule remains workable despite fact-sensitive disagreements, and businesses and States have organized investments and tax systems around it. The Court therefore declines to overrule ASARCO or Woolworth. Because neither is overruled, the conditional questions about retroactivity and replacement constitutional principles do not arise.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Mobil Oil Corp. v. Commissioner of Taxes, 445 U.S. 425 (1980): followed for the minimum connection, rational relationship, and unitary-business framework; a separate enterprise can remain outside the base.
- ASARCO Inc. v. Idaho Tax Commission, 458 U.S. 307 (June 29, 1982): reaffirmed; generalized corporate profit purpose does not establish a unitary connection, and capital gains receive the same constitutional treatment as dividends.
- F. W. Woolworth Co. v. Taxation and Revenue Department, 458 U.S. 354 (1982): reaffirmed; functional integration, centralized management and economies of scale remain practical indicia, while investor oversight alone is insufficient.
- Container Corp. of America v. Franchise Tax Board, 463 U.S. 159 (1983): followed and clarified; concrete operational value can support apportionment without a flow of goods or a unitary payor/payee relationship, but passive investment is insufficient.

##### Holding 2: The stipulated record requires exclusion of the ASARCO gain

**Controlling proposition:** Where the stipulated facts establish unrelated enterprises without functional integration, centralized management or economies of scale, and do not show that the stock itself performed an identifiable operational function in the taxpayer's unitary business, a general acquisition strategy and intended later use of sale proceeds cannot justify apportioning the capital gain. Bendix has met its burden on this record; New Jersey must exclude the approximately $211.5 million ASARCO gain from the challenged apportionable tax base.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by White, Stevens, Scalia, and Souter; five Justices adopt this application and the resulting reversal and remand. Stone separately reaches the same disposition on his separately stated territorial/operational ground. Blackmun, O'Connor, and Thomas reject the application and would affirm.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the agreed facts exclude the ASARCO gain from this tax base. ASARCO v. Idaho Tax Commission supplies the rule that an investment's contribution to corporate profitability is insufficient, and applies the same constitutional boundary to capital gains and dividends. F. W. Woolworth v. Taxation and Revenue Department makes potential control and ordinary investor oversight insufficient. Bendix and ASARCO shared no operational management, facilities, financing or meaningful integrated dealings; their stipulated separation negates the ordinary indicia of a unitary enterprise. Container Corporation v. Franchise Tax Board preserves an operational-capital route, but this record shows no working-capital use or other identifiable operational function for the shares. New Jersey's acquisition-and-divestiture theory merely restates the general business-purpose rationale. Intended later expenditure on a related acquisition does not establish the function of the ASARCO holding while it produced the gain. The stipulations satisfy the taxpayer's burden of showing unrelated value. The Court reserves genuine operational investments and fair-apportionment challenges involving a properly constituted tax base; calculations and appropriate refund treatment remain for the state courts.

**Precedent treatment:**

- ASARCO Inc. v. Idaho Tax Commission, 458 U.S. 307 (1982): applied to reject the business-purpose rationale and to treat capital gain under the same nexus rule as dividend income.
- F. W. Woolworth Co. v. Taxation and Revenue Department, 458 U.S. 354 (1982): applied; no actual integration is shown, and two noncontrolling board seats do not establish centralized operational management.
- Container Corp. of America v. Franchise Tax Board, 463 U.S. 159 (1983): distinguished on the facts; its operational finance and services are absent. Its taxpayer burden and review for use of the correct legal standard remain governing; duration alone is not made a constitutional test.

#### Limits and Current-Law Effect

The existing unitary-business limitation survives. Corporate presence and common ownership alone cannot establish an apportionable tax base. A genuine operational asset can qualify even when its issuer and holder do not form a unitary business. The holding neither gives constitutional force to every state-law definition of business income nor reduces the inquiry to investment duration. It excludes Bendix's ASARCO gain despite the acquisition strategy and intended later use of proceeds, and clarifies the operational qualification through a majority opinion. Both the unitary-relationship and operational-function routes remain available, although neither succeeds on this record.

Container's requirement that the taxpayer provide clear and cogent evidence, and its review for use of the correct legal standard, remain governing. A valid tax base and a fair allocation formula remain separate questions. Genuine operational investments and fair-apportionment disputes on a proper tax base remain reserved. UGC gain, related interest, and separate allocation-factor issues decided below are outside this proceeding. Because ASARCO and Woolworth remain in force, the conditional reargument questions concerning retroactivity and replacement constitutional principles are not reached.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stone concurs in the judgment. He retains the territorial and unitary-business limit, permits genuine operational investments without unity between issuer and holder, and finds no identifiable operational function for the ASARCO interest. Later use of proceeds alone is insufficient; calculations and appropriate refund treatment remain for the state courts. New Jersey has shown no collapse of the received territorial rule's premise or practical unworkability. Concrete multistate tax and investment reliance supports continuity, and disagreement over fact-sensitive applications does not justify abandoning the boundary. He reserves genuine operational investments and fair-apportionment disputes on a proper tax base. Corporate status neither defeats constitutional tax limits nor creates immunity from state taxation.

O'Connor, joined by Blackmun and Thomas, would affirm. They agree that common ownership is insufficient and that an operational investment can qualify without a unitary issuer and holder. They conclude that Bendix failed to prove the gain unrelated to its unitary aerospace business because intended deployment toward the Martin Marietta acquisition could establish the required operational link. They reject a constitutional distinction resting merely on investment duration and would defer to the state court's record-supported conclusion. Their position is noncontrolling where it differs from the Court's application.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Court reverses the New Jersey Supreme Court judgment, 125 N.J. 20, only as to the ASARCO gain presented here, and remands for proceedings consistent with its exclusion from the challenged apportionable base. The state courts must implement the exclusion and determine calculations and appropriate refund treatment. The Court fixes no refund amount or new apportionment factor and decides no other lower tax component.

Supreme Court merits review is complete. Ordinary mandate practice governs, and state tax proceedings resume on remand. No special timing order, retained Supreme Court jurisdiction, or institutional change is created.

**End of entry: Allied-Signal, Inc., as successor-in-interest to Bendix Corp. v. Director, Division of Taxation, merits decision, June 15, 1992.**
