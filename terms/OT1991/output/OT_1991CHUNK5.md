## Decisions and Dispositions

### Hudson v. McMillian, No. 90-6531

Merits decision, February 25, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 13, 1991. On writ of certiorari to the Fifth Circuit, 929 F.2d 1014. Review follows reversal of an $800 damages award entered after a consensual trial before a magistrate.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

The question is whether the Fifth Circuit applied the correct Eighth Amendment test when it rejected the claim arising from this incident of force for lack of significant injury. The accepted question does not require a general definition of punishment for every unauthorized assault.

Hudson seeks removal of the significant-injury bar; respondents seek affirmance, including on an asserted isolated/unauthorized-conduct ground. The October 30, 1983 beating occurred while Hudson was handcuffed and shackled. Undisturbed findings establish unnecessary blows by McMillian and Woods and express supervisory condonation by Mezo. Bruising, facial swelling, loosened teeth and a cracked dental plate accompanied the blows. The court of appeals found the force clearly excessive and wanton but treated the injuries as minor. No new injury, motive or damages finding is made.

#### Judgment

The Fifth Circuit is reversed, 7–2, and the case is remanded for further disposition consistent with the undisturbed trial findings and the rejection of the significant-injury bar.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Significant-injury dismissal | Reversed and remanded; 7-2 | Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter | Scalia, Thomas | Further disposition consistent with undisturbed findings; no increase in the $800 award, new immunity ruling or injunction. |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Parts A and C | O’Connor | Stone, White, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter | Supports reversal and remand | Part A: nontrivial malicious beating, injury and application; Part C: record and granted-question limits on the alternative punishment argument. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Limited injury does not defeat this established nontrivial malicious beating

**Controlling proposition:** A nontrivial beating inflicted maliciously and without disciplinary justification on a restrained convicted prisoner violates the Eighth Amendment even when the resulting physical injuries are limited, where the established conduct satisfies even Whitley’s demanding malicious-and-sadistic inquiry. Injury remains relevant evidence and bears on compensation; accidental harm or a merely technical battery is not enough.

**Authority:** Part A of O’Connor’s opinion, joined by Stone, White, Stevens, Kennedy and Souter: six Justices adopt this rule and its application. The general extension proposed in Part B has only four votes.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Constitution does not make protection from this beating depend on how much lasting injury the prisoner suffered. Whitley v. Albers distinguishes a good-faith effort to preserve discipline from force maliciously and sadistically applied to cause harm; the findings here satisfy even that demanding inquiry. Hudson was restrained, no force was needed, the guards repeatedly struck him, and their supervisor condoned the assault. His dental and facial injuries also establish that the force was not trivial. Wilson v. Seiter requires an objective constitutional deprivation, but its treatment of confinement conditions does not impose a separate significant-medical-injury requirement on these established acts of violence. Estelle v. Gamble supplies the prohibition on unnecessary and wanton pain while retaining its distinct medical-care requirements. The contrary injury floor would excuse serious custodial violence because the victim happened to withstand it. The Court leaves a comprehensive mental-state rule for other force settings, psychological injury alone, individual defenses and lawful measurement of damages undecided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Whitley v. Albers*, 475 U.S. 312 — applied as a sufficient ground on these findings; its disturbance rule remains in force, but a universal extension receives no majority here.
- *Wilson v. Seiter*, 501 U.S. 294 — distinguished by the character of the constitutional wrong; the objective-deprivation requirement survives without becoming a significant-injury floor for this beating.
- *Estelle v. Gamble*, 429 U.S. 97 — applied for unnecessary and wanton pain; serious medical need and deliberate indifference remain the medical-care rules.

#### Limits and Questions Not Reached

Part C rejects the alternate characterization on this record: the undisturbed supervisory-condonation finding and the granted question prevent affirmance on an assumed isolated, unauthorized assault. It announces no general rule defining punishment. Part B’s proposed universal Whitley standard is a four-Justice position. Combining the separate writings under Marks supplies no universal mental-state rule: the six-Justice narrow holding already controls this case, and the other writings do not adopt a common broader rationale. The ordinary exclusion of trivial force is not converted into an exhaustive rule for all unusual forms of cruelty.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Effective February 25, 1992, significant injury is not an additional necessary element for the established nontrivial malicious custodial beating described in Part A. Before this decision, Whitley directly governed disturbance-related force and Wilson governed conditions; afterward, the six-Justice application resolves this nontrivial beating without adopting a universal mental-state standard. The existing rules for medical care, conditions, legitimate discipline, personal defenses and compensatory proof remain distinct. No new mandatory test for every prison-force setting is created.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

O’Connor, joined by White, Kennedy and Souter in Part B, would apply Whitley’s malicious-and-sadistic standard to all prison force claims. That formulation has four votes. Stevens would reserve that heightened formulation for serious disturbances and otherwise apply the unnecessary-and-wanton-pain inquiry, although he agrees the heightened standard is met here. Blackmun likewise rejects a universal extension and rejects an injury floor that would shield intentional cruelty. Thomas, joined by Scalia, would require a serious objective deprivation as well as culpable intent; his view does not require permanent or visible physical injury in every case.

Dates and procedural background: [503 U.S. 1 (1992), pp. 1–29](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-503/pdf/USREPORTS-503-1.pdf).

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court merits review ends with reversal and remand. The Fifth Circuit must dispose of the case consistently with the intact findings and without its significant-injury bar. No amount above the existing $800 award is ordered; no immunity defense or broader injunction is adjudicated. No Supreme Court implementation jurisdiction is retained.

**End of entry: Hudson v. McMillian, merits decision, February 25, 1992.**

### United States v. Nordic Village, Inc., No. 90-1629

Merits decision, February 25, 1992

Argued December 9, 1991. On writ of certiorari to the Sixth Circuit, 915 F.2d 1049. The Sixth Circuit affirmed an order requiring the IRS to pay $20,000 to the bankruptcy trustee.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

The questions are whether original Bankruptcy Code §106(c) waives federal sovereign immunity for this monetary recovery, and whether general bankruptcy jurisdiction, an asserted property proceeding or trust principles independently sustain the judgment.

The United States seeks reversal; the trustee seeks restoration of estate assets through the affirmed money judgment. Nordic Village entered Chapter 11 in March 1984. Officer Josef Lah withdrew corporate money and used $20,000 to pay his individual federal tax liability. The trustee avoided the unauthorized postpetition transfer under §549 and obtained recovery under §550. The judgment is for payment of a sum, not return of an identified fund within the bankruptcy court’s control. Sovereign immunity was raised on appeal; the threshold remains available. No claim-linked waiver under §106(a) or (b) supplies this judgment.

The Sixth Circuit’s judgment is reversed, 7–2. The personal monetary judgment against the United States cannot stand without the required sovereign consent.

Stone, White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas vote to reverse the $20,000 recovery; Blackmun and Stevens would affirm. Scalia delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, White, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. It addresses original §106(c) and the alternative grounds for this money judgment, while leaving true proceedings concerning specific property distinct. Stevens dissents, joined by Blackmun.

Original Bankruptcy Code §106(c) does not unequivocally waive the United States’ immunity from an affirmative personal money judgment recovering an unauthorized transfer under §550. A plausible construction giving the clause binding nonmonetary or claim-determination effect defeats the required clear consent to this monetary remedy; §106(a) and (b) retain their express claim and offset operations.

Scalia’s opinion, joined by Stone, White, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, controls this statutory threshold with seven of nine Justices.

The Court holds that applying bankruptcy provisions to government does not by itself authorize every monetary remedy against the Treasury. Irwin requires unequivocal statutory consent, while Hoffman supplies persuasive statutory analysis without a majority holding binding this federal case. Subsections 106(a) and (b) expressly address claims and offsets; subsection (c) instead makes designated provisions applicable and determinations binding. Reading its paragraphs together allows meaningful determinations of governmental rights and estate liabilities without an affirmative payment order. Its introductory exception also permits a reading preserving the neighboring limits on monetary claims. The trustee’s broader construction is plausible, but that does not make it unequivocal. Legislative history cannot supply the clear statutory expression missing from the enacted provision. Section 550 establishes ordinary recovery authority but does not answer the separate sovereign-consent question. The decision does not approve Lah’s diversion, render subsection (c) ineffective, resolve state constitutional immunity, or determine the operation of a different waiver.

The Court applies *Irwin v. Department of Veterans Affairs*, 498 U.S. 89, which requires unequivocal statutory consent to the particular monetary remedy.

*United States v. Mitchell*, 445 U.S. 535, and *United States v. King*, 395 U.S. 1, supply the same requirement of express consent.

The Court adopts the plurality’s statutory reasoning in *Hoffman v. Connecticut Department of Income Maintenance*, 492 U.S. 96, for this federal issue; that case’s fractured state judgment is not controlling precedent on federal waiver.

*Ruckelshaus v. Sierra Club*, 463 U.S. 680, and *McMahon v. United States*, 342 U.S. 25, require that consent extend no further than Congress’s language requires.

*United States v. Menasche*, 348 U.S. 528, supports preserving operative work for both paragraphs of §106(c).

*Dellmuth v. Muth*, 491 U.S. 223, supports requiring clarity in the enacted language through its textual clear-statement reasoning; the Court makes no new decision about States.

A general grant of jurisdiction over bankruptcy-estate property in §1334(d), an asserted trust interest, or the origin of transferred money in estate assets does not supply federal consent to this personal money judgment. Because the trustee seeks a sum rather than identified property under the court’s control, this order cannot be sustained as a genuine property adjudication or as equitable recoupment.

The same seven Justices join Scalia’s resolution of each alternative ground necessary to this threshold disposition.

The Court also holds that the judgment cannot be preserved by changing its label. Blatchford distinguishes a court’s jurisdiction over a class of claims from the sovereign’s amenability to the requested relief. Section 1334(d) provides the former and does not conceal the latter. The trustee obtained a general obligation to pay $20,000; no identified property supports the asserted proceeding against a particular fund. Whiting Pools required return of tangible debtor property seized before bankruptcy and did not establish consent to payment of money from the Treasury. Begier’s attention to particular property does not turn the historical source of these funds into a presently identified fund. Nor do trust principles governing private owners enact sovereign consent. Bull concerns equitable recoupment, whose limited operation Dalm preserves; this affirmative recovery is not that defense. Genuinely distinct specific-property remedies require their own facts and authority and are not adjudicated without supporting facts here.

The Court applies *Blatchford v. Native Village of Noatak*, 501 U.S. 775, which distinguishes jurisdiction from immunity.

*United States v. Whiting Pools, Inc.*, 462 U.S. 198, concerned turnover of tangible debtor property and does not authorize this Treasury payment.

*Begier v. IRS*, 496 U.S. 53, is distinct because no identified fund supports the actual order here.

*United States v. Shaw*, 309 U.S. 495, retains its force against inferring monetary consent from a property characterization.

*Bull v. United States*, 295 U.S. 247, and *United States v. Dalm*, 494 U.S. 596, preserve equitable recoupment’s limited defensive operation; that defense does not sustain this affirmative award.

Effective February 25, original §106(c) supplies no waiver for the personal money recovery at issue. The rule before the event required unequivocal consent but left this federal application of Hoffman’s fractured analysis unresolved; the rule afterward resolves that application and rejects the specific alternative routes. Claim-linked waivers, actual property remedies and the underlying avoidance provisions retain their own requirements. No constitutional immunity rule for States or later statutory waiver is decided.

Stevens, joined by Blackmun, reads the notwithstanding-immunity clause, the covered term in §550 and the binding-determination language as authorizing recovery. They regard the narrower constructions as inconsistent with the enacted text and supported bankruptcy purpose, and would affirm the award. Their criticism of restrictive sovereign-immunity rules does not change the Court’s rule.

Dates and procedural background: [503 U.S. 30 (1992), pp. 30–46](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-503/pdf/USREPORTS-503-30.pdf).

Supreme Court review ends with reversal. The $20,000 personal judgment is set aside. Ordinary proceedings may address a genuinely distinct and properly supported remedy, but no substitute relief, fund identification or further Supreme Court event is ordered.

**End of entry: United States v. Nordic Village, Inc., merits decision, February 25, 1992.**

### Holywell Corp. v. Smith, No. 90-1361, No. 90-1484 (United States v. Smith)

Merits decision, February 25, 1992

Argued December 4, 1991. On writs of certiorari to the Eleventh Circuit, 911 F.2d 1539. Both petitions challenge the declaration that the liquidating trustee had no federal income-tax filing or payment duty.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

The questions are whether Smith is responsible for corporate returns and returns for the trust holding Gould’s former estate property, with the corresponding payment duties, and whether confirmation of a plan silent about taxes excuses those duties.

The debtors and United States seek reversal in both dockets. The confirmed plan transferred substantially all corporate property and Gould’s estate property to the separate Miami Center Liquidating Trust, effective October 10, 1985, with Smith authorized to liquidate and distribute it. It did not simply substitute a manager in an unchanged individual estate. The plan omitted taxes, and the United States did not object. The bankruptcy court, district court and Eleventh Circuit accepted Smith’s requested declaration of no filing or payment duty. The issue concerns postappointment return/payment obligations, not a final computation or taxes already due before appointment.

The judgment in both consolidated cases is reversed unanimously, and the cases are remanded for implementation of Smith’s statutory filing and payment duties.

Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas vote 9–0 to reverse and remand on both components in both dockets: corporate-property returns and payment, and separate-trust returns and payment, including the confirmation defense. Smith must file the prescribed corporate returns as assignee and pay the tax due with them under §6151; he must also file as trust fiduciary and pay the associated tax. Amounts and implementation remain for ordinary proceedings. Thomas delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter, covering the corporate assignee, the fiduciary of the individual-property trust, and the silent-plan defense.

An assignee holding all or substantially all of a corporation’s property must make its income-tax return under §6012(b)(3), whether or not the corporation dissolves or the assignee continues its business. Smith’s receipt of the corporate estate property satisfies that category, and §6151(a) requires payment of the tax due with those returns.

Thomas’s opinion, joined by Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter, supplies the unanimous controlling construction of the statute.

The Court holds that the tax duty follows the statutory assignment, not a narrower bankruptcy label. Section 6012(b)(3) expressly includes an assignee holding all or substantially all corporate property and expressly applies whether or not the property or business is being operated. The plan made Smith that assignee. He therefore must file the returns the corporations would have filed had the plan not assigned their property. The argument that only a conventional operating receiver or a fiduciary winding up a dissolved corporation qualifies cannot be reconciled with the broader enacted term and its express qualification. First National Bank of Greeley and Louisville Property illustrate applications to particular fiduciaries; they do not impose exclusive factual categories. Section 6151 connects the filing duty with payment when due. The Court need not decide whether Smith also qualifies under another statutory label, and it does not compute income, deductions, penalties or a tax amount.

*First National Bank of Greeley v. United States*, 86 F.2d 938, applied the statute in a winding-up setting but does not limit it to dissolution. *Louisville Property Co. v. Commissioner*, 140 F.2d 547, involved an operating fiduciary but does not exclude a qualifying assignee.

A fiduciary of a separate liquidating trust must file the trust’s return under §6012(b)(4) and pay the associated tax under §6151 when the plan and duties meet the governing trust and fiduciary definitions. Here Gould’s estate property passed directly into that trust rather than revesting in Gould, so the asserted grantor-trust theory does not displace Smith’s duty.

All nine Justices join Thomas’s classification of this trust and its corresponding payment duty.

The Court holds that Smith’s responsibility for Gould’s transferred property arises from his position as trust fiduciary. The plan created a separate liquidating trust and vested the estate property in it; it did not merely replace Gould as manager of the same bankruptcy estate. Treasury Regulation §301.7701-4(d) recognizes a trust organized to liquidate and distribute transferred assets, and Smith’s powers satisfy §7701(a)(6) and Regulation §301.7701-6. Restrictions on his discretion do not eliminate that fiduciary function. The grantor-trust objection also fails on the actual transfer. In re Sonner involved property apparently returned to the debtor before contribution to a trust; here the estate transferred the property directly and Gould made no such contribution. The Court does not approve or disapprove Sonner’s result on its different facts. Nor does it decide that all liquidating trusts are taxed alike or displace grantor provisions when their predicates actually exist. The corresponding tax amount remains unadjudicated.

*In re Sonner*, 53 B.R. 859, is distinguished because it involved revesting and contribution of property; its result on those different facts remains open.

Confirmation under §1141(a) of a plan silent about taxes, coupled with the United States’ failure to object, does not excuse the statutory return and payment obligations imposed on this assignee and trust fiduciary after appointment. The binding treatment of properly encompassed preconfirmation claims does not establish an exemption from these later obligations.

All nine Justices join Thomas’s rejection of the plan-confirmation defense.

The Court holds that the confirmed plan supplies no prospective exemption from the tax laws. The United States seeks recognition of Smith’s duties as assignee and fiduciary after his appointment, not payment from him of taxes already due before appointment. Section 6151 links the payment obligation to the time prescribed for the required return. Hartman v. Lauchli and Pan American Van Lines confirm that connection. Even assuming §1141(a) binds creditors with respect to claims that arose before confirmation, that premise does not bind them as to the later obligations at issue. The Code’s creditor definition reinforces the distinction. Government silence cannot supply an exemption the plan never stated or adjudicated. Respect for confirmation therefore does not justify the declaration that Smith has no duty at all. Existing tax claims expressly adjudicated through a plan present a different question. Ordinary tax and bankruptcy proceedings must determine the proper amounts and carry out the filing and payment duties.

*Hartman v. Lauchli*, 238 F.2d 881, supplies the connection between a return’s due date and the payment duty. *Pan American Van Lines v. United States*, 607 F.2d 1299, supplies the same timing principle.

Effective February 25, the tax statutes govern this plan through separate corporate-assignee and trust-fiduciary categories. No bankruptcy-policy or silent-confirmation exemption defeats the resulting duties. The decision resolves these previously unadjudicated applications without changing the grantor-trust predicates, taxes already due, or the ordinary process for computing and collecting the proper liability.

Dates and procedural background: [503 U.S. 47 (1992), pp. 47–59](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-503/pdf/USREPORTS-503-47.pdf).

Supreme Court review ends in both dockets with reversal and remand. Smith’s prescribed filing and accompanying postappointment payment obligations are declared; amounts and implementation return to ordinary tax and bankruptcy procedures. No Supreme Court supervision is retained.

**End of entry: Holywell Corp. v. Smith, merits decision, February 25, 1992.**

### Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools, No. 90-918

Merits decision, February 26, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 11, 1991. On writ of certiorari to the Eleventh Circuit, 911 F.2d 617. The district court dismissed the complaint on the categorical ground that Title IX did not permit damages; the Eleventh Circuit affirmed.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

The questions are whether compensatory damages are available in the recognized Title IX private action for intentional discrimination, and whether the Spending Clause notice principle bars that relief.

Franklin seeks reversal and ordinary litigation of her damages claim. She alleges repeated sexual harassment and abuse by teacher/coach Andrew Hill while she attended a federally funded high school, official awareness and failure to act, discouragement of her complaint and closure of the investigation following Hill’s conditional resignation. These are allegations, not judicial findings. She no longer attends the school. The lower court relied on its Title VI precedent Drayden and a Spending Clause limitation; Judge Johnson regarded Drayden as controlling without reaching the additional constitutional characterization. Respondents and the United States defend the damages bar. Backpay and prospective student relief would not redress the pleaded completed injury.

#### Judgment

The Eleventh Circuit is reversed, 9–0, and the case is remanded for further proceedings on the damages claim.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Categorical bar to Title IX damages | Reversed and remanded; 9-0 | Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | No opposing vote | Compensatory relief may be sought for proved intentional discrimination; liability, attribution, causation and amount remain to be adjudicated. |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | White | Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter | Supports reversal and remand | Appropriate-remedies rule and its application to intentional Title IX discrimination, including the Spending Clause objection. |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Scalia | Thomas | Supports reversal and remand on a narrower statutory ground | The 1986 remedies enactment acknowledges damages; no automatic full-remedies presumption for all judicially implied causes. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Compensatory relief in the established Title IX action

**Controlling proposition:** When a cognizable federal statutory cause of action exists, courts may award appropriate relief absent clear congressional direction limiting it; recognizing that remedy does not create a new cause of action. In Title IX’s established private action, compensatory damages are available for proved intentional discrimination, subject to proof of recipient responsibility, causation and compensable loss.

**Authority:** White’s opinion, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy and Souter; seven Justices control the remedial rule and application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Title IX does not confine an established private action to remedies incapable of redressing a completed personal injury. Cannon supplies the cause of action; Bell v. Hood and Davis v. Passman distinguish that authority to sue from the appropriate relief a court may grant. Borak confirms that statutory silence about remedy does not itself confine a recognized action to a declaration. Guardians and Darrone support compensatory relief for intentional violations of closely related discrimination statutes. Congress’s 1986 reference to remedies at law and in equity, followed by legislation preserving private enforcement, supplies no direction to bar damages. Recognizing compensation therefore implements the existing action rather than expanding judicial power to hear a new one. Franklin’s departure from school shows why backpay and prospective relief cannot compensate the pleaded past injury. The allegations still require adjudication; the Court awards nothing and decides neither a final recipient-attribution rule nor punitive or unintended-effects liability.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Cannon v. University of Chicago*, 441 U.S. 677 — applied as the established Title IX cause of action; not reopened.
- *Bell v. Hood*, 327 U.S. 678 — applied for appropriate remedies in an existing cognizable action, absent a congressional limit.
- *Davis v. Passman*, 442 U.S. 228 — applied to separate existence of an action from available relief.
- *J. I. Case Co. v. Borak*, 377 U.S. 426 — applied against limiting relief merely because the statute does not enumerate the remedy.
- *Guardians Association v. Civil Service Commission*, 463 U.S. 582 — its intentional-discrimination remedial support is applied without treating that decision as having a single majority rationale.
- *Consolidated Rail Corp. v. Darrone*, 465 U.S. 624 — analogous monetary relief supports the availability of compensation.

##### Notice does not bar damages for the pleaded intentional violation

**Controlling proposition:** The Spending Clause notice principle does not categorically bar compensatory relief for an intentional violation of Title IX’s express prohibition of sex discrimination within its established private action. Ambiguous funding conditions, unintentional effects and the separate requirements for attributing misconduct to a recipient are not resolved.

**Authority:** White’s opinion, joined by the same seven Justices, rejects the distinct funding-notice ground offered to sustain dismissal.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the asserted lack of notice cannot sustain this categorical damages bar. Pennhurst requires fair notice of conditions imposed on recipients of federal funds; it does not authorize intentional breach of a clear nondiscrimination condition while limiting the injured person to ineffective prospective relief. Title IX expressly forbids sex discrimination, and Cannon supplies the established enforcement action. The pleaded sexual harassment and abuse describe intentional discrimination of the kind addressed by Meritor Savings Bank’s explanation of sex-based harassment, without deciding here when a school is responsible for a teacher’s conduct. Darrone also defeats the premise that a spending-based statute necessarily excludes a monetary award for intentional violations. The objection cannot succeed merely by describing compensation as payment from a recipient’s treasury. This conclusion rests on the enacted condition and existing enforcement law, not a general power to redress every injury. Genuinely ambiguous conditions, proof of institutional responsibility and unintended discriminatory effects remain distinct questions.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman*, 451 U.S. 1 — its funding-condition notice rule is preserved; it does not bar relief for this alleged intentional violation.
- *Meritor Savings Bank, FSB v. Vinson*, 477 U.S. 57 — its identification of sex-based harassment informs the pleaded discrimination; no Title VII attribution formula is imported.
- *Consolidated Rail Corp. v. Darrone*, 465 U.S. 624 — applied against a categorical objection to monetary remedies under spending legislation.
- *Cannon v. University of Chicago*, 441 U.S. 677 — the established action supplies the enforcement premise for the notice analysis.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Effective February 26, appropriate relief in a cognizable statutory action is not barred by remedial silence alone, and Title IX permits compensation for intentional discrimination. Before this decision, the cause existed but the presented damages bar was unresolved; afterward, that bar and the categorical Spending Clause objection are rejected. No new implied action, punitive remedy, liability for unintended effects, automatic institutional responsibility, or finding against the school is established.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia, joined by Thomas, agrees that the damages claim may proceed because the 1986 enactment acknowledges legal as well as equitable remedies. They decline to presume every remedy merely from a cause of action that judges previously implied, and do not join the Court’s broader remedial reasoning.

Dates and procedural background: [503 U.S. 60 (1992), pp. 60–78](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-503/pdf/USREPORTS-503-60.pdf).

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court review ends with reversal and remand. The damages complaint returns for ordinary proceedings on liability, attribution, causation and compensable loss. No amount is awarded and no prospective school-supervision order is entered.

**End of entry: Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools, merits decision, February 26, 1992.**

### INDOPCO, Inc. v. Commissioner, No. 90-1278

Merits decision, February 26, 1992

Argued November 12, 1991. On writ of certiorari to the Third Circuit, 918 F.2d 426. The taxpayer, formerly National Starch and Chemical Corporation, seeks current deduction of professional acquisition expenses disallowed by the Tax Court and Third Circuit.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

The questions are whether the target’s professional expenses in this friendly acquisition are deductible under §162(a), and whether creation or enhancement of a separate and distinct asset is necessary for capitalization under §263.

Unilever’s negotiated acquisition was completed in August 1978. National Starch incurred investment-banking, legal and related expenses to evaluate and accomplish it. Stipulated facts and findings establish enduring corporate benefits from access to Unilever resources and conversion from an independent public corporation to subsidiary status. The taxpayer deducted banking expenses on its return and sought the additional deductions in Tax Court. Commercial propriety and reasonableness of the charges are not disputed. The Commissioner seeks capital classification. A shareholder’s estate-planning preference is not itself treated as a corporate benefit.

The Third Circuit’s judgment denying the immediate deductions is affirmed, 9–0.

Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas vote to affirm the denial of current deductions for the disputed acquisition expenses. Capital classification remains; the Court determines no amortization schedule or tax amount. Blackmun delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, White, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. The opinion addresses the separate-asset argument, the enduring corporate benefits, and classification of these transaction expenses.

Creation or enhancement of a separate and distinct asset is a sufficient ground for capitalization under §263, not an indispensable condition; the character, duration and extent of a transaction’s benefits also matter. The professional expenses facilitating this acquisition’s established enduring corporate restructuring benefits are capital expenditures, not immediately deductible §162(a) operating expenses; mere incidental future benefit alone does not compel capitalization.

Blackmun’s opinion, joined by the other eight Justices, supplies the unanimous statutory holding and its application to this record.

The Court holds that a sensible business expenditure need not be a current operating expense. Lincoln Savings classified the particular asset-producing payment before it as capital; it did not make creation of a separate asset the only route to capitalization. Welch recognizes that the distinction depends on the expenditure’s character and circumstances, while Woodward and Hilton Hotels connect professional costs to the capital transactions they facilitate. Here the record supports substantial corporate benefits extending beyond 1978: access to Unilever’s resources, simplified ownership and lasting changes in corporate structure. Those benefits belong to the corporation and do not depend solely on a shareholder’s estate-planning preference or a profit forecast absent from the record. The taxpayer’s proposed asset prerequisite would turn a sufficient explanation into an exclusive statutory definition. Its concern about routine expenses is answered by preserving the difference between substantial enduring benefits and incidental future usefulness. The Court affirms only these expenses’ capital classification and determines neither every acquisition’s treatment nor a later recovery period.

*Commissioner v. Lincoln Savings & Loan Association*, 403 U.S. 345, is clarified: a separate asset supplies a sufficient ground for capitalization, not an exclusive prerequisite.

The Court applies *Welch v. Helvering*, 290 U.S. 111, for the distinction, made in context, between necessary business outlays and current expenses.

*Woodward v. Commissioner*, 397 U.S. 572, connects professional expenses to the underlying capital acquisition; *United States v. Hilton Hotels Corp.*, 397 U.S. 580, likewise supports the capital character of professional costs connected with an acquisition.

*Commissioner v. Idaho Power Co.*, 418 U.S. 1, retains the rule that ordinary expense categories do not override required capitalization.

*Interstate Transit Lines v. Commissioner*, 319 U.S. 590, leaves the taxpayer’s burden to establish an authorized deduction unchanged.

Effective February 26, the asset-prerequisite contention is rejected and these friendly-acquisition expenses must be capitalized. Lincoln Savings retains full force for its sufficient asset-based category. The §162/§263 distinction, ordinary burden, incidental-benefit qualification and separate rules for later cost recovery remain in force; no automatic capitalization rule for every future benefit or professional fee is created.

Dates and procedural background: [503 U.S. 79 (1992), pp. 79–90](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-503/pdf/USREPORTS-503-79.pdf).

Supreme Court review ends with affirmance. The reviewed denial of immediate deductions remains operative. No remand for a different classification, amortization schedule or additional tax assessment is ordered.

**End of entry: INDOPCO, Inc. v. Commissioner, merits decision, February 26, 1992.**

### Arkansas v. Oklahoma, No. 90-1262, No. 90-1266 (EPA v. Oklahoma)

Merits decision, February 26, 1992

Argued December 11, 1991. On writs of certiorari to the Tenth Circuit, 908 F.2d 595. Both petitions challenge the judgment invalidating EPA’s federal discharge permit for Fayetteville’s sewage-treatment plant.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

The questions are whether EPA may condition this upstream federal permit on downstream state standards; whether existing downstream degradation categorically bars the discharge; and whether EPA’s compliance interpretation and supported technical findings survive judicial review.

Fayetteville applied in 1985 for a permit; effluent travels through creeks and the Illinois River for about 39 miles to Oklahoma. The permit includes numerical conditions and modification if a study requires tighter limits for Oklahoma compliance. EPA’s Chief Judicial Officer replaced an undue-impact test with a preponderance showing of no actual detectable violation. The administrative law judge made findings for each component meeting that direction, sustained by EPA. Arkansas contests an obligatory downstream rule; EPA defends its authority and actual determination; Oklahoma seeks protection of scenic-water standards. The Tenth Circuit imposed an already-degraded-waters prohibition not advanced by a party, without displacing the no-detectable-violation finding. The separate White River discharge is outside review.

The Tenth Circuit is reversed unanimously in both dockets. EPA’s reviewed permit determination is sustained under its existing compliance and modification conditions.

Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas vote 9–0 to reverse in both dockets and sustain EPA’s permit determination. The categorical degradation bar is removed; the permit’s existing standards, study and modification conditions remain. Stevens delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, White, Blackmun, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. The opinion covers federal permit authority, the absence of a categorical statutory bar, the incorporated standards, and administrative review.

The Clean Water Act authorizes EPA, in issuing this federal permit under §402(a), to require compliance with applicable downstream state water-quality standards through its valid regulations. A downstream State’s limited direct role in permitting does not restrict that federal authority; whether the Act itself commands identical compliance in every permitting setting remains open.

Stevens’s opinion, joined by all other Justices, supplies the unanimous holding on statutory authority.

The Court holds that EPA acted within Congress’s delegation when it protected Oklahoma’s water standards through the federal permit. Section 402(a)(2) gives the Administrator substantial authority to prescribe permit conditions, and §301(b)(1)(C) identifies attainment of applicable water-quality standards as a statutory objective. Regulation §122.4(d), requiring assurance of affected-state compliance, reasonably carries out that scheme. Milwaukee II identifies the Act’s displacement of federal nuisance law, while Ouellette limits an affected State’s independent common-law and direct permitting role. Neither case bars the federal agency from imposing conditions Congress authorized. Arkansas’s contrary argument confuses limits on a State’s own authority with limits on EPA’s delegated power. Chevron supports this reasonable exercise of that power; expertise alone would not create it. Because the actual federal permit lies within the authorized approach, the Court does not decide whether Congress compels the same formulation in every federal or state permit, or whether an independent source-state remedy applies.

*Milwaukee v. Illinois*, 451 U.S. 304, retains its rule that the statutory water-pollution scheme displaces federal nuisance common law.

*International Paper Co. v. Ouellette*, 479 U.S. 481, limits an affected State’s common-law and direct permitting role; those limits do not constrain this condition imposed under EPA’s delegated authority.

*Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, applies within Congress’s actual delegation; the Court does not revise agency deference generally.

*Lechmere, Inc. v. NLRB*, January 27, 1992, retains its requirement that deference operate within controlling statutory precedent; no contrary construction is displaced here.

The Clean Water Act does not categorically forbid a new discharge merely because its effluent will reach waters already failing a water-quality standard, irrespective of whether it will cause a detectable violation under the applicable permit standards. Courts may not substitute that categorical prohibition for the Act’s actual permitting and pollution-reduction scheme.

Stevens’s unanimous opinion rejects this independent statutory basis for the court of appeals’ invalidation of the permit.

The Court holds that the court of appeals added a prohibition Congress did not enact. The Act contains duties directed toward meeting water-quality standards and provisions for reducing existing pollution, including §303(d)’s framework for allocating reduction burdens. It does not say that every additional source must be barred whenever receiving waters already are degraded. Section 402(h), on which the lower court relied, concerns additional pollutants accepted by a treatment plant violating its own permit; it does not create the broader downstream prohibition. Milwaukee II’s recognition of a comprehensive statutory program is not authority to replace that program with a court’s preferred environmental policy. Existing degradation remains relevant when the governing standards make it so, but it does not eliminate the need to evaluate the proposed discharge under those standards. EPA applied the required no-detectable-violation approach here. The Court neither excuses a detectable violation nor decides later permit conditions on facts outside this record.

*Milwaukee v. Illinois*, 451 U.S. 304, describes a comprehensive statutory program; it does not authorize an additional categorical prohibition created by a court.

For this interstate federal permit, EPA’s valid regulation incorporates the applicable, federally approved downstream standards into federal permitting law, and its reasonable, consistently held construction receives substantial deference. EPA permissibly construed Oklahoma’s no-degradation requirement to prohibit an actual detectable or measurable violation, rather than every infinitesimal theoretical effect.

Stevens’s unanimous opinion controls interpretation of the standards as used in this federal permit; it does not decide state law for unrelated purposes.

The Court holds that the reviewing court must respect EPA’s permissible construction of the standards governing its federal permit. The standards were developed with federal guidance, approved by EPA and incorporated through Regulation §122.4(d); in this interstate permitting setting, they operate as part of federal water-pollution law. Chevron and the received regulatory-deference principle reflected in Seminole Rock support deference to a reasonable, consistently held interpretation within that framework. Requiring a detectable or measurable violation provides a means to determine compliance and does not make every theoretical molecule of additional effluent a downstream veto. National Center for Immigrants’ Rights’ acceptance of a supported construction tied to its governing regulation is consistent with that approach. The contrary categorical reading lacks support in the statutory or regulatory text. The Court preserves actual standards and their enforcement: it does not announce a general exemption for pollution described as trivial, excuse unreliable science, or confer authority to disregard a demonstrated detectable violation.

*Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, supports the authorized construction of federal permitting law.

*Bowles v. Seminole Rock & Sand Co.*, 325 U.S. 410, supplies the established principle for interpreting regulations within valid delegated authority.

*INS v. National Center for Immigrants’ Rights, Inc.*, December 16, 1991, supports a consistent regulatory construction tied to its governing source; its immigration rules are not imported.

A reviewing court must accept agency adjudicative findings supported by substantial evidence on the record as a whole and may not replace them merely because competing findings also could be supported. Here EPA’s supported finding that Fayetteville carried the administrative preponderance burden of no actual detectable violation sustains the permit; the court’s contrary policy does not establish arbitrary agency action.

Stevens’s unanimous opinion controls both the rule for appellate review and its application to this permit.

The Court holds that the permit survives review on the record actually decided by EPA. Universal Camera requires substantial evidence supporting the agency’s findings on the whole record, not evidence from which the reviewing court could make different findings. The ALJ addressed nutrient enrichment, appearance, dissolved oxygen and metals under the corrected no-detectable-violation standard and found the city’s preponderance burden satisfied. The identified errors concerning phosphorus in streams and the standards’ edition are harmless here: the findings addressed the more vulnerable lake, and the relevant editions did not materially differ. State Farm requires attention to important aspects of the problem, but the lower court made existing degradation decisive only by adopting its erroneous categorical rule. It could not then label EPA arbitrary for failing to follow that invented rule. The existing study and modification conditions remain safeguards for further information. No new discharge limit, scientific threshold or finding about later water quality is imposed.

*Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, requires acceptance of supported agency findings despite merely permissible alternative findings. *Motor Vehicle Manufacturers Association v. State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Co.*, 463 U.S. 29, directs attention to the actual governing problem, not a categorical policy created by the reviewing court.

Effective February 26, the federal permit scheme authorizes the downstream-compliance approach, rejects the categorical already-degraded-waters bar and sustains the supported no-detectable-violation determination. Before this event those precise statutory and permit applications were unresolved; afterward these rules govern at their stated federal-permit scope. EPA’s administrative preponderance burden, whole-record review, valid conditions and modification mechanism remain distinct and unchanged. General deference, state-law remedies and mandatory duties outside this setting are not revised.

Dates and procedural background: [503 U.S. 91 (1992), pp. 91–114](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-503/pdf/USREPORTS-503-91.pdf).

Supreme Court review ends in both dockets. EPA’s permit determination is sustained with its existing compliance, study and modification conditions. No new limit or order concerning the White River discharge is entered; no future scientific finding or Supreme Court supervision is presumed.

**End of entry: Arkansas v. Oklahoma, merits decision, February 26, 1992.**

### Collins v. City of Harker Heights, No. 90-1279

Merits decision, February 26, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 5, 1991. On writ of certiorari to the Fifth Circuit, 916 F.2d 284. The Court reviews dismissal of a widow’s municipal substantive-due-process claim following her husband’s death while working in a sewer.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

The questions are whether §1983 imposes an independent governmental-power-abuse element on public employees, and whether the pleaded deliberate failures to train, warn and equip establish a federal substantive-due-process deprivation.

Larry Collins died from asphyxia after entering a manhole to clear a sewer on October 21, 1988. The complaint alleges knowing policies of deficient training, warnings and equipment, a supervisor’s prior loss of consciousness, and intentional failure to comply with the Texas Hazard Communication Act. The city’s alleged knowledge and deliberate omissions are accepted at dismissal. There is no allegation that officials deliberately injured Collins or directed him into a known dangerous sewer. The district court found no constitutional violation; the Fifth Circuit affirmed on an additional governmental-abuse element without deciding that issue. Collins seeks reversal; the city defends both statutory and constitutional grounds.

#### Judgment

The Fifth Circuit’s judgment dismissing the federal claim is affirmed, 9–0, on constitutional grounds. Its additional statutory governmental-abuse requirement is rejected.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dismissal of the federal substantive-due-process claim | Affirmed; 9-0 | Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | No opposing vote | No federal damages or supervision order; independent state-law remedies remain for the proper courts. |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Parts A and B | Stevens | Stone, White, Blackmun, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Supports affirmance on the stated constitutional limits | Part A: no extra §1983 governmental-abuse element and separate municipal attribution; Part B: voluntary employment creates no general constitutional safe-workplace duty. |
| Opinion of the Court, Part C | Stevens | White, Blackmun, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Supplies the Court’s rejection of constitutional arbitrariness | The pleaded safety omissions, including assumed state-law duties, are not conscience shocking under the limited public-program rationale. |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Stone | No other Justice | Joins Parts A and B and affirms on his distinct ground concerning the alleged omissions | Knowledge does not supply custody or a pleaded affirmative deprivation; actual compulsion, deliberately inflicted injury and properly alleged affirmative danger remain open. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### No extra governmental-abuse element under §1983

**Controlling proposition:** A municipal employee who establishes deprivation of a federal right under color of state law and municipal responsibility need not also prove an independent, freestanding abuse-of-governmental-power element. The existence of the constitutional wrong and its attribution to municipal policy or custom remain separate required inquiries.

**Authority:** Part A of Stevens’s opinion, joined by all other Justices; unanimous statutory holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that public employment does not remove the protections Congress made enforceable through §1983. The statute does not distinguish a federal violation by an employer from a federal violation by a government acting in another role. Monell recognizes municipal responsibility for a qualifying policy or custom that causes a federal deprivation, while excluding liability merely because a city employs the wrongdoer. Canton addresses when inadequate training can supply that policy and causal connection; it does not establish a constitutional right whenever training is deficient. An additional abstract governmental-abuse element could defeat otherwise valid speech, equality or bodily-liberty claims solely because the injured person works for government. The circuit’s added requirement therefore cannot sustain dismissal. That error does not establish this plaintiff’s substantive right or require reversal if the actual complaint alleges no constitutional deprivation. The Court assumes attribution only for purposes of deciding that separate question and makes no final municipal-liability finding.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Monell v. Department of Social Services*, 436 U.S. 658 — applied: municipal policy and causation remain distinct from employment alone.
- *Canton v. Harris*, 489 U.S. 378 — clarified as an attribution doctrine, not a source of every asserted substantive right.
- *Oklahoma City v. Tuttle*, 471 U.S. 808 — preserved for the separation between a constitutional wrong and proof of municipal responsibility.

##### Voluntary public employment creates no general constitutional safety duty

**Controlling proposition:** The substantive Due Process Clause does not impose an independent general obligation on a municipality to provide its voluntary employees a workplace free from unreasonable risks of harm. Accepting public employment does not itself create the custody or deprivation of liberty that supports affirmative constitutional duties of care.

**Authority:** Part B of Stevens’s opinion, joined by all other Justices; nine Justices adopt this duty limitation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Constitution does not turn the employment relationship itself into protective custody. DeShaney distinguishes a restriction on the State’s own deprivations from a general guarantee that a person’s interests will not suffer harm. Youngberg’s affirmative care duties arise in involuntary confinement, where the State limits the person’s ability to protect himself; that premise is absent when a person voluntarily accepts municipal employment. Daniels also cautions that substantive due process does not replace ordinary tort duties, and Bishop confirms that the Constitution does not guarantee correct public-employment decisions. The complaint alleges serious risks and knowing failures of protection, but those allegations do not make Collins’s employment a custodial restraint. The contrary approach would constitutionalize a general safe-workplace duty without the necessary constitutional source. This holding does not insulate public employers from applicable federal rights, decide actual compulsion or deliberately inflicted bodily injury, or determine the availability of state safety and tort remedies.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *DeShaney v. Winnebago County Department of Social Services*, 489 U.S. 189 — applied to the absence of a general protective duty; distinct custody and affirmative-deprivation questions remain.
- *Youngberg v. Romeo*, 457 U.S. 307 — distinguished because voluntary employment is not involuntary custody.
- *Daniels v. Williams*, 474 U.S. 327 — preserved: constitutional due process does not replace ordinary tort law; the knowing allegations are not redescribed as mere negligence.
- *Bishop v. Wood*, 426 U.S. 341 — applied against treating employment mistakes as a general constitutional violation.

##### These pleaded omissions do not establish constitutional arbitrariness

**Controlling proposition:** The pleaded municipal failures to train, warn and equip sewer workers against known hazards do not constitute arbitrary or conscience-shocking deprivation under substantive due process in this voluntary-employment setting. Even assuming the Texas safety statute creates the asserted duties and a protected liberty interest, breach alone does not establish that distinct substantive constitutional wrong.

**Authority:** Part C of Stevens’s opinion, joined by White, Blackmun, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; eight Justices control this alternative rejection of the substantive claim. Stone agrees with the judgment on his separately stated ground, without joining this rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court also holds that the alleged omissions are not constitutionally arbitrary on the theory presented. Rochin’s protection against conscience-shocking government intrusion does not convert every knowing failure of workplace protection into that kind of deprivation. The complaint does not allege deliberate bodily injury or an order knowingly sending Collins into a dangerous sewer. Decisions about training and equipment occur within public programs whose administration ordinarily reflects competing social, political and economic choices. That legal presumption is not a factual finding that this city considered the risks responsibly or that a particular budget justified its conduct. Bishop and Daniels explain why federal substantive due process does not generally replace state employment and tort rules. Even assuming the Texas statute creates the claimed liberty interest, Harrah’s distinction between an entitlement and arbitrary deprivation leaves the same constitutional deficiency. The Court therefore affirms without approving the city’s conduct, adjudicating state remedies or resolving a different claim of affirmative injury.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Rochin v. California*, 342 U.S. 165 — distinguished: the pleaded protective omissions are not its form of conscience-shocking bodily intrusion.
- *Bishop v. Wood*, 426 U.S. 341 — applied to preserve the distinction between employment duties and substantive constitutional arbitrariness.
- *Daniels v. Williams*, 474 U.S. 327 — applied against substituting substantive due process for ordinary state duties.
- *Harrah Independent School District v. Martin*, 440 U.S. 194 — applied to distinguish an assumed state-created interest from constitutionally arbitrary deprivation.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Effective February 26, §1983 contains no freestanding governmental-abuse element beyond a federal violation and the required municipal responsibility. Voluntary employment creates no general federal safety guarantee; eight Justices also hold these pleaded omissions constitutionally nonarbitrary on Part C’s limited rationale. Monell and Canton remain attribution rules, not sources of the underlying right. No general affirmative-danger doctrine is adopted or categorically rejected, and no knowing allegation is reduced to negligence.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stone agrees that the complaint fails because knowledge and deliberate omission do not supply custody or a pleaded affirmative exercise of authority creating the specific danger. He joins the rejection of the circuit’s extra §1983 element and the general safe-workplace claim, but does not rely on a presumption that budget choices justified the omissions. He preserves different claims of actual compulsion, deliberately inflicted injury or properly alleged affirmative danger, without adopting a broader new rule here.

Dates and procedural background: [503 U.S. 115 (1992), pp. 115–130](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-503/pdf/USREPORTS-503-115.pdf).

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court review ends with affirmance of dismissal of the federal claim. No federal compensation or supervision is ordered. Independently available state-law remedies and their procedural treatment remain for the proper courts; their merits and timeliness are not decided.

**End of entry: Collins v. City of Harker Heights, merits decision, February 26, 1992.**

### Willy v. Coastal Corp., No. 90-1150

Merits decision, March 3, 1992

Argued December 3, 1991. On writ of certiorari to the Fifth Circuit, 915 F.2d 965. The Court reviews the power to preserve a Rule 11 sanction after the federal court was held to lack subject-matter jurisdiction over a removed employment action.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

The questions are whether the Rules authorize this sanction and whether Article III permits it despite the established absence of jurisdiction over the underlying claims.

Willy sued his former employer in Texas state court, alleging discharge for refusing to participate in violations of environmental law. Coastal removed the action. The district court rejected Willy’s jurisdictional objection, dismissed the claims and imposed Rule 11 fees jointly and severally on Willy and his lawyer. The Fifth Circuit, 855 F.2d 1160, reversed the merits disposition for want of federal jurisdiction, directed return to state court and upheld sanctioning authority while requiring recalculation. The district court fixed $19,307, and the Fifth Circuit affirmed in the judgment now reviewed. The sanctioned filings included disorganized voluminous materials and careless legal pleading; Willy’s successful jurisdictional objection was not the sanctioned conduct. The propriety of the Rule 11 violation, reasonable fee response and joint liability is taken as given on this limited review. Willy seeks elimination of the sanction for lack of jurisdiction; Coastal seeks affirmance.

The Fifth Circuit’s judgment sustaining the $19,307 Rule 11 sanction is affirmed, 9–0. The absence of federal jurisdiction over the employment claims remains settled.

Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas vote to affirm the sanctioning authority and preserve the $19,307 sanction jointly and severally against Willy and his attorney, without enlarging it or restoring the federal merits judgment. Stone delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined by White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. The opinion sustains both the Rules’ authority and the sanction’s constitutional validity as applied to this completed procedural violation, which is separate from the merits.

A federal district court may impose and preserve a valid Rule 11 sanction for litigation misconduct before it even when its assertion of subject-matter jurisdiction over the action is later held erroneous. The sanction must address an independently authorized procedural violation collateral to the merits; it does not validate the jurisdictional error or authorize adjudication of the underlying claim.

Stone’s opinion, joined by all eight Associate Justices, supplies the unanimous holding on both the Rules’ authorization and the Article III objection.

The Court holds that this procedural sanction survives the loss of the underlying federal action. Rules 1 and 81(c) apply the Federal Rules to civil proceedings after removal, and Rule 11 supplies the particular sanctioning authority. Sibbach preserves the limit that a procedural rule cannot enlarge substantive rights or jurisdiction. This order respects that limit because it determines misuse of the court’s procedure, not the legal merits of the employment claim. Cooter & Gell establishes that a Rule 11 violation presents an independent issue surviving termination of the action; that reasoning extends to the jurisdictional defect presented here. Mine Workers likewise recognizes the need for orderly proceedings while disputed authority is determined. Catholic Conference does not require cancellation: its coercive discovery served a merits action the court lacked power to pursue. Here the misconduct was complete and did not consist of Willy’s correct jurisdictional objection. The Court sustains the existing sanction without relying on general inherent power or reviving the invalid merits judgment.

*Sibbach v. Wilson & Co.*, 312 U.S. 1, preserves the limit that procedural rules cannot enlarge substantive rights or jurisdiction.

*Cooter & Gell v. Hartmarx Corp.*, 496 U.S. 384, extends from voluntary dismissal to the absence of merits jurisdiction presented here because adjudication under Rule 11 is separate from the merits.

*United States v. Mine Workers*, 330 U.S. 258, supports orderly determination of disputed judicial authority, without assuming power over the ultimate merits.

*United States Catholic Conference v. Abortion Rights Mobilization, Inc.*, 487 U.S. 72, is distinguished: coercive discovery serving an unauthorized merits action lacks the independence of this completed sanction.

The holding concerns the then-effective Rule 11 and this established procedural violation. It supplies neither hypothetical merits jurisdiction nor a universal collateral-order exception. Any different order requires its own lawful source and genuine independence. The Court does not decide inherent sanctioning power, reconsider the accepted misconduct or amount, or restore the dismissed federal merits judgment.

Effective March 3, Cooter & Gell’s collateral Rule 11 principle extends to this erroneous assertion of subject-matter jurisdiction. The independent procedural source and merits limit control together. The Rules create no merits jurisdiction, and no general inherent-power or universal collateral-order rule is adopted.

Dates and procedural background: [503 U.S. 131 (1992), pp. 131–139](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-503/pdf/USREPORTS-503-131.pdf).

Supreme Court review of the sanction ends with affirmance of $19,307, jointly and severally, on the reviewed terms. The prior jurisdictional direction returning the employment claims to state court remains operative. No new merits judgment, enlarged sanction or retained Supreme Court supervision is entered.

**End of entry: Willy v. Coastal Corp., merits decision, March 3, 1992.**

### McCarthy v. Madigan, No. 90-6861

Merits decision, March 4, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 9, 1991. On writ of certiorari to the Tenth Circuit, 914 F.2d 1411. The Court reviews dismissal of a federal prisoner’s damages-only constitutional medical-care action for failure to complete the Bureau of Prisons’ general grievance process.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

The questions are whether Congress requires this exhaustion and, if not, whether the particular grievance procedure’s purposes and practical effects justify making it a prerequisite to this damages action.

McCarthy alleges deliberate indifference by four Leavenworth prison employees to needs following a back operation and psychiatric problems. He expressly requests only money damages. The district court dismissed for nonexhaustion and denied reconsideration; the Tenth Circuit affirmed on the usefulness of administrative consideration and fact development even without damages authority. The general BOP regulations provide investigation and written responses but no hearing, formal evidentiary findings or monetary award. They require an initial complaint within 15 days, then appeals within 20 and 30 days, with extensions for valid reasons. An earlier grievance concerned a private cell; a later medical grievance was accepted despite lateness and denied by the warden, but its regional appeal was rejected as late. That sequence does not establish misconduct by grievance officials. McCarthy seeks reversal and ordinary damages litigation; respondents invoke agency autonomy, information, general statutory delegation, §1997e, possible conversion to a Federal Tort Claims Act (FTCA) claim and settlement.

#### Judgment

The Tenth Circuit’s exhaustion judgment is reversed, 9–0, and the case is remanded for ordinary proceedings on the damages complaint.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dismissal for failure to exhaust the general BOP grievance procedure | Reversed and remanded; 9-0 | Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | No opposing vote | The damages-only action may proceed without completion of this process; no liability or damages award is made. |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Blackmun | Stone, White, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter | Supports reversal and remand | Congressional intent, applicable judicial exhaustion principles and the particular claim/procedure balance. |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Scalia | Thomas | Supports the same reversal and remand on a narrower ground | The absence of a monetary administrative remedy suffices; no join in the broader analysis or reliance on the filing periods. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Statutory design governs the exhaustion inquiry

**Controlling proposition:** An express congressional exhaustion requirement controls according to its terms. Where Congress has not required exhaustion, courts must exercise sound discretion consistent with the statutory scheme, weighing prompt access to court against the actual interests in agency authority and judicial efficiency; neither the general BOP delegation nor the then-current §1997e imposes a mandatory gate for this federal prisoner’s medical-care claim for damages under Bivens.

**Authority:** Blackmun’s opinion, joined by Stone, White, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy and Souter; seven Justices control the governing inquiry and statutory application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a judicial exhaustion rule must fit Congress’s design and the purposes served by the actual administrative process. Patsy makes congressional intent central, while McKart and McGee explain why administrative expertise, correction of error and useful fact development matter when courts retain discretion. Coit distinguishes that discretion from a statutory command. Congress’s general assignment of prison administration to the Bureau says nothing requiring this prisoner to exhaust before seeking damages. Section 1997e’s limited treatment of certain state-prisoner actions does not enact a broader federal rule by implication. Carlson supplies the established medical-care damages channel; Bush and Schweiker concern comprehensive remedial choices Congress made in different programs. The Bureau’s own grievance procedure is not an equivalent congressional substitute. Respondents’ appeal to administrative autonomy therefore requires examination of what this procedure can accomplish, rather than automatic dismissal. The Court does not displace an express statutory requirement, announce a new Bivens action or determine the medical claim’s merits.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Patsy v. Board of Regents of Florida*, 457 U.S. 496 — applied for congressional primacy and the actual limited scope of contemporaneous §1997e.
- *McKart v. United States*, 395 U.S. 185 — applied for the purposes of agency authority, expertise, correction and efficiency.
- *McGee v. United States*, 402 U.S. 479 — applied for sound judicial discretion where Congress has not clearly required exhaustion.
- *Coit Independence Joint Venture v. FSLIC*, 489 U.S. 561 — applied to distinguish statutory exhaustion and the adequacy of an administrative route.
- *Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14 — preserved as the existing medical-care damages channel, without extending it to a new context.
- *Bush v. Lucas*, 462 U.S. 367 — distinguished: no comparable comprehensive congressional remedial scheme displaces this existing channel.
- *Schweiker v. Chilicky*, 487 U.S. 412 — distinguished on the same absence of a congressional replacement scheme.

##### This damages-only claim need not pass through the general BOP process

**Controlling proposition:** Absent a statutory mandate, a federal prisoner seeking only damages for the completed medical-care wrong alleged here need not exhaust the described BOP process: it cannot award compensation, its short filing periods risk forfeiture, and its limited adjudicative benefit does not outweigh access to court. The result does not decide a request for prospective relief, a procedure able to grant the sought compensation, or a materially different administrative adjudication.

**Authority:** The same seven Justices join Blackmun’s particular balancing and application. Scalia and Thomas agree with the result solely because no money remedy is available; their judgment concurrence does not join the deadline or broader balancing rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this grievance process provides too little countervailing benefit to justify barring the damages complaint. McKart requires attention to the process’s real function, not merely its administrative label. Investigation and written responses may help officials improve care, but they neither compensate this completed injury nor create formal findings on which a court can conclusively dispose of the claim. The successive filing periods also risk forfeiture; extensions exist, but their valid-reason condition does not remove that practical burden, illustrated by the rejected regional appeal. Coit’s concern for an adequate route and Turner’s protection of valid prisoner claims inform the balance. The proposed conversion to an FTCA claim and possible settlement do not establish an effective damages remedy in these regulations or this record; Hillsborough cautions against requiring an uncertain route to relief. Ordinary litigation remains available for developing facts and asserting defenses. The Court decides no medical violation, improper grievance handling or exhaustion rule for prospective relief.

#### Limits and Questions Not Reached

No general exception to a congressional exhaustion command is created. Prospective relief, an administrative monetary remedy and materially different adjudicative procedures remain open. The Court makes no finding of bias, deliberate indifference, damages entitlement or loss of a defense. It does not transform negligence into a constitutional violation or extend Bivens to a new context.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Effective March 4, courts may not dismiss this damages-only federal medical-care action for failure to complete the described general BOP grievance process. Seven Justices apply the inquiry that begins with the statute and considers the procedure’s purposes, including the deadline burden and limited factual record; the two Justices concurring in the judgment share only the narrower no-compensation ground. #### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia, joined by Thomas, agrees with reversal solely because the Bureau’s process offers no monetary relief to a claimant seeking only damages. McNeese v. Board of Education, 373 U.S. 668, and Montana National Bank v. Yellowstone County, 276 U.S. 499, support that effective-remedy ground. They withhold the broader general-exhaustion discussion and its unmodified application to Bivens, and reject treating these reasonable filing periods, with extensions, as a reason to excuse exhaustion.
