## Decisions and Dispositions

### Suter v. Artist M., No. 90-1488

Merits decision, March 25, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 2, 1991. On writ of certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) to the United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit, which decided *Artist M. v. Johnson*, 917 F.2d 980, on October 29, 1990. Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

Children in Cook County alleged that Illinois failed promptly to assign and replace the caseworkers through whom protective and reunification services were arranged. The Seventh Circuit affirmed denial of dismissal and a preliminary injunction requiring assignment and reassignment within three working days, together with weekly compliance reports. It relied on §1983 and an implied private action, principally discussing §671(a)(15) of the Adoption Assistance and Child Welfare Act. The complaint also presented claims under §671(a)(9) and §671(a)(16). The officials seek reversal of private enforcement and the resulting injunction; the children seek preservation of prospective relief. The separately dismissed constitutional count was not appealed. The B.H. decree covers a different class and specified obligations and includes no admission.

The question is whether the children may enforce the asserted reasonable-efforts and reporting duties through §1983 or directly under the Act, with separate consideration of the case-plan and review claim.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses private enforcement of the reasonable-efforts and reporting claims, vacates the existing preliminary relief, and preserves the independent case-plan and review claim for consideration below.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The separate B.H. decree leaves this controversy live

**Controlling proposition:** The B.H. consent decree neither moots this action nor prevents the officials from contesting the asserted statutory right: its class omits children remaining at home under protective orders, its obligations do not subsume this injunction, and its defined undertaking contains no admission of the independent statutory claim. The Court therefore reaches the private-enforcement questions presented here.

**Authority:** White's Opinion of the Court, Part I, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; all nine Justices adopt this threshold ruling.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a decree in another action ends this controversy only if its actual operation removes the live dispute. The B.H. class excludes children who remain in their homes under protective orders, while this action includes them. Its limits on caseloads and other defined duties do not supply the three-working-day assignment and reassignment requirement at issue here. The officials' agreement also disclaims an admission and specifies the efforts it requires. Those terms establish that accepting the decree does not concede the independent statutory entitlement asserted here. Rufo's preservation of consent obligations beyond constitutional minima provides an analogy, not a holding on this statute or this decree. The children's reliance on shared defendants and overlapping objectives cannot overcome the differences in coverage and relief. The Court leaves the B.H. decree in place and decides neither its modification nor any asserted violation of it.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Rufo v. Inmates of Suffolk County Jail, January 15, 1992 — its preservation of consent obligations beyond constitutional minima provides analogous support; its Rule 60(b)(5) standard is not applied to modify B.H., and its holding remains unchanged.

##### Section 671(a)(15) supplies no individual reasonable-efforts right enforceable under §1983

**Controlling proposition:** Section 671(a)(15), read in the Adoption Assistance and Child Welfare Act's plan and administrative-enforcement structure, does not confer on these beneficiaries an individual right enforceable through §1983 to compel the asserted reasonable efforts to prevent removal or reunify families. An approved plan's reasonable-efforts commitment, §671(a)(3)'s statewide-operation requirement, and the record's caseworker deficiencies do not supply that missing federal right.

**Authority:** White's Opinion of the Court, Part II, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; six Justices adopt this rule and reverse the §1983 ruling under §671(a)(15).

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a federal funding condition does not by itself identify an individual right enforceable through §1983. Thiboutot permits enforcement of statutory rights, while Wright and Wilder require examination of the particular entitlement Congress established. Pennhurst requires ascertainable obligations when States accept federal funds. Unlike the rent limitation in Wright and the reimbursement requirements in Wilder, this reasonable-efforts clause supplies no further measure of the services an individual child may demand. Illinois has an approved plan containing the commitment; the statewide-operation language does not convert that commitment into the asserted private entitlement. The serious absence of caseworkers and the feasibility of a three-day remedy cannot answer the prior question whether federal law creates that entitlement. Administrative funding review and the required removal findings give the clause operation without this private action. The Court does not hold that those mechanisms comprehensively foreclose otherwise existing §1983 rights, or that every requirement contained in a state plan is unenforceable.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Maine v. Thiboutot, 448 U.S. 1 (1980) — preserves statutory-right enforcement under §1983; a violation of federal law still must invade an enforceable right.
- Wright v. Roanoke Redevelopment and Housing Authority, 479 U.S. 418 (1987) — distinguishes its defined rent and utility entitlement; its enforceability holding and separate foreclosure inquiry remain operative.
- Wilder v. Virginia Hospital Association, 496 U.S. 498 (1990) — distinguishes its substantive reimbursement obligation and supplied benchmarks; neither its Medicaid holding nor its rejection of automatic foreclosure by funding withdrawal is overruled.
- Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman, 451 U.S. 1 (1981) — applies its clear-notice principle to the asserted funding obligation; no categorical immunity for state-plan provisions is created.

##### Section 671(a)(15) supplies no separate implied action

**Controlling proposition:** The Act does not create an implied private cause of action to enforce §671(a)(15)'s reasonable-efforts clause because the claimants have not established congressional intent to create that remedy. This conclusion concerns the distinct action asserted directly under the Act and does not make failure to imply an action a bar to an otherwise available enacted §1983 remedy.

**Authority:** White's Opinion of the Court, Part III, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; six Justices adopt this independently necessary ground for rejecting the second enforcement route sustained below.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a statute's beneficial purpose cannot substitute for congressional intent to authorize the private action asserted. Cort identifies the considerations bearing on an implied remedy, and Transamerica makes legislative intent the controlling inquiry. Children and families plainly benefit from the Act's objectives, but that fact does not resolve who may enforce every undertaking through a lawsuit. The reasonable-efforts clause directs the contents of the funding plan, leaves substantial choices to the State, and operates with administrative review and removal-related findings. Those features do not show an intention to create the additional private action sought here. The Court of Appeals' reliance on the desirability of effective services cannot supply the missing authorization. This inquiry remains distinct from determining whether an existing right may be enforced through §1983. Franklin likewise permits appropriate remedies only after a cognizable action exists; it cannot create this one. No other provision's implied remedy is resolved merely by this conclusion about reasonable efforts.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Cort v. Ash, 422 U.S. 66 (1975) — applies its inquiry into an implied private remedy, with congressional intent decisive.
- Transamerica Mortgage Advisors, Inc. v. Lewis, 444 U.S. 11 (1979) — applies its requirement of congressional intent to create the particular remedy asserted; statutory purpose alone is insufficient.
- Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools, February 26, 1992 — preserves the distinction between the existence of a cognizable action and appropriate relief within it; no Title IX holding is narrowed.

##### The asserted §671(a)(9) reporting claim is not enforceable through §1983

**Controlling proposition:** Section 671(a)(9)'s requirement that a state plan provide for reporting to the appropriate court or law-enforcement agency when a state agency has reason to believe a child's home or institution is unsuitable because of neglect, abuse, or exploitation, and the child's care is paid for in whole or in part under Title IV-E or IV-B, does not confer on these respondents a private federal right enforceable through §1983 on the claim presented. The reporting ground therefore cannot independently sustain the challenged caseworker injunction.

**Authority:** White's Opinion of the Court, Part IV-A, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; six Justices adopt this separate statutory holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the reporting provision does not supply these respondents with the private action missing from their reasonable-efforts claim. Section 671(a)(9) addresses a state agency's reason to believe a child's home or institution is unsuitable because of neglect, abuse, or exploitation, where the child's care is paid for in whole or in part under Title IV-E or IV-B. It specifies notice to the appropriate court or law-enforcement agency. That administrative reporting direction does not itself turn the plan feature into the individual federal entitlement asserted here. Wright and Wilder require identification of the right created by the particular statutory provision, not merely conduct a State has undertaken to include in its approved program. The lower court cannot sustain this injunction simply by grouping reporting with reasonable efforts. The Court rejects the asserted §1983 route while leaving the State's reporting undertaking and administrative enforcement intact. It does not decide the separately defined case-plan and review claim under §671(a)(16).

**Precedent treatment:**

- Wright, 479 U.S. 418 — applies the need to identify a privately enforceable statutory right; its distinct rent holding remains in force.
- Wilder, 496 U.S. 498 — applies provision-specific examination of a binding individual entitlement; no general rule against enforcement of plan provisions replaces it.

##### The asserted §671(a)(9) claim supplies no separate implied action

**Controlling proposition:** Section 671(a)(9) does not create an implied private action for these respondents to obtain the challenged caseworker relief: its reporting requirement and the Act's enforcement structure do not establish congressional intent to authorize that action. Rejection of this distinct statutory vehicle does not decide private enforcement of §671(a)(16).

**Authority:** White's Opinion of the Court, Part IV-B, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; six Justices adopt the ground rejecting direct implied enforcement of the reporting claim.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a duty to notify a designated public authority does not, without the required legislative intent, authorize the private suit asserted here. Cort and Transamerica require examination of the particular action Congress intended to permit. Section 671(a)(9) specifies reporting within the state-plan structure; it does not identify an additional private judicial vehicle for obtaining caseworker assignments. The children's interest in safe placements explains why the reporting obligation matters, but beneficial purpose and the usefulness of an injunction do not establish the missing congressional choice. The Act's administrative oversight remains available, and rejecting this asserted action neither excuses noncompliance with the plan nor decides that every more specifically defined obligation has the same enforcement consequences. Franklin's rule about appropriate relief in an existing action cannot bridge the absence of the asserted action itself. The Court therefore rejects this alternative route separately from its §1983 analysis and leaves the case-plan claim for its own consideration on remand.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Cort, 422 U.S. 66, and Transamerica, 444 U.S. 11 — apply the congressional-intent requirement to the distinct implied reporting action.
- Franklin, February 26, 1992 — applies its action/remedy distinction without changing the established Title IX action or relief available within it.

#### Limits and Questions Not Reached

The Court does not decide whether §671(a)(16), read with §675(1) and §675(5)(B), creates a privately enforceable right or implied action, whether the preserved allegations establish a violation, or what additional relief would be proper. Those matters return for provision-specific consideration. The Court does not find the reporting claim abandoned, revive the dismissed constitutional count, award damages, prescribe a nationwide three-day assignment deadline, modify the B.H. decree, or hold that administrative funding withdrawal necessarily forecloses an otherwise existing §1983 right. Safety, eligibility, actual statutory requirements and proper remedial scope remain separate questions wherever legally presented.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Effective March 25, the Court rejects §1983 and direct implied enforcement of the asserted §671(a)(15) and §671(a)(9) claims and removes their existing preliminary remedy. Section 671(a)(16) remains undecided for separate consideration below. The B.H. decree supplies no bar based on mootness or preclusion: it neither ends this live controversy nor prevents litigation of the asserted statutory entitlement.

The Court applies the distinctions among a statutory right, a cause of action, and the relief available in that action without overruling the precedents addressed above. It announces no general bar to enforcement of state-plan provisions, constitutional §1983 claims, or ordinary relief in existing actions. Administrative enforcement remains operative. The Court establishes no proof burden or substantive child-welfare standard for the unresolved claim.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Blackmun, joined by Stevens and, on §671(a)(15) only, Stone-Zsela, would apply *Wilder*'s protection of a binding individual entitlement to the mandatory, individualized reasonable-efforts obligation. The absence of the essential caseworker permits a judicial inquiry without prescribing every welfare decision; administrative funding withdrawal does not establish incompatibility with §1983. The supported three-day remedy is confined to this record. Blackmun and Stevens also would sustain the reporting claim under §1983, while Stone-Zsela leaves its enforceability and merits for provision-specific consideration. All three leave a redundant implied action under §671(a)(15) undecided. Stone-Zsela's separate writing preserves that narrower position on §671(a)(9) and distinguishes the existing §671(a)(15) injunction from possible additional relief on remand. These positions do not control the rejected reasonable-efforts and reporting claims.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The judgment is reversed in part and vacated in part, and the case is remanded to the Seventh Circuit for proceedings consistent with these directions. The existing three-working-day assignment and reassignment injunction and ancillary weekly monitoring order are vacated. The judgment is vacated insofar as it otherwise resolves the independent §671(a)(16) claim. Further proceedings below must separately consider that claim's enforceability, merits, and any additional lawful relief; no entitlement or violation is found, and the existing injunction is not transferred automatically to that claim. No §671(a)(9) claim remains available on the rejected private-enforcement theories. The constitutional count remains outside the case, and the B.H. decree is not modified. The Court orders no damages, nationwide assignment deadline, or retained Supreme Court supervision. This Supreme Court merits review ends with the remand.

**End of entry: Suter v. Artist M., No. 90-1488, merits decision, March 25, 1992.**

### United States Department of Commerce v. Montana, No. 91-860

Merits decision, March 31, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 4, 1992. On direct appeal under 28 U.S.C. §1253 from the three-judge United States District Court for the District of Montana, which decided *Montana v. United States Department of Commerce*, 775 F. Supp. 1358, on October 18, 1991, in No. CV 91-22-H-CCL. Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

The 1990 census allocation reduced Montana from two Representatives to one. With a statutorily fixed House of 435, fixed state boundaries, minimum state representation and whole seats, competing methods improve different measures of equality. Montana's proposed allocation would give it a second seat at Washington's expense. On Count I, the District Court declared 2 U.S.C. §2a unconstitutional and void and permanently enjoined its implementation. It found Count II unnecessary. The Government seeks reversal and judgment in its favor. Montana seeks affirmance on Count I and expressly requests remand on Count II if Count I fails. The Government says there is no occasion to decide the distinct legislative-standing issue here.

The questions are whether courts may review Congress's choice among population-based interstate apportionment methods and whether equal proportions satisfies the constitutional command to apportion Representatives among States according to their respective numbers. Count II separately challenges automatic decennial apportionment under Article I, §§2 and 7; its merits were not reached below.

#### Judgment

The Court unanimously reverses the judgment and relief resting on Count I and directs judgment for the Government on that count. It unanimously remands Count II without deciding its standing, justiciability, merits, or eventual relief.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Other positions | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Count I representation challenge | Reverse, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas | Unanimous. | Enter judgment for appellants on Count I; remove the declaration of invalidity and dissolve the permanent injunction resting on that count. |
| Count II automatic-decennial-apportionment challenge | Remand expressly undecided, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas | Unanimous. | Further proceedings below on the distinct claim, including any properly presented threshold issue; no Supreme Court merits or legislative-standing ruling. |

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Exact scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|

All nine join each controlling proposition and its qualifications. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Count I presents a continuing and redressable representation injury

**Controlling proposition:** On this record, Count I's challenge to the continuing legal allocation of Representatives presents a concrete voting-representation injury traceable to the statutory formula, with a substantial likelihood of redress through adjudication of that formula's validity. Completion of the officials' initial statutory steps does not establish that the allocation has ceased to affect the approaching election or that declaratory relief cannot redress the asserted injury.

**Authority:** Stevens's Opinion of the Court, Part I, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. All nine participating Justices adopt this proposition at its stated scope; it controls by an express majority, with no judgment-only or narrower-ground vote.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the present allocation creates a live dispute over the legal weight of representation. Montana challenges an operative population formula that gives it one Representative, not a completed act with no continuing consequence. The alleged injury is tied to that formula, and a declaration addressing its validity can remove the asserted legal obstacle to corrective implementation. The Court independently concludes that declaratory relief has a substantial likelihood of redressing the asserted inequality. Wesberry recognizes judicial protection of concrete congressional voting injuries while leaving appropriate relief for separate consideration; it does not require the Court to guarantee a particular replacement district or seat. The Government's completed-duties argument does not establish that this continuing allocation is beyond any declaratory relief. This conclusion assumes neither a new enactment nor a command to the President. The Court does not decide the distinct legislative injury asserted in Count II or approve any particular coercive remedy against absent parties.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Wesberry v. Sanders, 376 U.S. 1 (1964) — applies its recognition of concrete congressional voting injuries while preserving the distinction between reviewability and appropriate relief; no general rule governing presidential defendants is adopted.

##### Congress's apportionment choice remains subject to constitutional review

**Controlling proposition:** Courts may decide whether the equal-proportions method violates the constitutional requirement to apportion Representatives among States according to their respective numbers. Congress's responsibility to choose a method, and the possibility that several methods satisfy the Constitution, do not make this particular constitutional challenge a nonjusticiable political question.

**Authority:** Stevens's Opinion of the Court, Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. All nine participating Justices adopt this proposition at its stated scope; it controls by an express majority, with no judgment-only or narrower-ground vote.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that identifying the constitutional limits on apportionment is a judicial task. Baker directs attention to the particular question and to any constitutional commitment or other impediment inseparable from it. Muñoz-Flores rejects the premise that Congress's consideration of constitutionality or ordinary disagreement between branches eliminates judicial review. The Government argues more narrowly that choosing among population-related methods belongs to Congress because no legal standard identifies the best formula. That argument does not prevent the Court from deciding whether the Constitution requires the absolute-population measure Montana proposes. The answer may leave Congress more than one lawful choice. Such latitude is a conclusion about the constitutional requirement, not a reason to refuse to interpret it. The Court need not select a preferred national arrangement or supervise legislative deliberations to resolve the presented challenge. Baker's political-question limits remain operative in other settings, and the distinct challenge to congressional voting opportunities in Count II is left undecided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Baker v. Carr, 369 U.S. 186 (1962) — applies its issue-specific political-question inquiry; political subject matter alone does not foreclose constitutional adjudication.
- United States v. Muñoz-Flores, 495 U.S. 385 (1990) — applies ordinary judicial review of constitutional constraints on Congress; its Origination Clause holding is not extended to decide Count II.

##### Equal proportions satisfies the presented interstate population challenge

**Controlling proposition:** In allocating the statutorily fixed House among States using whole Representatives and respecting each State's constitutional minimum, Article I, §2 and the Fourteenth Amendment permit the equal-proportions method's population-based choice of relative representational equality. An alternative's improvement in selected absolute population disparities does not establish that equal proportions violates the constitutional population command when the change worsens other legitimate measures of equality; the Court neither requires equal proportions uniquely nor approves every population-related formula.

**Authority:** Stevens's Opinion of the Court, Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. All nine participating Justices adopt this proposition at its stated scope; it controls by an express majority, with no judgment-only or narrower-ground vote.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Montana's preferred measure does not establish the constitutional defect it asserts. Wesberry protects equality in congressional districts within a State, and Karcher requires a good-faith effort to avoid intrastate population differences, with specific justification for deviations. Interstate allocation presents a different constraint: state boundaries remain fixed, every State receives a seat, and Representatives cannot be divided. A transfer giving Montana a second seat reduces selected absolute disparities but increases the relative disparity between Montana and Washington and worsens Washington's distance from the ideal. The Constitution does not make Montana's favored comparison controlling among those competing measures. Congress may choose relative representational equality through this consistently population-based method. That ground answers the challenge without treating longevity or expert endorsement as an independent constitutional defense. The Court preserves Karcher's intrastate requirements, creates no percentage tolerance or new burden sequence, and does not decide whether the distinct constitutional claim in Count II requires a fresh enactment after each census.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Wesberry, 376 U.S. 1 — preserves its intrastate equal-vote holding while distinguishing allocation of indivisible seats among States; its historical explanation does not select Montana's interstate metric.
- Karcher v. Daggett, 462 U.S. 725 (1983) — distinguishes its intrastate line-drawing problem; its good-faith and specific-justification requirements remain unchanged, with no numerical safe harbor imported here.

##### Count II returns for initial consideration without a Supreme Court merits ruling

**Controlling proposition:** Reversal of the judgment and relief resting on Count I does not dispose of Count II's separate challenge to automatic decennial apportionment. Because the lower court left that claim's merits undecided and it is not necessary to resolve the presented Count I questions, Count II is remanded without a Supreme Court decision on its standing, justiciability, merits, or any further relief.

**Authority:** Stevens's Opinion of the Court, Part IV, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. All nine participating Justices adopt this proposition at its stated scope; it controls by an express majority, with no judgment-only or narrower-ground vote.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that its rejection of Count I cannot silently decide a different constitutional claim. The district court expressly found Count II unnecessary after granting relief on Count I. The appellate questions address judicial review and the equal-proportions formula, and Montana did not offer Count II as an alternative ground for affirmance. It instead expressly requested further proceedings on that claim if Count I failed. The Government likewise stated that there was no occasion to resolve the distinct legislative-standing issue. Singleton supplies the ordinary practice of leaving unadjudicated issues for orderly consideration below, while preserving discretion in appropriate exceptional cases. A general request for judgment for the Government does not establish such an exception here. The Court therefore limits judgment for appellants to Count I and preserves Count II for its own threshold and merits consideration. It adopts no categorical ban on first-instance appellate decision and expresses no view about the strength or ultimate disposition of the remaining claim.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Singleton v. Wulff, 428 U.S. 106 (1976), unanimous Part III — applies its ordinary remand practice and fair-opportunity rationale; its recognized discretionary exceptions remain available, and no new jurisdictional prohibition is announced.

#### Limits and Questions Not Reached

The Court does not decide Count II's legislative standing, political-question defenses, constitutional merits, or eventual relief. It does not order a transfer of Washington's seat, compel Congress to legislate, direct presidential action, decide the unestablished Rule 19 objection, or determine every proper-defendant and coercive-remedy question that a successful claim might present. It does not constitutionalize a House of 435, adopt a general percentage tolerance, import state-legislative apportionment standards, or treat every population-based formula as valid.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Effective March 31, the Court recognizes the live Count I dispute, reviews its constitutional question, and sustains equal proportions against the asserted population-equality objection. Its relative-equality rationale permits this method without making it uniquely required or approving every population formula.

The constitutional-review limits, intrastate voting-equality rules, and ordinary remand practice explained in the precedent-treatment statements above retain their force. The Court overrules no precedent, creates no numerical safe harbor, and establishes no new burden sequence. Count II's legislative-standing and reenactment issues remain undecided. Leaving the allocation operative during further proceedings does not resolve that claim on its merits.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The judgment and relief resting on Count I are reversed, and Count II is remanded. The District Court must enter judgment for appellants on Count I, remove the declaration of invalidity, and dissolve the permanent injunction resting on that count. It may consider the remaining claim and any properly presented threshold defenses or lawful relief without prejudgment by this Court. Count II is not affirmed, dismissed, or rejected through a blanket judgment for the Government.

The statutory allocation remains operative subject to further lawful order. The Court transfers no seat and directs no new enactment, presidential act, judicially chosen formula, or election timetable. Supreme Court merits review ends with this remand; the Court retains no proceeding.

**End of entry: United States Department of Commerce v. Montana, No. 91-860, merits decision, March 31, 1992.**

### United States v. Alaska, No. 118, Original

Original summary-judgment decision, April 21, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued February 24, 1992; decided April 21, 1992. 503 U.S. 569 (1992).

The case comes on an original bill of complaint and cross-motions for summary judgment; no lower-court judgment is under review. Leave to file the complaint was granted April 1, 1991. The parties' September 6, 1991 Joint Stipulation supplies the material record. Original jurisdiction rests on Article III and 28 U.S.C. §1251(b)(2).

The United States seeks a declaration that construction of the Nome port did not enlarge Alaska's submerged-land entitlement. Alaska seeks invalidation of the permit condition and disclaimer, a declaration of corresponding state title, and an injunction against requiring such disclaimers. The questions are whether §10 of the Rivers and Harbors Act permits the boundary-preserving condition and whether the separately presented regulatory, publication and notice (§552), rulemaking (§553), and arbitrary-action, delegation, and evidentiary (§706) objections defeat it.

The condition preserves the allocation before the project for approximately 730 disputed acres associated with the Nome extension. It does not surrender property or claims Alaska already possessed or concede the precise location of a previously disputed coastline. The disclaimer reserves judicial review of legal authority. A proposed mineral sale received no bids, and adverse title claims continue.

All nine Justices participated in the argument and decision: Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Byron R. White, Harry A. Blackmun, John Paul Stevens, Sandra Day O'Connor, Antonin Scalia, Anthony M. Kennedy, David H. Souter, and Clarence Thomas.

#### Judgment

The Court grants the United States' motion for summary judgment and denies Alaska's motion, 9–0. It declares that the permitted Nome extension does not enlarge Alaska's corresponding submerged-land entitlement through the effective disclaimer. Alaska's invalidity and competing-title declarations and its requested general injunction are denied. The objections under the Administrative Procedure Act are reached and rejected, rather than treated as forfeited.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### A live original controversy over the Nome lands

**Controlling proposition:** Continuing, adverse federal and state claims to the submerged lands affected by the Nome disclaimer present a live original controversy even though the identified mineral sale received no bids. The Court reaches the parties' cross-motions through its original jurisdiction.

**Authority:** White's opinion, Part I, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas; all nine Justices support this threshold holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the absence of bids does not end this dispute. The United States claims the submerged lands outside Alaska's preproject entitlement, and Alaska claims that the permitted artificial extension brought those lands within its statutory boundary. Those opposing claims concern present sovereign entitlement, not merely a possible payment from a particular sale. Article III and 28 U.S.C. §1251(b)(2) provide the original channel for resolving that controversy. The disclaimer expressly preserves a challenge to the Corps' legal authority, and Alaska's acceptance of the permitted project therefore does not eliminate the underlying statutory dispute. A declaration concerning the effect of this instrument determines the parties' corresponding entitlement whether or not a mineral lease produces revenue. The Court can resolve that concrete disagreement without deciding abstract disagreements about other permits or declaring the precise location of any previously disputed coastline.

**Precedent treatment:** This threshold holding applies Article III, §1251(b)(2), and the stipulated continuing title dispute; it changes no earlier jurisdictional rule.

##### Statutory authority for the boundary-preserving condition

**Controlling proposition:** Section 10 of the Rivers and Harbors Act permits the Corps to consider the effect of this proposed artificial coastal extension on federal submerged-land rights and to condition approval on preserving the existing federal-state boundary. The Submerged Lands Act does not require unconditional approval of the extension or invalidate this condition, which concerns only additional entitlement attributable to the Nome project and leaves existing state property and claims intact.

**Authority:** White's opinion, Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas; all nine Justices support this statutory holding and its stated limits.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the statutory power to approve this artificial extension includes the power to prevent its identified encroachment on federal submerged-land interests. Section 10 separately controls construction in navigable waters; its authorization function is not confined to eliminating navigational obstructions. United States v. California, 381 U.S. 139, recognizes both the effect of qualifying artificial changes on the coastline and federal protection against future encroachment through control of navigable waters and agreement. The Submerged Lands Act's allocation operates with that qualification; it does not compel the Secretary to approve every proposed extension. The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act identifies the federal interest without itself supplying a new Corps transfer power. The disclaimer preserves the existing allocation while allowing construction. Alaska's demand for identical domestic and international boundaries overlooks California's express accommodation of future agreements. The condition also answers the same boundary harm that supports withholding approval; Nollan's objection to unrelated exactions supplies no contrary result. Naturally accreted coastlines, other conditions, and surrender of existing state territory remain outside this decision.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. California, 381 U.S. 139 (1965) — applied: the artificial-coast rule and the qualification permitting protection against future encroachment through agreement remain effective together; the decision imposes no absolute domestic/international boundary identity.
- United States ex rel. Greathouse v. Dern, 289 U.S. 352 (1933) — limited supporting analogy only: its refusal of equitable mandamus did not decide the reserved general extent of §10 discretion.
- Nollan v. California Coastal Commission, 483 U.S. 825 (1987) — distinguished: this condition directly addresses the identified purpose supporting denial and does not exact an unrelated existing property interest; no general holding about a State's constitutional takings remedies follows.
- Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc., 467 U.S. 837 (1984) — applied within the actual statutory delegation and controlling boundary precedent; its general validity and scope are not reconsidered.
- Lechmere, Inc. v. NLRB, January 27, 1992 — preserved: agency construction does not displace a binding judicial construction.
- Arkansas v. Oklahoma, February 26, 1992 — preserved: agency permit conditions must rest on the authority and regulations governing that particular scheme; its Clean Water Act holding supplies no independent §10 power.

##### The published regulation and the Corps' decisional responsibility

**Controlling proposition:** The public-interest and coastline provisions of 33 C.F.R. §320.4(a) and (f) permit consideration of the Nome project's prospective effect on federal shelf rights; subsection (g)(6)'s exclusion of disputes about an applicant's construction-site property interest does not forbid that inquiry. The Corps' documented adoption of the boundary concern and its own permit decision reject Alaska's claim of unlawful delegation on this record, without authorizing Interior or the Attorney General to exercise an independent permitting veto.

**Authority:** White's opinion, Part III, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas; eight Justices support both propositions. Stone-Zsela agrees with the disposition of this component only.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the specific coastline provision governs the prospective effect at issue. It expressly requires attention to federal outer continental rights when construction may move the baseline. The property-dispute provision concerns whether the applicant has the interest needed to undertake its proposed activity; it does not cancel the neighboring instruction concerning federal shelf rights. The original published scheme also reserves final action to the Army after interagency coordination. Alaska is correct that consultation cannot transfer statutory responsibility to another department. But concurrence in another department's stated concern does not itself establish such a transfer. The Corps adopted the identified boundary objection in its own July 1983 communication, evaluated the conditioned project in its July 1984 findings, and executed the permit through district officials. That documentary course answers the alleged surrender of the decision to Interior. The Court does not decide compliance with an unpresented internal-routing requirement, recognize an automatic Interior veto, or authorize disregard of governing rules in another proceeding.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Arkansas v. Oklahoma, February 26, 1992 — applied at its source-specific limit: a valid regulation guides the agency's own permit determination; it does not transfer responsibility to a different agency.
- National Railroad Passenger Corp. v. Boston & Maine Corp., March 25, 1992 — applied: the agency's own discernible ground supports review; agreement with an adviser is not a substitute for finding that ground in the agency record.

##### Publication of the criterion under §552

**Controlling proposition:** Section 552(a)(1) does not invalidate this condition as adverse application of a secret review criterion: the governing public-interest and coastline regulations published consideration of the project's effects on federal outer continental rights. The absence of an express catalogue of every possible curative disclaimer does not make that published criterion an undisclosed rule.

**Authority:** White's opinion, Part IV-A, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas; eight Justices adopt this independently sufficient ground for rejecting the §552 challenge. Stone-Zsela agrees with its disposition only.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the basis for the challenged condition was publicly stated. Section 552 requires publication or availability of the specified agency materials so that affected persons need not confront undisclosed law. The published coastline provision expressly identifies possible shifts in the baseline and their effects on federal outer continental rights. Those are the effects identified in the Solicitor's letter and adopted by the Corps for the Nome application. Morton v. Ruiz, 415 U.S. 199, forbids use of an unpublished eligibility restriction contrary to the agency's required procedures; it does not require a regulation to enumerate every means of curing a concern the regulation already discloses. The disclaimer here implements the published concern by preserving the existing boundary while permitting the project. This conclusion concerns the criterion actually applied to Nome. It does not approve an undisclosed, independently binding rule for every coastal project, and it does not dispense with any distinct rulemaking duty imposed by §553.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Morton v. Ruiz, 415 U.S. 199 (1974) — distinguished on the applied criterion and preserved: undisclosed eligibility restrictions and disregard of required agency procedures remain impermissible; this case applies a published shelf-effects criterion.

##### Actual and timely notice under §552

**Controlling proposition:** Independently, §552(a)(1)'s bar on adverse application of unpublished matter does not protect a person who had actual and timely notice of its terms. Alaska had that notice of the Nome condition and its stated ground before executing the disclaimer, so any failure separately to publish those particular terms supplies no basis for invalidating the disclaimer as applied to Alaska.

**Authority:** White's opinion, Part IV-B, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas; eight Justices adopt this independently sufficient alternative holding. Stone-Zsela agrees with its disposition only.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the statute's express notice qualification independently answers Alaska's claim. The July 1, 1983 Corps letter transmitted Interior's stated concern, specified preservation of the coastline and federal-state boundary as the required cure, and invited further discussion. The stipulated record establishes communication to Alaska, with a copy to Nome; the record does not establish a particular receipt day. Alaska then had approximately ten months to consider and negotiate the condition before executing the May 9, 1984 instrument. Its transmittal identified review of the form by state and federal legal representatives. Those facts establish actual and timely notice of the terms applied in this proceeding. Morton v. Ruiz preserves, rather than eliminates, §552's express notice qualification. This alternative holding does not depend on comparing other permits. Notice of this particular condition is not notice of every possible general policy, and actual notice does not independently cure a violation of §553 or another mandatory procedure.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Morton v. Ruiz, 415 U.S. 199 (1974) — applied with the statutory actual-notice qualification intact; its protection against undisclosed restrictions is not converted into invalidation regardless of timely knowledge.

##### Section 553 and the individual licensing condition

**Controlling proposition:** The underlying public-interest and coastline provisions were adopted through notice and comment, and §553 did not require the Corps to promulgate an additional legislative rule before specifying this curative condition in Nome's individual licensing proceeding. This holding depends on application of those existing criteria and the absence of an established, separately binding Corps rule requiring every coastal permit to contain a disclaimer.

**Authority:** White's opinion, Part V, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas; eight Justices support the rule and application. Stone-Zsela agrees with the disposition of the §553 component only.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Alaska identifies no missing rulemaking step required for this condition. The 1973 proposal expressly presented coastline shifts and federal shelf rights for public participation; the 1974 final rule adopted those subjects after considering comments. The contention that this underlying subject was never noticed therefore fails. A distinct question concerns the chosen cure. Section 551 includes conditioning permits within licensing and treats licensing as adjudication. SEC v. Chenery Corp., 332 U.S. 194, and NLRB v. Bell Aerospace Co., 416 U.S. 267, permit an agency to develop and apply a principle through an individual decision within its authority. Here the Corps used the published criterion to identify a condition permitting the harbor while preserving the boundary. Interior's stated policy does not establish a separately binding Corps rule, and the stipulation does not establish universal treatment. The Court does not exempt legislative rules from §553, rely on its public-property exception, or approve an automatic disclaimer policy in other cases.

**Precedent treatment:**

- SEC v. Chenery Corp., 332 U.S. 194 (1947) — applied: permissible development through adjudication does not require a separate rule merely because a particular decision articulates policy; statutory and procedural limits remain.
- NLRB v. Bell Aerospace Co., 416 U.S. 267 (1974) — applied: an agency may ordinarily choose adjudication for an individual determination, subject to review of an improper exercise of that choice.
- NLRB v. Wyman-Gordon Co., 394 U.S. 759 (1969) — its divided rationales are kept distinct: the case supplies no majority rule requiring notice and comment before every individual licensing condition. No new rule is assembled from its separate opinions.

##### Reasoned action under §706

**Controlling proposition:** The Corps' Nome condition is not arbitrary or capricious under §706(2)(A): its contemporaneous record identifies the prospective loss of federal shelf rights, adopts that concern, and evaluates the project with a condition that directly removes the concern. The independent statutory, regulatory, and procedural analyses above also reject the asserted excess-authority and unlawful-procedure grounds under §706(2)(C) and (D); lawful authority alone would not excuse an unexplained exercise of discretion.

**Authority:** White's opinion, Part VI, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas; eight Justices support this review holding. Stone-Zsela agrees with the §706 disposition only.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the reason for this condition appears in the agency's own record. State Farm requires an explanation connecting the relevant facts to the agency's choice, and the Court's Amtrak decision permits recognition of an agency ground that is actually discernible while forbidding a new litigation rationale. The May 1983 Interior letter identified the proposed boundary effect, and the Corps expressly adopted that concern in July. Its 1984 findings addressed the conditioned harbor's public benefits, environmental safeguards, alternatives, and overall public interest. The condition removes the identified federal detriment while allowing those benefits. Alaska's automatic-veto argument does not overcome this documented decision. No separate monetary valuation is necessary to understand the stated connection, and the Court supplies none. Later mineral information cannot repair a missing contemporaneous reason. The Court does not hold that possible statutory authority alone establishes a reasonable application, or that every permit-related procedural error is harmless.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Motor Vehicle Manufacturers Association v. State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Co., 463 U.S. 29 (1983) — applied: the agency must connect relevant facts to its choice; a discernible path suffices, but post hoc grounds do not.
- National Railroad Passenger Corp. v. Boston & Maine Corp., March 25, 1992 — applied: this contemporaneously expressed and adopted ground satisfies the own-ground requirement; no new agency rationale is supplied by the Court.
- Marsh v. Oregon Natural Resources Council, 490 U.S. 360 (1989) — applied for careful review of the agency record within the governing statutory setting, without adding a quantitative mineral-valuation duty.
- Arkansas v. Oklahoma, February 26, 1992 — preserved: judicial review tests the agency decision against its actual governing law and record, without replacing it with a judicial policy.

##### The asserted substantial-evidence requirement

**Controlling proposition:** Section 706(2)(E)'s substantial-evidence requirement applies to the hearing or statutory-record proceedings identified in that provision; a public notice and opportunity for comments alone do not establish that predicate. Alaska has established no such predicate for this permit, and its separate assertion that the condition lacks a factual foundation fails under §706(2)(A) because the stipulated project and contemporaneously identified boundary effect support the actual ground.

**Authority:** White's opinion, Part VII, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas; eight Justices support the statutory-review holding and the distinct record application. Stone-Zsela agrees with the disposition of this §706 component only.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the applicable review provision must be identified before its evidentiary demand is imposed. Section 706(2)(E) specifies proceedings subject to §§556 and 557 or otherwise reviewed on a statutorily required agency-hearing record. Alaska has identified no such requirement for this permit. The agency's invitation for comments does not itself turn the licensing process into that statutory proceeding. Arbitrary-action review remains available, and it requires a real foundation for the reason given. The stipulated plans and the contemporaneous correspondence establish the artificial extension and its identified effect on the baseline; disagreement over whether federal shelf rights may lawfully be considered is a legal objection, separately resolved above. Arkansas v. Oklahoma's whole-record substantial-evidence holding concerned its own adjudicative record and supplies no universal hearing requirement. The Court orders no evidentiary hearing here and expresses no view about the result in a proceeding that actually satisfies §706(2)(E)'s statutory predicate.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Arkansas v. Oklahoma, February 26, 1992 — distinguished as to the review channel: its whole-record substantial-evidence rule does not establish §706(2)(E)'s hearing predicate for every informal permit decision.

#### Limits and Questions Not Reached

The declaration concerns only the change attributable to the permitted Nome extension and the corresponding disputed entitlement. It does not locate any otherwise disputed preproject coastline, alter existing state property or claims, decide naturally accreted coastlines, approve different conditions or compulsory surrender of existing territory, adjudicate an automatic nationwide disclaimer policy, revise Chevron generally, or make a general ruling about States' constitutional takings remedies. No valuation, damages, distribution from an actual mineral sale, new construction injunction, or separate Clean Water Act compliance decision is made. The separate APA grounds are reached and rejected; they are not treated as waived. All holdings have an express majority; no Marks analysis is needed.

#### Current-Law Effect

Effective April 21, 1992, the Court adds the stated §10 and Submerged Lands Act boundary-condition holding and its case-specific regulatory and Administrative Procedure Act applications. It preserves the two independent answers under §552, the distinction between publication and rulemaking, the need for an actual agency ground, and the statutory predicate for §706(2)(E). The particular Nome applications were unresolved before this decision; the stated holdings now control at their stated scope.

Chevron's general rule concerning agency interpretations, California's ordinary artificial-coast rule and future-agreement qualification, State Farm and Chenery's reason-giving requirements, and the separate current-term Amtrak, Arkansas, and Lechmere rules retain the force described with the holdings above. No precedent is overruled, no general agency veto or property exception is created, and no distinct burden, proof rule, hearing entitlement, or other-coastline doctrine is changed.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The judgment supplies the original-action declaration: the disclaimer remains effective as to boundary change attributable to the permitted Nome extension, and Alaska obtains no corresponding enlargement by reason of that construction. The declaration leaves existing property and claims, and disputes about the precise line before the project, intact. Alaska's competing relief is denied.

The original cross-motions are finally resolved. There is no lower judgment to reverse and no remand, monetary award, compulsory surrender of unrelated lands, new construction injunction, or nationwide permitting injunction. The permit and construction are not vacated. The Court retains no implementation proceeding and schedules no additional decree, hearing, filing, or return. No separate decree-submission deadline or later mandate-issuance date is established; any later nonroutine act must rest on an actual filing or order.

### Foucha v. Louisiana, No. 90-5844

Merits decision, May 18, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 4, 1991; decided May 18, 1992. On writ of certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1257 to the Louisiana Supreme Court, 563 So.2d 1138 (June 14, 1990), which affirmed continued psychiatric confinement after an insanity acquittal. The question is whether the State may continue that confinement on dangerousness and the acquittee's failure to prove safety after disclaiming present mental illness.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses the judgment sustaining Foucha's continued psychiatric confinement and remands for implementation.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Continued custody under Louisiana's challenged insanity-acquittee provision | Reversed, 6–3 | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter | Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | End confinement on that basis; remand for implementation. The equal-protection theory supplies no separate claim for additional relief and no majority holding. The Court does not find Foucha harmless or prescribe conditions for every possible alternative preventive statute.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. The insanity judgment no longer authorizes this psychiatric confinement

**Controlling proposition:** When the State concedes that an insanity acquittee is no longer mentally ill, it may not continue his psychiatric confinement under the existing insanity-acquittee authority solely because he cannot prove that he will be harmless. The original acquittal supports neither a suspended criminal sentence nor indefinite psychiatric custody after its illness predicate disappears.

**Authority:** White's Part II-A, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter: six of nine participating Justices adopt this proposition and application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Louisiana must identify a present lawful basis for the restraint it imposes. Jones v. United States permits initial commitment because an insanity verdict supports an inference of illness and dangerousness; its rejection of a hypothetical criminal-sentence ceiling rests on those continuing conditions. O'Connor v. Donaldson establishes that a permissible commitment cannot continue after its supporting basis disappears. Jackson v. Indiana requires the nature and duration of custody to remain reasonably related to its purpose. Louisiana no longer claims that Foucha is mentally ill. His antisocial personality and the doctor's unwillingness to guarantee safety do not restore that conceded absence. The State's substantial interest in protecting the public therefore cannot perpetuate this particular psychiatric commitment. The original criminal conduct does not make the acquittal a conviction authorizing punishment. The Court neither finds Foucha harmless nor sets the maximum duration of an otherwise valid commitment. Any separate lawful restraint must rest on its own established authority and procedures.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Jones v. United States, 463 U.S. 354 (1983): applied to the continuing illness-and-danger basis of insanity commitment; initial verdict-based commitment and rejection of an automatic hypothetical-sentence ceiling remain intact.
- O'Connor v. Donaldson, 422 U.S. 563 (1975): applied to the requirement that confinement end when its authorizing basis disappears; the Court does not equate a conditional-release recommendation with a finding of harmlessness.
- Jackson v. Indiana, 406 U.S. 715 (1972): applied to the required relationship between confinement's nature, duration and purpose.

##### 2. Psychiatric custody requires appropriate grounds and procedures

**Controlling proposition:** Once the insanity-acquittal basis for holding Foucha as a mental patient has ended, Louisiana cannot preserve that psychiatric custody without constitutionally adequate proceedings establishing the grounds required for it. If the State invokes ordinary civil commitment, it must establish current mental illness and dangerousness by clear and convincing evidence; additional procedure cannot supply an absent substantive ground.

**Authority:** White's Part II-B, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter: five of nine participating Justices adopt this independently sufficient ground. Stone-Zsela does not join this section's separate Vitek-based procedural characterization.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a hearing about dangerousness alone cannot authorize confinement as a mental patient after the State disclaims mental illness. Vitek v. Jones recognizes that placement in a psychiatric institution imposes a distinct deprivation requiring appropriate justification and procedure, even for someone already lawfully confined. Jackson v. Indiana requires a new lawful basis and the protections belonging to it when the original commitment purpose no longer supports custody. Addington v. Texas supplies the clear-and-convincing burden for ordinary civil commitment. Louisiana instead requires Foucha to disprove danger, although its conceded facts cannot presently establish the illness required for that civil route. The State's reliance on a counseled release hearing does not answer this defect: fairness in deciding the wrong statutory condition does not establish the missing condition. Baxstrom v. Herold reinforces that an expired original basis does not remove the safeguards of the new proceeding. The Court does not prescribe a universal burden for every possible preventive regime or disturb the distinct rules governing initial insanity commitment.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Vitek v. Jones, 445 U.S. 480 (1980): applied to psychiatric institutionalization's distinct liberty deprivation and need for appropriate grounds and procedures.
- Jackson v. Indiana, 406 U.S. 715 (1972): applied to the need for an independently authorized commitment proceeding after the initial basis expires.
- Addington v. Texas, 441 U.S. 418 (1979): applied unchanged to the clear-and-convincing burden for ordinary civil commitment; that burden is not made universal for every preventive custody channel.
- Baxstrom v. Herold, 383 U.S. 107 (1966): relied on for the ordinary protections attending a new civil commitment after the prior detention basis ends; no separate majority equal-protection holding is created here.

##### 3. Public safety does not sustain this unbounded dangerousness regime

**Controlling proposition:** Louisiana's scheme permitting indefinite psychiatric confinement of a no-longer-mentally-ill acquittee because he has not proved nondangerousness violates due process. A legitimate preventive purpose and periodic review do not cure the absence of a carefully limited substantive authority; the Court leaves materially narrower preventive regimes undecided.

**Authority:** White's Part II-C, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter: six of nine participating Justices adopt this independently sufficient rejection of Louisiana's asserted alternative preventive justification. The holding adopts no new general scrutiny tier or universal dangerousness proof standard.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the exceptional power to prevent danger cannot be detached from the limits that justify the particular restraint. United States v. Salerno sustained a narrowly focused pretrial scheme with an identified threat, government proof, an adversarial hearing, review and strict temporal limits. Louisiana's rule instead permits confinement without an endpoint while requiring the acquittee to prove safety. The doctor's reluctance to certify Foucha as harmless is enough under that rule, although the State concedes that the psychiatric illness has ended. A fair procedure for predicting danger does not supply substantive authority for an otherwise unauthorized restraint. The original verdict supplies no punitive authority under Jones. If later conduct constitutes a crime, ordinary criminal proceedings remain available; the Court makes no new finding that a particular assault occurred or warrants conviction. A better hearing alone cannot cure this regime's substantive defect. The Court reserves narrowly drawn alternatives rather than converting today's holding into a universal prohibition of preventive detention.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Salerno, 481 U.S. 739 (1987): distinguished because its confined pretrial detention authority and safeguards do not authorize Louisiana's indefinite post-acquittal psychiatric restraint.
- Jones v. United States, 463 U.S. 354 (1983): applied to the absence of authority to punish an insanity acquittee as a convicted offender.

#### Current-Law Effect and Limits

Effective May 18, 1992, Jones's distinct initial-commitment rule remains in force, but Louisiana cannot continue this psychiatric confinement after conceding the end of illness on the acquittee's failure to prove safety. Five Justices additionally require appropriate psychiatric grounds and procedures; ordinary civil commitment retains Addington's clear-and-convincing burden. Six reject the indefinite dangerousness alternative under this scheme. These are applied and clarified channel limits, not a new universal scrutiny tier or dangerousness standard. No majority adopts the plurality's equal-protection rule. Maximum duration of an otherwise valid commitment, materially narrower preventive regimes, and the scope of state insanity defenses remain undecided. No Marks inference is needed because each controlling due-process proposition has at least five direct joins.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

White, joined by Blackmun, Stevens and Souter, concludes in Part III that Louisiana also denies equal protection by withholding ordinary commitment safeguards from a sane acquittee while not permitting comparable indefinite dangerousness confinement for other persons whose original custody basis has ended. The four would require a particularly convincing justification for that disparity; this is a plurality position, not law.

O'Connor emphasizes that the holding concerns this indefinite psychiatric regime. She leaves open detention carefully limited in nature and duration to substantial safety concerns and questions extended confinement beyond what a conviction could have permitted, without deciding that question. No constitutional requirement that a State provide an insanity defense is adopted.

Stone-Zsela agrees that the psychiatric channel's ended predicate requires relief. He reserves independent equal protection, a new general scrutiny tier, universal proof rules and narrowly defined alternative regimes. Implementation cannot allow indefinite continuation while the State looks for a new justification; separate custody requires its own established authority and procedures.

Kennedy would affirm because the insanity adjudication follows proof of criminal conduct and belongs to a distinct legal channel. He views state definitions of criminal insanity and incapacitation as constitutionally relevant and rejects automatic transfer of ordinary civil-commitment rules to this setting.

Thomas, joined by Scalia, would affirm because acquittees differ from ordinary civil committees, the State may attach continued safety-based custody to that status, and periodic counseled review is adequate here. They reject an expansive new substantive right against this confinement and distinguish its lack of a fixed endpoint from a refusal ever to provide release review. Neither dissent controls.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court merits review is complete. The Louisiana courts must implement reversal by ending custody under the challenged insanity-acquittee basis. The mandate permits no indefinite continuation of that custody while the State searches for a substitute rationale. Any independently lawful custody requires its own established authority and procedures; the Court orders neither automatic release from a separate lawful restraint nor a new civil commitment. No future hearing date, factual diagnosis, new charge, compliance finding or retained Supreme Court proceeding is created.

**End of entry: Foucha v. Louisiana, merits decision, May 18, 1992.**

### Riggins v. Nevada, No. 90-8466

Merits decision, May 18, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 15, 1992; decided May 18, 1992. On writ of certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1257 to the Nevada Supreme Court, 107 Nev.178, 808 P.2d535 (March 28, 1991), which affirmed murder and robbery convictions and a death sentence. The granted question concerns forced medication through trial and the Sixth and Fourteenth Amendment right to a full and fair trial.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses, vacates the convictions and dependent sentence, and remands for a new trial.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Murder and robbery convictions and dependent death sentence | Reversed and vacated, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter | Scalia, Thomas | New trial if the State proceeds. | Fifth Amendment demeanor/self-incrimination theory | Not reached; all nine agree | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | No Justice opposes nonreach | No independent Fifth Amendment holding; the preserved medication and fair-trial ground resolves the case. |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | O'Connor | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Souter | Reversal and new-trial mandate | Parts I–IV: record and scope, justification rule, trial-risk holding and mandate; six Justices support these parts |

| Concurrence in the judgment | Kennedy | No other Justice | Joins reversal and new-trial judgment | Distinct extraordinary-showing protection for trial participation |
| Dissent | Thomas | Scalia | Would affirm | Parts I, II-B and II-C: distinction between personal-liberty remedies and proven trial unfairness |
| Dissent | Thomas | No other Justice | Would affirm | Part II-A: additional objections about proof of involuntariness and the preservation and scope of an independent liberty claim |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. The State must justify antipsychotic medication continued over objection

**Controlling proposition:** Once a pretrial detainee objects to continued antipsychotic medication, the State must establish medical appropriateness and a need sufficient to override the protected refusal interest, considering reasonable less intrusive means. The unexplained order here supplies neither an essential justification nor findings about alternatives and cannot authorize the compelled treatment through trial.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens and Souter: six of nine participating Justices adopt this rule and application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that clinical acceptability does not itself establish legal authority to override refusal. Washington v. Harper recognizes the substantial liberty interest affected by unwanted antipsychotic medication and permits justified treatment of dangerous, mentally ill prisoners. An accused awaiting trial retains the protected refusal interest recognized in Harper. Riggins initially sought treatment, but continued administration became involuntary after his objection. Medical appropriateness is accepted for this review; necessity is not. The hearing contained testimony that he could remain competent without Mellaril and questions about the high dosage. The trial court's unexplained denial establishes neither an essential safety need nor that less intrusive means could not permit adjudication. The State's legitimate interest in a competent trial cannot replace those missing determinations. The Court does not impose a new documentary ritual, universal proof quantum or strict-scrutiny formula. Medically appropriate treatment essential to safety after considering alternatives remains possible. The complete conditions for competency-restoration medication, and whether a defendant may insist on becoming incompetent, remain undecided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Washington v. Harper, 494 U.S. 210 (1990): applied for the protected refusal interest and requirement of overriding justification and medical appropriateness; its convicted-prisoner procedures do not automatically govern trial medication.
- Illinois v. Allen, 397 U.S. 337 (1970): relied on for the State's legitimate trial interest and the possibility of necessary courtroom measures; no comprehensive competency-medication exception is adopted.

##### 2. This unsupported medication order requires a new trial

**Controlling proposition:** When unjustified compelled antipsychotic medication through trial creates a supported, substantial risk of impairing the defense that the record cannot reliably reconstruct, the defendant need not demonstrate exactly how an unmedicated trial would have differed. The risk here extends beyond visible demeanor to testimony, comprehension and communication with counsel; explanatory expert testimony does not cure it, and the convictions and dependent sentence must be vacated for a new trial.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens and Souter: six of nine participating Justices adopt this record-specific rule and remedy. Kennedy concurs in the reversal and new-trial judgment on a distinct trial-integrity ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this trial record cannot establish that the unsupported treatment left the defense intact. Illinois v. Allen and Estelle v. Williams recognize that compelled practices can affect an accused's trial in ways a transcript cannot measure. The doctors' testimony identifies a strong possibility that this dosage affected attention, communication and the substance of testimony, not merely the appearance the jury observed. Expert explanation may help jurors interpret demeanor, but cannot restore a conversation with counsel or a response that medication changed. The contrary demand for a precise account of the trial that would have occurred without treatment would therefore require speculation. Allen permits necessary measures to protect the trial, but this record contains no finding establishing an essential justification for this treatment. Reversal and a new trial follow from this combination of unjustified treatment and supported, unreconstructable trial risk. The Court neither finds Riggins incompetent nor decides that every medication error is structural. Renewed compelled treatment requires a constitutionally adequate contemporaneous justification.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Illinois v. Allen, 397 U.S. 337 (1970): applied by analogy to potentially prejudicial compelled trial measures and the need for an essential justification.
- Estelle v. Williams, 425 U.S. 501 (1976): applied by analogy to effects of compelled trial appearance that cannot be fully reconstructed from a transcript; its particular waiver disposition is not extended.

#### Current-Law Effect and Limits

Effective May 18, 1992, an objection to continued trial-related antipsychotic medication obligates the State to establish its medical appropriateness and overriding need with attention to reasonable alternatives. Unjustified treatment with the supported, unreconstructable trial risk present here requires reversal and a new trial. Harper remains governing in its convicted-prisoner setting; this decision adds the stated application for an objecting detainee at trial. No universal proof quantum, strict-scrutiny formula, structural-error category or comprehensive competency-medication test is adopted. Kennedy's extraordinary-showing standard and Stone-Zsela's express Chapman analysis remain noncontrolling supplemental grounds. The unpreserved Eighth Amendment sentencing-display issue and competent-defendant refusal that would produce incompetence remain open. Six direct joins resolve each holding; no Marks construction is necessary.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Kennedy concurs in the judgment. He would require an extraordinary showing that involuntary medication for competency presents no significant risk of materially altering the accused's reactions, participation or assistance to counsel. He doubts that the contemporary medical evidence often permits that showing; if competence cannot be restored without such impairment, lawful civil commitment rather than trial may be the available route. This proposed standard is not the Court's rule.

Stone-Zsela joins the Court and separately applies Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18 (1967): the State bears the burden of showing constitutional trial error harmless beyond a reasonable doubt, and the medication record fails that burden. His concurrence supplies no general structural-error classification, categorical ban on competency treatment or order to stop medically necessary care. The Court's holding remains the record-specific unjustified-treatment and trial-risk rule.

Thomas, joined by Scalia except in Part II-A, would affirm. They regard expert testimony and the competence finding as adequate protection of the trial, distinguish a refusal-interest violation remediable in a civil action from an established basis to reverse a conviction, and reject reversal based on possible impairment alone. Thomas alone also disputes proof of involuntariness and whether a separate Harper liberty theory was preserved and included in the grant; Scalia does not join those additional objections.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court merits review is complete. The case returns to Nevada with the murder and robbery convictions and dependent death sentence vacated, for a new trial if the State proceeds. Renewed involuntary medication must have a constitutionally adequate contemporaneous justification under the Court's holding; a new trial is not an order to withhold medically necessary treatment. The Court does not authorize trial of an incompetent defendant, fix a treatment plan, decide an unpresented sentencing claim, set a new date or retain supervision.

**End of entry: Riggins v. Nevada, merits decision, May 18, 1992.**

### Wyatt v. Cole, No. 91-126

Merits decision, May 18, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 14, 1992; decided May 18, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the Fifth Circuit, 928 F.2d 718 (April 17, 1991), concerning the qualified-immunity protection afforded Cole and attorney Robbins for using Mississippi's replevin procedure. The granted issue is whether private defendants in actions under §1983 may claim the good-faith immunity afforded public officials; the operative controversy concerns Harlow immunity for these private litigants. The petitioner seeks reversal and continued proceedings, not a Supreme Court damages award. Reporter reference: 504 U.S. 158.

| Robbins's private qualified immunity | Reversed, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy | Souter, Thomas | Independently determine Robbins's attribution and other remaining issues; attorney status alone settles neither attribution nor immunity |

There is no additional liability finding, new trial command, or date-specific mandate direction.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | O'Connor | White, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia and Kennedy in full; Stone-Zsela as to private litigants invoking state replevin machinery and the stated reservations | Reverse and remand | Six Justices join the entire opinion: no private Harlow immunity in replevin, garnishment or attachment; defense reservation, individual attribution and remand. #### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Private litigants and immunity from suit

**Controlling proposition:** Private litigants who invoke state replevin, garnishment, or attachment procedures do not receive the objective immunity from suit recognized in Harlow merely because their qualifying joint action may be attributable to the State under §1983. Whether such defendants have a distinct good-faith or probable-cause defense, whether the plaintiff bears additional elements, and the allocation of any such burden remain undecided.

**Authority:** O'Connor's opinion for the Court, joined in full by White, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia and Kennedy: six of nine participating Justices adopt the proposition covering replevin, garnishment and attachment. Stone-Zsela joins the opinion only as applied to private litigants invoking state replevin machinery, including its stated reservations; seven adopt that narrower proposition. He takes no position on its extension to the other procedures. Souter and Thomas dissent. The six full joins suffice for the broader rule; no fractured-decision analysis is required.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that private use of compulsory state process does not itself carry public-official immunity. Lugar v. Edmondson Oil Co. supplies the attribution requirements but expressly reserved immunity. Owen v. City of Independence requires an independent historical and functional basis for protection under §1983. The analogies to malicious prosecution and abuse of process may support defenses involving good faith or probable cause; they do not establish the immunity from litigation that Cole and Robbins obtained. Pierson v. Ray recognized a good-faith protection for police officers, while Harlow v. Fitzgerald reformulated official immunity to protect discretionary public service from disruptive inquiry. Mitchell v. Forsyth gives that protection consequences beyond a defense to liability, including immediate appeal. Cole pursued a private commercial claim, and Robbins represented it; neither held an office whose public performance required this protection. Reasonable reliance on legislation therefore does not justify the requested extension. The Court reserves any independently supported defense and its burden, and requires further consideration of each defendant's state-action status before liability can be imposed.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Lugar v. Edmondson Oil Co., 457 U.S. 922 (1982) — answers its reserved immunity question while preserving its two-part attribution inquiry; joint action does not itself establish immunity or either defendant's liability.
- Owen v. City of Independence, 445 U.S. 622 (1980) — applies its historical and functional inquiry; immunity is not assumed for every person reached by §1983.
- Pierson v. Ray, 386 U.S. 547 (1967) — distinguishes its police good-faith protection from automatic private immunity from suit; no private merits defense is adjudicated.
- Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800 (1982) — declines to extend its objective public-official immunity to these private litigants; eligible officials' protection remains unchanged.
- Mitchell v. Forsyth, 472 U.S. 511 (1985) — applies its distinction between immunity from litigation and a defense to liability; it does not supply private eligibility.
- Malley v. Briggs, 475 U.S. 335 (1986) — uses its complaining-witness historical analogy without turning analogous tort elements into Harlow immunity.
- Dennis v. Sparks, 449 U.S. 24 (1980) — preserves its refusal to transfer judicial immunity to private joint actors; the independent reliance theory also fails for the reasons stated here.

**Application and questions not reached:** The Fifth Circuit's qualified-immunity disposition concerning both Cole and Robbins is reversed. The case returns for the remaining §1983 predicates and any legally available defense; no damages are awarded, no defendant's liability or subjective good faith is finally determined, and the officers' separate judgments and other fee issues outside the granted question are not reopened.

#### Current-Law Effect and Limits

Effective May 18, 1992, the private-immunity question reserved in Lugar is answered for litigants invoking state replevin, garnishment and attachment procedures: Harlow immunity is unavailable. Public-official immunity remains unchanged, including the Court's earlier Hunter administration of the judge's Rule 56 inquiry; Hafer's individual-capacity rule remains distinct. No independent private good-faith defense, burden, categorical state-action rule or damages entitlement enters current law. No precedent is overruled.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Kennedy, joined by Scalia, explains why preserving historical protection for reasonable private reliance does not justify Harlow immunity. The analogous torts attended to malice, probable cause and, when material, actual belief. A court may test deficient proof through ordinary summary judgment; the immunity ruling does not decide the elements or burdens of an eventual private defense. Souter, joined by Thomas, would affirm: the historical good-faith protection can support objective immunity, and citizens should be able to rely reasonably on unrepealed laws that officials continue to enforce. Both positions concern the distinction central to the Court's decision; neither creates an affirmative defense as controlling law.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court merits review is complete; the action is remanded through the Fifth Circuit for further proceedings on the unresolved private defendants' §1983 predicates and any available defenses. No scheduled return to this Court, rehearing, mandate-issuance date, or subsequent filing is established.

**End of entry: Wyatt v. Cole, merits decision, May 18, 1992.**

### Wade v. United States, No. 91-5771

The questions concern the Government-motion prerequisite for substantial-assistance sentencing relief and judicial review of a refusal to move. The drug counts carried a ten-year minimum and the firearm count a consecutive five years. No Government motion or enforceable plea promise is established.

#### Judgment

The Court affirms the sentence, 9–0, while rejecting categorical insulation of a refused motion from constitutional review.

| Component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Requested substantial-assistance reduction and inquiry into the refusal | Affirm, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | No Justice | The 180-month sentence stands. No new sentencing proceeding, discovery, hearing or merits remand is ordered. |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part A | Souter | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Affirmance | Government-motion prerequisite and constitutional review; nine-Justice Court rule |

| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Agrees that this proffer fails and the sentence stands | Joins Part A; reserves the discovery standard for a materially supported claim |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Government motion is the statutory trigger

**Controlling proposition:** Substantial assistance alone does not authorize a sentencing court to depart under 18 U.S.C. §3553(e) or USSG §5K1.1 without the Government's required motion. Those provisions give the Government power to move, not a duty to do so whenever assistance has been useful; their independent operation is reserved on this record.

**Authority:** Part A of Souter's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. All nine adopt the statutory rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress made a Government motion the trigger for the substantial-assistance authority at issue. Section 3553(e) permits a sentence below a statutory minimum upon that motion, and §5K1.1 uses the same condition for a Guidelines departure. A judge's finding that cooperation was valuable cannot substitute for the specified act. The statutory objective of encouraging assistance does not erase the means Congress selected to encourage it. Wade supplied information useful to another prosecution, but the Government made no motion. The sentencing court therefore could not reduce the sentence simply by reassessing that assistance. Santobello v. New York requires fulfillment of a material prosecutorial promise inducing a plea; no such promise is established here, and the Court does not resolve that distinct branch. The parties treat the two motion provisions as equivalent obstacles in this case. The Court leaves their possible independent operation open and does not classify this sentencing restriction as a limit on Article III jurisdiction.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Santobello v. New York, 404 U.S. 257 (1971): distinguished; enforcement of an established plea promise remains available on its own requirements, which are not presented here.

##### Constitutional review of a refusal remains available

**Controlling proposition:** A district court may review the Government's refusal to file a substantial-assistance motion and grant relief when the refusal rests on an unconstitutional purpose, including race or religion. This case establishes no particular corrective remedy because no unconstitutional refusal has been shown.

**Authority:** Part A of Souter's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. All nine reject categorical immunity from constitutional review.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that discretion over a substantial-assistance motion remains subject to the Constitution. Wayte v. United States recognizes both the breadth of prosecutorial choice and the constitutional limits upon it. The decision whether to reward cooperation is another exercise of public authority, and the statutory motion requirement does not authorize forbidden discrimination. A refusal because of race or religion would therefore permit judicial relief. The contrary rule would mistake the absence of a statutory entitlement to a motion for the absence of any enforceable constitutional restraint. The Court nevertheless distinguishes unlawful purpose from an ordinary disagreement about cooperation's usefulness. A prosecutor may assess the costs and benefits of moving, even when assistance has value. Wade has identified no supported unconstitutional purpose. The Court accordingly rejects the Fourth Circuit's categorical no-inquiry rationale while leaving the sentence intact and reserving the particular remedy for an established violation.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Wayte v. United States, 470 U.S. 598 (1985): applied to the departure-motion decision; broad prosecutorial discretion remains constitutionally limited.

##### Refusals unrelated to a legitimate governmental end

**Controlling proposition:** A defendant is entitled to relief if the prosecutor's refusal to file a substantial-assistance motion is not rationally related to any legitimate governmental end. Useful assistance alone does not establish that defect, because the Government may rationally assess the costs and benefits of moving; no particular corrective remedy is selected here.

**Authority:** Part B of Souter's Opinion of the Court, joined by White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. Eight Justices adopt this independent constitutional ground; Stone-Zsela does not join the separate formulation and resolves only the unsupported proffer under his stated ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a departure-motion refusal unrelated to any legitimate governmental end would entitle a defendant to relief. The Government's power to choose does not authorize arbitrary treatment without a lawful governmental purpose. Wayte v. United States preserves constitutional limits upon prosecutorial discretion; the rationality requirement applies that distinction to the asserted refusal here. But constitutional review does not make the sentencing judge the prosecutor's supervisor in evaluating every instance of cooperation. Wade's assistance may have been valuable while the Government still reasonably concluded that moving would not serve its legitimate objectives. His counsel identified no contrary factual reason when invited to describe the evidence supporting a hearing. A useful contribution to another prosecution therefore does not, by itself, establish an irrational refusal. The Court rejects a demand for judicial inquiry based only on the asserted value of assistance, leaves the sentence intact, and reserves the remedy that would be appropriate after proof of a refusal actually failing this constitutional requirement.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Wayte v. United States, 470 U.S. 598 (1985): applied for constitutionally constrained prosecutorial choice; the legitimate-end requirement is adopted for the refusal at issue.

##### Threshold for discovery or an evidentiary hearing

**Controlling proposition:** A defendant seeking discovery or an evidentiary hearing about a refused substantial-assistance motion must make a substantial threshold showing of improper motive; useful assistance alone, with or without generalized accusations, is insufficient. Wade's assistance-only proffer does not justify discovery, a hearing, a remand to develop an unspecified claim, or a sentence reduction.

**Authority:** Part B of Souter's Opinion of the Court, joined by White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas: eight Justices adopt the discovery threshold and its application. Stone-Zsela agrees that this proffer fails but does not join the general threshold; his separate concurrence is not a limit on the eight-Justice rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an allegation of useful cooperation does not itself open prosecutorial decisionmaking to discovery. Wayte v. United States recognizes broad prosecutorial discretion and the costs of motive inquiries; it reserved the discovery question there. A defendant must make a substantial threshold showing directed to improper motive before obtaining discovery or an evidentiary hearing. That threshold does not require the sentencing court to treat every refusal as unreviewable. It requires something beyond the assistance itself and a generalized charge of bad faith. Wade contends that the district court's view of its authority prevented him from developing his claim. The record defeats that contention: the court invited counsel to identify the evidence he would offer, and counsel described only Wade's cooperation. That proffer is consistent with a lawful assessment of the costs and benefits of moving. It identifies no suspect reason for refusal. A remand to search for an unspecified constitutional violation is therefore unwarranted, and the judgment is affirmed.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Wayte v. United States, 470 U.S. 598 (1985): its constitutional limits and concern with intrusive inquiry are applied; it reserved discovery entitlement, and the substantial-assistance threshold is adopted here.

**Limits and questions not reached.** No plea promise is found. No particular remedy for a proved unconstitutional refusal, separate §3553(e)/§5K1.1 interaction, departure amount, or independent challenge to the consecutive firearm term is decided.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Santobello's plea-promise rule is preserved but not triggered. Wayte's constitutional limits and concern with unsupported prosecutorial inquiry are applied. No earlier Supreme Court holding is overruled.

Effective May 18, the motion remains the trigger; constitutional review remains available, and the eight-Justice substantial-threshold rule governs discovery and evidentiary hearings. The court may not grant a reduction or inquiry simply because assistance was valuable. The eight-Justice rule separately permits relief for a refusal not rationally related to any legitimate governmental end; no specific corrective remedy is selected. Williams v. United States (March 9) still governs correction of an actual Guidelines error; United States v. Wilson (March 24) still assigns initial credit computation to the Bureau of Prisons. Neither displaces the required motion. Stone-Zsela's reservation does not qualify the controlling eight-Justice threshold.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stone-Zsela concurs in part and in the judgment. Counsel identified no discrimination, retaliation, broken promise or other actionable factual theory, so this proffer fails without selecting the discovery standard for a stronger claim. He reserves that question and rejects a rule requiring proof of hidden discrimination before access to evidence needed to test a supported claim. He does not join Part B's general threshold.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court sentence review ends. The Fourth Circuit's affirmance remains in place on the grounds stated here; no new sentence or lower-court evidentiary proceeding is required. No later filing, rehearing or retained Supreme Court matter is established.

### Burson v. Freeman, No. 90-1056

Merits decision, May 26, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

504 U.S. 191.

Argued October 8, 1991; decided May 26, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the Supreme Court of Tennessee, 802 S.W.2d 210 (October 1, 1990). The question is whether Tenn. Code Ann. §2-7-111(b)'s prohibition of campaign solicitation, display and distribution within 100 feet of polling-place entrances violates the First and Fourteenth Amendments. Freeman sought declaratory and permanent injunctive relief. The Chancellor dismissed her challenge; the Tennessee Supreme Court reversed on the federal speech ground. Tennessee seeks reversal of that federal invalidation.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter participated at argument and decision. Justice Thomas took no part; argument preceded his October 23, 1991 oath, and no reargument is established.

#### Judgment

The judgment invalidating the 100-foot campaign boundary on federal constitutional grounds is reversed and remanded, 5–3.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Federal constitutional invalidation of the 100-foot electioneering boundary | Reversed and remanded, 5–3 | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Scalia, Kennedy | Stevens, O'Connor, Souter | Remove the federal basis for invalidation of this boundary; return for proceedings consistent with the judgment |

Thomas takes no part. The Court does not award damages, adjudicate other distances or applications, or direct broader election restrictions. The judgment binds this facial challenge but supplies no unified majority rationale for its validity.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Plurality opinion | Blackmun | Stone-Zsela, White, Kennedy | Reverse and remand | Public-forum/content classification, strict scrutiny, and the particular historical and practical showing sustaining the 100-foot boundary; larger zones and other applications reserved |
| Concurrence | Kennedy | No other Justice | Joins plurality and reversal | The analysis identifies genuine accommodation of the right to vote rather than a license for content censorship; retains his objection to general content-based balancing |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Scalia | No other Justice | Reverse and remand on a different ground | Historically administered polling environs constitute a nonpublic forum during voting; the boundary is reasonable and viewpoint neutral; no plurality join |

No Opinion of the Court addresses the restriction's validity. The governing scrutiny proposition has the separate seven-Justice support stated below; the judgment and each rationale retain their different coalitions.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Content-based electioneering restrictions on public sidewalks

**Controlling proposition:** Tennessee's campaign-specific prohibition is a content-based restriction on political speech, and ordinary public sidewalks and streets within the 100-foot boundary remain public forums. The State must show that this restriction is necessary to serve a compelling interest and narrowly drawn to achieve that end; viewpoint neutrality and the availability of speech beyond the boundary do not by themselves lower that scrutiny.

**Authority:** Seven participating Justices expressly adopt this governing proposition at the same level of generality: Blackmun's plurality, joined by Stone-Zsela, White and Kennedy, and Stevens's dissent, joined by O'Connor and Souter. It is an independently majority-supported proposition, not a seven-Justice judgment coalition or an Opinion of the Court. Scalia rejects the forum classification and applies a different standard.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the subject of speech determines whether Tennessee's prohibition applies, so the law requires the strict scrutiny used for content-based restrictions. Perry Education Ass'n v. Perry Local Educators' Ass'n supplies that standard in traditional public forums. United States v. Grace preserves ordinary sidewalks' public character despite proximity to a government institution. Boos v. Barry requires scrutiny of restrictions directed at political subjects; viewpoint neutrality does not make subject discrimination neutral. Mills v. Alabama protects election-day advocacy while leaving the distinct regulation of polling places open. Tennessee's protection of free voting presents a compelling interest, but identifying that interest does not dispense with necessity and tailoring. The Justices adopting this governing rule disagree about whether the history, outdoor reach and available alternatives satisfy it here. Their shared rule therefore establishes the applicable scrutiny, not a common resolution of the statute's validity or permission to suppress election advocacy generally.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Perry Education Ass'n v. Perry Local Educators' Ass'n, 460 U.S. 37 (1983) — applies its traditional-public-forum and content-based scrutiny framework to the ordinary streets and sidewalks reached here.
- United States v. Grace, 461 U.S. 171 (1983) — applies its protection of ordinary public sidewalks; government-building proximity does not alone remove that status.
- Boos v. Barry, 485 U.S. 312 (1988) — applies strict scrutiny to political subject discrimination; a claim of regulatory purpose does not automatically make this campaign restriction content neutral.
- Mills v. Alabama, 384 U.S. 214 (1966) — preserves its protection of general election-day advocacy and its reservation concerning conduct in and around polls; it does not automatically settle this boundary's validity.
- Simon & Schuster, Inc. v. Members of the New York State Crime Victims Board, December 10, 1991, decision — preserves exacting review of content-based burdens; this case's divided application creates no general exception to that rule.
- Ward v. Rock Against Racism, 491 U.S. 781 (1989) — distinguishes its content-neutral time, place and manner standard; it does not replace strict scrutiny here.

##### Judgment without a controlling validity rationale

**Judgment and authority.** Five Justices—Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Scalia and Kennedy—reverse the judgment invalidating Tennessee's 100-foot campaign boundary and remand. Blackmun, Stone-Zsela, White and Kennedy sustain it under strict scrutiny; Scalia sustains it as reasonable and viewpoint neutral in a nonpublic forum. Stevens, O'Connor and Souter would affirm invalidation. No opinion speaks for the Court on the justification sufficient to uphold the restriction.

**Fractured-decision boundary.** Marks v. United States, 430 U.S. 188 (1977), does not supply a controlling application rationale here. The plurality's rule assumes a public forum and requires necessity and narrow tailoring; Scalia's rule assumes a nonpublic forum and requires reasonableness and viewpoint neutrality. Those different forum predicates are not demonstrated logical subsets. His conclusion that the same history makes the restriction reasonable does not adopt the plurality's necessity or tailoring determination. The five-vote disposition binds this case; neither the four-Justice strict-scrutiny application nor the one-Justice nonpublic-forum theory becomes a controlling exception.

**Limits and questions not reached.** The judgment concerns this 100-foot boundary during voting. It does not decide the separately classified 300-foot provisions, larger zones, restrictions beyond actual voting periods, unrelated political speech, discriminatory enforcement, hypothetical incidental bumper-sticker displays or unusual highway applications. The Court does not resolve state constitutional grounds not decided on review or approve a general election-day campaign ban.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

The treatments supporting the controlling scrutiny rule are stated above. Marks yields no controlling validity rationale. The plurality's use of Munro v. Socialist Workers Party, 479 U.S. 189 (1986), to support preventive tailoring has four votes and does not change that precedent's operation. No precedent is overruled.

Effective May 26, 1992, seven Justices establish the public-forum, content-based classification and strict-scrutiny requirement for the ordinary streets and sidewalks reached by this law. The 100-foot restriction survives this facial challenge by the five-vote judgment. No unified rationale validates it as a reusable strict-scrutiny exception, and no nonpublic-forum exemption becomes law. Perry, Grace, Boos and Simon & Schuster remain operative; Norman's ballot-access rules do not replace speech scrutiny. The plurality's preventive application of Munro and its history-based necessity conclusion remain noncontrolling. No numerical safe harbor for every location or broader restriction is created, and no precedent is overruled.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Blackmun's plurality, joined by Stone-Zsela, White and Kennedy, concludes that the historical mechanism justifies this limited protection of the unavoidable approach to voting. Campaign agents could confront and observe voters in ways that enabled intimidation or fraudulent control. Starting protection only inside the door and punishing proved intimidation afterward do not equally prevent subtle or completed pressure. Campaign-specific coverage addresses the documented source of that harm; it does not prefer candidates or viewpoints. The plurality treats the 100-foot line as a confined implementation of protection while campaigning remains available beyond it. It does not demand an experiment exposing voters to coercion or proof that each foot is indispensable, and does not treat tradition alone as sufficient. This application has four votes and is noncontrolling.

Kennedy's concurrence explains that a law facially distinguishing content may call for examination of its actual justification. He regards this narrow accommodation of free voting as compatible with his objection to general censorship through compelling-interest balancing. His concurrence does not lower the standard joined in this case.

Scalia concurs only in the judgment. In his view, the longstanding exclusion of electioneering from functioning polling environs means that those areas are not traditional public forums during voting. He finds the restriction reasonable and viewpoint neutral and accepts the historical considerations as adequate for that standard. His forum theory has one vote.

Stevens, joined by O'Connor and Souter, agrees that strict scrutiny governs but concludes that Tennessee has not met it. The State's concrete disruption testimony concerns the interior, while the prohibition reaches peaceful campaign speech across public streets and sidewalks. A tradition adopted with secret ballots does not itself prove the continuing necessity of this outdoor exclusion. Existing laws against intimidation and more limited entrance protections are insufficiently answered; permitting other solicitation while banning campaign speech further weakens the asserted fit. The dissent would leave the federal invalidation in place.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court merits review is complete. The case returns to the Tennessee Supreme Court for proceedings consistent with the reversal of federal invalidation of the 100-foot restriction. No new Supreme Court hearing, later filing, mandate-issuance date or further judgment is established. Remaining state-law matters may be addressed only if procedurally available; this Court makes no state constitutional decision or directive concerning the separate 300-foot provisions.

**End of entry: Burson v. Freeman, merits decision, May 26, 1992.**

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. No independent demand or initiation element

**Controlling proposition:** Official-right extortion under 18 U.S.C. §1951 does not require that the public official initiate, demand or separately induce the payment when he obtains money to which he is not entitled knowing that it is given in return for official acts. The required interstate-commerce element and proof beyond a reasonable doubt remain.

**Authority:** Parts I–II of Stevens’s opinion, joined by White, Blackmun, O’Connor and Souter; five Justices adopt this statutory ground. Kennedy and Stone-Zsela independently support the judgment without joining those parts, and their reasons are not necessary to make this holding controlling.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an official cannot avoid this offense merely because the payer proposed the transaction. Morissette supplies the presumption that Congress carries established legal meaning into a borrowed term of art, and Taylor recognizes the same interpretive starting point. The official-right branch retains the prohibition against taking unauthorized payment for official service; the Hobbs Act’s expansion to private force and fear offenses does not narrow that branch. The text supplies no first-speaker condition, and even reading induced to modify official right does not necessarily require the official to initiate the transaction. Evans’s contrary construction confuses initiation with the knowing receipt of payment for the exercise of public power. The Government must still establish the prohibited exchange rather than rely upon lawful contributions or official status alone. This construction resolves the presented demand objection without deciding every gift, gratuity or disputed promise. The statute’s commerce element and the ordinary criminal burden remain intact.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S.246 — applied for the established content of a borrowed criminal-law term; no strict-liability offense is created.
- Taylor v. United States, 495 U.S.575 — applied for the common-law interpretive presumption, subject to contrary statutory direction.
- United States v. Nardello, 393 U.S.286 — distinguishes expansion of extortion beyond public officials from narrowing its official-right branch.

##### 2. The charge requires an exchange; promised performance need not be completed

**Controlling proposition:** A charge requiring a public official knowingly to accept money in exchange for a specific requested exercise of official power, while excluding a contribution standing alone even when the donor has pending business, satisfies McCormick’s exchange requirement on this record. The offense is complete upon receipt in return for the official undertaking; actual fulfillment of the promised act is not an additional element.

**Authority:** Parts I–II of Stevens’s opinion, joined by White, Blackmun, O’Connor and Souter; five Justices sustain the instruction and distinguish agreement from later performance.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this charge distinguished a purchased official act from legitimate political support. McCormick supplies the protection for campaign contributions: a contribution must be given in return for an explicit promise or undertaking of official action, rather than merely because the contributor hopes for favor. The charge here first told the jury that accepting a contribution, even from a donor with pending business, is lawful by itself. It then required demand or acceptance in exchange for a specific requested exercise of official power. Read with the knowing-and-willful instructions, that language required the prohibited undertaking and did not make the disclosed check or an official’s position an offense. Evans’s separate demand objection adds a condition the statute lacks. Nor must the Government prove completion of the promised rezoning assistance: receipt in return for the undertaking completes the offense. The Court does not substitute new credibility findings for the verdict or decide cases lacking the required exchange.

**Precedent treatment:**

- McCormick v. United States, 500 U.S.257 — applied: legitimate campaign support remains protected absent the required official exchange; completed performance is not required.
- Glasser v. United States, 315 U.S.60 — applied for the favorable-to-verdict treatment of supported factual inferences; statutory construction and charge review remain legal questions.

##### 3. No false claim of lawful entitlement is indispensable

**Controlling proposition:** Official-right extortion is not confined to obtaining payment by falsely asserting that law entitles the official to collect it. A knowing unauthorized payment in return for official acts can violate the official-right branch without deceiving the payer that the payment is a lawful fee.

**Authority:** Part III of Stevens’s opinion, joined by White, Blackmun, Kennedy and Souter; five Justices decide this construction. O’Connor does not reach it, and Stone-Zsela does not join or supply an additional ground on it.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an official’s false claim to a lawful fee is one form of official-right extortion, not an indispensable feature of every form. Morissette supplies the common-law interpretive inquiry, but identifying frequent examples does not establish an exclusive element. Commonwealth v. Wilson recognizes official extortion beyond excessive fees, while the pre-enactment decisions in Commonwealth v. Brown and State v. Barts supply examples of corrupt payment for the use or withholding of public authority. Collier v. State distinguishes payment for private service from a payment exacted in an official capacity; it does not make the payer’s belief that a fee is lawful the defining condition. The contrary argument would erase the distinction between an illustration and a required element. The payment here concerned specific official assistance and was not lawfully due. The Court rejects the proposed false-pretense limitation without making all private payments to officials criminal or eliminating the exchange required for this conviction.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S.246 — applied to identify rather than artificially narrow the borrowed offense.
- Commonwealth v. Wilson, 30 Pa.Super.26; Commonwealth v. Brown, 23 Pa.Super.470; and State v. Barts, 132 N.J.L.74 — considered as pre-enactment evidence that official extortion was not confined to overcharged lawful fees.
- Collier v. State, 55 Ala.125 — distinguished: private-service payment is different from payment for official action; its result does not establish the asserted universal false-pretense element.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No entrapment claim is reopened. The conditional tax claim is not reached by the affirming coalition; Scalia and Thomas separately reject disturbing that count because the instructional objection was not preserved. Mere gratuities and cases lacking the required undertaking are not decided. The Court creates no new commerce element or proof standard. No Marks construction is necessary because each reached controlling proposition has an express five-Justice opinion coalition.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Morissette, Taylor, Nardello, McCormick and Glasser retain the precise force described above; none is overruled. The pre-enactment state cases support the historical meaning of the federal term rather than become independent federal offenses. Enmons’s treatment of wrongful force or fear in its labor setting does not replace the official-right branch. R.L.C.’s juvenile ceiling remains; its separate disagreements about legislative history and lenity were never a majority methodology. Jacobson remains the independently-existing-predisposition rule for a presented entrapment issue; it does not reopen that defense in this grant. The present decision neither overrides that defense nor presumes predisposition from the existence of a conviction in a later properly presented challenge.

Effective May 26, the official-right branch has the stated controlling no-separate-demand construction, charge application and rejection of a universal false-lawful-fee limitation. Before this decision McCormick reserved the general inducement issue; afterward no first-speaker or demand element may be imposed for the exchange adjudicated here. McCormick’s campaign-contribution protection remains operative, and promised performance need not be completed. Statutory commerce nexus, proof beyond reasonable doubt and distinct defenses remain unchanged. The two five-Justice part coalitions must be kept separate. Kennedy’s account of implied communication and Stone-Zsela’s rejection of status-based coercion or silence as an independent interpretive substitute are their separate grounds, not additional elements silently added to the Court’s rule.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

O’Connor joins Parts I–II but would leave the unbriefed false-pretense issue undecided under the question-presented limitation. Kennedy joins Part III and concurs in the judgment because inducement applies to the official-right branch but may be supplied by a knowingly communicated corrupt exchange, including words or conduct understood that way; he does not adopt the grammar-based removal of inducement. Stone-Zsela separately concludes that induced does not identify the party who must speak first and that this specific-undertaking charge protects legitimate contributions. He relies neither on a presumption that every office coerces nor on congressional silence alone, and reserves gratuities, ambiguous general support and any case without the required undertaking. Thomas, joined by Scalia, would reverse the Hobbs disposition because the statute requires inducement and the historically distinct misuse of a claimed official entitlement. Their lenity and federalism concerns do not disturb the tax count, whose separate objection they regard as unpreserved.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court merits review ends in affirmance of the Eleventh Circuit. The Hobbs Act and tax convictions remain in place. The Court orders no retrial, resentencing, acquittal, new factual hearing or further Supreme Court action. The unpresented entrapment and collateral evidentiary issues are not reopened. No later filing or enforcement event is established.

**End of entry: Evans v. United States, merits decision, May 26, 1992.**

### Quill Corp. v. North Dakota, No. 91-194

Merits decision, May 26, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

504 U.S. 298.

Argued January 22, 1992; decided May 26, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the North Dakota Supreme Court, 470 N.W.2d 203, which reversed summary judgment for Quill and directed enforcement of the State's collection duty. Quill challenges that duty under the Commerce and Due Process Clauses and seeks reversal of liability for tax, interest and penalties sought from July 1, 1987. The potential retroactive consequences of overruling Bellas Hess were excluded from the grant.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The judgment is reversed and the case remanded, 8–1. Bellas Hess’s surviving Commerce Clause collection rule bars the challenged duty on this record. Eight Justices also reject a physical-presence requirement under due process; Stone-Zsela does not reach that independent constitutional ground.

| Enforceability of Quill collection duty | Reverse and remand, 8–1 | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | White | The challenged collection liability fails on this record under the Commerce Clause. |
| Due-process challenge | Rejected by the eight reaching it, 8–0 | White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | No Justice votes to sustain it; Stone-Zsela does not reach it | This ruling supplies no collection liability in light of the Commerce disposition. |

The consumer’s use-tax duty and other taxes remain outside the judgment. The Court creates no retrospective liability, tax amount, interest award or penalty; the challenge to this duty succeeds. The state-court §1983/§1988 cross-appeal, left unaddressed below after its constitutional ruling, receives no Supreme Court merits decision or fee award and may be handled in lawful further proceedings.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. Purposeful contacts can satisfy due process without physical presence

**Controlling proposition:** A mail-order seller that purposefully and continuously solicits a State’s consumers may have the minimum connection required for a use-tax collection obligation even without physical presence, when the obligation is rationally related to benefits received through that market. Quill’s sustained solicitation and approximately $1 million in annual North Dakota sales satisfy due process; Bellas Hess and earlier holdings are overruled only insofar as they make physical presence indispensable for this collection duty.

**Authority:** Parts I–III of Stevens’s opinion, joined by White, Blackmun, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; eight Justices. Stone-Zsela expressly does not reach due process and supplies no join.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that deliberate, continuing participation in the State’s market supplies the fair connection required here. Miller Brothers supplies the requirement of a definite link between the State and the person or transaction, while International Shoe explains why purposeful contacts matter more than a formal presence label. Burger King confirms that commercial activity deliberately directed to a State does not escape its authority solely because the seller communicates from elsewhere; Shaffer applies the same rejection of mechanical presence rules to adjudicative jurisdiction. Comparable reasoning governs this collection obligation without equating every rule for judicial jurisdiction with every rule for legislative authority. Quill repeatedly solicits North Dakota customers and conducts substantial sales there, and the use tax relates to the benefits of access to that market. The contrary physical-presence prerequisite cannot survive that reasoning. Due process and the Commerce Clause impose distinct limits, however, so satisfying this connection does not itself authorize the collection duty that the Court rejects on the other ground.

**Precedent treatment:**

- National Bellas Hess v. Department of Revenue, 386 U.S.753 — overruled only to the extent its due-process holding requires physical presence for use-tax collection; its Commerce rule is treated separately below.
- Miller Brothers v. Maryland, 347 U.S.340 — the minimum-connection requirement remains; any indispensable physical-entry implication is displaced at the stated collection scope.
- International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S.310 — applied for purposeful affiliation and fair connection.
- Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz, 471 U.S.462 — its nonphysical purposeful-contact reasoning is extended by analogy to the collection obligation; every personal-jurisdiction rule is not imported.
- Shaffer v. Heitner, 433 U.S.186 — applied by comparable reasoning against a mechanical presence prerequisite.
- Moorman Manufacturing Co. v. Bair, 437 U.S.267 — the requirement that the tax relate rationally to state-connected values remains.

##### 2. The specific Commerce collection precedent remains controlling

**Controlling proposition:** Bellas Hess’s specific Commerce Clause rule continues to bar imposing a sales-or-use-tax collection duty on a seller whose customer connections are limited to mail or common-carrier commerce without the requisite local presence. Quill’s solicitation and remote deliveries do not themselves displace that rule; Complete Auto’s separate substantial-nexus requirement is not satisfied merely by purposeful market access or the due-process showing.

**Authority:** The common, narrower ground expressly supported by Stevens, Blackmun, O’Connor and Souter in Part IV, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas in their concurrence, and Stone-Zsela in his concurrence in the judgment; eight Justices retain the particular precedent. The full Part IV substantive policy analysis has four joins and is not an Opinion of the Court. The software application has the separate five-Justice authority identified below.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that its specific collection precedent remains in force. Complete Auto abandoned a formal immunity based on how a State labels its tax, but it preserved substantial nexus as a distinct condition and did not overrule Bellas Hess’s mail-order collection boundary. National Geographic and D.H. Holmes distinguished that precedent rather than announcing its abandonment. The State’s account of economic growth and easier calculation presents a substantial argument for change, but it does not remove the particular precedent or the reliance of sellers that arranged distribution and completed transactions under it. Adherence to precedent supports retaining that defined boundary, while Congress can make a coordinated adjustment to collection obligations. Adherence does not require every Justice to endorse the precedent’s original substantive reasoning. This ground does not extend physical presence to every tax or establish a quantitative sales threshold. It preserves the particular rule and the independent constitutional limits that any different measure must satisfy.

**Precedent treatment:**

- National Bellas Hess v. Department of Revenue, 386 U.S.753 — retained for its specific Commerce Clause sales-and-use collection rule; no general immunity for interstate commerce is restored.
- Complete Auto Transit v. Brady, 430 U.S.274 — substantial nexus remains distinct from fair apportionment, nondiscrimination and fair relation to state services; its rejection of Spector’s labeling rule remains.
- National Geographic Society v. California Board of Equalization, 430 U.S.551, and D.H. Holmes Co. v. McNamara, 486 U.S.24 — their express distinctions of Bellas Hess preserve reasonable reliance on the particular collection precedent rather than establish its abandonment.
- Patterson v. McLean Credit Union, 491 U.S.164, and Payne v. Tennessee, 501 U.S.808 — their congressional-correction and reliance principles support the narrower adherence ground at the stated scope.
- Prudential Insurance Co. v. Benjamin, 328 U.S.408 — preserves Congress’s power to authorize otherwise dormant-Commerce burdens; Congress cannot authorize a due-process violation.

##### 3. The limited software interests do not establish sufficient collection nexus

**Controlling proposition:** Quill’s insignificant interests in the licensed ordering software on this record do not supply the local commercial presence required to take its mail-order sales outside the retained Commerce Clause collection rule. This conclusion rejects the State’s asserted alternative nexus for this seller without prescribing a general rule for all software, licenses or in-state property.

**Authority:** Stevens, Blackmun, O’Connor and Souter in Part IV, together with Stone-Zsela’s expressly matching record-specific software ground in his concurrence in the judgment; five Justices adopt this application at the same scope. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas support reversal but are not counted as joining this substantive software explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the limited ordering-software interests do not establish the local commercial activity missing from this record. Bellas Hess supplies the specific collection boundary that the Court retains, and Tyler Pipe identifies the significance of activity within the State that establishes and maintains the seller’s market. Quill’s licensed ordering materials facilitate communication with its out-of-state business; the insignificant property interest does not turn those materials into the local business operation or representative activity on which the State’s alternative depends. The argument that any retained property must end the inquiry gives the bare ownership label more significance than this record warrants. The Court rejects that alternative on the record before it and does not find an unproved warehouse, employee or agent. Nor does it define a universal property-value threshold or decide that every software license is insufficient. Its holding concerns these limited interests and this challenged collection demand alone.

**Precedent treatment:**

- National Bellas Hess v. Department of Revenue, 386 U.S.753 — applied to this mail-order collection demand after rejection of the asserted distinguishing property interest.
- Tyler Pipe Industries v. Washington Department of Revenue, 483 U.S.232 — distinguished for actual in-state market-maintaining activity, including representatives; no employee-only nexus requirement is adopted.
- National Geographic Society v. California Board of Equalization, 430 U.S.551 — Part IV’s additional rejection of a slightest-presence standard supports its four signers’ explanation; Stone-Zsela’s fifth vote adopts the stated record-specific insufficiency without a whole-opinion join. Its local-office holding remains undisturbed.

**Fractured authority:** There is an express majority for the particular surviving-precedent rule, not merely eight matching dispositions. Marks v. United States, 430 U.S.188, does not authorize importing the plurality’s broader economic-policy theory through the concurring votes. The narrower adherence ground is a genuine common ground of the reversal writings and a logical subset of Part IV’s precedent-based reason for the same result. The record-specific software application independently has the five expressly identified supporters. Neither conclusion creates a general physical-presence theory for other taxes.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The consumer’s use tax, other taxes and general tax immunity are outside the holding. No numerical transaction threshold, general software-nexus rule or result under a future statute is announced. Potential retroactive effects of overruling the Commerce rule were excluded from the grant and are not reached. The Court makes no §1983 or §1988 entitlement ruling. Stone-Zsela reserves due process; the eight-Justice due-process holding nevertheless controls.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Bellas Hess has distinct present force: the due-process physical-presence prerequisite is overruled at the collection scope, while the Commerce collection rule remains. Complete Auto’s four separate requirements and rejection of Spector’s formal tax-label immunity survive. National Geographic, D.H. Holmes and Tyler Pipe retain the local-activity applications described above. The ground based on adherence to precedent does not constitutionalize a particular technology or sales threshold. Wyoming’s discriminatory-coal-quota and congressional-authorization holdings remain separate; no collection-nexus holding is taken from them. Hilton’s FELA coverage holding also remains separate; its limited treatment of precedent supplies no automatic fiscal rule. No other earlier holding is overruled here.

Effective May 26, the two constitutional inquiries must be kept separate. Purposeful sustained solicitation on this record satisfies due process without physical presence; Congress cannot dispense with that constitutional limit. Bellas Hess’s specific dormant-Commerce collection rule continues to bar this demand, including the asserted minimal software basis. Congress remains able to change dormant-Commerce constraints through valid legislation. Before this decision Bellas Hess supplied both grounds; afterward its due-process physical-presence requirement is displaced while its specific Commerce rule survives. The broad Part IV account of bright-line rules and national economic burdens has four joins; only the narrower expressly shared precedent ground controls across the eight reversal votes. Neither consumer liability nor every other state tax changes.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia, joined by Kennedy and Thomas, joins the Court’s due-process analysis on the understanding that analogous reasoning does not equate every adjudicative and legislative jurisdiction standard. On Commerce, they retain the square precedent through adherence to precedent, justified reliance and Congress’s authority, without revisiting its substantive merits. Stone-Zsela separately retains the specific collection rule within Complete Auto, concludes that insignificant software is no substitute for local market-maintaining activity, and reserves due process because the Commerce ground resolves the demand. His reliance and congressional-adjustment reasons support adherence without extending a physical-presence condition to all taxation. White joins the due-process ruling but would overrule Bellas Hess’s Commerce rule: sustained economic exploitation and market benefits should meet nexus, and the physical distinction creates unjustified competitive differences. He also questions deciding the software issue against the State without further inquiry. The plurality’s broader defense of the Commerce boundary commands four votes only.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court review ends with reversal and remand to North Dakota for further proceedings consistent with rejection of the challenged collection duty on this record. No tax, interest or penalty amount is imposed; no retrospective authorization is created. The unaddressed state-court civil-rights and fees cross-appeal remains subject to lawful further proceedings without a Supreme Court entitlement finding. The consumer’s use-tax liability is untouched. No new filing, deadline, enforcement finding or retained Supreme Court proceeding is established.
