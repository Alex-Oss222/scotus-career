### Dawson v. Delaware, No. 90-6704

Merits decision, March 9, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 12, 1991. [On writ of certiorari to the Delaware Supreme Court, 581 A.2d 1078](https://static.case.law/a2d/581/html/1078-01.html).

Question presented: Whether Delaware violated the First and Fourteenth Amendments by introducing the stipulated Aryan Brotherhood membership evidence at capital sentencing; no challenge to the convictions is decided.

Dawson and the murder victim were white. The parties stipulated that the Aryan Brotherhood began as a racist California prison gang and that a separate gang with that name existed in Delaware. The stipulation and tattoo evidence did not establish the Delaware organization’s violent conduct or aims, an organizational connection with the crime, or racial motivation for this murder. Dawson preserved his constitutional objection to the agreed evidentiary presentation. His evidence favoring a lesser sentence, or mitigation, included family kindness and participation in drug and alcohol programs; the State established three statutory aggravating circumstances supporting a harsher sentence. Delaware upheld the death sentence, treating the membership evidence as relevant to character and mitigation. Dawson seeks relief from that sentence; Delaware defends relevance and alternatively raises harmless error. The stipulation is not evidence of every fact about other prison gangs. [Record materials, 503 U.S. 159](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-503/pdf/USREPORTS-503-159.pdf).

#### Judgment

The judgment sustaining the death sentence is vacated and the case remanded, 8–1. The convictions remain intact.

| Component | Disposition | Vote | Supporting Justices | Other positions | Remedy |

| Judgment sustaining the death sentence | Vacated and remanded | 8–1 | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter | Thomas | Delaware must conduct further proceedings consistent with the constitutional relevance ruling; convictions remain intact. |
| Direction on harmless error | Availability and application left open | 7–2 | White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter | Stone-Zsela, Thomas | Stone-Zsela would require Chapman review and a new sentencing proceeding unless the State proves harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt. #### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Protected association must bear on a legitimate sentencing issue

**Controlling proposition:** The State may not use protected association to aggravate a capital sentence when the evidence establishes only abstract beliefs and supplies no supported connection to the crime, future danger, another legitimate sentencing issue, or the mitigation actually offered. The stipulated association evidence here lacks that connection and its admission violates the First and Fourteenth Amendments.

**Authority:** Part A of White’s opinion; Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter join White at this level of generality.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an unpopular association does not become sentencing evidence merely because its beliefs are objectionable. NAACP and Bates protect association; Abel shows that those protections do not exclude evidence that actually establishes a witness’s bias. Barclay likewise involved demonstrated racial motivation for the crime. This stipulation supplies neither connection. Even assuming that the Delaware group shared racist beliefs, the State established no link between those beliefs and this murder, and no violent organizational objective attributable to Dawson. The evidence also does not contradict his actual mitigation concerning family kindness and participation in rehabilitation programs. Payne permits relevant rebuttal, not punishment for a belief labeled bad character. Zant’s distinction between legitimate aggravation and protected beliefs therefore controls the application. The Court announces no universal bar against evidence of association and no rule that a white victim always excludes racial motive. A materially different evidentiary record may establish relevance that this stipulation does not.

**Precedent treatment:**

- NAACP v. Alabama, 357 U.S. 449 — applied: freedom of association protects unpopular affiliations.
- Bates v. Little Rock, 361 U.S. 516 — applied: protected association cannot itself justify adverse governmental treatment.
- United States v. Abel, 469 U.S. 45 — distinguished on the facts: association established witness bias there; relevant association remains admissible.
- Barclay v. Florida, 463 U.S. 939 — distinguished: demonstrated racial motivation connected the association to the crime.
- Zant v. Stephens, 462 U.S. 862 — applied: protected beliefs cannot themselves supply an aggravating circumstance.
- Payne v. Tennessee, 501 U.S. 808 — applied with its relevance limit: rebuttal of mitigation does not make unrelated belief probative.
- Schware v. Board of Bar Examiners, 353 U.S. 232 — applied by analogy: an affiliation without a rational connection to the relevant character inquiry is insufficient.

##### Scope of the remand

**Controlling proposition:** The state judgment sustaining this sentence cannot stand on the constitutional reasoning it adopted. On remand the state court may consider Delaware’s harmless-error contention; this Court decides neither whether harmless-error analysis is available for this violation nor whether the error was harmless.

**Authority:** Part B of White’s opinion, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter; seven Justices control the remand’s reserved scope.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that correction of the constitutional error requires vacating the judgment that sustained the sentence, while leaving the convictions intact. The relevance ruling answers the question reviewed here. It does not establish how the challenged evidence affected this jury’s sentencing decision, and it does not decide every condition under which constitutional evidentiary error can be harmless. Delaware advanced harmlessness as an alternative ground, but the state court did not decide that contention under the constitutional rule now stated. Chapman supplies a demanding burden when its form of harmless-error review applies; citing that framework does not decide its availability for this particular infringement. The proper course is to permit consideration of the issue on remand without prejudging either branch. Neither the existence of other aggravators nor the Court’s finding of irrelevance supplies an automatic harmlessness finding. Conversely, the Court does not now command a new sentencing hearing regardless of further lawful review.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18 — preserved: its constitutional harmless-error burden is not relaxed; applicability and satisfaction in this case remain undecided.

#### Current-Law Effect

Effective March 9, the constitutional relevance rule is applied to this stipulation; no categorical association-evidence ban is added. The remand’s seven-Justice reservation leaves both availability and application of harmless-error treatment open. Stone-Zsela’s mandatory Chapman/new-sentencing sequence is not the Court’s instruction. No earlier rule is displaced.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stone-Zsela concurs in the relevance ruling and vacatur but would require Delaware to prove harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt on the trial record, with new sentencing otherwise. Blackmun joins the Court and emphasizes that the permissibility of harmless-error treatment itself remains unresolved; this safeguard may protect against chilling association as well as an unreliable sentence. Thomas dissents: voluntary prison-gang membership can support a character and future-danger inference and rebut favorable character evidence. He would treat ordinary evidentiary fairness, rather than a categorical First Amendment insulation, as the inquiry. His inference is not a Court finding about this Delaware group.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court review of the sentence ends; the case returns to Delaware for further proceedings. No resentencing date, harmlessness finding, stay, or retained Supreme Court supervision is created. The convictions remain intact.

**End of entry: Dawson v. Delaware, Merits decision, March 9, 1992.**

### General Motors Corp. v. Romein, No. 90-1390

Merits decision, March 9, 1992

Argued December 10, 1991. [On writ of certiorari to the Michigan Supreme Court, 436 Mich. 515, 462 N.W.2d 555](https://static.case.law/mich/436/html/0515-01.html).

Question presented: Whether Michigan’s retroactive requirement to repay workers’ compensation offsets violates the Contract Clause or the Due Process Clause.

Michigan’s 1981 legislation permitted coordination of workers’ compensation with specified pension and other benefits, effective March 31, 1982. General Motors and Ford applied offsets to workers injured before that date, and Chambers later accepted that reading. In 1987 Michigan required repayment of those pre-effective-injury offsets, restoring its intended compensation arrangement and equalizing employers that had withheld payments with those that had not. The challenged repayments approached $25 million. The collective agreements predated the coordination law and promised no compensation offset or protection against future statutory adjustment. Michigan’s Supreme Court sustained the repayment requirement; the employers seek reversal on contract and retroactive-due-process grounds. [Record materials, 503 U.S. 181](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-503/pdf/USREPORTS-503-181.pdf).

The Court affirms the Michigan Supreme Court’s judgment, 9–0, rejecting both the Contract Clause challenge and the retroactive due-process challenge. Justice O’Connor delivers the unanimous Opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. All nine support each disposition and join both the holding that no contractual obligation was impaired and the holding that retroactive repayment has a rational basis.

A Contract Clause challenge requires an identified contractual obligation that the challenged law impairs; employment regulation does not become an immutable contractual term merely because it governs the relationship. Michigan’s repayment law impairs no offset promise or protected contractual enforcement rule in these employers’ agreements, so substantial-impairment balancing is unnecessary.

Justice O’Connor’s unanimous opinion controls this threshold holding; all nine Justices adopt it.

The Court holds that the employers have identified no contract right to retain the challenged offsets. Energy Reserves requires a contractual obligation before a court asks how substantially legislation impaired it, and Anderson requires this Court to examine that threshold independently while respecting the state court’s assessment. The agreements predated the coordination law and contained no promise of the later offset. The employers’ alternative argument—that all governing law entered their contracts—would freeze workplace regulation whenever parties contracted. United States Trust protects the legal force and enforceability of actual obligations; it does not convert every statutory payment rule into a promise against legislative revision. Nor has Michigan been shown to have supplied a rule at contracting that constitutionally closed completed payment periods to adjustment. The repayment law leaves the bargained obligations and their enforcement intact. Because the necessary contractual predicate is missing, the Court neither balances a substantial impairment against public necessity nor denies that a genuinely impaired employment contract could receive constitutional protection.

The Court applies Energy Reserves Group v. Kansas Power & Light, 459 U.S. 400: identify the contractual obligation before measuring impairment. The Court distinguishes Allied Structural Steel v. Spannaus, 438 U.S. 234: the identified contractual pension obligations there do not create an offset promise here. The Court applies Anderson v. Brand, 303 U.S. 95: federal review independently determines whether the asserted contractual obligation exists. The Court applies United States Trust Co. v. New Jersey, 431 U.S. 1, within its stated boundary: contractual enforceability is protected, but every workplace regulation is not an immutable contract term. Home Building & Loan Association v. Blaisdell, 290 U.S. 398, retains its force: law may inform actual contractual obligations without freezing all public regulation. The Court distinguishes Von Hoffman v. City of Quincy, 4 Wall. 535: withdrawal of contractual enforcement support is absent. The Court distinguishes McCracken v. Hayward, 2 How. 608: this law does not remove a remedy enforcing the employers’ contracts.

Retroactive economic legislation satisfies due process when its retroactive operation rationally advances a legitimate legislative purpose. Requiring repayment of these offsets rationally restores Michigan’s compensation compromise and equalizes affected employers, notwithstanding their reliance on the earlier coordination rule.

Justice O’Connor’s unanimous opinion also adopts this independent ground rejecting the due-process challenge.

The Court holds that Michigan could rationally restore the compensation arrangement through repayment of the disputed offsets. R.A. Gray requires a justification for retroactive operation, rather than assuming that a permissible prospective rule automatically validates a retrospective one. That justification exists here: the offsets disrupted the balance struck in the compensation legislation and created different treatment depending on whether an employer had withheld benefits for earlier injuries. Requiring repayment restores that balance and removes the disparity. Turner Elkhorn establishes that economic reliance does not itself prevent a legislature from adjusting burdens connected with prior conduct, and Sperry confirms the legitimacy of allocating comparable financial burdens among affected parties. The employers’ substantial repayment liability therefore does not show irrationality merely because the law unsettles their prior calculations or follows a favorable judicial interpretation. The holding concerns this restoration and equalization measure. It supplies no general immunity for retroactive legislation and no rule that reliance or the scope of a retrospective burden can never matter.

The Court applies Pension Benefit Guaranty Corp. v. R.A. Gray & Co., 467 U.S. 717: retroactivity itself must rationally serve a legitimate purpose. The Court applies Usery v. Turner Elkhorn Mining Co., 428 U.S. 1: reasonable retrospective economic burdens can survive reliance objections. The Court applies United States v. Sperry Corp., 493 U.S. 52: rational allocation of comparable financial burdens is legitimate.

Effective March 9, existing contract-threshold and rational-retroactivity doctrine is applied without changing its tests. The employers have no protected offset obligation on these agreements; the repayment law is enforceable. No substantial-impairment balancing rule is added and no categorical validation of retrospective legislation follows.

Supreme Court review ends with affirmance. Michigan’s repayment obligation may be enforced on its existing terms; the Court orders no new benefit award, additional remedy, contract modification, or retained supervision.

**End of entry: General Motors Corp. v. Romein, Merits decision, March 9, 1992.**

### Williams v. United States, No. 90-6297

Merits decision, March 9, 1992

Argued November 6, 1991. [On writ of certiorari to the Seventh Circuit, 910 F.2d 1574](https://static.case.law/f2d/910/html/1574-01.html).

Question presented: When may an appellate court affirm a sentence above the Guidelines range—an upward departure—resting on both permissible and impermissible grounds under 18 U.S.C. §3742(f)?

The district court imposed 27 months, departing from an 18–24 month range on grounds including old convictions and a record of arrests. The circuit recognized that the arrest record was an improper departure basis but affirmed because it regarded the departure as reasonable on other grounds. References to prior same-offense conduct and threats explained selection of the sentence after departure; the Court does not relabel them as independently established departure grounds. Williams seeks correction of the mixed-ground sentence. Whether old nonsimilar convictions can support departure is not clearly presented or briefed, and remains undecided. The convictions are not at issue. [Record materials, 503 U.S. 193](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-503/pdf/USREPORTS-503-193.pdf).

The Court vacates the judgment affirming the sentence and remands to the Seventh Circuit, 7–2. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Souter and Thomas support vacatur and remand; Justices White and Kennedy dissent. Justice O’Connor delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Souter and Thomas, on the invalid departure factor, causal harmlessness and its burden sequence, and the separate review of departure reasonableness. The convictions remain intact.

A departure based on a factor that the Commission has forbidden, or has already fully considered in the applicable range, rests on an incorrect application of the Guidelines under §3742(f)(1). A policy statement expressly prohibiting a sentencing action is authoritative guidance in applying the relevant Guideline, and an arrest record itself is not a permissible departure ground here.

Justice O’Connor’s opinion, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Souter and Thomas, controls this rule.

The Court holds that choosing a departure ground is part of applying the Guidelines. Section 3553(b) limits departure to circumstances not adequately considered by the Commission, and the relevant policy statement expressly excludes an arrest record itself. Treating that prohibition as irrelevant because the sentence departs from the range would remove the very limitation that makes the departure lawful. Section 3742(f)(1) therefore reaches reliance on the invalid factor even if the numerical range was calculated correctly. The competing reading would place every departure solely within reasonableness review under paragraph (2); that approach fails to give effect to the distinct command governing incorrect application. This conclusion does not make every policy statement binding for every purpose. It concerns an express prohibition that guides application of the relevant sentencing provision. Nor does the Court decide whether nonsimilar old convictions may lawfully support a departure: that separate issue was not sufficiently presented for decision in this case.

No earlier Supreme Court decision is overruled; this is a construction of §§3553(b), 3742(f)(1) and the applicable §4A1.3 prohibition. Griffin v. United States, December 3, 1991, remains distinct: unsupported facts under a valid legal theory do not establish the rule for a prohibited sentencing factor.

Once the challenger establishes reliance on an invalid departure factor, remand is required unless the reviewing court determines from the whole record that the district court would have imposed the same sentence without that factor. The challenger need not additionally prove that the error determined the sentence; the party defending the sentence must persuade the reviewing court that the error was harmless.

The same seven-Justice opinion adopts this causal standard under §3742(f)(1) and Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(a).

The Court holds that an unlawful factor does not automatically require resentencing, but an appellate court must establish that the error made no difference to the sentence actually chosen. Rule 52(a) permits disregard of an error that affects no substantial right, and §3742(f)(1)’s causal language is consistent with that principle. The challenger first identifies the sentencing court’s reliance on a forbidden factor. Williams has met that burden. Requiring him also to reconstruct the sentencing judge’s unspoken counterfactual would improperly place the consequence of an ambiguous record on the party who established the error. The party defending the sentence must instead persuade the reviewing court, from the whole record, that the judge would have selected the same sentence without it. A conclusion that the judge could lawfully have selected that sentence is insufficient. This statutory error does not acquire Chapman’s constitutional proof standard, and the Court leaves the actual harmlessness determination to the circuit on remand.

The Court applies Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(a) as the governing harmless-error rule for this nonconstitutional sentencing error. The Court does not import the standard of Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18: the Court adopts no beyond-reasonable-doubt burden for this statutory error.

Even when an invalid departure ground was harmless under §3742(f)(1), a reviewing court must separately determine under §3742(f)(2) that the departure’s extent is reasonable in light of the valid grounds actually identified by the sentencing court. A reasonable sentence that the district court could have imposed does not establish that the erroneous factor was harmless.

The same seven-Justice opinion makes both review requirements conditions of affirmance.

The Court holds that effect and reasonableness answer different questions. The first asks whether the error influenced the sentence selected; the second asks whether the lawful reasons justify the extent of departure. Section 3742 preserves both inquiries, so satisfaction of one cannot substitute for the other. The circuit found the arrest factor improper but treated the remaining reasons and a reasonable result as sufficient to affirm. That disposition did not resolve whether this judge would have chosen 27 months without the prohibited factor. Nor may an appellate court invent new departure grounds from comments made in choosing a sentence after departure. Solem recognizes the sentencing court’s primary role, and the statutory scheme assigns correction of an improper sentence through remand rather than appellate selection of a new one. The circuit must therefore perform both inquiries on the actual record. The Court decides neither the ultimate reasonable magnitude nor the unpresented question concerning old nonsimilar convictions.

The Court applies Solem v. Helm, 463 U.S. 277, for the primary sentencing role of the trial court, not to add a proportionality issue.

Effective March 9, §3742(f) now expressly requires separate causal harmlessness and departure-reasonableness review. The changed components are the effect-of-error inquiry, burden sequence after demonstrated reliance, and the relationship between paragraphs (1) and (2). The valid-ground requirement and the district court’s sentencing role remain. No constitutional sentencing-fact rule or blanket policy-statement rule is added.

Justice White, joined by Justice Kennedy, dissents. He reads the specific departure-review provision as controlling this sentence: once the departure is justified by sufficient lawful reasons and is reasonable, affirmance is permissible. He disputes the majority’s use of §3742(f)(1), not the prohibition on using an arrest record itself. The Court requires causal harmlessness in addition to reasonableness.

Supreme Court sentence review ends with remand to the Seventh Circuit. That court must decide whether the same sentence would have been imposed and whether the departure is independently reasonable; if either required condition fails, it must remand for resentencing. No new sentence or automatic district-court hearing is imposed now.

**End of entry: Williams v. United States, Merits decision, March 9, 1992.**

### Stringer v. Black, No. 90-6616

Merits decision, March 9, 1992

Argued December 9, 1991. [On writ of certiorari to the Fifth Circuit, 909 F.2d 111](https://static.case.law/f2d/909/html/0111-01.html).

Question presented: Whether Teague bars consideration of the vague-aggravator claim because Maynard or the requested application to Mississippi’s weighing system announced a new rule after Stringer’s sentence became final.

Stringer’s death sentence became final on February 19, 1985. The jury found three aggravating circumstances, including an undefined especially heinous, atrocious or cruel factor, in Mississippi’s system requiring aggravators to be weighed against mitigation. The state appellate court upheld the sentence. The federal district court denied habeas relief both on procedural-default and merits grounds. Following an earlier remand for consideration in light of Clemons, the Fifth Circuit again denied relief on Teague new-rule grounds without deciding the procedural bar. Stringer seeks reversal of that retroactivity ruling. The State invokes finality and the asserted distinction between weighing and nonweighing systems. Whether adequate state correction actually occurred and whether procedural default prevents relief remain for further proceedings. [Record materials, 503 U.S. 222](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-503/pdf/USREPORTS-503-222.pdf).

The Court reverses the Fifth Circuit’s judgment and remands, 6–3. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor and Kennedy support reversal; Justices Scalia, Souter and Thomas dissent. Justice Kennedy delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens and O’Connor. That opinion controls both the determination that the claim invokes an existing rule and the requirement of genuine reweighing or constitutional harmless-error review in this weighing system.

For a sentence final on February 19, 1985, Godfrey already prohibited the unguided heinousness aggravation later addressed in Maynard, and applying that protection to Mississippi’s weighing system does not create a new rule. Teague therefore does not bar consideration of this claim on the ground that Maynard or the corrective principles discussed in Clemons postdated finality.

Justice Kennedy’s opinion, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens and O’Connor, controls this determination.

The Court holds that Teague protects reasonable reliance on existing law, not a refusal to recognize what that law already required. Butler requires attention both to the rule announced by a later decision and to whether its proposed application extends the precedent in a new way. Godfrey had already rejected an aggravating phrase that gave the sentencer no meaningful guidance. Maynard applied that same prohibition to closely equivalent language. Mississippi’s formal weighing of aggravation against mitigation makes the defect consequential to sentence selection; it does not exempt the system from the preexisting constitutional protection. Zant expressly distinguished its nonweighing arrangement and supplies no affirmative permission to weigh an invalid factor. The State’s finality argument therefore does not make this particular application novel. Clemons identifies lawful methods of correcting the error rather than first creating the right to challenge it. The Court does not hold that every later capital decision applies retroactively, and it decides no Teague exception or unresolved procedural default.

The Court applies the existing rule of Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S. 288: the claim seeks an old rule at the relevant finality date. The Court applies Butler v. McKellar, 494 U.S. 407: both novelty of the later rule and novelty of its application must be considered. The Court applies Godfrey v. Georgia, 446 U.S. 420: meaningful guidance was already required for the heinousness aggravator. Maynard v. Cartwright, 486 U.S. 356, applies Godfrey’s existing rule to equivalent language. The Court applies Clemons v. Mississippi, 494 U.S. 738, for its permissible methods of correction; Clemons did not create a new protection in this setting.

When a capital sentencer weighs a constitutionally invalid aggravating factor against mitigation, remaining valid aggravators do not by themselves cure the error. The state judicial system must supply genuine reweighing without the invalid factor, where authorized, or constitutional harmless-error review establishing that the invalid factor did not affect the sentence; the adequacy of any cure here remains for further proceedings.

Justice Kennedy’s six-Justice opinion also controls the weighing and correction rule. Its application and any eventual conditional remedy remain reserved.

The Court holds that an invalid factor placed on the statutory scale cannot be ignored simply by counting the valid factors that remain. Zant sustained a system in which the invalid factor did not do the formal weighing work present here. Barclay also involved actual review of the sentencing choice and a different kind of invalidity; it does not permit automatic affirmance of this constitutional weighing error. Clemons recognizes that a properly authorized state appellate court can itself weigh valid aggravation against mitigation, or can determine under constitutional harmless-error principles that the error did not affect the sentence. Chapman places the harmlessness burden on the State beyond a reasonable doubt. Bare proportionality review or an assertion that other aggravators exist does not establish either correction. The Court leaves the actual state review and the unresolved procedural-default defense to the lower courts. If habeas relief becomes warranted after those matters are resolved, a conditional remedy may permit lawful state correction; death eligibility is not automatically negated.

The Court distinguishes Zant v. Stephens, 462 U.S. 862: its nonweighing holding does not automatically cure a weighing-system error. The Court distinguishes Barclay v. Florida, 463 U.S. 939: its actual review and state-law invalidity do not justify uncorrected constitutional aggravation. The Court applies Clemons v. Mississippi, 494 U.S. 738: genuine appellate reweighing or constitutional harmlessness can correct a weighing error. The Court applies Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18, to the harmless-error route: the State must establish harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt. The Court distinguishes Lowenfield v. Phelps, 484 U.S. 231: duplication in narrowing does not answer vague-factor weighting and cannot supply a prefinality reliance premise.

Effective March 9, the Court applies Teague to this specific Mississippi claim and makes the weighing/nonweighing remedial distinction controlling at the stated scope. No universal retroactivity exception or automatic cure from surviving aggravators is added. Genuine reweighing and constitutional harmlessness remain different routes. Actual cure, procedural default and any eventual conditional-writ terms are unresolved.

Souter, joined by Scalia and Thomas, dissents because Zant reserved the weighing question and Barclay did not clearly require the precise constitutional correction now demanded. In his view, reasonable jurists at finality could distinguish constitutionally protected conduct from a vague aggravator and regard the Mississippi application as unsettled. The majority holds that the statutory weighing role strengthens the old guidance requirement rather than making it new.

Supreme Court review ends with remand to the Fifth Circuit. The preserved procedural-default defense remains undecided; the lower courts must consider it and any adequate state correction. If relief is otherwise warranted, a conditional writ may permit resentencing or authorized appellate cure. No unconditional writ, immediate release, automatic life sentence or finding that a particular state cure failed is made.

**End of entry: Stringer v. Black, Merits decision, March 9, 1992.**

### Connecticut National Bank v. Germain, No. 90-1791

Merits decision, March 9, 1992

Argued January 21, 1992. [On writ of certiorari to the Second Circuit, 926 F.2d 191](https://static.case.law/f2d/926/html/0191-01.html).

Question presented: Whether 28 U.S.C. §158(d) excludes otherwise available §1292 review of a district court order entered in its bankruptcy appellate capacity.

A bankruptcy trustee sued Connecticut National Bank and demanded a jury trial. The bankruptcy court refused to strike that demand; the district court permitted an appeal, affirmed, and certified a question for review before final judgment, or interlocutory review. The bank sought circuit permission under §1292(b). The Second Circuit dismissed for lack of jurisdiction, reasoning that §158(d)’s final-order provision implicitly foreclosed interlocutory review. The bank seeks reversal of that jurisdictional dismissal. No jury entitlement or final satisfaction of the discretionary appeal requirements is before this Court for decision. [Record materials, 503 U.S. 249](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-503/pdf/USREPORTS-503-249.pdf).

The Court reverses the jurisdictional dismissal and remands, 9–0 in judgment. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas support reversal of the categorical §158(d) bar. Justice Thomas delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Scalia, Kennedy and Souter. Those five Justices adopt its explanation that §§158 and 1292 coexist under their enacted terms. Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens and O’Connor concur in the judgment on the separate grounds described below.

Section 158(d) does not preclude court-of-appeals review under §1292 of a district court’s order entered in its bankruptcy appellate capacity. Review remains subject to the particular §1292 route’s requirements, including certification and circuit permission where §1292(b) applies; the provision does not grant an automatic appeal.

Justice Thomas’s opinion, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Scalia, Kennedy and Souter, supplies the controlling rationale. Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens and O’Connor agree in judgment on their separately stated reasoning.

The Court holds that an affirmative grant of jurisdiction over final bankruptcy decisions does not silently remove another statute’s grant over specified interlocutory district-court orders. Section 1292 speaks of district courts without distinguishing their original and appellate capacities. Section 158(d) contains no contrary command. Wood requires that both statutes operate where they do not positively conflict, and Ron Pair and Rubin direct attention to the enacted terms. Some overlap with final-order jurisdiction under §1291 does not justify creating an exclusion Congress did not write; the statutes also have different reach, including the bankruptcy appellate panels covered by §158. The bank’s proposed review must therefore be considered under the express interlocutory statute. This does not expand jurisdiction for convenience or dispense with certification, timely application or discretionary acceptance. It also does not extend district-court language to bankruptcy appellate panels without statutory authority. The Court decides the categorical jurisdictional objection only, leaving the jury question and remaining prerequisites for further proceedings.

The Court applies Wood v. United States, 16 Pet. 342: overlapping statutes remain effective absent positive conflict. The Court applies United States v. Ron Pair Enterprises, 489 U.S. 235: the enacted bankruptcy-related text governs. The Court applies Rubin v. United States, 449 U.S. 424: unambiguous statutory words control the inquiry. The Court applies United States v. Goldenberg, 168 U.S. 95: courts give effect to legislative text rather than assumed omissions.

Effective March 9, the Court rejects an implied categorical §158(d) exclusion of §1292 review for district-court bankruptcy appellate orders. The text/coexistence explanation has five votes, while the reversing judgment has nine. Certification, timeliness, circuit discretion and subject-matter limits remain; no jury-trial or bankruptcy-appellate-panel holding is added.

Stevens, writing alone, concurs in the judgment because both statutory language and legislative history support the available route. O’Connor, joined by White and Blackmun, accepts that redundancy is a real issue but finds the historical statutory structure insufficient to show withdrawal of interlocutory review. These four Justices agree with reversal without joining Thomas’s complete textual methodology.

Supreme Court review of the jurisdictional dismissal ends. The case returns to the Second Circuit to consider §1292’s applicable requirements and whether to permit the appeal. The underlying jury dispute remains undecided here.

### United States v. R. L. C., No. 90-1577

Merits decision, March 24, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 10, 1991. [On writ of certiorari to the Eighth Circuit, 915 F.2d 320](https://static.case.law/f2d/915/html/0320-01.html).

Question presented: Whether the juvenile-detention ceiling in 18 U.S.C. §5037(c)(1)(B) incorporates the maximum Guidelines sentence authorized for the comparable adult offender and offense.

At age 16, R.L.C. caused a child’s death while driving recklessly and intoxicated in Indian country. After a bench adjudication the district court found juvenile delinquency corresponding to involuntary manslaughter and imposed three years, the offense statute’s adult maximum. The Eighth Circuit held that §5037’s adult comparison incorporates the Guidelines; the agreed comparable adult range was 15–21 months. It vacated and remanded. Without a Government stay, the district court subsequently imposed 18 months, which R.L.C. has served. The Government seeks restoration of the original statutory-maximum approach, so possible renewed detention prevents mootness. No specific additional departure basis is established by this record for present decision. [Record materials, 503 U.S. 291](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-503/pdf/USREPORTS-503-291.pdf).

#### Judgment

The Eighth Circuit’s judgment is affirmed, 7–2. The adult Guidelines ceiling governs the statutory comparison.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Guidelines-based juvenile ceiling and circuit judgment | Affirmed | 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Blackmun, O’Connor | The circuit’s disposition stands; its remand has already produced an 18-month term. The Court creates no new sentencing hearing or additional detention. A higher adult ceiling requires a lawful departure basis. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The juvenile ceiling incorporates the lawful adult Guidelines maximum

**Controlling proposition:** For juvenile detention governed by §5037(c)(1)(B), the maximum authorized adult sentence includes the applicable Guidelines ceiling for the comparable offender and offense, subject to a properly supported lawful departure. The Guidelines determine that upper limit only; they do not impose a Guidelines minimum or otherwise require plenary adult Guidelines sentencing for the juvenile.

**Authority:** Parts I, II-A and III of Souter’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; seven Justices. The legislative-history and competing lenity explanations do not command five votes.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a sentence is authorized only if it complies with statutes that limit the sentencing judge as well as those that define the offense. Section 3553(b)’s command to apply the Guidelines is itself statutory. The Government’s focus on the three-year offense maximum therefore does not establish that an adult court could impose that term despite the applicable Guidelines ceiling. Section 5037 compares this juvenile with an adult sentenced in comparable circumstances, which requires identifying the proper adult range. Here that range is 15–21 months, absent a supported lawful departure. This conclusion does not make the adult Guidelines govern juvenile disposition in full. The juvenile court retains its discretion below the applicable cap and remains subject to the other statutory limitations. The dissent’s concern for rehabilitation does not remove the enacted upper limit, while calculating the limit does not require adoption of every adult sentencing rule. No particular additional departure ground is approved and no common methodological rule for lenity is announced.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Villamonte-Marquez, 462 U.S. 579 — applied to the threshold controversy: possible restoration of the longer detention keeps the case live.
- Williams v. United States, March 9, 1992 — preserved in its relationship to existing law: only lawful departure grounds can support a higher adult ceiling; this case approves none.
- Moskal v. United States, 498 U.S. 103 — not overruled or revised by a majority; the competing lenity explanations remain noncontrolling.
- United States v. Bass, 404 U.S. 336 — remains an authority on lenity; no common new timing rule is adopted here.
- Bifulco v. United States, 447 U.S. 381 — remains an authority on lenity; no common new timing rule is adopted here.

#### Current-Law Effect

Effective March 24, §5037(c)(1)(B) has a controlling adult-Guidelines upper-limit construction. A lawful departure can affect that ceiling, but none is established here; the adult Guidelines minimum does not become a juvenile floor. Seven join the Court’s text/result sections; Souter’s history/lenity sections have three votes. Scalia’s restriction on legislative history and Thomas’s qualification remain noncontrolling. The seven-Justice ceiling rests on a majority opinion, so no analysis under Marks to extract a rule from the separate opinions is needed. Those separate writings supply no common majority method.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Souter, joined by White and Stevens, additionally relies on statutory development and legislative history and regards lenity as a last resort after that inquiry. Scalia, joined by Kennedy and Thomas, would not use legislative history to resolve textual punishment ambiguity against the accused. Thomas separately stresses that established legal rules of construction precede lenity. Stone-Zsela relies on text and statutory context, uses residual lenity only to confirm narrower punishment, and preserves juvenile discretion below the ceiling. O’Connor, joined by Blackmun, dissents: in her view, the offense-statute maximum preserves the separate rehabilitative scheme, and calculating an adult range imports procedures Congress did not require. The Court adopts the cap without resolving those methodological disagreements.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court review ends in affirmance of the Eighth Circuit. The district court’s already-entered 18-month disposition is not replaced by a new remand order. No additional detention or new departure finding is made. The Government’s effort to restore the three-year approach fails.

### Nationwide Mutual Insurance Co. v. Darden, No. 90-1802

Merits decision, March 24, 1992

Argued January 21, 1992. [On writ of certiorari to the Fourth Circuit, 922 F.2d 203](https://static.case.law/f2d/922/html/0203-01.html).

Question presented: Whether the Employee Retirement Income Security Act (ERISA) definition of employee incorporates general common-law agency principles or the circuit’s benefits-expectation, reliance and bargaining-power test.

Darden sold Nationwide insurance under agreements providing retirement benefits and forfeiture conditions tied to post-termination competition. After termination he sold competitors’ policies, and Nationwide denied benefits. The district court initially regarded him as an independent contractor. The Fourth Circuit instead required reasonable benefit expectations, reliance and insufficient bargaining power; on remand Darden prevailed under that test and the circuit affirmed. Nationwide seeks application of the common-law employee category. The United States supports reversal but proposed some remedial modification of the common-law approach. The circuit did not finally decide Darden’s status under the proper agency test. The denied cross-petition concerning the Extended Earnings Plan is outside review. [Record materials, 503 U.S. 318](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-503/pdf/USREPORTS-503-318.pdf).

The Court reverses the Fourth Circuit’s judgment and remands, 9–0, for application of general common-law agency principles. Justice Souter delivers the unanimous Opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. All nine support reversal and remand and join the common-law test, its complete inquiry into all relevant factors, and rejection of the circuit’s expectations test.

ERISA’s circular definition of employee incorporates the general common law of agency, not a particular State’s law or a substitute test of benefit expectations, reliance and bargaining power. Courts must weigh all incidents of the relationship—including control over manner and means, skill, tools, work location, duration, assignment rights, scheduling discretion, payment, assistants, regular-business integration, the hiring party’s business, benefits and tax treatment—with no single factor decisive.

Justice Souter’s unanimous opinion controls both the statutory definition and the requirement to consider all relevant factors.

The Court holds that an expectation of retirement benefits cannot itself establish membership in the employee category Congress protected. Reid presumes that a statutory employee term carries its settled agency meaning unless Congress directs otherwise. ERISA’s circular definition supplies no such direction, and its general protective purpose does not identify a different class. United Insurance requires weighing all incidents of the relationship; a contractual label or one favorable factor does not settle the matter. Hearst and Silk do not justify replacing this inquiry with remedial purpose, particularly after Congress’s contrary responses to their interpretations. Rutherford Food remains different because the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) expressly reaches work suffered or permitted, language ERISA lacks. The circuit’s expectations test also assumes the disputed statutory entitlement when it disregards the agreed forfeiture condition, and its reliance prong can treat identical jobs differently according to personal savings choices. The proper inquiry concerns the employment relationship. Darden’s ultimate classification and any resulting vesting or forfeiture consequence remain for remand.

The Court applies Community for Creative Non-Violence v. Reid, 490 U.S. 730, to ERISA: general common-law agency is the default meaning of an unhelpfully defined employee term. The Court applies NLRB v. United Insurance Co., 390 U.S. 254: all incidents must be assessed with no single decisive factor. The Court limits NLRB v. Hearst Publications, 322 U.S. 111, as a general interpretive model: purpose alone does not displace agency meaning in ERISA. The Court does not follow the general purposive emphasis of United States v. Silk, 331 U.S. 704, for undefined employee terms; Reid supplies the governing presumption. The Court distinguishes and preserves Rutherford Food Corp. v. McComb, 331 U.S. 722: FLSA’s broader express definition supports its different reach. Kelley v. Southern Pacific, 419 U.S. 318, remains a supporting application of ordinary agency meaning. Baker v. Texas & Pacific, 359 U.S. 227, and Robinson v. Baltimore & Ohio, 237 U.S. 84, retain the same supporting force.

Effective March 24, Reid’s presumption governs ERISA’s employee definition, with the complete multifactor agency inquiry and no decisive single factor. The circuit’s expectations/reliance/bargaining-power substitute is rejected. FLSA’s broader statutory category remains distinct; Darden’s status, available benefits and independent state-contract remedies are not decided.

Supreme Court review ends with remand to the Fourth Circuit for the proper classification inquiry and further proceedings consistent with that result. Neither Darden’s employee status nor an entitlement to benefits is finally determined. The excluded cross-petition issue is not reopened.

**End of entry: Nationwide Mutual Insurance Co. v. Darden, Merits decision, March 24, 1992.**

### United States v. Wilson, No. 90-1745

Merits decision, March 24, 1992

Argued January 15, 1992. [On writ of certiorari to the Sixth Circuit, 916 F.2d 1115](https://static.case.law/f2d/916/html/1115-01.html).

Question presented: Whether the sentencing court or the Attorney General through the Bureau of Prisons (BOP) initially computes presentence detention credit under §3585(b), and at what stage.

Tennessee detained Wilson beginning October 5, 1988. The federal district court imposed a 96-month Hobbs Act sentence on November 29, 1989 and refused the requested state-custody credit. On December 12 the state court imposed its sentence, credited 429 days, and transferred Wilson to federal custody, when his federal sentence began. The Sixth Circuit held that the federal sentencing court should have awarded credit. The Government seeks reversal; Wilson relies on Congress’s deletion of the Attorney General’s name from former §3568 and the federal sentence’s earlier date. The Court reviews actor and timing, not a separate challenge to a completed BOP calculation. [Record materials, 503 U.S. 329](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-503/pdf/USREPORTS-503-329.pdf).

The Court reverses the Sixth Circuit’s direction to award detention credit at sentencing, 7–2. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas support reversal; Justices White and Stevens dissent. Justice Thomas delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter. They join its rulings on the responsible official, timing, statutory revision and prohibition against counting the same detention credit twice.

Section 3585(b) does not authorize a district court to compute detention credit at sentencing; the Attorney General through BOP must make the initial computation after the federal sentence commences. Administrative review and proper subsequent judicial review remain available, so the calculation is not unreviewable discretion.

Justice Thomas’s opinion, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter, controls this rule.

The Court holds that the credit calculation belongs to administration of the sentence after it begins. Section 3585(b) looks to detention already spent before commencement and to credit already given against another sentence. Otte and Gwaltney show why the verbs’ temporal meaning matters: at sentencing, the court may not know when detention will end or when another sentence will receive credit. Section 3621 assigns administration of the federal term to BOP, which must determine the remaining time and therefore must compute the statutory credit. Congress’s deletion of the Attorney General’s name during a complete rewrite does not affirmatively transfer this task to sentencing judges. Russello’s inference from changed wording is overcome by the present text and structure; Robertson cautions against assuming an unexplained abandonment of established administration. The contrary approach would make the initial decision depend on incomplete information. Prisoners retain administrative review and proper judicial review; neither a new credit award nor an unreviewable executive power follows.

The Court applies Otte v. United States, 419 U.S. 43: Congress’s choice of verb tense informs statutory timing. The Court applies Gwaltney of Smithfield v. Chesapeake Bay Foundation, 484 U.S. 49, for the same principle that verb tense informs statutory timing. The Court treats the inference from changed wording in Russello v. United States, 464 U.S. 16, as rebuttable; the rewritten text and administration scheme overcome it here. The Court applies Robertson v. Railroad Labor Board, 268 U.S. 619: a departure from long-established procedure is not lightly inferred. The Court distinguishes McCarthy v. Madigan, March 4, 1992: its damages-only process could not provide the requested relief, unlike a credit computation process.

Section 3585(b) forbids federal detention credit for time already credited against another sentence when the federal sentence begins. A defendant cannot avoid that condition merely because the federal sentencing hearing occurred before the other court awarded its credit.

The same seven-Justice opinion adopts the prohibition on double credit as a ground supporting its disposition on responsibility and timing.

The Court holds that the sequence of sentencing hearings does not create a right to count the same detention twice. Section 3585(b) expressly excludes time credited against another sentence, and its commencement-based calculation gives that condition practical effect. Wilson’s federal hearing occurred before Tennessee awarded its 429 days, but the award preceded his transfer and the start of the federal term. Measuring eligibility only at the earlier hearing would make the federal credit depend on the happenstance of which court sentenced first. Turkette’s instruction to avoid absurd statutory results supports rejecting that arbitrary consequence. The revised provision also broadens some eligible detention and changes other language, so preserving administrative calculation does not render the revision meaningless. This Court does not calculate a fresh numerical credit, define every form of official detention, or decide a later challenge to BOP’s application. It holds only that the established state credit cannot be disregarded on the timing theory accepted below.

The Court applies United States v. Turkette, 452 U.S. 576: avoid an arbitrary statutory result unsupported by the text’s purpose.

Effective March 24, §3585(b)’s initial actor and timing are settled: BOP computes after commencement, applying the prohibition on credit already used against another sentence. The changed wording does not transfer initial calculation to the sentencing judge. Proper review remains, and McCarthy creates no exemption for this relief-capable administrative process. No particular BOP calculation or universal definition of official detention is adjudicated.

Justice Stevens, joined by Justice White, dissents. He would permit the sentencing court to resolve a ripe credit entitlement when counsel and the record are available, leaving ministerial arithmetic or later adjustments to the appropriate official. He reads the passive wording and placement in the sentencing chapter as allowing shared responsibility and rejects the premise that occasional incomplete information bars every sentencing-stage determination. The Court instead adopts initial administrative computation after commencement. The dissent does not claim unreviewable executive discretion or discard the statutory no-double-credit language.

Supreme Court review ends in reversal of the circuit’s direction to award credit at sentencing. BOP remains responsible for the initial calculation after commencement, with administrative and judicial review through proper channels. The Court awards no days of credit and makes no new detention order.

**End of entry: United States v. Wilson, Merits decision, March 24, 1992.**
