#### Dawson v. Delaware — 1992-03-09

**Record:** `records/Dawson_v_Delaware_merits_1992-03-09.md`.

##### Protected association must bear on a legitimate sentencing issue

**Holding and operative rule:** The State may not use protected association to aggravate a capital sentence when the evidence establishes only abstract beliefs and supplies no supported connection to the crime, future danger, another legitimate sentencing issue, or the mitigation actually offered. The stipulated association evidence here lacks that connection and its admission violates the First and Fourteenth Amendments.

**Authority:** Part A of White’s opinion; Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter join White at this level of generality.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an unpopular association does not become sentencing evidence merely because its beliefs are objectionable. NAACP and Bates protect association; Abel shows that those protections do not exclude evidence that actually establishes a witness’s bias. Barclay likewise involved demonstrated racial motivation for the crime. This stipulation supplies neither connection. Even assuming that the Delaware group shared racist beliefs, the State established no link between those beliefs and this murder, and no violent organizational objective attributable to Dawson. The evidence also does not contradict his actual mitigation concerning family kindness and participation in rehabilitation programs. Payne permits relevant rebuttal, not punishment for a belief labeled bad character. Zant’s distinction between legitimate aggravation and protected beliefs therefore controls the application. The Court announces no universal bar against evidence of association and no rule that a white victim always excludes racial motive. A materially different evidentiary record may establish relevance that this stipulation does not.

**Precedent treatment:**

- NAACP v. Alabama, 357 U.S. 449 — applied: freedom of association protects unpopular affiliations.
- Bates v. Little Rock, 361 U.S. 516 — applied: protected association cannot itself justify adverse governmental treatment.
- United States v. Abel, 469 U.S. 45 — distinguished on the facts: association established witness bias there; relevant association remains admissible.
- Barclay v. Florida, 463 U.S. 939 — distinguished: demonstrated racial motivation connected the association to the crime.
- Zant v. Stephens, 462 U.S. 862 — applied: protected beliefs cannot themselves supply an aggravating circumstance.
- Payne v. Tennessee, 501 U.S. 808 — applied with its relevance limit: rebuttal of mitigation does not make unrelated belief probative.
- Schware v. Board of Bar Examiners, 353 U.S. 232 — applied by analogy: an affiliation without a rational connection to the relevant character inquiry is insufficient.

##### Scope of the remand

**Holding and operative rule:** The state judgment sustaining this sentence cannot stand on the constitutional reasoning it adopted. On remand the state court may consider Delaware’s harmless-error contention; this Court decides neither whether harmless-error analysis is available for this violation nor whether the error was harmless.

**Authority:** Part B of White’s opinion, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter; seven Justices control the remand’s reserved scope.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that correction of the constitutional error requires vacating the judgment that sustained the sentence, while leaving the convictions intact. The relevance ruling answers the question reviewed here. It does not establish how the challenged evidence affected this jury’s sentencing decision, and it does not decide every condition under which constitutional evidentiary error can be harmless. Delaware advanced harmlessness as an alternative ground, but the state court did not decide that contention under the constitutional rule now stated. Chapman supplies a demanding burden when its form of harmless-error review applies; citing that framework does not decide its availability for this particular infringement. The proper course is to permit consideration of the issue on remand without prejudging either branch. Neither the existence of other aggravators nor the Court’s finding of irrelevance supplies an automatic harmlessness finding. Conversely, the Court does not now command a new sentencing hearing regardless of further lawful review.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18 — preserved: its constitutional harmless-error burden is not relaxed; applicability and satisfaction in this case remain undecided.

**Current operation and change:** Effective March 9, the constitutional relevance rule is applied to this stipulation; no categorical association-evidence ban is added. The remand’s seven-Justice reservation leaves both availability and application of harmless-error treatment open. Stone’s mandatory Chapman/new-sentencing sequence is not the Court’s instruction. No earlier simulated rule is displaced.

**Posture and limits:** Supreme Court review of the sentence ends; the case returns to Delaware for further proceedings. No resentencing date, harmlessness finding, stay, or retained Supreme Court supervision is created. The convictions remain intact.

#### General Motors Corp. v. Romein — 1992-03-09

**Record:** `records/General_Motors_Corp_v_Romein_merits_1992-03-09.md`.

##### No relevant contractual obligation was impaired

**Holding and operative rule:** A Contract Clause challenge requires an identified contractual obligation that the challenged law impairs; employment regulation does not become an immutable contractual term merely because it governs the relationship. Michigan’s repayment law impairs no offset promise or protected contractual enforcement rule in these employers’ agreements, so substantial-impairment balancing is unnecessary.

**Authority:** O’Connor’s unanimous opinion; all nine Justices adopt the threshold holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the employers have identified no contract right to retain the challenged offsets. Energy Reserves requires a contractual obligation before a court asks how substantially legislation impaired it, and Anderson requires this Court to examine that threshold independently while respecting the state court’s assessment. The agreements predated the coordination law and contained no promise of the later offset. The employers’ alternative argument—that all governing law entered their contracts—would freeze workplace regulation whenever parties contracted. United States Trust protects the legal force and enforceability of actual obligations; it does not convert every statutory payment rule into a promise against legislative revision. Nor has Michigan been shown to have supplied a rule at contracting that constitutionally closed completed payment periods to adjustment. The repayment law leaves the bargained obligations and their enforcement intact. Because the necessary contractual predicate is missing, the Court neither balances a substantial impairment against public necessity nor denies that a genuinely impaired employment contract could receive constitutional protection.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Energy Reserves Group v. Kansas Power & Light, 459 U.S. 400 — applied: identify the contractual obligation before measuring impairment.
- Allied Structural Steel v. Spannaus, 438 U.S. 234 — distinguished: the identified contractual pension obligations there do not create an offset promise here.
- Anderson v. Brand, 303 U.S. 95 — applied: federal review independently determines whether the asserted contractual obligation exists.
- United States Trust Co. v. New Jersey, 431 U.S. 1 — applied with its boundary: contractual enforceability is protected, but every workplace regulation is not an immutable contract term.
- Home Building & Loan Association v. Blaisdell, 290 U.S. 398 — preserved: law may inform actual contractual obligations without freezing all public regulation.
- Von Hoffman v. City of Quincy, 4 Wall. 535 — distinguished: withdrawal of contractual enforcement support is absent.
- McCracken v. Hayward, 2 How. 608 — distinguished: this law does not remove a remedy enforcing the employers’ contracts.

##### The retroactive repayment requirement has a rational basis

**Holding and operative rule:** Retroactive economic legislation satisfies due process when its retroactive operation rationally advances a legitimate legislative purpose. Requiring repayment of these offsets rationally restores Michigan’s compensation compromise and equalizes affected employers, notwithstanding their reliance on the earlier coordination rule.

**Authority:** O’Connor’s unanimous opinion; the due-process ground independently rejects the second constitutional challenge.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Michigan could rationally restore the compensation arrangement through repayment of the disputed offsets. R.A. Gray requires a justification for retroactive operation, rather than assuming that a permissible prospective rule automatically validates a retrospective one. That justification exists here: the offsets disrupted the balance struck in the compensation legislation and created different treatment depending on whether an employer had withheld benefits for earlier injuries. Requiring repayment restores that balance and removes the disparity. Turner Elkhorn establishes that economic reliance does not itself prevent a legislature from adjusting burdens connected with prior conduct, and Sperry confirms the legitimacy of allocating comparable financial burdens among affected parties. The employers’ substantial repayment liability therefore does not show irrationality merely because the law unsettles their prior calculations or follows a favorable judicial interpretation. The holding concerns this restoration and equalization measure. It supplies no general immunity for retroactive legislation and no rule that reliance or the scope of a retrospective burden can never matter.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Pension Benefit Guaranty Corp. v. R.A. Gray & Co., 467 U.S. 717 — applied: retroactivity itself must rationally serve a legitimate purpose.
- Usery v. Turner Elkhorn Mining Co., 428 U.S. 1 — applied: reasonable retrospective economic burdens can survive reliance objections.
- United States v. Sperry Corp., 493 U.S. 52 — applied: rational allocation of comparable financial burdens is legitimate.

**Current operation and change:** Effective March 9, existing contract-threshold and rational-retroactivity doctrine is applied without changing its tests. The employers have no protected offset obligation on these agreements; the repayment law is enforceable. No substantial-impairment balancing rule is added and no categorical validation of retrospective legislation follows.

**Posture and limits:** Supreme Court review ends with affirmance. Michigan’s repayment obligation may be enforced on its existing terms; the Court orders no additional award, contract modification, or retained supervision.

#### Williams v. United States — 1992-03-09

**Record:** `records/Williams_v_United_States_merits_1992-03-09.md`.

##### An impermissible departure ground is a Guidelines application error

**Holding and operative rule:** A departure based on a factor that the Commission has forbidden, or has already fully considered in the applicable range, rests on an incorrect application of the Guidelines under §3742(f)(1). A policy statement expressly prohibiting a sentencing action is authoritative guidance in applying the relevant Guideline, and an arrest record itself is not a permissible departure ground here.

**Authority:** O’Connor’s opinion, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Souter and Thomas; seven Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that choosing a departure ground is part of applying the Guidelines. Section 3553(b) limits departure to circumstances not adequately considered by the Commission, and the relevant policy statement expressly excludes an arrest record itself. Treating that prohibition as irrelevant because the sentence departs from the range would remove the very limitation that makes the departure lawful. Section 3742(f)(1) therefore reaches reliance on the invalid factor even if the numerical range was calculated correctly. The competing reading would place every departure solely within reasonableness review under paragraph (2); that approach fails to give effect to the distinct command governing incorrect application. This conclusion does not make every policy statement binding for every purpose. It concerns an express prohibition that guides application of the relevant sentencing provision. Nor does the Court decide whether nonsimilar old convictions may lawfully support a departure: that separate issue was not sufficiently presented for decision in this case.

**Precedent treatment:**

- No earlier Supreme Court decision is overruled; this is a construction of §§3553(b), 3742(f)(1) and the applicable §4A1.3 prohibition.
- Griffin v. United States, simulated December 3, 1991 — kept distinct: unsupported facts under a valid legal theory do not establish the rule for a prohibited sentencing factor.

##### Harmlessness requires the same sentence without the invalid factor

**Holding and operative rule:** Once the challenger establishes reliance on an invalid departure factor, remand is required unless the reviewing court determines from the whole record that the district court would have imposed the same sentence without that factor. The challenger need not additionally prove that the error determined the sentence; the party defending the sentence must persuade the reviewing court that the error was harmless.

**Authority:** O’Connor’s seven-Justice opinion; this is the controlling causal standard under §3742(f)(1) and Rule 52(a).

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an unlawful factor does not automatically require resentencing, but an appellate court must establish that the error made no difference to the sentence actually chosen. Rule 52(a) permits disregard of an error that affects no substantial right, and §3742(f)(1)’s causal language is consistent with that principle. The challenger first identifies the sentencing court’s reliance on a forbidden factor. Williams has met that burden. Requiring him also to reconstruct the sentencing judge’s unspoken counterfactual would improperly place the consequence of an ambiguous record on the party who established the error. The party defending the sentence must instead persuade the reviewing court, from the whole record, that the judge would have selected the same sentence without it. A conclusion that the judge could lawfully have selected that sentence is insufficient. This statutory error does not acquire Chapman’s constitutional proof standard, and the Court leaves the actual harmlessness determination to the circuit on remand.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(a) — applied as the governing harmless-error rule for this nonconstitutional sentencing error.
- Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18 — not imported: the Court adopts no beyond-reasonable-doubt burden for this statutory error.

##### Departure reasonableness remains a separate condition of affirmance

**Holding and operative rule:** Even when an invalid departure ground was harmless under §3742(f)(1), a reviewing court must separately determine under §3742(f)(2) that the departure’s extent is reasonable in light of the valid grounds actually identified by the sentencing court. A reasonable sentence that the district court could have imposed does not establish that the erroneous factor was harmless.

**Authority:** O’Connor’s seven-Justice opinion; both review requirements govern affirmance.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that effect and reasonableness answer different questions. The first asks whether the error influenced the sentence selected; the second asks whether the lawful reasons justify the extent of departure. Section 3742 preserves both inquiries, so satisfaction of one cannot substitute for the other. The circuit found the arrest factor improper but treated the remaining reasons and a reasonable result as sufficient to affirm. That disposition did not resolve whether this judge would have chosen 27 months without the prohibited factor. Nor may an appellate court invent new departure grounds from comments made in choosing a sentence after departure. Solem recognizes the sentencing court’s primary role, and the statutory scheme assigns correction of an improper sentence through remand rather than appellate selection of a new one. The circuit must therefore perform both inquiries on the actual record. The Court decides neither the ultimate reasonable magnitude nor the unpresented question concerning old nonsimilar convictions.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Solem v. Helm, 463 U.S. 277 — applied for the primary sentencing role of the trial court, not to add a proportionality issue.

**Current operation and change:** Effective March 9, §3742(f) now expressly requires separate causal harmlessness and departure-reasonableness review. The changed components are the effect-of-error inquiry, burden sequence after demonstrated reliance, and the relationship between paragraphs (1) and (2). The valid-ground requirement and the district court’s sentencing role remain. No constitutional sentencing-fact rule or blanket policy-statement rule is added.

**Posture and limits:** Supreme Court sentence review ends with remand to the Seventh Circuit. That court must decide whether the same sentence would have been imposed and whether the departure is independently reasonable; if either required condition fails, it must remand for resentencing. No new sentence or automatic district-court hearing is imposed now.

#### Stringer v. Black — 1992-03-09

**Record:** `records/Stringer_v_Black_merits_1992-03-09.md`.

##### This application is not a new rule under Teague

**Holding and operative rule:** For a sentence final on February 19, 1985, Godfrey already prohibited the unguided heinousness aggravation later addressed in Maynard, and applying that protection to Mississippi’s weighing system does not create a new rule. Teague therefore does not bar consideration of this claim on the ground that Maynard or the corrective principles discussed in Clemons postdated finality.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s opinion, joined by Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens and O’Connor; six Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Teague protects reasonable reliance on existing law, not a refusal to recognize what that law already required. Butler requires attention both to the rule announced by a later decision and to whether its proposed application extends the precedent in a new way. Godfrey had already rejected an aggravating phrase that gave the sentencer no meaningful guidance. Maynard applied that same prohibition to closely equivalent language. Mississippi’s formal weighing of aggravation against mitigation makes the defect consequential to sentence selection; it does not exempt the system from the preexisting constitutional protection. Zant expressly distinguished its nonweighing arrangement and supplies no affirmative permission to weigh an invalid factor. The State’s finality argument therefore does not make this particular application novel. Clemons identifies lawful methods of correcting the error rather than first creating the right to challenge it. The Court does not hold that every later capital decision applies retroactively, and it decides no Teague exception or unresolved procedural default.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S. 288 — applied, not replaced: the claim seeks an old rule at the relevant finality date.
- Butler v. McKellar, 494 U.S. 407 — applied: both novelty of the later rule and novelty of its application must be considered.
- Godfrey v. Georgia, 446 U.S. 420 — applied: meaningful guidance was already required for the heinousness aggravator.
- Maynard v. Cartwright, 486 U.S. 356 — treated as applying Godfrey’s old rule to equivalent language.
- Clemons v. Mississippi, 494 U.S. 738 — applied as identifying permissible correction, not a newly created protection in this setting.

##### A weighing error needs a constitutionally adequate cure

**Holding and operative rule:** When a capital sentencer weighs a constitutionally invalid aggravating factor against mitigation, remaining valid aggravators do not by themselves cure the error. The state judicial system must supply genuine reweighing without the invalid factor, where authorized, or constitutional harmless-error review establishing that the invalid factor did not affect the sentence; the adequacy of any cure here remains for further proceedings.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s six-Justice opinion; Stone joins the weighing/correction rule with his approved conditional remedy and reserved application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an invalid factor placed on the statutory scale cannot be ignored simply by counting the valid factors that remain. Zant sustained a system in which the invalid factor did not do the formal weighing work present here. Barclay also involved actual review of the sentencing choice and a different kind of invalidity; it does not permit automatic affirmance of this constitutional weighing error. Clemons recognizes that a properly authorized state appellate court can itself weigh valid aggravation against mitigation, or can determine under constitutional harmless-error principles that the error did not affect the sentence. Chapman places the harmlessness burden on the State beyond a reasonable doubt. Bare proportionality review or an assertion that other aggravators exist does not establish either correction. The Court leaves the actual state review and the unresolved procedural-default defense to the lower courts. If habeas relief becomes warranted after those matters are resolved, a conditional remedy may permit lawful state correction; death eligibility is not automatically negated.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Zant v. Stephens, 462 U.S. 862 — distinguished: its nonweighing holding does not automatically cure a weighing-system error.
- Barclay v. Florida, 463 U.S. 939 — distinguished: its actual review and state-law invalidity do not justify uncorrected constitutional aggravation.
- Clemons v. Mississippi, 494 U.S. 738 — applied: genuine appellate reweighing or constitutional harmlessness can correct a weighing error.
- Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18 — applied to the harmless-error route: the State must establish harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt.
- Lowenfield v. Phelps, 484 U.S. 231 — distinguished: duplication in narrowing does not answer vague-factor weighting and cannot supply a prefinality reliance premise.

**Current operation and change:** Effective March 9, the Court applies Teague to this specific Mississippi claim and makes the weighing/nonweighing remedial distinction controlling at the stated scope. No universal retroactivity exception or automatic cure from surviving aggravators is added. Genuine reweighing and constitutional harmlessness remain different routes. Actual cure, procedural default and any eventual conditional-writ terms are unresolved.

**Posture and limits:** Supreme Court review ends with remand to the Fifth Circuit. The preserved procedural-default defense remains undecided; the lower courts must consider it and any adequate state correction. If relief is otherwise warranted, a conditional writ may permit resentencing or authorized appellate cure. No immediate release, automatic life sentence or finding that a particular state cure failed is made.

##### Section 158(d) does not exclude eligible interlocutory review

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 158(d) does not preclude court-of-appeals review under §1292 of a district court’s order entered in its bankruptcy appellate capacity. **Current operation and change:** Effective March 9, the Court rejects an implied categorical §158(d) exclusion of §1292 review for district-court bankruptcy appellate orders. The text/coexistence explanation has five votes, while the reversing judgment has nine. Certification, timeliness, circuit discretion and subject-matter limits remain; no jury-trial or bankruptcy-appellate-panel holding is added.

#### Holmes v. Securities Investor Protection Corp. — 1992-03-24

Correction insertion: [Canonical Decision Record](records/Holmes_v_Securities_Investor_Protection_Corp_merits_1992-03-24.md). Both SIPC claim components are reversed and remanded, 9–0, with judgment for Holmes on the specified claims. This law becomes effective March 24 for later dates and does not enter the independent same-day decisions.

##### Civil RICO requires a sufficiently direct causal relation

**Holding and operative rule:** A plaintiff seeking damages under 18 U.S.C. §1964(c) must establish both factual causation and proximate cause, including some direct relation between the injury asserted and the alleged §1962 violation. Injury that merely follows harm to another person is insufficient on the causal chain presented here; directness is a contextual requirement informed by attribution, overlapping recovery and the availability of directly injured plaintiffs, not a mechanical rule resolving every third-party injury.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part II, joined by Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas; eight Justices adopt this rule. Scalia agrees that proximate cause is required but joins no part of the Court's opinion; his distinct reasoning is not added to the opinion's support.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that factual connection alone does not establish the right to recover under civil RICO. Congress used the injury and causation language of the Clayton Act. Agency Holding and Sedima identify that statutory relationship; Associated General Contractors explains the established causal limits attached to the borrowed language. Congress's adoption of those words carries their settled judicial meaning. A direct relation matters because distant injury makes damages harder to attribute to the violation, risks overlapping recoveries at different levels, and adds claims when immediately injured persons can enforce the law themselves. These considerations explain the inherited requirement; they are not newly created elements that replace the statute. SIPC's advance obligation and the predictability of customer losses therefore cannot alone establish recovery. Sedima's rejection of a special racketeering injury remains intact, and no antitrust-injury requirement enters RICO. The Court does not announce a formula for every causal chain or decide whether a securities purchaser-seller restriction independently limits this action.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Associated General Contractors of California, Inc. v. Carpenters, 459 U.S. 519 (1983) — its account of common-law causal limits in the borrowed Clayton Act language is extended to §1964(c); its antitrust-specific injury requirement is not imported.
- Agency Holding Corp. v. Malley-Duff & Associates, Inc., 483 U.S. 143 (1987) — relied on for the close statutory relationship between civil RICO and the Clayton Act; its limitations holding remains unchanged.
- Sedima, S.P.R.L. v. Imrex Co., 473 U.S. 479 (1985) — preserved: no special racketeering injury or prior conviction is required; the enacted causal limitation still must be satisfied.
- Cannon v. University of Chicago, 441 U.S. 677 (1979) — applied for Congress's presumed awareness of the settled interpretation of borrowed language; no new implied action is recognized.

##### The nonpurchasing customers' derivative losses are too remote

**Holding and operative rule:** Even assuming SIPC may be subrogated to the nonpurchasing customers' rights against Holmes, it cannot recover under §1964(c) on the asserted stock-manipulation theory because their unpaid claims depend on the broker-dealers' prior losses and intervening insolvency. The trustees' distinct claims remain undisturbed, and SIPC may share in any estate recovery according to the priority SIPA lawfully gives its claim.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part III-A, joined by Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas; eight Justices adopt the application and its limits. The validity and source of the asserted common-law subrogation are assumed, not established.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that replacing the customers with their asserted subrogee cannot make the customers' injury more direct. The alleged manipulation first reduced the brokers' assets; those losses then left the brokers unable to satisfy the nonpurchasing customers' claims. Associated General Contractors supplies the reasons this intervening injury matters. Calculating customer damages would require separating the manipulation's effects from other causes of broker failure and apportioning recovery between the brokers and their creditors. Blue Shield v. McCready and Hawaii v. Standard Oil identify the concern about overlapping damages, while Southern Pacific v. Darnell-Taenzer describes the ordinary preference for recovery at the immediate level of injury. The trustees already represent the brokers' direct claims, so denying these derivative claims does not leave that injury without a plaintiff. SIPC's statutory obligations and RICO's remedial purpose do not justify bypassing that causal limit or liquidation priorities. The Court assumes responsibility for coconspirators' conduct without deciding it and leaves separately grounded parking, purchasing-customer and directly injured claims outside this ruling.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Associated General Contractors, 459 U.S. 519 — applied to the derivative creditor-loss chain, attribution difficulties, overlapping recovery and available directly injured plaintiffs.
- Blue Shield of Virginia v. McCready, 457 U.S. 465 (1982) — relied on for causal limits and avoidance of overlapping damages; its recognition of a directly injured claimant's action is not displaced.
- Hawaii v. Standard Oil Co. of California, 405 U.S. 251 (1972) — applied for the concern about duplicate recovery; no state parens patriae issue is decided here.
- Southern Pacific Co. v. Darnell-Taenzer Lumber Co., 245 U.S. 531 (1918) — applied as a general direct-injury principle, without converting its first-step formulation into an exceptionless rule.

##### SIPA participation rights do not create damages liability

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 78eee(d) of SIPA gives SIPC party-in-interest status, a right to be heard and deemed intervention in liquidation matters; it does not itself create an independent right to recover administration advances as damages from Holmes. Neither that participation provision alone nor its combination with §1964(c) supplies a missing substantive recovery right or dispenses with RICO's own conditions.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part III-B, joined by Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas; eight Justices adopt this independently sufficient statutory ground for rejecting SIPC's own §78eee(d) claim. This is distinct from the customer-subrogation causation holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a right to participate in a proceeding does not itself establish a right to collect damages from another party. Section 78eee(d) identifies SIPC's status in liquidation matters and gives it the same opportunity to be heard that an allowed intervention would provide. It says nothing about conduct making Holmes liable or conditions for an affirmative monetary recovery against him. Section 78fff-3(b)(2) requires SIPC to fund administration when the general estate is insufficient, but that obligation does not turn participation language into a reimbursement action. Adding §1964(c) does not cure the problem: that statute retains its own requirements for recovery. The Court rejects only the asserted statutory source of liability and does not decide every claim SIPC might possess under other law. Its statutory participation remains effective even though this particular damages theory fails. The distinct suggestion of subrogation to trustees for expense advances, raised only at argument and not developed in the merits brief, is not decided on its merits.

**Precedent treatment:** No earlier decision is independently construed, extended or overruled by this provision-specific holding. Its participation/recovery distinction is consistent with the existing action/remedy distinction; it does not announce a general rule foreclosing implied or subrogated actions under other law.

##### Limits and questions not reached

The Court does not decide whether all civil RICO plaintiffs alleging securities fraud as a predicate must have purchased or sold securities. Part IV's decision to leave that question open has five joins: Souter, Stone, Blackmun, Kennedy and Thomas. White, Stevens and O'Connor withhold that join; Scalia joins no Court opinion. Their separate rejection of a purchaser-seller restriction does not create a Court holding on that question.

No fraud, conspiracy participation, actual causal contribution or damages amount is found. Responsibility for coconspirators' acts is assumed because that question was outside the limited grant. The Court neither validates the source of customer subrogation nor decides claims of purchasing customers, the parking allegations not pursued as a separate recovery theory in this Court, a claim of directly aimed injury to SIPC, or the merits of the unbriefed expense-advance subrogation theory. The unappealed separate Rule 10b-5 summary judgment is not reopened. No broad prohibition on foreseeable third-party injury, new burden of proof, general zone-of-interests rule, or automatic transfer of trustees' claims is adopted.

Before this event, no simulated RICO directness or §78eee(d) holding existed. The eight-Justice rules above now control; Sedima, ordinary proof rules and actual advance/priority provisions remain. Stone's additional own-advances RICO ground is noncontrolling. No Article III, generic tort, securities purchaser-seller or trustee-capacity change is made.

#### Nationwide Mutual Insurance Co. v. Darden — 1992-03-24

**Record:** `records/Nationwide_Mutual_Insurance_Co_v_Darden_merits_1992-03-24.md`.

##### ERISA employee status follows general common-law agency

**Holding and operative rule:** ERISA’s circular definition of employee incorporates the general common law of agency, not a particular State’s law or a substitute test of benefit expectations, reliance and bargaining power. Courts must weigh all incidents of the relationship—including control over manner and means, skill, tools, work location, duration, assignment rights, scheduling discretion, payment, assistants, regular-business integration, the hiring party’s business, benefits and tax treatment—with no single factor decisive.

**Authority:** Souter’s unanimous opinion; all nine Justices adopt the statutory definition and multifactor application requirement.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an expectation of retirement benefits cannot itself establish membership in the employee category Congress protected. Reid presumes that a statutory employee term carries its settled agency meaning unless Congress directs otherwise. ERISA’s circular definition supplies no such direction, and its general protective purpose does not identify a different class. United Insurance requires weighing all incidents of the relationship; a contractual label or one favorable factor does not settle the matter. Hearst and Silk do not justify replacing this inquiry with remedial purpose, particularly after Congress’s contrary responses to their interpretations. Rutherford Food remains different because the FLSA expressly reaches work suffered or permitted, language ERISA lacks. The circuit’s expectations test also assumes the disputed statutory entitlement when it disregards the agreed forfeiture condition, and its reliance prong can treat identical jobs differently according to personal savings choices. The proper inquiry concerns the employment relationship. Darden’s ultimate classification and any resulting vesting or forfeiture consequence remain for remand.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Community for Creative Non-Violence v. Reid, 490 U.S. 730 — applied to ERISA: general common-law agency is the default meaning of an unhelpfully defined employee term.
- NLRB v. United Insurance Co., 390 U.S. 254 — applied: all incidents must be assessed with no single decisive factor.
- NLRB v. Hearst Publications, 322 U.S. 111 — limited as a general interpretive model: purpose alone does not displace agency meaning in ERISA.
- United States v. Silk, 331 U.S. 704 — its general purposive emphasis is not followed for undefined employee terms; Reid supplies the governing presumption.
- Rutherford Food Corp. v. McComb, 331 U.S. 722 — distinguished and preserved: FLSA’s broader express definition supports its different reach.
- Kelley v. Southern Pacific, 419 U.S. 318; Baker v. Texas & Pacific, 359 U.S. 227; Robinson v. Baltimore & Ohio, 237 U.S. 84 — retained as supporting applications of ordinary agency meaning.

**Current operation and change:** Effective March 24, Reid’s presumption governs ERISA’s employee definition, with the complete multifactor agency inquiry and no decisive single factor. The circuit’s expectations/reliance/bargaining-power substitute is rejected. FLSA’s broader statutory category remains distinct; Darden’s status, available benefits and independent state-contract remedies are not decided.

**Posture and limits:** Supreme Court review ends with remand to the Fourth Circuit for the proper classification inquiry and further proceedings consistent with that result. Neither Darden’s employee status nor an entitlement to benefits is finally determined. The excluded cross-petition issue is not reopened.

#### United States v. Wilson — 1992-03-24

**Record:** `records/United_States_v_Wilson_merits_1992-03-24.md`.

##### BOP makes the initial credit computation after commencement

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 3585(b) does not authorize a district court to compute detention credit at sentencing; the Attorney General through BOP must make the initial computation after the federal sentence commences. Administrative review and proper subsequent judicial review remain available, so the calculation is not unreviewable discretion.

**Authority:** Thomas’s opinion, joined by Stone, Blackmun, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter; seven Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the credit calculation belongs to administration of the sentence after it begins. Section 3585(b) looks to detention already spent before commencement and to credit already given against another sentence. Otte and Gwaltney show why the verbs’ temporal meaning matters: at sentencing, the court may not know when detention will end or when another sentence will receive credit. Section 3621 assigns administration of the federal term to BOP, which must determine the remaining time and therefore must compute the statutory credit. Congress’s deletion of the Attorney General’s name during a complete rewrite does not affirmatively transfer this task to sentencing judges. Russello’s inference from changed wording is overcome by the present text and structure; Robertson cautions against assuming an unexplained abandonment of established administration. The contrary approach would make the initial decision depend on incomplete information. Prisoners retain administrative review and proper judicial review; neither a new credit award nor an unreviewable executive power follows.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Otte v. United States, 419 U.S. 43 — applied: Congress’s choice of verb tense informs statutory timing.
- Gwaltney of Smithfield v. Chesapeake Bay Foundation, 484 U.S. 49 — applied for the same temporal-text principle.
- Russello v. United States, 464 U.S. 16 — applied as a rebuttable inference, overcome here by the rewritten text and administration scheme.
- Robertson v. Railroad Labor Board, 268 U.S. 619 — applied: a departure from long-established procedure is not lightly inferred.
- McCarthy v. Madigan, simulated March 4, 1992 — distinguished: its damages-only process could not provide the requested relief, unlike a credit computation process.

##### Credit already applied to another sentence cannot be counted again

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 3585(b) forbids federal detention credit for time already credited against another sentence when the federal sentence begins. A defendant cannot avoid that condition merely because the federal sentencing hearing occurred before the other court awarded its credit.

**Authority:** Thomas’s seven-Justice opinion; the no-double-credit condition supports the actor/timing disposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the sequence of sentencing hearings does not create a right to count the same detention twice. Section 3585(b) expressly excludes time credited against another sentence, and its commencement-based calculation gives that condition practical effect. Wilson’s federal hearing occurred before Tennessee awarded its 429 days, but the award preceded his transfer and the start of the federal term. Measuring eligibility only at the earlier hearing would make the federal credit depend on the happenstance of which court sentenced first. Turkette’s instruction to avoid absurd statutory results supports rejecting that arbitrary consequence. The revised provision also broadens some eligible detention and changes other language, so preserving administrative calculation does not render the revision meaningless. This Court does not calculate a fresh numerical credit, define every form of official detention, or decide a later challenge to BOP’s application. It holds only that the established state credit cannot be disregarded on the timing theory accepted below.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Turkette, 452 U.S. 576 — applied: avoid an arbitrary statutory result unsupported by the text’s purpose.

**Current operation and change:** Effective March 24, §3585(b)’s initial actor and timing are settled: BOP computes after commencement, applying the prohibition on credit already used against another sentence. The changed wording does not transfer initial calculation to the sentencing judge. Proper review remains, and McCarthy creates no exemption for this relief-capable administrative process. No particular BOP calculation or universal definition of official detention is adjudicated.

**Posture and limits:** Supreme Court review ends in reversal of the circuit’s direction to award credit at sentencing. BOP remains responsible for the initial calculation after commencement, with administrative and judicial review through proper channels. The Court awards no days of credit and makes no new detention order.

##### Section 671(a)(15) supplies no individual reasonable-efforts right enforceable under §1983

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 671(a)(15), read in the Adoption Assistance and Child Welfare Act's plan and administrative-enforcement structure, does not confer on these beneficiaries an individual right enforceable through §1983 to compel the asserted reasonable efforts to prevent removal or reunify families. An approved plan's reasonable-efforts commitment, §671(a)(3)'s statewide-operation requirement, and the record's caseworker deficiencies do not supply that missing federal right.

**Authority:** White's Opinion of the Court, Part II, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; six Justices adopt this rule and reverse the §1983 ruling under §671(a)(15).

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a federal funding condition does not by itself identify an individual right enforceable through §1983. Thiboutot permits enforcement of statutory rights, while Wright and Wilder require examination of the particular entitlement Congress established. Pennhurst requires ascertainable obligations when States accept federal funds. Unlike the rent limitation in Wright and the reimbursement requirements in Wilder, this reasonable-efforts clause supplies no further measure of the services an individual child may demand. Illinois has an approved plan containing the commitment; the statewide-operation language does not convert that commitment into the asserted private entitlement. The serious absence of caseworkers and the feasibility of a three-day remedy cannot answer the prior question whether federal law creates that entitlement. Administrative funding review and the required removal findings give the clause operation without this private action. The Court does not hold that those mechanisms comprehensively foreclose otherwise existing §1983 rights, or that every requirement contained in a state plan is unenforceable.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Maine v. Thiboutot, 448 U.S. 1 (1980) — preserves statutory-right enforcement under §1983; a violation of federal law still must invade an enforceable right.
- Wright v. Roanoke Redevelopment and Housing Authority, 479 U.S. 418 (1987) — distinguishes its defined rent and utility entitlement; its enforceability holding and separate foreclosure inquiry remain operative.
- Wilder v. Virginia Hospital Association, 496 U.S. 498 (1990) — distinguishes its substantive reimbursement obligation and supplied benchmarks; neither its Medicaid holding nor its rejection of automatic foreclosure by funding withdrawal is overruled.
- Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman, 451 U.S. 1 (1981) — applies its clear-notice principle to the asserted funding obligation; no categorical immunity for state-plan provisions is created.

##### Section 671(a)(15) supplies no separate implied action

**Holding and operative rule:** The Act does not create an implied private cause of action to enforce §671(a)(15)'s reasonable-efforts clause because the claimants have not established congressional intent to create that remedy. This conclusion concerns the distinct action asserted directly under the Act and does not make failure to imply an action a bar to an otherwise available enacted §1983 remedy.

**Authority:** White's Opinion of the Court, Part III, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; six Justices adopt this independently necessary ground for rejecting the second enforcement route sustained below.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a statute's beneficial purpose cannot substitute for congressional intent to authorize the private action asserted. Cort identifies the considerations bearing on an implied remedy, and Transamerica makes legislative intent the controlling inquiry. Children and families plainly benefit from the Act's objectives, but that fact does not resolve who may enforce every undertaking through a lawsuit. The reasonable-efforts clause directs the contents of the funding plan, leaves substantial choices to the State, and operates with administrative review and removal-related findings. Those features do not show an intention to create the additional private action sought here. The Court of Appeals' reliance on the desirability of effective services cannot supply the missing authorization. This inquiry remains distinct from determining whether an existing right may be enforced through §1983. Franklin likewise permits appropriate remedies only after a cognizable action exists; it cannot create this one. No other provision's implied remedy is resolved merely by this conclusion about reasonable efforts.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Cort v. Ash, 422 U.S. 66 (1975) — applies its inquiry into an implied private remedy, with congressional intent decisive.
- Transamerica Mortgage Advisors, Inc. v. Lewis, 444 U.S. 11 (1979) — applies its requirement of congressional intent to create the particular remedy asserted; statutory purpose alone is insufficient.
- Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools, February 26, 1992, simulated decision — preserves the distinction between the existence of a cognizable action and appropriate relief within it; no Title IX holding is narrowed.

##### The asserted §671(a)(9) reporting claim is not enforceable through §1983

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 671(a)(9)'s requirement that a state plan provide for reporting to the appropriate court or law-enforcement agency when a state agency has reason to believe a child's home or institution is unsuitable because of neglect, abuse, or exploitation, and the child's care is paid for in whole or in part under Title IV-E or IV-B, does not confer on these respondents a private federal right enforceable through §1983 on the claim presented. The reporting ground therefore cannot independently sustain the challenged caseworker injunction.

**Authority:** White's Opinion of the Court, Part IV-A, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; six Justices adopt this separate statutory holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the reporting provision does not supply these respondents with the private action missing from their reasonable-efforts claim. Section 671(a)(9) addresses a state agency's reason to believe a child's home or institution is unsuitable because of neglect, abuse, or exploitation, where the child's care is paid for in whole or in part under Title IV-E or IV-B. It specifies notice to the appropriate court or law-enforcement agency. That administrative reporting direction does not itself turn the plan feature into the individual federal entitlement asserted here. Wright and Wilder require identification of the right created by the particular statutory provision, not merely conduct a State has undertaken to include in its approved program. The lower court cannot sustain this injunction simply by grouping reporting with reasonable efforts. The Court rejects the asserted §1983 route while leaving the State's reporting undertaking and administrative enforcement intact. It does not decide the separately defined case-plan and review claim under §671(a)(16).

**Precedent treatment:**

- Wright, 479 U.S. 418 — applies the need to identify a privately enforceable statutory right; its distinct rent holding remains in force.
- Wilder, 496 U.S. 498 — applies provision-specific examination of a binding individual entitlement; no general rule against enforcement of plan provisions replaces it.

##### The asserted §671(a)(9) claim supplies no separate implied action

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 671(a)(9) does not create an implied private action for these respondents to obtain the challenged caseworker relief: its reporting requirement and the Act's enforcement structure do not establish congressional intent to authorize that action. Rejection of this distinct statutory vehicle does not decide private enforcement of §671(a)(16).

**Authority:** White's Opinion of the Court, Part IV-B, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; six Justices adopt the ground rejecting direct implied enforcement of the reporting claim.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a duty to notify a designated public authority does not, without the required legislative intent, authorize the private suit asserted here. Cort and Transamerica require examination of the particular action Congress intended to permit. Section 671(a)(9) specifies reporting within the state-plan structure; it does not identify an additional private judicial vehicle for obtaining caseworker assignments. The children's interest in safe placements explains why the reporting obligation matters, but beneficial purpose and the usefulness of an injunction do not establish the missing congressional choice. The Act's administrative oversight remains available, and rejecting this asserted action neither excuses noncompliance with the plan nor decides that every more specifically defined obligation has the same enforcement consequences. Franklin's rule about appropriate relief in an existing action cannot bridge the absence of the asserted action itself. The Court therefore rejects this alternative route separately from its §1983 analysis and leaves the case-plan claim for its own consideration on remand.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Cort, 422 U.S. 66, and Transamerica, 444 U.S. 11 — apply the congressional-intent requirement to the distinct implied reporting action.
- Franklin, February 26, 1992, simulated decision — applies its action/remedy distinction without changing the established Title IX action or relief available within it.

##### Limits and questions not reached

Those matters return for provision-specific consideration. The Court does not find the reporting claim abandoned, revive the dismissed constitutional count, award damages, prescribe a nationwide three-day assignment deadline, modify the B.H. decree, or hold that administrative funding withdrawal necessarily forecloses an otherwise existing §1983 right. Safety, eligibility, actual statutory requirements and proper remedial scope remain separate questions wherever legally presented.

#### United States v. Felix — 1992-03-25

**Authority:** [Canonical Decision Record](records/United_States_v_Felix_merits_1992-03-25.md).

**1. Reused evidence and separate substantive offenses.** The use of evidence of an uncharged crime in one trial does not itself place the defendant in jeopardy for that crime. The later Oklahoma substantive charges concern different acts from the Missouri attempt and are not barred merely because evidence of those acts helped prove intent in Missouri.

**Controlling authority:** White, Part I, joined by Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas: nine votes make this an Opinion of the Court.

- Dowling v. United States, 493 U.S. 342 — applied: evidentiary use does not itself constitute prosecution for the described offense.
- Grady v. Corbin, 495 U.S. 508 — applied and confined to essential-conduct analysis; its express rejection of a same-evidence test remains operative.

**2. Agreement remains a distinct offense.** A conspiracy and its substantive objects are distinct offenses; prosecution of the substantive crime does not bar prosecution of the agreement merely because the same acts help prove both. The overlapping Missouri conduct therefore does not bar this Oklahoma conspiracy.

**Controlling authority:** White, Part II, joined by Stone, White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: seven votes control this rationale; Stevens and Blackmun agree only with the judgment on a narrower ground.

- United States v. Bayer, 331 U.S. 532 — applied: the agreement is distinct from its execution.
- Pinkerton v. United States, 328 U.S. 640 — applied: substantive crime and conspiracy remain separately punishable offenses.
- Iannelli v. United States, 420 U.S. 770 — applied: conspiracy’s independent character survives overlapping proof.
- Garrett v. United States, 471 U.S. 773 — applied by analogy to the treatment of multilayered criminal conduct.
- Grady v. Corbin, 495 U.S. 508 — clarified without overruling: it does not abrogate the established conspiracy/substantive-offense distinction.

**Current-law effect and boundaries:** Distinct evidentiary use does not itself create prior jeopardy for an uncharged offense. The established agreement/substantive-offense distinction governs this conspiracy. Grady remains in force within its proper scope; ordinary evidentiary objections and a different single-incident successive prosecution remain open.

#### National Railroad Passenger Corp. v. Boston & Maine Corp. — 1992-03-25

**Authority:** [Canonical Decision Record](records/National_Railroad_Passenger_Corp_v_Boston_and_Maine_Corp_merits_1992-03-25.md).

**1. Passenger-service acquisition and reconveyance.** Section 562(d) permits the ICC’s construction that property required for passenger service includes property usefully and appropriately acquired for Amtrak’s rail operations; it does not require indispensable permanent Amtrak ownership. The statutory need presumption and express service-promoting reconveyance authority therefore permit this transaction, subject to the statute’s independent conditions.

**Controlling authority:** Kennedy, joined by Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia and Souter: six votes support this holding and explanation.

- Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. NRDC, 467 U.S. 837 — applied: permissible agency construction governs the unresolved statutory ambiguity.
- K mart Corp. v. Cartier, Inc., 486 U.S. 281 — applied: statutory text and structure constrain deference.
- Sullivan v. Everhart, 494 U.S. 83 — applied: the meaning of a disputed term is assessed within the whole statute.
- Lechmere, Inc. v. NLRB, current-term decision January 27, 1992 — preserved: no prior binding construction is displaced here.

**2. The agency’s discernible ground.** A reviewing court may recognize an interpretation necessarily embodied in the agency’s own decision even when the agency did not state that interpretation in the exact words later used by counsel. That rule sustains this ICC order because its application of the need presumption and its disposition of the competing remedies disclose the statutory construction; it does not permit a court to supply a new agency ground.

**Controlling authority:** Kennedy, joined by Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia and Souter: six votes support this holding and explanation.

Inability to agree on sale terms.** Section 562(d)’s inability-to-agree prerequisite permits the ICC to find from the parties’ positions that further sale negotiations will not produce agreement; it does not impose an unstated mandatory bargaining ritual. **Controlling authority:** Kennedy, joined by Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia and Souter: six votes support this holding and explanation.

- Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. NRDC, 467 U.S. 837 — applied to the ICC’s interpretation of the independent sale-negotiation prerequisite.

**Controlling authority:** Kennedy, joined by Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia and Souter: six votes support this holding and explanation.

- Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. NRDC, 467 U.S. 837 — applied: the statute permits consideration of mitigation within the transaction.
- Arkansas v. Oklahoma, current-term decision February 26, 1992 — consistent with, and unchanged by, review of an agency conclusion against the actual record.

**5. Rebuttal: alternative property.** The ICC permissibly reads the alternative-property condition of §562(d) to ask whether an adequate alternative route is available on the statutory terms, rather than whether a lesser interest in the same segment would suffice. **Controlling authority:** Kennedy, joined by Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia and Souter: six votes support this holding and explanation.

- Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. NRDC, 467 U.S. 837 — applied: the ICC’s route-based construction is permissible within the statutory scheme.

**Controlling authority:** Kennedy, joined by Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia and Souter: six votes support this holding and explanation.

- Berman v. Parker, 348 U.S. 26 — applied: private implementation does not defeat an otherwise public purpose.

**Current-law effect and boundaries:** Section 562(d) allows the approved service-connected acquisition and reconveyance. #### United States Department of Commerce v. Montana — 1992-03-31

Correction insertion: [Canonical Decision Record](records/United_States_Department_of_Commerce_v_Montana_merits_1992-03-31.md).

##### Count I presents a continuing and redressable representation injury

**Holding and operative rule:** On this record, Count I's challenge to the continuing legal allocation of Representatives presents a concrete voting-representation injury traceable to the statutory formula, with a substantial likelihood of redress through adjudication of that formula's validity. Completion of the officials' initial statutory steps does not establish that the allocation has ceased to affect the approaching election or that declaratory relief cannot redress the asserted injury.

**Authority:** Stevens's Opinion of the Court, Part I, joined by Stone, White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. All nine participating Justices adopt this proposition at its stated scope; it controls by an express majority, with no judgment-only or narrower-ground vote.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the present allocation creates a live dispute over the legal weight of representation. Montana challenges an operative population formula that gives it one Representative, not a completed act with no continuing consequence. The alleged injury is tied to that formula, and a declaration addressing its validity can remove the asserted legal obstacle to corrective implementation. The Court independently concludes that declaratory relief has a substantial likelihood of redressing the asserted inequality. Wesberry recognizes judicial protection of concrete congressional voting injuries while leaving appropriate relief for separate consideration; it does not require the Court to guarantee a particular replacement district or seat. The Government's completed-duties argument does not establish that this continuing allocation is beyond any declaratory relief. This conclusion assumes neither a new enactment nor a command to the President. The Court does not decide the distinct legislative injury asserted in Count II or approve any particular coercive remedy against absent parties.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Wesberry v. Sanders, 376 U.S. 1 (1964) — applies its recognition of concrete congressional voting injuries while preserving the distinction between reviewability and appropriate relief; no general rule governing presidential defendants is adopted.

##### Congress's apportionment choice remains subject to constitutional review

**Holding and operative rule:** Courts may decide whether the equal-proportions method violates the constitutional requirement to apportion Representatives among States according to their respective numbers. Congress's responsibility to choose a method, and the possibility that several methods satisfy the Constitution, do not make this particular constitutional challenge a nonjusticiable political question.

**Authority:** Stevens's Opinion of the Court, Part II, joined by Stone, White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. All nine participating Justices adopt this proposition at its stated scope; it controls by an express majority, with no judgment-only or narrower-ground vote.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that identifying the constitutional limits on apportionment is a judicial task. Baker directs attention to the particular question and to any constitutional commitment or other impediment inseparable from it. Muñoz-Flores rejects the premise that Congress's consideration of constitutionality or ordinary disagreement between branches eliminates judicial review. The Government argues more narrowly that choosing among population-related methods belongs to Congress because no legal standard identifies the best formula. That argument does not prevent the Court from deciding whether the Constitution requires the absolute-population measure Montana proposes. The answer may leave Congress more than one lawful choice. Such latitude is a conclusion about the constitutional requirement, not a reason to refuse to interpret it. The Court need not select a preferred national arrangement or supervise legislative deliberations to resolve the presented challenge. Baker's political-question limits remain operative in other settings, and the distinct challenge to congressional voting opportunities in Count II is left undecided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Baker v. Carr, 369 U.S. 186 (1962) — applies its issue-specific political-question inquiry; political subject matter alone does not foreclose constitutional adjudication.
- United States v. Muñoz-Flores, 495 U.S. 385 (1990) — applies ordinary judicial review of constitutional constraints on Congress; its Origination Clause holding is not extended to decide Count II.

##### Equal proportions satisfies the presented interstate population challenge

**Holding and operative rule:** In allocating the statutorily fixed House among States using whole Representatives and respecting each State's constitutional minimum, Article I, §2 and the Fourteenth Amendment permit the equal-proportions method's population-based choice of relative representational equality. An alternative's improvement in selected absolute population disparities does not establish that equal proportions violates the constitutional population command when the change worsens other legitimate measures of equality; the Court neither requires equal proportions uniquely nor approves every population-related formula.

**Authority:** Stevens's Opinion of the Court, Part III, joined by Stone, White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. All nine participating Justices adopt this proposition at its stated scope; it controls by an express majority, with no judgment-only or narrower-ground vote.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Montana's preferred measure does not establish the constitutional defect it asserts. Wesberry protects equality in congressional districts within a State, and Karcher requires a good-faith effort to avoid intrastate population differences, with specific justification for deviations. Interstate allocation presents a different constraint: state boundaries remain fixed, every State receives a seat, and Representatives cannot be divided. A transfer giving Montana a second seat reduces selected absolute disparities but increases the relative disparity between Montana and Washington and worsens Washington's distance from the ideal. The Constitution does not make Montana's favored comparison controlling among those competing measures. Congress may choose relative representational equality through this consistently population-based method. That ground answers the challenge without treating longevity or expert endorsement as an independent constitutional defense. The Court preserves Karcher's intrastate requirements, creates no percentage tolerance or new burden sequence, and does not decide whether the distinct constitutional claim in Count II requires a fresh enactment after each census.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Wesberry, 376 U.S. 1 — preserves its intrastate equal-vote holding while distinguishing allocation of indivisible seats among States; its historical explanation does not select Montana's interstate metric.
- Karcher v. Daggett, 462 U.S. 725 (1983) — distinguishes its intrastate line-drawing problem; its good-faith and specific-justification requirements remain unchanged, with no numerical safe harbor imported here.

##### Count II returns for initial consideration without a Supreme Court merits ruling

**Holding and operative rule:** Reversal of the judgment and relief resting on Count I does not dispose of Count II's separate challenge to automatic decennial apportionment. Because the lower court left that claim's merits undecided and it is not necessary to resolve the presented Count I questions, Count II is remanded without a Supreme Court decision on its standing, justiciability, merits, or any further relief.

**Authority:** Stevens's Opinion of the Court, Part IV, joined by Stone, White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. All nine participating Justices adopt this proposition at its stated scope; it controls by an express majority, with no judgment-only or narrower-ground vote.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that its rejection of Count I cannot silently decide a different constitutional claim. The district court expressly found Count II unnecessary after granting relief on Count I. The appellate questions address judicial review and the equal-proportions formula, and Montana did not offer Count II as an alternative ground for affirmance. It instead expressly requested further proceedings on that claim if Count I failed. The Government likewise stated that there was no occasion to resolve the distinct legislative-standing issue. Singleton supplies the ordinary practice of leaving unadjudicated issues for orderly consideration below, while preserving discretion in appropriate exceptional cases. A general request for judgment for the Government does not establish such an exception here. The Court therefore limits judgment for appellants to Count I and preserves Count II for its own threshold and merits consideration. It adopts no categorical ban on first-instance appellate decision and expresses no view about the strength or ultimate disposition of the remaining claim.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Singleton v. Wulff, 428 U.S. 106 (1976), unanimous Part III — applies its ordinary remand practice and fair-opportunity rationale; its recognized discretionary exceptions remain available, and no new jurisdictional prohibition is announced.

##### Limits and questions not reached

The Court does not decide Count II's legislative standing, political-question defenses, constitutional merits, or eventual relief. It does not order a transfer of Washington's seat, compel Congress to legislate, direct presidential action, decide the unestablished Rule 19 objection, or determine every proper-defendant and coercive-remedy question that a successful claim might present. It does not constitutionalize a House of 435, adopt a general percentage tolerance, import state-legislative apportionment standards, or treat every population-based formula as valid. No later census decision enters these holdings.

Before March 31, no simulated decision had decided the validity of interstate equal-proportions apportionment or this distinct Count II claim. Effective March 31, the Court recognizes this live Count I dispute, reviews its constitutional question, and sustains equal proportions against the asserted population-equality objection. Its relative-equality rationale permits the actual method without making that method uniquely required or every population formula valid.

Baker and Muñoz-Flores retain their particular constitutional-review limits; Wesberry and Karcher retain their intrastate voting-equality rules and Karcher's existing justification framework. Singleton remains an ordinary practice with discretionary exceptions. The Court overrules no precedent, creates no numerical safe harbor, and establishes no new burden sequence. Count II's legislative-standing and reenactment issues remain undecided; leaving the allocation operative during further proceedings is not a merits resolution of that claim.

#### Jacobson v. United States — 1992-04-06

#### United States v. Alaska — 1992-04-21

**Authority:** [Canonical Decision Record](records/United_States_v_Alaska_original_summary_judgment_1992-04-21.md).

##### A live original controversy over the Nome lands

**Holding and operative rule:** Continuing, adverse federal and state claims to the submerged lands affected by the Nome disclaimer present a live original controversy even though the identified mineral sale received no bids. The Court reaches the parties' cross-motions through its original jurisdiction.

**Authority:** White's opinion, Part I, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas; all nine Justices support this threshold holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the absence of bids does not end this dispute. The United States claims the submerged lands outside Alaska's preproject entitlement, and Alaska claims that the permitted artificial extension brought those lands within its statutory boundary. Those opposing claims concern present sovereign entitlement, not merely a possible payment from a particular sale. Article III and 28 U.S.C. §1251(b)(2) provide the original channel for resolving that controversy. The disclaimer expressly preserves a challenge to the Corps' legal authority, and Alaska's acceptance of the permitted project therefore does not eliminate the underlying statutory dispute. A declaration concerning the effect of this instrument determines the parties' corresponding entitlement whether or not a mineral lease produces revenue. The Court can resolve that concrete disagreement without deciding abstract disagreements about other permits or declaring the precise location of any previously disputed coastline.

**Precedent treatment:** This threshold holding applies Article III, §1251(b)(2), and the stipulated continuing title dispute; it changes no earlier jurisdictional rule.

##### Statutory authority for the boundary-preserving condition

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 10 of the Rivers and Harbors Act permits the Corps to consider the effect of this proposed artificial coastal extension on federal submerged-land rights and to condition approval on preserving the existing federal-state boundary. The Submerged Lands Act does not require unconditional approval of the extension or invalidate this condition, which concerns only additional entitlement attributable to the Nome project and leaves existing state property and claims intact.

**Authority:** White's opinion, Part II, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas; all nine Justices support this statutory holding and its stated limits.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the statutory power to approve this artificial extension includes the power to prevent its identified encroachment on federal submerged-land interests. Section 10 separately controls construction in navigable waters; its authorization function is not confined to eliminating navigational obstructions. United States v. California, 381 U.S. 139, recognizes both the effect of qualifying artificial changes on the coastline and federal protection against future encroachment through control of navigable waters and agreement. The Submerged Lands Act's allocation operates with that qualification; it does not compel the Secretary to approve every proposed extension. The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act identifies the federal interest without itself supplying a new Corps transfer power. The disclaimer preserves the existing allocation while allowing construction. Alaska's demand for identical domestic and international boundaries overlooks California's express accommodation of future agreements. The condition also answers the same boundary harm that supports withholding approval; Nollan's objection to unrelated exactions supplies no contrary result. Naturally accreted coastlines, other conditions, and surrender of existing state territory remain outside this decision.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. California, 381 U.S. 139 (1965) — applied: the artificial-coast rule and the qualification permitting protection against future encroachment through agreement remain effective together; the decision imposes no absolute domestic/international boundary identity.
- United States ex rel. Greathouse v. Dern, 289 U.S. 352 (1933) — limited supporting analogy only: its refusal of equitable mandamus did not decide the reserved general extent of §10 discretion.
- Nollan v. California Coastal Commission, 483 U.S. 825 (1987) — distinguished: this condition directly addresses the identified purpose supporting denial and does not exact an unrelated existing property interest; no general holding about a State's constitutional takings remedies follows.
- Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc., 467 U.S. 837 (1984) — applied within the actual statutory delegation and controlling boundary precedent; its general validity and scope are not reconsidered.
- Lechmere, Inc. v. NLRB, January 27, 1992 — preserved: agency construction does not displace a binding judicial construction.
- Arkansas v. Oklahoma, February 26, 1992 — preserved: agency permit conditions must rest on the authority and regulations governing that particular scheme; its Clean Water Act holding supplies no independent §10 power.

##### The published regulation and the Corps' decisional responsibility

**Holding and operative rule:** The public-interest and coastline provisions of 33 C.F.R. §320.4(a) and (f) permit consideration of the Nome project's prospective effect on federal shelf rights; subsection (g)(6)'s exclusion of disputes about an applicant's construction-site property interest does not forbid that inquiry. The Corps' documented adoption of the boundary concern and its own permit decision reject Alaska's claim of unlawful delegation on this record, without authorizing Interior or the Attorney General to exercise an independent permitting veto.

**Authority:** White's opinion, Part III, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas; eight Justices support both propositions. Stone agrees with the disposition of this component only.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the specific coastline provision governs the prospective effect at issue. It expressly requires attention to federal outer continental rights when construction may move the baseline. The property-dispute provision concerns whether the applicant has the interest needed to undertake its proposed activity; it does not cancel the neighboring instruction concerning federal shelf rights. The original published scheme also reserves final action to the Army after interagency coordination. Alaska is correct that consultation cannot transfer statutory responsibility to another department. But concurrence in another department's stated concern does not itself establish such a transfer. The Corps adopted the identified boundary objection in its own July 1983 communication, evaluated the conditioned project in its July 1984 findings, and executed the permit through district officials. That documentary course answers the alleged surrender of the decision to Interior. The Court does not decide compliance with an unpresented internal-routing requirement, recognize an automatic Interior veto, or authorize disregard of governing rules in another proceeding.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Arkansas v. Oklahoma, February 26, 1992 — applied at its source-specific limit: a valid regulation guides the agency's own permit determination; it does not transfer responsibility to a different agency.
- National Railroad Passenger Corp. v. Boston & Maine Corp., March 25, 1992 — applied: the agency's own discernible ground supports review; agreement with an adviser is not a substitute for finding that ground in the agency record.

##### Publication of the criterion under §552

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 552(a)(1) does not invalidate this condition as adverse application of a secret review criterion: the governing public-interest and coastline regulations published consideration of the project's effects on federal outer continental rights. The absence of an express catalogue of every possible curative disclaimer does not make that published criterion an undisclosed rule.

**Authority:** White's opinion, Part IV-A, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas; eight Justices adopt this independently sufficient ground for rejecting the §552 challenge. Stone agrees with its disposition only.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the basis for the challenged condition was publicly stated. Section 552 requires publication or availability of the specified agency materials so that affected persons need not confront undisclosed law. The published coastline provision expressly identifies possible shifts in the baseline and their effects on federal outer continental rights. Those are the effects identified in the Solicitor's letter and adopted by the Corps for the Nome application. Morton v. Ruiz, 415 U.S. 199, forbids use of an unpublished eligibility restriction contrary to the agency's required procedures; it does not require a regulation to enumerate every means of curing a concern the regulation already discloses. The disclaimer here implements the published concern by preserving the existing boundary while permitting the project. This conclusion concerns the criterion actually applied to Nome. It does not approve an undisclosed, independently binding rule for every coastal project, and it does not dispense with any distinct rulemaking duty imposed by §553.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Morton v. Ruiz, 415 U.S. 199 (1974) — distinguished on the applied criterion and preserved: undisclosed eligibility restrictions and disregard of required agency procedures remain impermissible; this case applies a published shelf-effects criterion.

##### Actual and timely notice under §552

**Holding and operative rule:** Independently, §552(a)(1)'s bar on adverse application of unpublished matter does not protect a person who had actual and timely notice of its terms. Alaska had that notice of the Nome condition and its stated ground before executing the disclaimer, so any failure separately to publish those particular terms supplies no basis for invalidating the disclaimer as applied to Alaska.

**Authority:** White's opinion, Part IV-B, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas; eight Justices adopt this independently sufficient alternative holding. Stone agrees with its disposition only.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the statute's express notice qualification independently answers Alaska's claim. The July 1, 1983 Corps letter transmitted Interior's stated concern, specified preservation of the coastline and federal-state boundary as the required cure, and invited further discussion. The stipulated record establishes communication to Alaska, with a copy to Nome; the Court does not invent a particular receipt day. Alaska then had approximately ten months to consider and negotiate the condition before executing the May 9, 1984 instrument. Its transmittal identified review of the form by state and federal legal representatives. Those facts establish actual and timely notice of the terms applied in this proceeding. Morton v. Ruiz preserves, rather than eliminates, §552's express notice qualification. This alternative holding does not depend on comparing other permits. Notice of this particular condition is not notice of every possible general policy, and actual notice does not independently cure a violation of §553 or another mandatory procedure.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Morton v. Ruiz, 415 U.S. 199 (1974) — applied with the statutory actual-notice qualification intact; its protection against undisclosed restrictions is not converted into invalidation regardless of timely knowledge.

##### Section 553 and the individual licensing condition

**Holding and operative rule:** The underlying public-interest and coastline provisions were adopted through notice and comment, and §553 did not require the Corps to promulgate an additional legislative rule before specifying this curative condition in Nome's individual licensing proceeding. This holding depends on application of those existing criteria and the absence of an established, separately binding Corps rule requiring every coastal permit to contain a disclaimer.

**Authority:** White's opinion, Part V, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas; eight Justices support the rule and application. Stone agrees with the disposition of the §553 component only.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Alaska identifies no missing rulemaking step required for this condition. The 1973 proposal expressly presented coastline shifts and federal shelf rights for public participation; the 1974 final rule adopted those subjects after considering comments. The contention that this underlying subject was never noticed therefore fails. A distinct question concerns the chosen cure. Section 551 includes conditioning permits within licensing and treats licensing as adjudication. SEC v. Chenery Corp., 332 U.S. 194, and NLRB v. Bell Aerospace Co., 416 U.S. 267, permit an agency to develop and apply a principle through an individual decision within its authority. Here the Corps used the published criterion to identify a condition permitting the harbor while preserving the boundary. Interior's stated policy does not establish a separately binding Corps rule, and the stipulation does not establish universal treatment. The Court does not exempt legislative rules from §553, rely on its public-property exception, or approve an automatic disclaimer policy in other cases.

**Precedent treatment:**

- SEC v. Chenery Corp., 332 U.S. 194 (1947) — applied: permissible development through adjudication does not require a separate rule merely because a particular decision articulates policy; statutory and procedural limits remain.
- NLRB v. Bell Aerospace Co., 416 U.S. 267 (1974) — applied: an agency may ordinarily choose adjudication for an individual determination, subject to review of an improper exercise of that choice.
- NLRB v. Wyman-Gordon Co., 394 U.S. 759 (1969) — its divided rationales are kept distinct: the case supplies no majority rule requiring notice and comment before every individual licensing condition. No new rule is assembled from its separate opinions.

##### Reasoned action under §706

**Holding and operative rule:** The Corps' Nome condition is not arbitrary or capricious under §706(2)(A): its contemporaneous record identifies the prospective loss of federal shelf rights, adopts that concern, and evaluates the project with a condition that directly removes the concern. The independent statutory, regulatory, and procedural analyses above also reject the asserted excess-authority and unlawful-procedure grounds under §706(2)(C) and (D); lawful authority alone would not excuse an unexplained exercise of discretion.

**Authority:** White's opinion, Part VI, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas; eight Justices support this review holding. Stone agrees with the §706 disposition only.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the reason for this condition appears in the agency's own record. State Farm requires an explanation connecting the relevant facts to the agency's choice, and the Court's Amtrak decision permits recognition of an agency ground that is actually discernible while forbidding a new litigation rationale. The May 1983 Interior letter identified the proposed boundary effect, and the Corps expressly adopted that concern in July. Its 1984 findings addressed the conditioned harbor's public benefits, environmental safeguards, alternatives, and overall public interest. The condition removes the identified federal detriment while allowing those benefits. Alaska's automatic-veto argument does not overcome this documented decision. No separate monetary valuation is necessary to understand the stated connection, and the Court supplies none. Later mineral information cannot repair a missing contemporaneous reason. The Court does not hold that possible statutory authority alone establishes a reasonable application, or that every permit-related procedural error is harmless.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Motor Vehicle Manufacturers Association v. State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Co., 463 U.S. 29 (1983) — applied: the agency must connect relevant facts to its choice; a discernible path suffices, but post hoc grounds do not.
- National Railroad Passenger Corp. v. Boston & Maine Corp., March 25, 1992 — applied: this contemporaneously expressed and adopted ground satisfies the own-ground requirement; no new agency rationale is supplied by the Court.
- Marsh v. Oregon Natural Resources Council, 490 U.S. 360 (1989) — applied for careful review of the agency record within the governing statutory setting, without adding a quantitative mineral-valuation duty.
- Arkansas v. Oklahoma, February 26, 1992 — preserved: judicial review tests the agency decision against its actual governing law and record, without replacing it with a judicial policy.

##### The asserted substantial-evidence requirement

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 706(2)(E)'s substantial-evidence requirement applies to the hearing or statutory-record proceedings identified in that provision; a public notice and opportunity for comments alone do not establish that predicate. Alaska has established no such predicate for this permit, and its separate assertion that the condition lacks a factual foundation fails under §706(2)(A) because the stipulated project and contemporaneously identified boundary effect support the actual ground.

**Authority:** White's opinion, Part VII, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas; eight Justices support the statutory-review holding and the distinct record application. Stone agrees with the disposition of this §706 component only.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the applicable review provision must be identified before its evidentiary demand is imposed. Section 706(2)(E) specifies proceedings subject to §§556 and 557 or otherwise reviewed on a statutorily required agency-hearing record. Alaska has identified no such requirement for this permit. The agency's invitation for comments does not itself turn the licensing process into that statutory proceeding. Arbitrary-action review remains available, and it requires a real foundation for the reason given. The stipulated plans and the contemporaneous correspondence establish the artificial extension and its identified effect on the baseline; disagreement over whether federal shelf rights may lawfully be considered is a legal objection, separately resolved above. Arkansas v. Oklahoma's whole-record substantial-evidence holding concerned its own adjudicative record and supplies no universal hearing requirement. The Court orders no evidentiary hearing here and expresses no view about the result in a proceeding that actually satisfies §706(2)(E)'s statutory predicate.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Arkansas v. Oklahoma, February 26, 1992 — distinguished as to the review channel: its whole-record substantial-evidence rule does not establish §706(2)(E)'s hearing predicate for every informal permit decision.

##### Limits and questions not reached

The declaration concerns only the change attributable to the permitted Nome extension and the corresponding disputed entitlement. It does not locate any otherwise disputed preproject coastline, alter existing state property or claims, decide naturally accreted coastlines, approve different conditions or compulsory surrender of existing territory, adjudicate an automatic nationwide disclaimer policy, revise Chevron generally, or make a general ruling about States' constitutional takings remedies. No valuation, damages, distribution from an actual mineral sale, new construction injunction, or separate Clean Water Act compliance decision is made. The separate APA grounds are reached and rejected; they are not treated as waived. All holdings have an express majority; no Marks analysis is needed.

**Current-law effect, effective April 21, 1992:** The Court adds the stated §10/SLA boundary-condition holding and its case-specific regulatory and APA applications. It expressly preserves §552's two independent answers, the distinction between publication and rulemaking, the need for an actual agency ground, and §706(2)(E)'s statutory predicate. Before this decision the particular Nome applications were unresolved; afterward the specified holdings control at their stated scope. General Chevron doctrine, California's ordinary artificial-coast rule and future-agreement qualification, State Farm/Chenery reason-giving, and the separate current-term Amtrak, Arkansas and Lechmere rules remain in force. No precedent is overruled, no general agency veto or property exception is created, and no distinct burden, proof rule, hearing entitlement or other-coastline doctrine is silently changed.

#### Barker v. Kansas — 1992-04-21

**Durable authority:** [Canonical Decision Record](records/Barker_v_Kansas_merits_1992-04-21.md).

### 1. Military retirement and source-neutral taxation

**Holding and operative rule.** For 4 U.S.C. §111, the military retirement benefits at issue are deferred compensation for past service, comparable to exempt Kansas state and local retirement benefits. Continued military status, restrictions and possible recall do not justify taxing the entire military retirement stream while exempting those comparable benefits; the asserted difference must directly relate to and justify the unequal tax treatment.

**Authority.** White’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas; nine votes control. Stevens and Thomas also concur separately without limiting their joins.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that Kansas has not identified a significant difference that justifies its unequal taxation. Davis applies section 111 to retirement compensation and requires the unequal treatment to relate directly to a meaningful difference between the compared classes. Phillips Chemical supplies that justification requirement. Military retired pay principally depends on completed years of active service and the rank attained, rather than duties actually performed after retirement. Continued status and possible recall do not explain why that entire pension should be taxed when comparable state pensions are exempt. Tyler addressed statutory eligibility for a pay increase; it did not classify retirement benefits for every legal purpose. McCarty expressly left the current-pay characterization unresolved, and Congress subsequently permitted state treatment of retirement pay as marital property. Federal and Kansas treatment of retirement pay for retirement-account contributions further undermines the asserted current-service distinction. The Court decides the section 111 comparison, leaving compensation specifically tied to new service and preserved retrospective-remedy questions for appropriate proceedings.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Davis v. Michigan Department of Treasury, 489 U.S. 803 — applied: retirement income receives §111 protection and a tax-relevant significant difference must justify unequal treatment.
- Phillips Chemical Co. v. Dumas Independent School District, 361 U.S. 376 — applied through Davis: the difference must directly relate to and justify the disparity.
- United States v. Tyler, 105 U.S. 244 — confined to its statutory pay-increase context; continuing military status does not establish current compensation for all purposes.
- McCarty v. McCarty, 453 U.S. 210 — distinguished: it reserved the characterization question and its community-property prohibition was displaced by the Uniformed Services Former Spouses’ Protection Act; no surviving rule authorizes this tax disparity.

**Law after decision:** Davis applies to the military retirement payments at issue: past-service rank and tenure determine their §111 comparison, and formal continuing status or recall does not justify Kansas’s source-based disparity. Tyler retains its actual statutory result; McCarty does not govern the tax characterization. The decision creates no automatic refund sum or rule resolving every consequence of military retirement.

#### United States Department of Energy v. Ohio — 1992-04-21

**Durable authority:** [Canonical Decision Record](records/United_States_Department_of_Energy_v_Ohio_merits_1992-04-21.md).

### 1. Citizen-suit authorization does not supply the disputed punitive waiver

**Holding and operative rule.** The CWA and RCRA citizen-suit provisions authorize proceedings against the United States but do not unequivocally expose it to the punitive civil penalties incorporated from their separate enforcement provisions. Those incorporations include the limits of the penalty provisions, whose generally defined liable persons exclude the United States.

**Authority.** Souter, Part II-A, joined by Stone, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas; six votes control. White, Blackmun and Stevens dissent on this issue.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that permission to sue the United States does not by itself answer which monetary remedies Congress authorized. Mitchell requires unequivocal consent, and Nordic Village applies that requirement to the particular monetary liability sought. The citizen-suit clauses expressly include the United States among possible defendants, but their civil-penalty remedies arise through references to separate provisions. Engel explains that incorporation carries the incorporated provision’s limits as well as its affirmative authority. Here the relevant general definitions include several governmental bodies but omit the United States. Elsewhere Congress expressly extended special definitions throughout whole sections or subchapters; the citizen-suit language does not make that broader extension. Ohio’s competing reading would transfer a local defendant description into a separate remedial limitation. The enacted text does not clearly require that transfer. This construction leaves both clauses useful: punitive penalties remain available against covered defendants, and proceedings against federal agencies retain their conceded compliance function. The Court leaves other express waivers and later enactments outside this decision.

**Precedent treatment.**

- United States v. Mitchell, 445 U.S. 535 — applied: federal consent to the asserted liability must be unequivocal.
- United States v. Nordic Village, Inc., decision of February 25, 1992 — applied: a plausible narrower construction defeats asserted consent to the particular affirmative monetary remedy.
- Engel v. Davenport, 271 U.S. 33 — applied: statutory incorporation includes the referenced provision’s limitations.
- Ruckelshaus v. Sierra Club, 463 U.S. 680, and McMahon v. United States, 342 U.S. 25 — applied: waiver is not enlarged beyond what the enacted language requires.

### 2. CWA process and sanctions do not unequivocally reach past-violation punishment

**Holding and operative rule.** The CWA federal-facilities provision’s submission to requirements, process and sanctions does not unequivocally waive immunity from the punitive fines sought for completed violations. In its statutory context, sanctions directed to enforcing process support coercive compliance relief without clearly adding retrospective punishment.

**Authority.** Souter, Part II-B.1, joined by Stone, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas; six votes control. White, Blackmun and Stevens dissent.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the word sanctions must be read in the enforcement setting Congress supplied. Penfield distinguishes measures compelling obedience from punishment for completed conduct; Hicks likewise recognizes that a conditional sanction can secure compliance. The CWA twice links sanctions with process, while separately listing substantive and procedural requirements. That arrangement points toward measures giving effect to orders and proceedings rather than every penalty for violating substantive law. Ohio correctly observes that sanctions can include punishment in other settings. But a term capable of both meanings does not establish unequivocal consent to the broader one. Shell Oil requires attention to context, and the repeated process pairing supplies a reasonable narrower reading here. Mitchell’s waiver rule and Nordic Village’s remedy-specific application therefore preclude the asserted punitive liability on this language. The Court does not excuse federal facilities from their enacted pollution duties. Prospective orders and conceded coercive enforcement remain distinct, and no particular coercive sanction or settled pollution allegation is adjudicated.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Penfield Co. of California v. SEC, 330 U.S. 585 — applied to distinguish coercive compliance sanctions from punishment.
- Hicks v. Feiock, 485 U.S. 624 — applied to the conditional character of coercive sanctions, without deciding a contempt case here.
- Shell Oil Co. v. Iowa Department of Revenue, 488 U.S. 19 — applied: statutory context determines the relevant meaning.
- United States v. Mitchell, 445 U.S. 535, and United States v. Nordic Village, Inc., February 25, 1992 — applied: ambiguity about the particular punitive remedy defeats the claimed waiver.

### 3. CWA civil penalties and the federal-law limitation

**Holding and operative rule.** State-law punitive penalties do not arise under federal law for the CWA federal-facilities waiver merely because EPA approved the state pollution program. The provision’s qualified reference to civil penalties, read with its coercive-enforcement language and the separate penalty definitions, does not unequivocally authorize the requested punitive fines under either asserted source.

**Authority.** Souter, Part II-B.2, joined by Stone, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas; six votes control. White, Blackmun and Stevens dissent.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that federal approval does not convert Ohio’s penalty statute into federal law for this waiver. Gully explains that a state-law claim can retain its state source even when federal law permits the State to act. International Bridge likewise distinguishes congressional approval from creation of the underlying right. Congress’s use of familiar statutory language supports that distinction here. Arkansas v. Oklahoma recognizes the CWA’s federal and state partnership in pollution control, but it does not decide consent to state punitive fines. Ohio’s broader reading cannot remove the provision’s separate limitation to penalties arising under federal law. Nor does that phrase independently create a federal punitive remedy: the incorporated federal penalty provision still excludes the United States from its liable persons. The express reference to civil penalties creates tension with the narrower coercive language, but does not resolve it unequivocally. Under Mitchell and Nordic Village, that unresolved tension cannot enlarge the waiver. The Court decides only the invoked provisions and requested penalties.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Gully v. First National Bank in Meridian, 299 U.S. 109 — applied by statutory analogy: federal permission does not make a state-law claim arise under federal law.
- International Bridge Co. v. New York, 254 U.S. 126 — applied: congressional approval does not itself create the state-law right.
- ICC v. Brotherhood of Locomotive Engineers, 482 U.S. 270 — applied: familiar statutory language may carry its established construction into another statute.
- Arkansas v. Oklahoma, decision of February 26, 1992 — distinguished: federal character of permit administration does not determine punitive-waiver scope or convert every state penalty into federal law.
- United States v. Mitchell, 445 U.S. 535, and United States v. Nordic Village, Inc., February 25, 1992 — applied: uncertain implications do not expand a clearer, narrower consent.

### 4. RCRA federal-facilities provision is limited to compliance and injunctive enforcement

**Holding and operative rule.** RCRA’s federal-facilities provision does not unequivocally waive federal immunity from punitive fines for past violations. Its submission to substantive and procedural requirements, including permits, reporting, injunctive relief and sanctions enforcing that relief, reaches compliance mechanisms without clearly authorizing retrospective penalties.

**Authority.** Souter, Part II-C, joined by Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas; all nine Justices join this holding.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the RCRA facilities text does not authorize the punitive recovery Ohio seeks. The specific examples concern permits and reports used to assess compliance, or injunctions and sanctions used to secure future obedience. The final sentence again links process and sanctions to enforcement of injunctive relief. None identifies a mechanism punishing completed violations. Mitzelfelt’s reading gives effect to all requirements while recognizing that substantive obligations and the means of punishing their breach are different questions. Ohio invokes Hancock’s discussion of language needed to subject federal facilities to state permitting procedures. But Hancock did not equate full substantive and procedural compliance with consent to every monetary enforcement device. The amended text expressly reaches injunctions without comparably identifying punitive fines. Mitchell requires that additional consent to be clear. This construction leaves the enacted compliance duties and conceded coercive remedies effective. It does not resolve other express waivers, later amendments, or the merits of the settled pollution claims.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Mitzelfelt v. Department of Air Force, 903 F.2d 1293 — adopted on this point: requirements can encompass standards and their implementation without punitive measures.
- Hancock v. Train, 426 U.S. 167 — distinguished: subjection to permitting procedures does not determine the scope of punitive remedies; amended statutory language receives its enacted effect.
- EPA v. California, 426 U.S. 200 — retained as pre-amendment statutory context, not authority to disregard the later express compliance duties.
- United States v. Mitchell, 445 U.S. 535 — applied: no unequivocal consent to punitive liability appears in the specified injunctive-enforcement language.

**Law after decision:** None of the four invoked provisions unequivocally permits the punitive fines sought from DOE for completed violations. Citizen-suit defendant authorization does not expand incorporated penalty definitions; CWA process/sanctions and its qualified civil-penalty clause do not supply the requested consent; EPA approval alone does not federalize the state penalty; and RCRA facilities language reaches compliance/injunction enforcement without punitive liability. Enacted compliance duties and conceded coercive remedies remain distinct and effective. Nordic Village is applied, not broadened into immunity from every remedy.

#### Taylor v. Freeland & Kronz — 1992-04-21

**Durable authority:** [Canonical Decision Record](records/Taylor_v_Freeland_and_Kronz_merits_1992-04-21.md).

### Untimely objection to a claimed exemption

**Holding and operative rule:** Under 11 U.S.C. §522(l) and the applicable Bankruptcy Rule 4003(b), property claimed as exempt becomes exempt when no party in interest objects within 30 days after the creditors' meeting concludes or a relevant amendment to the exemption list is filed, whichever is later, and the court grants no further time within that period. The trustee cannot later contest the claimed exemption merely because it lacked a colorable statutory basis; this holding concerns the scheduled proceeds claimed here and does not decide a different claim's scope, an unscheduled asset, or an unpreserved fraud or §105(a) theory.

**Authority:** Thomas's opinion for the Court, joined in full by Stone, White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter. Eight participating Justices adopt the rule and its explanation at the same level of generality; no fractured-opinion analysis is needed.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a trustee must use the objection procedure while it remains available. Section 522(b) identifies substantive exemptions, while §522(l) states the consequence of a claim left unchallenged. Rule 4003(b) supplies the time to object or obtain an extension, and Rule 9006(b)(3) confines enlargement to that rule's conditions. Ron Pair directs courts to enforce a coherent statutory scheme according to its terms. Taylor knew about the lawsuit and its possible substantial recovery. Uncertainty about its value permitted timely inquiry and an objection; it did not require waiting for collection. A colorability exception would reopen the very merits dispute that the objection period closes. The possibility of an undeserved exemption does not authorize that revision. Misconduct remedies remain available only under their own requirements, and the Court imposes none. The §105(a) argument was not presented below or in the petition and is not decided. Nor does this record require a decision about fraud, unscheduled property, or differently limited exemption language.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Ron Pair Enterprises, Inc., 489 U.S. 235 — applied: a coherent Bankruptcy Code provision is enforced according to its terms; no general power to replace explicit procedure with an equitable exception follows from bankruptcy administration.
- Connecticut National Bank v. Germain, March 9, 1992 — consistent with its text/coexistence rule: substantive exemption eligibility and the procedure for contesting a claim perform different work. Its separate bankruptcy appellate-jurisdiction holding remains unchanged.
- In re Dembs, 757 F.2d 777, and In re Peterson, 920 F.2d 1389 — rejected insofar as their colorability requirement permits a late objection of this kind; that requirement cannot govern the §522(l) consequence decided here.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No §105(a) construction or retroactivity decision; no fraud ruling or sanction; no ruling on unscheduled assets or the scope of differently limited claims. Rule 4003(c)'s objector burden remains applicable in a properly presented objection proceeding. The Court does not adjudicate respondents' alternative valuation, lien or good-faith arguments.

**Law after decision:** Effective April 21, §522(l)/Rule 4003(b) foreclose this late exemption objection despite lack of colorable statutory basis; §105(a), unpreserved fraud, unscheduled assets and differently limited claims remain undecided. Other bankruptcy rules and properly supported misconduct remedies retain their own operation.

#### Gomez v. United States District Court for the Northern District of California — 1992-04-21

**Durable authority:** [Canonical Decision Record](records/Gomez_v_United_States_District_Court_application_to_vacate_stay_1992-04-21.md).

### Equitable basis for vacating the execution stay

**Holding and operative rule:** A court considering an execution stay may weigh the State's interest in enforcing its judgment and an applicant's unjustified delay in presenting a long-available claim independently of any habeas procedural bar. On this record, the unexplained postponement of the cyanide-gas challenge through four prior federal habeas proceedings leaves the April 20 stays without sufficient equitable support; those stays must be vacated even assuming the claim is cognizable under §1983 and not barred by abuse-of-writ doctrine.

**Authority:** The per curiam opinion is joined by White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. Six of nine participating Justices adopt this independent equitable ground. Stone, Blackmun and Stevens dissent. No other ground is necessary to the judgment, and no fractured-opinion rule is invoked.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the supported seriousness of a constitutional claim does not alone establish an entitlement to last-minute equitable relief. Delo supplies the Court's authority to vacate an execution stay that abuses equitable discretion; Barefoot rejects an automatic stay for successive litigation. California has a substantial interest in carrying out its judgment after the opportunities already provided for review. Harris could have challenged this established method years earlier. The recent Harding account adds support for the claim, but it does not explain withholding the underlying challenge through four federal proceedings. The medical submissions and irreversible consequence of execution deserve weight; they do not, on this record, overcome the unexplained delay. This conclusion assumes the procedural premise most favorable to Harris. The Court therefore need not settle the §1983 boundary, apply every McCleskey element, or decide Younger abstention. It also does not decide whether cyanide gas violates the Eighth Amendment. The ruling vacates the identified stays and creates no general prohibition on later federal orders.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Delo v. Stokes, 495 U.S. 320 — applied for full-Court review and vacatur of an unsupported execution stay; its successive-habeas holding remains intact. The equitable assessment here does not treat Kennedy's separate writing as prior Court law.
- Barefoot v. Estelle, 463 U.S. 880 — applied: successive litigation does not itself require a stay, and substantial grounds for relief matter to interim protection.
- In re Blodgett, January 13, 1992 — distinguished: its requirement to seek focused relief below before mandamus neither resolves this application nor supplies a broader rule about capital-litigation delay.
- McCleskey v. Zant, 499 U.S. 467 — application to this §1983 action is reserved; its cause-and-prejudice and miscarriage-of-justice rules remain governing within their proper habeas domain.
- Preiser v. Rodriguez, 411 U.S. 475; Younger v. Harris, 401 U.S. 37; Huffman v. Pursue, Ltd., 420 U.S. 592; Pennzoil Co. v. Texaco Inc., 481 U.S. 1 — their respective channel and abstention rules are left intact; the Court decides no extension to this execution-method claim.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court decides only the equitable support for the identified April 20 stays. It does not determine the final method claim, establish a universal alternative-method requirement, adjudicate every habeas-default element, issue a permanent injunction, or direct that no federal court may enter another stay.

**Law after decision:** Effective April 21, equitable delay supplies an independent ground to vacate the identified execution stays even assuming an open §1983 channel. The Court adopts no categorical channel rule, final Eighth Amendment holding, universal alternative-method requirement or ban on further federal stays; Delo/Barefoot and McCleskey retain their stated domains.

#### Keeney v. Tamayo-Reyes — 1992-05-04

**Durable authority:** [Canonical Decision Record](records/Keeney_v_Tamayo_Reyes_merits_1992-05-04.md).

### Hearing on inadequately developed material facts

**Holding and operative rule.** When a federal habeas claim properly presented to the state courts turns on material facts inadequately developed in state proceedings, Townsend’s fifth mandatory-hearing circumstance remains governed by its deliberate-bypass qualification: ordinary counsel negligence, without deliberate bypass, does not bar the necessary federal evidentiary hearing. Tamayo-Reyes is entitled to a hearing confined to whether the manslaughter mental-state element was adequately explained; the supported findings and denial of a new hearing concerning translation of the plea form and plea proceeding remain undisturbed.

**Authority.** O’Connor’s Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, and Kennedy. All five adopt this rule, its application, and the limited hearing remedy; five of nine participating Justices constitute a majority. No independently sufficient alternative holding or fractured-opinion analysis is necessary.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that Townsend v. Sain governs the missing factual development here. Townsend requires a hearing when facts material to an available constitutional claim were inadequately developed, unless the prisoner deliberately bypassed the state process. Coleman v. Thompson addresses a different obstacle: an independent and adequate state procedural ground preventing consideration of the claim. Tamayo-Reyes presented his claim, and the State identifies no such procedural bar. Vasquez v. Hillery confirms that federal clarification of facts supporting an exhausted claim does not necessarily create a new claim. The State’s concern about duplicative proceedings is substantial, but uniform treatment of different procedural questions does not itself justify replacing the directly applicable hearing rule. Section 2254(d)(3), which removes the presumption of correctness when material facts were inadequately developed, remains consistent with that rule without itself supplying every condition for a hearing. Counsel’s omission here was negligent, not deliberate. The hearing concerns only the explanation of manslaughter’s mental state; it neither reopens supported findings about other translations nor establishes that the plea was unconstitutional.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Townsend v. Sain, 372 U.S. 293 (1963): retained and applied; its fifth circumstance and deliberate-bypass qualification continue to govern inadequate factual development. Its other hearing grounds are not displaced.
- Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. 391 (1963): its deliberate-bypass discussion remains operative through Townsend for this hearing question; the Court does not revive Fay’s displaced claim-default rule.
- Coleman v. Thompson, 501 U.S. 722 (1991): distinguished; its cause-and-prejudice rule for independent and adequate state procedural defaults remains in force and is not extended to this hearing entitlement.
- Vasquez v. Hillery, 474 U.S. 254 (1986): relied on for the distinction between supplementing facts and fundamentally altering an exhausted claim; its deliberate-withholding reservation remains open.

**Law after decision:** Townsend’s fifth mandatory-hearing circumstance continues to require the necessary hearing on materially incomplete state factual development absent deliberate bypass; ordinary counsel negligence alone does not defeat that entitlement. Section 2254(d)’s rules for state factual findings remain distinct and operative. Coleman’s independent-and-adequate-state-ground doctrine remains in force in its own domain. The State’s proposed cause-and-prejudice extension is rejected; no new general hearing restriction is created. Other Townsend circumstances, adequately supported findings, and the constitutional merits remain governed by their existing rules. This is retention and application of existing reusable law, effective in this case May 4, 1992.

#### Denton v. Hernandez — 1992-05-04

**Durable authority:** [Canonical Decision Record](records/Denton_v_Hernandez_merits_1992-05-04.md).

### Factual frivolousness under section 1915(d)

**Holding and operative rule.** A court may dismiss a claim brought in forma pauperis (without paying filing fees first) as factually frivolous under 28 U.S.C. §1915(d) when its allegations are clearly baseless—irrational or wholly incredible—even without judicially noticeable facts contradicting them. The initial assessment must favor the plaintiff; mere improbability, extraordinary circumstances, or ordinary disbelief does not suffice, and screening cannot resolve genuine factual disputes.

**Authority.** O’Connor’s Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, White, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas. These seven Justices adopt the rule and the judgment-supporting explanation. Stevens’s dissent, joined by Blackmun, agrees with this factual-screening standard but disagrees with the additional appellate remand; their agreement is not needed to establish controlling authority.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that factual frivolousness requires more than a judge’s conclusion that an allegation is unlikely. Neitzke v. Williams distinguishes the statutory power to screen clearly baseless facts from dismissal for failure to state a legal claim. That power reaches irrational or wholly incredible allegations even when no judicially noticeable fact disproves them; otherwise much of the special screening authority would disappear. But Neitzke also preserves arguable claims, and Haines v. Kerner requires fair treatment of a prisoner’s pleading without counsel. Screening therefore cannot become a trial of ordinary credibility before the plaintiff has had an opportunity to prove a claim. Here the magistrate relied on the combined pattern of complaints while acknowledging that each complaint was not necessarily frivolous. The Esquer affidavit and proposed Pierce affidavit bear on particular alleged events and must be considered as submitted, without deciding their truth from that pattern alone. The Court corrects the restrictive appellate premise and leaves application to the Court of Appeals; it decides neither whether these allegations satisfy the standard nor whether any assault occurred.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Neitzke v. Williams, 490 U.S. 319 (1989): applied and clarified; its clearly-baseless category reaches irrational or wholly incredible allegations without requiring a judicially noticeable contradiction, while preserving the distinction from Rule 12(b)(6).
- Haines v. Kerner, 404 U.S. 519 (1972): applied; liberal treatment of pleadings filed without counsel remains part of the screening inquiry and does not adjudicate their truth.

### Appellate review and the consequences of dismissal

**Holding and operative rule.** A dismissal under section 1915(d) is reviewed for abuse of discretion; an erroneous legal premise remains independently correctable as law. Review must address relevant circumstances, including pro se status, resolution of genuine factual disputes, legal errors, the adequacy of the stated reasons, prejudice, and whether more specific pleading could cure the defect; the dismissal is not a merits adjudication barring a paid complaint, although its possible effect on later in forma pauperis frivolousness determinations makes prejudice and curability material.

**Authority.** O’Connor’s Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, White, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas. Seven participating Justices adopt this review rule and its remand instructions. No separate proposition establishes a categorical preclusion rule for every later fee application or an absolute right to amend.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that section 1915(d) entrusts the frivolousness determination to the court considering the complaint, subject to review for abuse of discretion. Boag v. MacDougall recognizes that discretion while requiring a lawful ground and an explanation sufficient for appellate review. Deference to a screening judgment therefore does not insulate an incorrect legal rule. Haines v. Kerner makes the litigant’s lack of counsel relevant, and Neitzke v. Williams preserves the difference between screening baseless proceedings and resolving their merits. The Court of Appeals must apply that framework to the three appealed actions, including the affidavits, proposed amendment, and aggregate-pattern rationale. Because a statutory screening dismissal does not determine the merits, it does not bar filing a paid complaint with the same allegations. Its possible effect on later fee-screening decisions nevertheless requires attention to prejudice and whether greater specificity could cure the stated defect. The Court leaves the circuit’s categorical notice-and-amendment rule undecided and does not direct affirmance of the district court’s dismissals; it requires a new appellate assessment under the proper standard.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Boag v. MacDougall, 454 U.S. 364 (1982): applied; statutory discretion does not preserve a dismissal resting on legal error, and reasons must permit intelligent appellate review.
- Haines v. Kerner, 404 U.S. 519 (1972): applied to review of pleadings filed without counsel and the significance of potentially curable deficiencies.
- Neitzke v. Williams, 490 U.S. 319 (1989): applied; section 1915(d)’s screening function remains distinct from disposition of the merits under ordinary pleading rules.

**Law after decision:** Section 1915(d)’s clearly-baseless factual-screening power now expressly covers irrational or wholly incredible allegations without a judicially noticeable contradiction, while excluding ordinary credibility adjudication and mere improbability. Appellate review is for abuse of discretion with independent correction of legal error and attention to reasons, pro se status, curability, and prejudice. A screening dismissal does not bar a paid complaint on the merits; no comprehensive rule governing preclusion in later fee applications is adopted. Before this decision Neitzke supplied the distinct screening function and clearly-baseless category; this decision clarifies its factual boundary and settles the appellate review rule. Neither a categorical notice-and-amendment entitlement nor the frivolousness of these particular complaints is decided.

#### United States v. Williams — 1992-05-04

**Durable authority:** [Canonical Decision Record](records/United_States_v_Williams_merits_1992-05-04.md).

### Review of the question passed upon below

**Holding and operative rule.** The Court may review an important federal question expressly decided by the court of appeals even though a party did not renew its challenge to binding circuit precedent in this case, where adherence to that precedent did not concede its correctness. This is a permissible exercise of review, not an obligation to decide every unpressed question or a rule excusing an affirmative waiver.

**Authority.** Stone’s reviewability opinion, joined by White, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter: five Justices adopt the rule and explanation. The four Justices who prefer dismissal of the writ do not join this rule.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the question is properly before it because the court of appeals actually decided the legal duty now challenged. Stevens v. Department of Treasury permits review of a question passed upon below even when the party did not properly press it. The Tenth Circuit expressly applied Page’s disclosure obligation and used it to sustain dismissal. The Government’s recognition that Page bound the panel did not concede that Page correctly stated federal law; the Government had opposed that rule in the proceeding establishing it. Requiring a renewed demand that the panel disregard its recent controlling precedent would not improve the presentation of this already decided legal issue. The contrary vehicle argument treats compliance with binding precedent as abandonment of a legal objection, although no affirmative abandonment is established. The Court exercises its discretion on this federal appellate record. It does not displace a governing trial-objection requirement, decide the effect of an express concession, or prescribe a broader rule for review of state judgments.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Stevens v. Department of Treasury, 500 U.S. 1 — applied: actual decision below can satisfy the pressed-or-passed-upon practice.
- United States v. Page, 808 F.2d 723 — its binding force within the Tenth Circuit explains the litigation posture; adherence does not establish agreement with its correctness.

### Disclosure of known evidence directly negating guilt

**Holding and operative rule.** Before seeking an indictment, a federal prosecutor must present or otherwise disclose to the grand jury personally known, substantial evidence directly negating the charged offense when its omission would materially distort the grand-jury presentation. This duty requires neither an investigative search for undiscovered favorable evidence nor presentation of every possible defense; a violation alone does not establish entitlement to dismissal.

**Authority.** Stevens’s Parts II and III, joined by Blackmun, O’Connor and Thomas, adopt the personally-known/direct-negation duty. Stone’s separate merits opinion adopts that duty where omission materially distorts the presentation. Under Marks, these five judgment supporters establish the stated common requirement; the four-Justice formulation does not remove Stone’s material-distortion qualification from the controlling rule.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that a prosecutor may not use the grand jury’s independent role to justify the materially misleading omission described here. Calandra recognizes both the grand jury’s protection against unfounded prosecution and enforceable limits on its process. Bank of Nova Scotia recognizes judicial remedies for prejudicial misconduct while separating an actual violation from its effect on an indictment. The obligation here regulates the prosecutor’s presentation of substantial evidence already personally known; it does not require the prosecutor to investigate the defense. Costello prevents challenges to the competence or sufficiency of evidence in an otherwise lawful presentation, and that protection remains. The contrary institutional argument correctly rejects a preliminary trial of every defense, but does not dispose of this confined misconduct question. The Government’s categorical objection to authority therefore fails. The character of the particular accounting documents is outside the question accepted for review. Dismissal requires a separate remedial showing, and constitutional duties and negligent discovery remain open.

**Precedent treatment.**

- United States v. Calandra, 414 U.S. 338 — its protective function and enforceable process limits support the confined duty; ordinary evidentiary freedom remains.
- Bank of Nova Scotia v. United States, 487 U.S. 250 — its misconduct and prejudice inquiries remain separate; violation does not automatically warrant dismissal.
- Costello v. United States, 350 U.S. 359 — preserved for competence and sufficiency challenges to an otherwise lawful presentation; distinguished from the specified prosecutorial omission.
- Marks v. United States, 430 U.S. 188 — applied to the judgment supporters’ shared direct-negation duty with the material-distortion qualification retained.

### Confined supervisory power over a materially misleading presentation

**Holding and operative rule.** Federal supervisory authority permits dismissal without prejudice when a prosecutor withholds personally known, substantial evidence directly negating the charged offense, the omission materially distorts the presentation, the evidence would plainly preclude a finding of probable cause, and the violation substantially influenced the indictment or leaves grave doubt that the indictment was free from that influence. This establishes the stated sufficient ground for dismissal; it requires neither a search for undiscovered favorable evidence nor presentation of every potential defense.

**Authority.** Stevens’s merits Parts II and III, joined by Blackmun, O’Connor and Thomas, supply the narrower judgment-supporting rule. Stone concurs in affirmance under the broader material-distortion formulation, covering known evidence substantially undermining an essential element with established Bank of Nova Scotia prejudice. Under Marks v. United States, 430 U.S. 188, the four-Justice rule is the controlling logical subset: every known, directly negating omission that plainly defeats probable cause, materially distorts the presentation and causes the stated prejudice also satisfies Stone’s broader rule. The five judgment supporters together establish its force. Stone is not represented as formally joining the narrower qualification. The four-Justice and broader one-Justice applications are not combined to remove that qualification from the sufficient ground recognized here. Neither the plurality nor the five judgment supporters adopt an exclusive rule forbidding relief in every other circumstance.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court recognizes a confined power to prevent a prosecutor from materially misleading the grand jury while preserving its independent charging function. Bank of Nova Scotia requires actual charging prejudice before dismissal for nonconstitutional misconduct; that safeguard remains necessary here. Calandra recognizes the grand jury’s protective function and legally enforceable limits on its process. The disclosure duty concerns personally known, substantial evidence directly negating guilt. Evidence plainly defeating probable cause supplies a sufficient case for dismissal when the separate prejudice condition is met. Costello protects an otherwise lawful presentation from judicial reassessment of the quality or quantity of the evidence, and Calandra preserves the grand jury’s freedom from ordinary trial restrictions. Neither requires acceptance of the materially distorted presentation covered by this narrow rule. The institutional objection limits supervision but does not eliminate it. Here the accepted question concerns authority; the specific evidentiary application is not reviewed. A lawful new presentation remains possible. Applications outside this sufficient dismissal class, constitutional duties, state grand juries and negligent discovery remain unresolved.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Bank of Nova Scotia v. United States, 487 U.S. 250 — its prejudice requirement is retained; limited supervisory power is extended to the specified known-evidence omission. Its evidence-reliability prohibition survives for lawful presentations.
- Costello v. United States, 350 U.S. 359 — distinguished: the confined misconduct inquiry is not a challenge to the competence or sufficiency of evidence in an otherwise lawful presentation. Its hearsay and ordinary adequacy rules remain.
- United States v. Calandra, 414 U.S. 338 — preserved for grand-jury independence and freedom from trial evidence rules; no exclusionary-rule extension occurs.
- United States v. Mechanik, 475 U.S. 66 — its completed-trial harmlessness holding is not extended to erase a prejudicial violation challenged and resolved before trial; the charging-prejudice formulation adopted in Bank remains operative.
- Brady v. Maryland, 373 U.S. 83 — not transplanted into grand-jury proceedings; its trial disclosure duty remains unchanged.
- Marks v. United States, 430 U.S. 188 — applied to the genuine logical subset of the five judgment supporters’ rules; the shared authority supports the sufficient dismissal ground, without creating a single majority merits opinion or an exclusive boundary on other relief.
- United States v. Page, 808 F.2d 723 — the judgment applying it in Williams is sustained against the categorical no-authority challenge; Page’s full disclosure formulation and any rule requiring every fact relevant to a possible defense are not adopted.

### Application, limits and questions not reached

The affirmed dismissal binds this case. The Government presented the existence of supervisory authority, rather than a renewed challenge to the nature of the omitted evidence. The four-Justice writing does not independently find that these accounting documents plainly preclude probable cause; it preserves its doubt about the lower courts’ reasonable-doubt formulation while rejecting the categorical no-power answer on the question granted. Stone separately finds that the supported hearing findings satisfy his broader material-distortion and prejudice rule. Accordingly, affirmance does not establish that any evidence bearing on intent necessarily meets the narrower controlling test, nor does it impose a new finding that the prosecutor knowingly fabricated evidence. No determination of guilt or innocence follows. A distinct negligent-discovery claim, state-grand-jury duty, constitutional disclosure rule and immunity or fabrication claim remain open.

**Law after decision:** Effective May 4, federal supervisory authority supports the limited disclosure duty and prejudice-conditioned dismissal stated in Holdings. The sufficient dismissal ground includes the plainly-precluding-probable-cause qualification; applications outside that class remain unresolved. That qualification does not define the disclosure duty itself. Stone’s broader material-distortion application is not a general holding. Review of the passed-upon question is permitted on the specified federal appellate posture. Trial disclosure, ordinary probable-cause charging, grand-jury evidentiary latitude and the right to seek a lawful new indictment remain.

#### Holmes correction insertion — published separate positions, March 24, 1992

O'Connor, joined by White and Stevens, agrees with the causal and SIPA grounds but would answer the purchaser-seller question. Section 1964(c) creates an express action for a person injured in business or property by reason of a RICO violation; Blue Chip Stamps shaped an implied securities action and does not add a purchaser-seller requirement to that enacted right. His statutory-scope account is separate from the Court's borrowed-antitrust explanation; it does not provide a Court holding on the reserved question.

Stone concurs while joining the Court. No other Justice joins it, and it creates no additional controlling holding.

For continuity, Scalia also treats proximate cause and the protected class of statutory interests as distinct background limits that can vary with predicate offenses, including his qualification concerning multiple predicates.
