## Controlling holdings

### 1. Distinct parts and service demand and conditional sales present a triable tying claim

**Holding and operative rule.** For a Sherman Act § 1 tying claim, functionally related products are distinct when sufficient separate consumer demand makes separate provision efficient. Evidence that Kodak parts and service meet that test, and that Kodak conditions parts sales on buying its service or forgoing independent service, creates genuine disputes about distinct products and conditioning; respondents may proceed but must prove all remaining tying elements.

**Authority.** Blackmun’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Kennedy, and Souter, controls with six votes, including the author. The commerce volume is not disputed here; no liability judgment follows.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that practical consumer demand, rather than a product’s functional relationship to another product, determines whether the two are distinct for tying purposes. Jefferson Parish Hospital District No. 2 v. Hyde supplies that demand-based inquiry and the requirements of tying power and a substantial amount of tied commerce. The record shows that independent organizations have supplied service separately, that customers have preferred independent service, and that some customers service their own equipment. Those materials permit a finding that separate provision is efficient. Kodak’s restrictions on parts sales to independent organizations and on customers’ use of independent service also permit a finding of conditioning. The fact that parts are installed during service does not conclusively combine both into one product. Respondents still must establish the necessary market power and other elements; the Court holds only that the district court could not end this claim for want of separate products or an arguable tie.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Jefferson Parish Hospital District No. 2 v. Hyde, 466 U.S. 2 (1984): applied for separate demand, conditioning, tying power, and tied commerce; functional integration alone does not merge products.
- Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317 (1986): applied to preserve respondents’ burden of producing evidence on the elements challenged at summary judgment.

### 2. Equipment-market competition is relevant but not a categorical defense

**Holding and operative rule.** Competition in an equipment market does not as a matter of law preclude market power in its parts or service markets. Under Rule 56, a party cannot obtain summary judgment solely through an economic prediction contradicted by evidence permitting a rational inference of power, including material information and switching costs and actual price or exclusion effects; unsupported speculation remains insufficient.

**Authority.** Blackmun’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Kennedy, and Souter, controls with six votes. The holding rejects a conclusive defense, not the relevance of equipment competition.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that Kodak’s proposed equipment-market constraint must be tested against the record. Matsushita Electric Industrial Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp. requires a plausible inference from evidence; it does not authorize a court to replace disputed facts with an economic model. Here the alleged conduct can produce immediate aftermarket gains, unlike the prolonged and implausible loss-producing conspiracy examined there. Evidence of costly equipment replacement and difficult lifetime service-price comparisons permits a finding that some installed customers cannot readily escape unfavorable terms. Evidence of price and exclusion effects supports testing that account at trial. Kodak may show that informed new purchasers and equipment rivalry adequately discipline its policies, but that conclusion is not inevitable as a matter of law. Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc. preserves the factfinder’s role when a genuine dispute could affect the result. The Court neither presumes power from imperfect information nor treats every durable-product seller as a monopolist.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Matsushita Electric Industrial Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp., 475 U.S. 574 (1986): applied as a rational-inference requirement and distinguished on the alleged economic mechanism; it creates no categorical equipment-market defense.
- Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242 (1986): applied to identify genuine material disputes; evidence and reasonable inferences receive the ordinary Rule 56 treatment.
- Jefferson Parish Hospital District No. 2 v. Hyde, 466 U.S. 2 (1984): preserved; respondents must prove actual tying power despite rejection of a conclusive equipment-competition defense.

### 3. A single brand may define a relevant market when actual substitutes do not constrain it

**Holding and operative rule.** A relevant product market may consist of one brand’s parts or service when the actual commercial alternatives and competitive constraints support that definition; brand identity alone neither establishes nor defeats such a market. Evidence of Kodak-specific compatibility, a very high parts share, a high service share, and entry and switching barriers creates a genuine dispute about monopoly power sufficient to resist summary judgment on § 2.

**Authority.** Blackmun’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Kennedy, and Souter, controls with six votes. The record permits a market and power finding; it does not conclusively establish either.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that market boundaries turn on the alternatives buyers can actually use. United States v. Grinnell Corp. requires monopoly power in a relevant market, and United States v. E. I. du Pont de Nemours & Co. directs attention to substitution and competitive restraint. Owners needing compatible parts cannot necessarily substitute another brand’s parts, and changing equipment may cost substantially more than the aftermarket service at issue. Respondents also present evidence of Kodak’s dominant parts position, high service share, and obstacles to independent supply. Together these materials permit an inference of power to control prices or exclude competition. Kodak’s equipment competitors remain relevant because they may constrain lifetime prices; the Court does not exclude that evidence. But their existence does not settle the market question. The result establishes neither a single-brand rule for all manufacturers nor a monopoly presumption from a patent, a proprietary design, or numerical share considered apart from actual constraints.

**Precedent treatment.**

- United States v. Grinnell Corp., 384 U.S. 563 (1966): applied to require monopoly power in a relevant market, distinct from exclusionary conduct.
- United States v. E. I. du Pont de Nemours & Co., 351 U.S. 377 (1956): applied to practical substitution and competitive constraints; no categorical prohibition on a one-brand market follows.

### 4. Exclusionary maintenance and Kodak’s business explanations require factual determination

**Holding and operative rule.** Monopolization under § 2 requires monopoly power plus willful acquisition or maintenance of that power, distinguished from success through a superior product, business acumen, or accident. Evidence of parts-access restrictions that may exclude service rivals creates a genuine issue of unlawful maintenance, and Kodak’s asserted quality, inventory, and free-riding explanations do not conclusively eliminate it on this record; no general duty to supply rivals or final finding of pretext is imposed.

**Authority.** Blackmun’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Kennedy, and Souter, controls with six votes. Attempted monopolization remains a distinct claim with its own intent and dangerous-probability requirements, not a consequence automatically established by this holding.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the reasons and competitive effect of Kodak’s restrictions cannot be settled on this summary-judgment record. Grinnell distinguishes unlawful maintenance of monopoly power from lawful commercial success. Aspen Skiing Co. v. Aspen Highlands Skiing Corp. makes the character of challenged exclusion and its business justification material without imposing a general obligation to help competitors. Respondents’ evidence permits a finding that restricting access to necessary parts protects Kodak’s service position. The asserted quality rationale must be considered with the continued self-service exception and evidence about independent service. Inventory explanations must be tested against evidence that equipment failures and parts needs do not change with the provider, while supplier restrictions may impede alternative sources. Calling independent service free riding because the providers did not enter the equipment and parts businesses does not conclusively justify exclusion. Kodak may prove legitimate reasons and defeat liability. The Court awards no damages, orders no compulsory supply, and leaves ultimate liability and injury for further proceedings.

**Precedent treatment.**

- United States v. Grinnell Corp., 384 U.S. 563 (1966): applied to distinguish willful monopoly maintenance from lawful success.
- Aspen Skiing Co. v. Aspen Highlands Skiing Corp., 472 U.S. 585 (1985): applied to the challenged conduct and offered business reasons; no universal obligation to deal is created.
- Brunswick Corp. v. Pueblo Bowl-O-Mat, Inc., 429 U.S. 477 (1977): preserved; injury to a competitor alone does not establish antitrust injury or entitlement to damages.

## Law after the decision and reserved questions

Effective June 8, 1992, equipment competition remains relevant evidence but cannot be invoked as a categorical legal bar to a sufficiently supported aftermarket claim. Separate demand may distinguish parts from service; a single brand may comprise a relevant market where actual substitutes and constraints support that conclusion. Power and exclusion remain separate elements, and commercial success through superior products remains lawful. The business reasons offered for the challenged exclusion require factual evaluation here.

The Court applies received tying and monopolization law with ordinary Rule 56 burdens; it creates no automatic patent or brand monopoly, no universal duty to deal, and no present recovery right. Monopolization and attempt remain distinct. Ultimate markets, power, unlawful conduct, antitrust injury, causation, damages, and remedial design remain for further proceedings. Holmes’s actual March 24 RICO directness and SIPA participation holdings remain effective but do not decide these disputed Sherman Act elements or establish injury here. No prior decision is overruled.

## Published separate writings and complete noncontrolling continuity

Scalia, joined by O’Connor and Thomas, would reverse and restore summary judgment on both preserved claims. They regard ordinary dependence on a brand’s replacement parts after buying durable equipment as derivative power that equipment competition generally disciplines. In their view, information and switching costs of the kind asserted do not warrant extending the per se tying category or treating every brand-specific aftermarket as an antitrust monopoly absent the necessary primary-market power. They consider the Court’s approach liable to burden ordinary integration, distribution choices, and proprietary component sales without the competitive power the Sherman Act requires. Their disagreement concerns the legal significance of this asserted aftermarket dependence and the sufficiency of the evidence, not a finding of respondents’ entitlement to final relief. The separate rule-of-reason route was not preserved below. This writing is noncontrolling; it does not displace the Court’s evidence-based inquiry or create an immunity for manufacturers.
