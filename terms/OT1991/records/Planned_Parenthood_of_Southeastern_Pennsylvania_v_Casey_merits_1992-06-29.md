## Controlling holdings

### 1. Protected choice and the boundary of the decision

**Holding and operative rule.** The Fourteenth Amendment protects a woman’s choice to obtain an abortion before viability against prohibition and substantial state interference. Roe’s protection after viability remains subject to the State’s authority to restrict abortion with an exception when necessary to preserve the woman’s life or health; the State has legitimate health and potential-life interests throughout pregnancy.

**Authority.** Joint opinion Part I of O’Connor, Kennedy and Souter, joined by Stone, Blackmun and Stevens: six Justices adopt these propositions. This majority does not adopt a single general standard for all regulation of abortion.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court retains the central constitutional protection recognized in Roe. Griswold, Eisenstadt and Carey protect personal reproductive decisions and refute the argument that liberty extends only to activities individually named in the Constitution. Roe also recognizes the State’s interests in maternal health and potential life. Those interests do not authorize prohibition of the protected previability choice. The central boundary remains workable, and neither subsequent doctrine nor the record’s medical facts have removed its constitutional foundation. Reliance includes decisions about family life and personal futures; it is not confined to commercial arrangements. Webster did not overrule this protection. Disagreement with a prior decision, or a change in the Court’s membership, is not itself a sufficient reason to withdraw it. The Court decides the specified regulations and preserves the existing viability and life-and-health boundary. It announces no constitutional fetal-personhood rule and adjudicates no unchallenged postviability prohibition.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Roe v. Wade, 410 U.S. 113 — core choice, viability and necessary life/health protection retained; specific regulatory treatment appears below.
- Doe v. Bolton, 410 U.S. 179 — life-and-health safeguard retained.
- Griswold v. Connecticut, 381 U.S. 479 — applied to reproductive liberty.
- Eisenstadt v. Baird, 405 U.S. 438 — applied to individual reproductive choice.
- Carey v. Population Services International, 431 U.S. 678 — applied to reproductive liberty, without mechanically resolving abortion regulation.
- Webster v. Reproductive Health Services, 492 U.S. 490 — its fragmentation did not overrule Roe’s core.

### 2. The protective emergency construction

**Holding and operative rule.** Section 3203 is sustained as construed to permit a physician exercising good-faith clinical judgment to proceed without compliance with the Act’s consent and notice requirements when compliance would significantly threaten the woman’s life or health, including the three conditions disputed here. The physician need not await the threatened serious injury before acting; contrary enforcement remains challengeable.

**Authority.** Joint opinion Part II-A, joined by all nine Justices. This common construction does not adopt Stone’s additional mental-health discussion as a nine-Justice holding.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court evaluates the provision as the Court of Appeals construed it. Brockett supports respect for a lower federal court’s supportable construction of state law. The construction here prevents compliance with the abortion regulations from producing a significant threat to life or health. It covers preeclampsia, inevitable abortion and prematurely ruptured membranes, the three conditions on which the facial objection rests. A rule directed to serious risk must permit treatment before the anticipated injury occurs. The Court therefore rejects the argument that compliance must continue until the threatened harm becomes irreversible. This reading resolves the claimed exclusion of the disputed conditions without supplying hypothetical findings or a judicial list of further emergencies. The disclosure-specific defense in §3205(c) does not replace the emergency exception. Neither this judgment nor the adopted construction approves enforcement requiring harmful delay in an actual case.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Brockett v. Spokane Arcades, Inc., 472 U.S. 491 — applied to respect a supportable state-law construction.
- Doe v. Bolton, 410 U.S. 179 — its protection of medical judgment remains; no exhaustive emergency list is adopted.

### 3. Facial vagueness of the clinical standard

**Holding and operative rule.** Section 3203’s familiar medical judgments and subjective good-faith clinical standard provide constitutionally adequate warning on this facial record. The provision is not sustained on an assumed power to impose hindsight objective liability contrary to that construction.

**Authority.** Joint opinion Part II-B, O’Connor, Kennedy and Souter joined by White, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia and Thomas: eight ground votes. Stone sustains the emergency provision on his approved protective construction and does not join this additional ground.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court rejects the contention that the emergency definition gives physicians constitutionally inadequate notice. Vuitch and Doe recognize that physicians routinely determine whether a procedure is required to preserve a patient’s life or health. Those ordinary medical judgments do not become impermissibly vague merely because their application requires clinical evaluation. The provision here expressly uses the physician’s good-faith judgment, and the adopted construction protects decisions made under that subjective standard. Colautti presented uncertainty about the governing viability trigger and whether the physician faced an objective or subjective obligation. That uncertainty does not control this different text. Together with the life-and-health construction, the good-faith standard gives fair warning on the present facial record. The Court does not approve an enforcement practice that disregards that standard, and it does not determine a physician’s liability in any actual prosecution or disciplinary proceeding.

**Precedent treatment.**

- United States v. Vuitch, 402 U.S. 62 — applied to ordinary medical judgments.
- Doe v. Bolton, 410 U.S. 179 — applied to clinical judgment.
- Colautti v. Franklin, 439 U.S. 379 — distinguished because of its different uncertain viability trigger.

### 4. Ordinary medical information and voluntary written consent

**Holding and operative rule.** The State may require voluntary written informed consent and accurate, medically relevant disclosure of the procedure, material risks and alternatives, and childbirth risks. Accurate gestational-age information is also permissible informed-consent content; these content holdings do not themselves decide who must speak or how long the patient must wait.

**Authority.** Joint opinion Part IV-A has all nine Justices on the ordinary written-consent and medical-risk proposition; Stevens alone does not join sustaining compulsory gestational-age content. Stone and Blackmun’s content joins do not approve the separately disputed physician and timing conditions.

**Controlling explanation.** Danforth permits written informed consent because a patient should understand the nature and consequences of the procedure she chooses. The disclosures concerning the abortion procedure, material risks and alternatives, and the medical risks of carrying the pregnancy to term address that purpose directly. Their content enables a medical choice rather than presuming that the patient has made the wrong choice. Accurate gestational age also describes the pregnancy to which that decision relates. The content holding remains distinct from the conditions under which information must be supplied. Accepting truthful medical information does not by itself approve compulsory state persuasion, exclusive physician delivery or a fixed waiting period. Those restrictions receive separate judgments and grounds below. Nor does this holding convert an optional printed publication into mandatory reading. It preserves ordinary medical advice and a written record of lawful consent while leaving the disputed conditions to their own analysis.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Danforth, 428 U.S. 52 — applied to voluntary informed written consent.
- Akron, 462 U.S. 416 — the permissibility of relevant medical information is preserved; its delivery and timing restrictions receive separate treatment below.

### 5. Prescribed information about alternatives, assistance and support

**Holding and operative rule.** The specified truthful and nonmisleading disclosures about available state materials, assistance and paternal support may be required on this facial record, with receipt of printed materials left to the woman’s choice. Under the narrow judgment-supporting rationale, the legitimate informed-choice and potential-life purposes may be served because these requirements do not establish a purpose or effect creating a substantial obstacle; certification may enforce the requirements sustained in this decision.

**Authority.** Joint opinion Part IV-B. O’Connor, Kennedy and Souter’s joint opinion supplies this narrow judgment-supporting permission; White, Scalia and Thomas concur under broader rational review. Under Marks, the permission is a true logical subset of that broader rationale, and the six writers support this same judgment component. Stone, Blackmun and Stevens dissent. This is not a majority adoption of the general Part III undue-burden formula.

**Controlling explanation.** Danforth’s informed-consent principle permits a State to ensure that a patient has relevant information before choosing. The disclosures here tell the woman that printed materials and assistance may be available and that the father bears support obligations, subject to the stated rape qualification. Their truth and nonmisleading character matter. The woman need not accept or read the printed materials. The State may hope that information leads her to choose childbirth; that preference alone does not transform accurate advice into an unconstitutional obstacle under the narrower rationale supporting this judgment. The facial record does not establish the necessary obstruction from these disclosures. Thornburgh and Akron require a different result insofar as they prohibit such truthful prescribed information because it favors childbirth. Their contrary rules are displaced to this extent. This decision does not permit misinformation, ideological assent or materially different coercive disclosures, and written certification remains tied to duties actually sustained.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Danforth — informed-consent principle applied.
- Thornburgh — contrary prescribed-information holding overruled to the extent described.
- Akron — contrary prescribed-information holding overruled to the extent described.
- Marks, 430 U.S. 188 — the narrower judgment-supporting permission controls this component.

### 6. Physician delivery of medical information

**Holding and operative rule.** Pennsylvania’s requirement that the performing or referring physician deliver the specified medical information survives this facial challenge. Under the narrow judgment-supporting rationale, the requirement reasonably serves informed medical choice and the record does not establish a purpose or effect creating a substantial obstacle; no categorical permission for all physician-exclusive counseling follows.

**Authority.** Joint opinion Part IV-B. O’Connor, Kennedy and Souter’s joint opinion supplies this narrow judgment-supporting permission; White, Scalia and Thomas concur under broader rational review. Under Marks, the permission is a true logical subset of that broader rationale, and the six writers support this same judgment component. Stone, Blackmun and Stevens dissent. This is not a majority adoption of the general Part III undue-burden formula.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court separates the qualifications of the speaker from the information’s content. The requirement concerns medical facts about the procedure, its risks and alternatives, the pregnancy and childbirth. A State may reasonably conclude that a physician can explain those subjects and answer resulting medical questions. The record shows that qualified counselors can convey information and that physician delivery may increase cost. Those facts support the opposing view, but they do not establish a substantial obstacle under the narrower rationale supporting the judgment. The physician requirement is therefore sustained on this facial record. Akron’s rule invalidating this restriction notwithstanding that showing cannot remain controlling to the contrary extent. The holding does not require a physician to perform every counseling task or establish that every physician-only requirement is valid. It also does not alter the physician’s responsibility for appropriate medical judgment or decide a later challenge supported by materially different access burdens.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Akron — its contrary physician-delivery holding overruled to the extent it requires invalidation under the rule applied here.
- Marks — the narrower judgment-supporting permission controls the physician-delivery component.

### 7. The 24-hour interval

**Holding and operative rule.** The 24-hour interval between the required disclosures and abortion survives this facial record under the narrow judgment-supporting rule permitting reasonable informed-choice measures that do not have the purpose or effect of creating a substantial obstacle. The emergency exception continues to protect prompt care, and materially different demonstrated burdens remain open.

**Authority.** Joint opinion Part IV-B. O’Connor, Kennedy and Souter’s joint opinion supplies this narrow judgment-supporting permission; White, Scalia and Thomas concur under broader rational review. Under Marks, the permission is a true logical subset of that broader rationale, and the six writers support this same judgment component. Stone, Blackmun and Stevens dissent. This is not a majority adoption of the general Part III undue-burden formula.

**Controlling explanation.** The 24-hour condition is assessed independently of the information that begins the interval. The record establishes added travel, expense, repeat visits and delays that may exceed a single day, particularly for women with limited resources. The narrower rationale supporting the judgment does not deny those burdens. It concludes that the record does not establish a substantial obstacle and that time for considered choice reasonably advances the State’s legitimate interests. Maternal health is not the State’s only permissible concern; the decision also concerns potential life. The emergency construction prevents required waiting when compliance would significantly threaten life or health. Akron’s contrary mandatory-delay holding is overruled to the extent it requires invalidation of this rule. This is a facial judgment on the existing evidence, not a finding that every woman can bear every delay. A different actual application or materially different proof remains subject to constitutional review.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Akron — contrary mandatory-waiting holding overruled to the stated extent.
- Marks — applied to this particular sustaining judgment, without adopting the joint general test as a majority opinion.

### 8. The providers’ distinct speech claim

**Holding and operative rule.** The First Amendment does not invalidate the truthful, nonmisleading factual disclosures at issue as reasonable regulation of the provision of medical services. This holding authorizes no compelled ideological endorsement and does not classify all professional speech as commercial speech.

**Authority.** Joint opinion Part IV-C, joined on this independent provider-speech proposition by White, Scalia and Thomas in addition to O’Connor, Kennedy and Souter: six ground votes. Stone joins rejection of the separate providers’ First Amendment claim by the approved silent-component fallback and adds no ground; his contrary abortion-liberty judgment is unchanged. Blackmun and Stevens approve ordinary medical disclosure but do not reach an additional speech ground against the persuasion commands they independently invalidate.

**Controlling explanation.** The providers assert their own First Amendment interests in addition to the patients’ protected abortion choice. The challenged factual disclosures do not require a physician to affirm that abortion is morally wrong or to profess agreement with the State’s preference. They require accurate information in providing a regulated medical service. The Court’s conclusion depends on that limited character. The State’s power to regulate informed consent would be defeated if every factual disclosure requirement necessarily infringed the provider’s freedom of speech. Conversely, describing a medical service as commercial does not authorize unrelated ideological commands. The Court rejects the providers’ facial claim on the truthful and nonmisleading disclosures presented here. This conclusion neither establishes the validity of every possible professional-speech restriction nor supplies the rule governing the distinct burdens on the patient’s abortion decision. Those burdens receive their separate constitutional analysis, and the Justices’ different votes on that analysis remain distinct.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Danforth — its recognition of informed-consent regulation supplies the medical-service setting, not a universal First Amendment exemption. The judgment below’s broader reliance on a commercial-speech classification is not adopted as a general rule.

### 9. The independent parental bypass

**Holding and operative rule.** When a State conditions an unemancipated minor’s abortion on parental consent, it must provide an effective independent route to authorization alternatively on maturity and informed choice or on the minor’s best interests, with confidentiality and expedition and without requiring a prior parental request. Pennsylvania’s basic one-parent/bypass structure satisfies those requirements on this facial record.

**Authority.** Joint opinion Part IV-D’s constitutional bypass rule, O’Connor, Kennedy and Souter joined by Stone, Blackmun and Stevens: six votes. White, Scalia and Thomas sustain the basic procedure under broader rational review without joining the constitutionally necessary safeguards; the basic-structure judgment is nine to zero. Incorporated counseling, timing and delivery conditions are separately addressed.

**Controlling explanation.** Danforth forbids giving a parent an absolute veto over the minor’s protected decision. Bellotti II and Ashcroft require an effective independent route when parental consent is imposed, and Pennsylvania supplies that alternative. A mature and informed minor may obtain authorization on her own choice, and an immature minor may obtain it when abortion is in her best interests. She need not first seek a parent’s consent, so access to court does not depend on a parent’s willingness to cooperate. Counsel and confidentiality protect the proceeding. The trial court must act within three business days and the appellate court within five more business days. Ohio v. Akron Center and Hodgson support evaluating those practical safeguards without assuming that they will fail. Emergencies require prompt care. The Court sustains the basic structure while preserving review of an actually unavailable bypass and separately deciding the disputed informed-parent conditions.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Danforth — prohibition of an absolute parental veto applied.
- Bellotti v. Baird, 443 U.S. 622 — alternative maturity or best-interests route, confidentiality and expedition applied.
- Ashcroft, 462 U.S. 476 — sufficient bypass applied.
- Ohio v. Akron Center, 497 U.S. 502 — practical confidentiality and expedition applied.
- Hodgson, 497 U.S. 417 — effective independent alternative applied.

### 10. Incorporated informed-parental requirements

**Holding and operative rule.** The actual informed-parental-consent requirement survives this facial challenge because its incorporated disclosures and related conditions survive the separate judgments in this decision and the independent bypass remains available. The narrower sustaining rule does not permit an invalid substantive condition to be revived by requiring a parent’s signature.

**Authority.** Joint opinion Part IV-D. O’Connor, Kennedy and Souter’s joint opinion supplies this narrow judgment-supporting permission; White, Scalia and Thomas concur under broader rational review. Under Marks, the permission is a true logical subset of that broader rationale, and the six writers support this same judgment component. Stone, Blackmun and Stevens dissent. This is not a majority adoption of the general Part III undue-burden formula.

**Controlling explanation.** A valid bypass does not answer every objection to the information required for parental consent. The Court therefore considers the incorporation separately. The challenged disclosures and conditions have been sustained on this facial record, and asking that the participating parent be informed does not itself establish an additional substantial obstacle under the narrower judgment-supporting rationale. The independent judicial procedure remains available when the minor elects not to obtain parental consent. Bellotti II and Ashcroft support that route; neither permits a parent to exercise an absolute veto. The opposing Justices would remove the incorporated commands because they would invalidate those commands in the adult informed-consent provision. The controlling judgments reject that premise for the requirements sustained here. No rule authorizes incorporation of an unconstitutional duty, delay that defeats the bypass or failure to provide emergency care. Severability and implementation must conform to the actual component holdings.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Bellotti II — independent route applied without authorization to incorporate an invalid duty.
- Ashcroft — sufficient bypass applied without authorization to incorporate an invalid duty.
- Marks — applied only to the narrow sustaining rationale for the complete informed-parent component.

### 11. The operation of spousal notice

**Holding and operative rule.** Section 3209’s compulsory spousal notice, certification and dependent enforcement are unconstitutional on this record because they transfer practical control of the protected abortion decision through coercion that the statutory exceptions do not adequately prevent. A formal distinction between notice and a signature veto does not save that operation.

**Authority.** Joint opinion Part V-A, O’Connor, Kennedy and Souter joined by Stone, Blackmun and Stevens: six votes. The distinct facial denominator rule is addressed next; Stone does not join that further formulation.

**Controlling explanation.** Danforth prevents the State from transferring control of a woman’s abortion decision to her spouse. The formal absence of a spouse’s consent requirement does not resolve the practical operation of this notice rule. The trial record establishes that compulsory disclosure can produce violence, economic pressure and other coercion preventing a woman from obtaining an abortion. The exceptions do not cover all serious coercion or threatened harm to others, and requiring an assault report may itself defeat the claimed protection. A spouse’s interest in family life does not authorize making such disclosure a legal condition of treatment of another competent adult. The parent-minor cases concern different legal responsibilities and an independent bypass; they do not support this adult-spouse condition. The Court therefore preserves injunctive relief against §3209 and its dependent enforcement. Voluntary consultation remains unrestricted, and the Court does not adjudicate every conceivable notice arrangement.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Danforth — protection against transferred spousal control applied to notice’s actual operation.
- Hodgson — distinguished as a parent-minor procedure with different authority and safeguards.
- Ohio v. Akron Center — distinguished as a parent-minor procedure with different authority and safeguards.

### 12. The class relevant to this facial challenge

**Holding and operative rule.** For the §3209 facial challenge, the relevant class consists of women for whom the law imposes a restriction, including married women who would not otherwise notify and lack an applicable exception. The rule is invalid because a large fraction of that class faces a substantial obstacle; voluntary notifiers and other women outside the restriction do not supply the denominator that defeats the claim.

**Authority.** Joint opinion Part V-B, O’Connor, Kennedy and Souter joined by Blackmun and Stevens: five votes. Stone’s additional notice-invalidity vote adopts neither a percentage threshold nor this further formulation.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court assesses the notice requirement where it actually restricts conduct. Most married women’s voluntary consultation says little about the effect of compelling those who would not give notice and lack an applicable exception. The record identifies serious coercive obstacles within that constrained group. For a large fraction of those women, the rule prevents the protected decision from being exercised freely. Counting every woman who seeks an abortion would dilute that constitutional inquiry with persons upon whom the challenged command imposes no restriction. Salerno’s broad facial-challenge language does not require that result in this abortion challenge. Danforth and the abortion cases examine the operation of categorical burdens on the protected choice. The Court therefore rejects the proposed all-patients denominator. It does not announce a fixed numerical percentage, determine a universal denominator for all statutes or replace facial-review doctrine in unrelated constitutional settings.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Danforth — categorical protection of abortion choice applied.
- Salerno, 481 U.S. 739 — not applied to defeat this abortion claim merely because other persons voluntarily do what the challenged law commands; general facial-review doctrine otherwise reserved.

### 13. The notice-dependent report

**Holding and operative rule.** Section 3214(a)(12)’s reporting of marital and notice status and reasons for failing to notify is invalid as part of the unconstitutional notice machinery. A report cannot preserve the invalid condition by demanding the information that the condition unlawfully requires; remand must address severability and dependent enforcement.

**Authority.** Joint opinion Part V-A’s dependent-report proposition, joined by Stone, Blackmun and Stevens with O’Connor, Kennedy and Souter: six votes. Its rationale follows the notice invalidation rather than approving a general ban on marital or medical data.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court considers the notice paragraph separately from the ordinary medical reports. Section 3214(a)(12) asks whether the patient is married, whether her spouse received notice and why notice was not given. Those questions serve the notice arrangement the Court holds unconstitutional. The State cannot retain the same disclosure machinery merely by placing its demands on a report sent by the provider. Danforth permits legitimate health records while forbidding their use to accomplish an unconstitutional restriction, and that limit resolves this dependency. The report’s confidentiality does not cure its connection to the invalid condition. This conclusion does not prohibit independently justified health information or decide that every possible inquiry into marital status is unlawful. It invalidates this notice-linked paragraph on the actual statutory arrangement. The courts on remand must conform the injunction and determine Pennsylvania-law severability while preserving independently functioning valid provisions.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Danforth — the limit against using reporting to accomplish an unconstitutional restriction applied.
- The ruling on §3209 — controls its reporting dependency within this coordinated decision.

### 14. Confidential medical-judgment and referral reports

**Holding and operative rule.** The challenged non-notice procedure reports, including the specified medical-judgment bases and referring-physician identification, are sustained under Danforth because they have concrete health and lawful compliance purposes, protect patient confidentiality and are not shown to use recordkeeping detail to accomplish an unconstitutional restriction. This native reporting rule does not adopt a new general substantial-obstacle threshold.

**Authority.** Joint opinion Part VI-A, O’Connor, Kennedy and Souter joined by Stone and Stevens: five votes at this specified level. White, Scalia and Thomas concur in the judgment under rational review; Blackmun dissents.

**Controlling explanation.** Danforth permits confidential health reporting and forbids using recordkeeping detail to accomplish an otherwise unconstitutional restriction. These reports identify procedures, gestation, complications and the medical judgments supporting lawful exceptions. Referring physicians can perform consent and gestational-age functions, making their identity relevant to those responsibilities. The established confidentiality differs from the threatened patient exposure addressed in Thornburgh. The recorded expense and possible reluctance of referring physicians do not establish the use of these particular reports to restrict protected care contrary to Danforth. The Court relies on those limited health-purpose, confidentiality and anti-restriction grounds, not on agreement about a general threshold governing every abortion regulation. This conclusion approves neither patient identification nor materially different burdens. Reports also cannot enforce an invalid duty: the notice paragraph is excluded for that reason. Independently operating valid health records remain subject to the severability proceedings required by the remand.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Danforth — confidential health reporting and its anti-restriction limit applied.
- Thornburgh — patient-exposure rule distinguished on the established confidentiality; no general patient-identification permission is created.

### 15. Public facility and aggregate reports

**Holding and operative rule.** The specified public facility-identity, affiliation and aggregate reports survive this facial challenge when state appropriations trigger public access because they concern institutional accountability, do not identify patients and lack an established connection to an additional unconstitutional restriction on protected care. The ruling does not approve materially different disclosures, reprisals or reporting burdens.

**Authority.** Joint opinion Part VI-A’s public-report application, O’Connor, Kennedy and Souter joined by Stone and Stevens: five votes. White, Scalia and Thomas concur in the judgment; Blackmun dissents. The independent funds-condition objection is separately resolved below.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court separates public institutional information from private patient information. Sections 3207(b) and 3214(f) make specified facility and aggregate reports public when the statutory state-funding conditions apply. Those reports identify institutions, affiliations and activity, rather than individual patients. Public accountability supplies a concrete reason for access to information concerning the use of appropriations. The record describes demonstrations and concern about increased harassment, but it does not establish that these particular disclosures cause an additional unconstitutional restriction on protected care. Thornburgh’s patient-exposure problem is therefore absent from this record. This conclusion does not disregard a demonstrated risk of identification or reprisals in a different case. It sustains only the particular disclosures challenged here, with their funding and institutional-information limits. The underlying filing duties were not themselves the target of this public-access objection, and no new duty to disclose patient identity follows.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Thornburgh — distinguished because the present public reports do not expose individual patients.
- Danforth — its anti-restriction principle remains; confidential medical reporting does not itself authorize every public disclosure.

### 16. The distinct public-funds condition objection

**Holding and operative rule.** Making the specified institutional and aggregate reports of a state-funded facility public does not, on this record, impose an unconstitutional condition on a benefit: no surrender of privately financed protected abortion activity or demonstrated additional access obstacle is established. The State receives no general authority to prohibit privately financed abortions as a condition of support.

**Authority.** Joint opinion Part VI-B, joined by White, Stevens, Scalia and Thomas with O’Connor, Kennedy and Souter: seven ground votes. Stone sustains the public-report component on his approved reporting/privacy ground without joining this additional theory. Blackmun opposes the challenged public disclosures.

**Controlling explanation.** The challenged condition makes limited information about institutions receiving state appropriations available to the public. It does not require a recipient to cease providing abortions with private resources, and the reports do not identify the patients. Public accountability is a legitimate reason to disclose the institutions and aggregate services supported by public money. The record establishes concern about demonstrations but does not establish the necessary connection between this disclosure and a new barrier to protected care. The unconstitutional-conditions objection therefore supplies no independent ground to invalidate these provisions. The Court does not infer from the State’s power to define a funded program that it may suppress a recipient’s separately financed protected activity. That distinction limits the judgment. The underlying institutional filing duties were not themselves the target of this public-access challenge, and the Court announces no new rule compelling disclosure of patient identities or excusing a proven burden on access.

**Precedent treatment.**

- The distinction between a funded program and separately financed activity remains; no general funding doctrine is enlarged. The judgment does not adopt every step of the Court of Appeals’ unconstitutional-conditions analysis.
