# Canonical Decision Record — United States v. Alvarez-Machain

## Event and chronology

Natural key: United States v. Alvarez-Machain, No. 91-712, merits, June 15, 1992, October Term 1991. 504 U.S. 655 (historical reporter reference). Argument April 1, 1992; official date provenance. Certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) to the Ninth Circuit, 946 F.2d 1466 (October18,1991), affirming the treaty-based bar to trial and repatriation order and stating that dismissal was required.

Question: Whether the United States–Mexico Extradition Treaty bars this prosecution following a DEA-sponsored forcible abduction of a Mexican national from Mexico over Mexico’s formal objection.

Alvarez-Machain, a Mexican citizen and resident, was indicted for alleged participation in the kidnapping and murder of a DEA agent and the agent’s pilot. The district court found that persons paid to act at DEA behest abducted him from his medical office in Guadalajara and brought him to the United States. Official responsibility does not imply that regular DEA personnel physically performed the seizure. The Government did not contest those findings on appeal. Mexico formally protested and consistently demanded his return. The district court denied dismissal on outrageous-governmental-conduct grounds, found that the Treaty violation barred trial for lack of personal jurisdiction, discharged him, and ordered immediate repatriation. Its published order did not separately dismiss the indictment. The Ninth Circuit characterized its action as affirming dismissal and repatriation under Verdugo-Urquidez and stated that dismissal was required. The Government disputed that dismissal characterization; the Court reviews the appellate treaty-based trial bar and return disposition without inventing a separate district dismissal order. No extradition proceeding under the Treaty produced this transfer. The allegations of participation in the charged crimes are not adjudicated by this event.

The United States seeks reversal and restoration of its prosecution. Respondent seeks preservation of dismissal and repatriation on the Treaty ground. No acquittal, damages award, or adjudication of a freestanding customary-international-law claim is sought within the granted question.

## Controlling holdings

### This Treaty does not bar prosecution after the abduction

**Holding and operative rule.** The United States–Mexico Extradition Treaty does not prohibit this abduction or make extradition its exclusive means of obtaining a defendant’s presence. Because the Treaty supplies no restriction barring this prosecution, the Ker–Frisbie rule permits trial notwithstanding the DEA-sponsored forcible transfer and Mexico’s protest; the treaty-based dismissal and repatriation order must be reversed.

**Authority.** White’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas; five votes support the treaty construction, its application, and resulting remand.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that this Treaty does not withdraw the court’s power to try respondent. Its text creates obligations to surrender persons in specified circumstances and prescribes procedures when extradition is invoked. Article9 reserves a decision within that process; it does not state that every acquisition of custody must occur through it. Rauscher enforces limits attached to an actual treaty surrender, and Cook enforces an established territorial seizure restriction. Neither requires courts to infer this different prohibition from an agreement that supplies none. Ker and Frisbie therefore govern the consequence for trial authority. Official sponsorship and Mexico’s protest make the conduct different from a private kidnapping, but do not themselves add a term to the Treaty. General international-law objections likewise cannot establish a particular extradition obligation without support in the compact or relevant extradition practice. The Court’s conclusion permits prosecution; it does not authorize abduction, adjudicate guilt, or resolve the conduct’s legality under other law. Diplomatic return outside this Treaty remains an Executive matter.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Ker v. Illinois, 119 U.S.436 (1886): applied to trial authority after this official international abduction because the Treaty supplies no contrary limitation; not treated as affirmative authorization to seize.
- Frisbie v. Collins, 342 U.S.519 (1952): retained and applied for the distinction between unlawful capture and the power to conduct a lawful trial.
- United States v. Rauscher, 119 U.S.407 (1886): retained; its enforceable treaty limitation follows actual extradition on specified terms and does not make this different Treaty exclusive.
- Cook v. United States, 288 U.S.102 (1933): distinguished; an established treaty restriction on governmental seizure controls there, and no comparable restriction is found here.
- Air France v. Saks, 470 U.S.392 (1985), and Valentine v. United States ex rel. Neidecker, 299 U.S.5 (1936): applied to begin with the Treaty’s terms and determine the adopted obligation rather than assume it.

## Alternative paths

**Reverse on nonexclusive treaty reading.** Extradition obligations specify a means of formal surrender, not the exclusive way a defendant may be brought before a court. Article 9 preserves the requested State's discretion within that process; it does not expressly withdraw ordinary trial authority when no process was invoked. Ker and Frisbie supply the background trial rule; Rauscher concerns a surrender actually made under treaty conditions. Strongest counterargument: government-sponsored abduction nullifies the negotiated discretion and differs from Ker's private act. Response sufficient for this path: misconduct and sovereign remedies do not themselves create a prosecution bar in an otherwise nonexclusive treaty.

**Affirm on an implied treaty limitation.** Under Rauscher, the treaty's bilateral process and Article 9's reserved decision are operative obligations, not empty forms. A party cannot avoid those obligations by ordering a seizure that the other sovereign has expressly protested. Ker describes a kidnapping not purportedly authorized by the United States, making governmental responsibility a relevant distinction. Strongest counterargument: the treaty says nothing prohibiting seizure and lawful surrender outside extradition is possible. Response sufficient for this path: nonexclusive consensual transfer does not establish permission for a treaty party's unilateral coercion; the needed implication is confined to this official protested transfer.

## Verdugo: inherited legal basis and material limits

The Ninth Circuit's majority held that government-authorized or sponsored abduction without the other treaty party's consent violates the extradition treaty. A timely defendant's objection, an outstanding official protest and the other nation's willingness to receive repatriation prevent trial jurisdiction. It remanded only for an evidentiary hearing on actual U.S. responsibility, Mexican consent and related matters, retained appellate jurisdiction, and did not make those contested facts itself. A protest withdrawn, or an offer of return rejected, would remove the treaty barrier under that circuit's theory. These conditional findings and retained jurisdiction belong to Verdugo, not Alvarez-Machain.

Browning agreed that Article 9 specifically barred the alleged seizure of a Mexican national, reserving the majority's broader rule for all treaties/persons. He objected to treating Mexico's ambiguous protest in Verdugo as necessarily demanding return and would remand the derivative-right question. His complete separate opinion expressly contrasts Alvarez-Machain: Mexico's request there was prompt and unequivocal, called for return and Mexican investigation/trial, and the November 9, 1990 Consul General letter reaffirmed it. That disagreement therefore supplies no need for another protest hearing in Alvarez-Machain.
