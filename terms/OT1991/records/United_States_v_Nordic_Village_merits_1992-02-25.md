## Event and chronology

United States v. Nordic Village, Inc.; No. 90-1629; OT1991. Historical reporter anchor: 503 U.S. 30 (1992). Merits decision, 1992-02-25. Argued December 9, 1991. On writ of certiorari to the Sixth Circuit, 915 F.2d 1049. The Sixth Circuit affirmed an order requiring the IRS to pay $20,000 to the bankruptcy trustee.

Questions: Whether original Bankruptcy Code §106(c) waives federal sovereign immunity for this monetary recovery, and whether general bankruptcy jurisdiction, an asserted property proceeding or trust principles independently sustain the judgment.

Chronology: The three February 25 decisions are uncoordinated and share the law effective through January 27; none uses another February 25 decision as entering law.

Reservations concerning a different genuine property case do not decide that reserved issue.

Relief sought and material record: The United States seeks reversal; the trustee seeks restoration of estate assets through the affirmed money judgment. Nordic Village entered Chapter 11 in March 1984. Officer Josef Lah withdrew corporate money and used $20,000 to pay his individual federal tax liability. The trustee avoided the unauthorized postpetition transfer under §549 and obtained recovery under §550. The judgment is for payment of a sum, not return of an identified fund within the bankruptcy court’s control. Sovereign immunity was raised on appeal; the threshold remains available. No claim-linked waiver under §106(a) or (b) supplies this judgment.

Stage before: plenary merits review. Stage after: Supreme Court review ends with reversal. The $20,000 personal judgment is set aside. ## Law of the decision

### Original §106(c) does not clearly authorize this monetary recovery

**Holding and operative rule:** Original Bankruptcy Code §106(c) does not unequivocally waive the United States’ immunity from an affirmative personal money judgment recovering an unauthorized transfer under §550. A plausible construction giving the clause binding nonmonetary or claim-determination effect defeats the required clear consent to this monetary remedy; §106(a) and (b) retain their express claim and offset operations.

**Authority:** Scalia’s opinion, joined by Stone, White, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; seven of nine Justices control the statutory threshold.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that applying bankruptcy provisions to government does not by itself authorize every monetary remedy against the Treasury. Irwin requires unequivocal statutory consent, while Hoffman supplies persuasive statutory analysis without a majority holding binding this federal case. Subsections 106(a) and (b) expressly address claims and offsets; subsection (c) instead makes designated provisions applicable and determinations binding. Reading its paragraphs together allows meaningful determinations of governmental rights and estate liabilities without an affirmative payment order. Its introductory exception also permits a reading preserving the neighboring limits on monetary claims. The trustee’s broader construction is plausible, but that does not make it unequivocal. Legislative history cannot supply the clear statutory expression missing from the enacted provision. Section 550 establishes ordinary recovery authority but does not answer the separate sovereign-consent question. The decision does not approve Lah’s diversion, render subsection (c) ineffective, resolve state constitutional immunity, or determine the operation of a different waiver.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Irwin v. Department of Veterans Affairs*, 498 U.S. 89 — applied: the particular monetary remedy requires unequivocal statutory consent.
- *United States v. Mitchell*, 445 U.S. 535 — applied for the same express-consent requirement.
- *United States v. King*, 395 U.S. 1 — applied for the same express-consent requirement.
- *Hoffman v. Connecticut Department of Income Maintenance*, 492 U.S. 96 — its plurality’s statutory reasoning is adopted for this federal issue; its fractured state judgment is not treated as controlling federal-waiver precedent.
- *Ruckelshaus v. Sierra Club*, 463 U.S. 680 — applied: consent is not enlarged beyond what Congress’s language requires.

### Jurisdictional and property labels do not sustain this money judgment

**Holding and operative rule:** A general grant of jurisdiction over bankruptcy-estate property in §1334(d), an asserted trust interest, or the origin of transferred money in estate assets does not supply federal consent to this personal money judgment. Because the trustee seeks a sum rather than identified property under the court’s control, this order cannot be sustained as a genuine property adjudication or as equitable recoupment.

**Authority:** Scalia’s opinion, joined by the same seven Justices, resolves each alternative ground necessary to the threshold disposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court also holds that the judgment cannot be preserved by changing its label. Blatchford distinguishes a court’s jurisdiction over a class of claims from the sovereign’s amenability to the requested relief. Section 1334(d) provides the former and does not conceal the latter. The trustee obtained a general obligation to pay $20,000; no identified property supports the asserted proceeding against a particular fund. Whiting Pools required return of tangible debtor property seized before bankruptcy and did not establish consent to payment of money from the Treasury. Begier’s attention to particular property does not turn the historical source of these funds into a presently identified fund. Nor do trust principles governing private owners enact sovereign consent. Bull concerns equitable recoupment, whose limited operation Dalm preserves; this affirmative recovery is not that defense. Genuinely distinct specific-property remedies require their own facts and authority and are not adjudicated on an invented record here.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *United States v. Whiting Pools, Inc.*, 462 U.S. 198 — distinguished: tangible debtor-property turnover does not authorize this Treasury payment.
- *Begier v. IRS*, 496 U.S. 53 — distinguished: no identified fund supports the actual order.
- *United States v. Shaw*, 309 U.S. 495 — preserved as contrary to inferring monetary consent from a property characterization.
- *Bull v. United States*, 295 U.S. 247 — equitable recoupment retains its limited defensive operation; it does not sustain this affirmative award.
- *United States v. Dalm*, 494 U.S. 596 — equitable recoupment retains its limited defensive operation; it does not sustain this affirmative award.

* **Stone's Remedy and Remand Position:** Set aside the personal money judgment for want of the required sovereign consent. Leave genuinely distinct, properly supported remedies for ordinary proceedings; invent neither a traceable res nor a substitute award.

## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Consent to the particular remedy

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Decide the sovereign-immunity threshold before enforcing §550 against the Treasury.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply the entering unequivocal-waiver rule, with independent attention to the whole enacted provision. Hoffman is persuasive on text, not a binding statutory majority.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** The original §106(c) does not unequivocally authorize the personal monetary judgment entered here.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The clause's reference to immunity is significant and must do work, but its specified consequence is that applicable provisions and determinations bind governmental units. That consequence can resolve claims and rights without authorizing every available monetary remedy. Neighboring express claim and offset waivers reinforce the distinction. The broader reading is plausible; the existing clear-consent rule requires more than plausibility. Section 550 creates recovery authority in ordinary cases, but it does not eliminate the separate question whether this sovereign has consented to that remedy. Respect for creditors' lawful entitlements does not authorize a court to fill the statutory gap by equitable preference.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** No validation of the diversion, no holding that §106(c) has no effect, and no decision about state immunity or the validity of a later express waiver. There is no occasion to replace the native immunity rule.

### Issue 2: No conversion of a money judgment into a res

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Address each alternative offered to sustain the judgment.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply the distinction between jurisdiction over estate property and sovereign consent to pay money.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Neither §1334(d) nor trust terminology supplies the missing waiver for this order.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The order demands $20,000 from the United States, rather than adjudicating an identified fund in the bankruptcy court's possession. Its historical origin in corporate assets does not change the ordered remedy. A trust theory about beneficial ownership likewise cannot alone waive immunity.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Genuine specific-property proceedings remain a different question requiring their actual facts and legal authority.
